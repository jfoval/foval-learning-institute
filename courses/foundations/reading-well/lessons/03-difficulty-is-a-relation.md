Here is a paragraph of ordinary English. Every word in it is a word you know.

> He came round the wicket and got one to leave her off a length, and she followed it and edged to second slip, who put it down. Two overs later the captain brought on the off-spinner, set a leg slip, and she was caught off the glove for eleven.

If you follow cricket, you have just read a small story with a beginning and an end, and you can say who did what and why the captain made the change he made.

If you do not, you read every word and got nothing. Not a vague impression: nothing. You cannot say who was out, or how, or whether eleven is a lot.

Now notice what did not cause that. No word in the paragraph is technical in the sense of being unfamiliar. Round, wicket, length, followed, edged, slip, overs, captain, glove. You know all of them.

## Difficulty is a relation, not a property

This lesson is about one idea, and it changes what you do when a book defeats you.

Difficulty is not a property of a text, the way length is. It is a relation between a text and a particular reader. The cricket paragraph is trivial for one reader and impenetrable for another, and the paragraph did not change.

That sounds obvious when it is put that way, and almost nobody acts as though it were true. The usual response to a page that will not open is to conclude something about yourself, and the sentence people reach for is "I am a bad reader". Sometimes the honest version of that sentence is "I lack the background this text assumes", and those two diagnoses point at completely different actions.

## The mechanism: every text leaves most of itself out

Here is why background matters as much as it does.

No writer writes everything down. It would be impossible, and the result would be unreadable. So every text states some things and leaves the rest to be supplied by the reader: the relations between the things named, the reason one sentence follows another, the significance of a number.

My cricket paragraph never says that second slip is a fielding position, that dropping a catch gives the batter another life, that an off-spinner is a different kind of bowler from the one who came before, that a leg slip is set for a particular kind of edge, or that eleven is a low score. It assumes all of it. A reader who has it fills those gaps without noticing they were gaps.

So understanding is not the passive reception of what is on the page. It is the page plus what you bring, and when what you bring is missing, you are doing a different and much harder job than the fluent reader beside you, on the same words.

:::predict Before the study: think of a text that defeated you recently. Was it that you did not know the words, or that you knew the words and could not see what they added up to?
Most people, asked to be specific, land on the second. Which is worth noticing, because the action people take is nearly always the one that fits the first: look up the terms, reread more slowly.

Looking up terms helps when terms are the problem. The rest of the time it hands you a definition and leaves the relations between the definitions exactly as absent as they were.
:::

## The study everybody quotes, and the reason to hold it loosely

The famous demonstration is Recht and Leslie, 1988.[1] Sixty-four junior-high students were sorted two ways at once: by measured reading ability, and by how much they knew about baseball. That crossing is the whole design, because it produces a group who read well and know nothing about baseball, and a group who read poorly and know the game.

All of them read a passage describing half an inning, and then reconstructed what had happened using wooden pieces on a model field, which is a clever way of measuring understanding without measuring writing.

The result is the one the study is famous for: the poor readers who knew baseball did as well as the good readers who did not. Knowledge of the subject compensated for measured reading ability.

That is a striking finding and it gets quoted constantly, including by people who have not looked at it closely. So here is the part that usually gets left off.

In 2025, Reynolds, Hattan and Markham [reviewed nineteen baseball studies](https://doi.org/10.1002/rrq.575) published between 1978 and 2018, and found that thirteen of them used the same two measures of baseball knowledge.[2] Their argument is that the passages in these studies demand "trivia" and specialised vocabulary rather than conceptual knowledge, and that they are "uniquely designed to make non-baseball fans appear to be poor comprehenders of that text".[2]

Read that carefully, because it is easy to over-read in either direction. The review is not saying that prior knowledge does not matter. It is saying that this family of studies may be measuring familiarity with baseball jargon rather than the deeper thing it claims to measure, and that a literature with thirteen studies sharing two instruments is narrower than its citation count suggests.

So where does that leave the claim?

The claim that prior knowledge is a large determinant of comprehension is well supported, from many directions, and is not in doubt. The flagship demonstration of it is weaker than its fame. Both of those are true at once, and holding them together is a skill this course wants you to have, because you will meet the pattern constantly: a famous study, a real finding, and a gap between the two that nobody mentions when they cite it.

Notice also that my cricket paragraph is open to exactly the same objection. It leans on jargon: slip, over, off-spinner. A fairer test would use plain words and hide the difficulty in the relations between them, which is what the next section does.

:::checkpoint A friend says "the baseball study was debunked, so prior knowledge doesn't really matter". Answer in two sentences, and say what each sentence is doing.
The first sentence concedes the narrow point: a 2025 review argues that the baseball texts test familiarity with the game's vocabulary rather than conceptual knowledge, so the study is weaker evidence than its reputation. The second sentence refuses the wide one: the claim that prior knowledge drives comprehension does not rest on that study, and a critique of one demonstration is not a refutation of the thing demonstrated.

What the two sentences are doing is separating a finding from its flagship, which is the move that keeps you from throwing away a well-supported claim because its most-quoted example turned out to be shaky.
:::

## Three obstacles, and how to tell them apart

When a passage defeats you, one of three things is usually happening. They feel identical from the inside and they need different responses, so the diagnosis is the skill.

**Vocabulary.** There are words you do not know, and they are load-bearing. The signature is that you can point at them. The fix is a dictionary or a glossary, and it is quick.

**Missing background.** You know every word and cannot say what the passage claims. The signature is the cricket paragraph: fluent reading, no meaning. The fix is not in this text. It is another text, easier, on the same subject.

**Syntax.** One particular sentence will not resolve, and you can feel yourself rereading it and losing your place. The signature is that you can restate the paragraph around it but not that sentence. The fix is structural: find the main verb, find its subject, and put the qualifications to one side.

Here is a sentence built to be the third case, in plain words:

> The clause that the tenant, having given the notice that the agreement, unless the landlord had objected within the period the schedule sets out, required, believed sufficient was not.

Every word there is ordinary. The difficulty is entirely in the nesting, and the fix is mechanical. Find the main clause: *the clause ... was not [sufficient]*. Then take the interruptions one at a time. Nothing about the world is missing. You are untangling, not learning.

That is the distinction worth carrying: with missing background, more time on this page will not help, because what you need is not on this page. With syntax, more time on this page is exactly what helps.

:::checkpoint Which obstacle is this, and what would you do? "The yield rose because the price fell, which is what you would expect, and the fund's duration meant the effect on its value was larger than the move in the yield suggests."
Missing background. Every word is ordinary English, the sentence is short and its structure is simple, and you can restate it word by word. What you cannot do, without knowing what duration means for a bond fund, is see why the second clause follows from the first or why the effect was larger.

The fix is not to reread it. It is to read something that explains the relation between price, yield and duration, and then come back. Fifteen minutes elsewhere buys you this sentence and every sentence like it.
:::

## What to do when the diagnosis is "missing background"

This is the case people handle worst, because the honest response feels like defeat.

The response is to find an easier text on the same subject, survey it with the six steps from lesson 2, and read enough of it to get the relations the hard text assumes. Then go back.

Three things make that less painful than it sounds.

It is usually much less reading than you fear. What you are missing is a small number of relations, not a subject. The cricket paragraph needs about a page of explanation, not a season.

Surveying tells you whether the easier book has what you need, in twenty minutes, before you commit to it. That is what lesson 2's procedure is for, and this is its most common use.

And you have already met the rule that covers the other half of this, in lesson 2: on a first pass through a hard book, read it through without stopping to look up or ponder what you do not understand.[3] Now you know why that rule works. The first pass tells you which gaps are load-bearing, so the trip to the easier book is aimed.

## What people get wrong

**"I am a bad reader."** Sometimes true, and much more often a misdiagnosis of missing background. The test is the cricket paragraph: if you can read fluently and understand nothing, the problem is not the reading.

**"Difficulty is a property of the book."** It is a relation. This is also the mistake behind reading-ease scores, which count syllables and sentence lengths and cannot see the thing that actually made a passage hard for you.

**"Reading it again more slowly will fix it."** It fixes syntax. It does nothing for missing background, because the second pass has the same gaps as the first. [How to Learn Anything](/courses/how-to-learn-anything) has the evidence on why rereading feels more useful than it is, in its lessons on retrieval and on study techniques; this lesson adds the reason rereading cannot help here.

**"Looking things up as I go is the responsible approach."** On a second pass, yes. On a first pass it is how people spend a month on chapter 1 and abandon the book, which is the behaviour Adler's rule exists to prevent.

**"If I need an easier book I should not be reading the hard one."** The easier book is how you become the reader the hard one assumes. That is a route, not a retreat, and the alternative on offer is not reading the hard book successfully; it is reading it unsuccessfully.

## Practice

:::exercise Diagnose one text that beat you
Find a text that defeated you in the last year. A paper, a chapter, a contract, a manual.

1. Reread one paragraph of it and write down which of the three obstacles you hit. Be specific: name the words you did not know, or the relation you could not see, or the sentence that would not resolve.
2. If it was vocabulary, look the words up and reread the paragraph. Note whether that was enough.
3. If it was syntax, take the worst sentence and rewrite it as three short ones. Note how long that took.
4. If it was missing background, find one easier text on the same subject. Survey it with lesson 2's six steps, decide on the evidence whether it has what you need, and read its first chapter. Then go back to the original paragraph and read it again.

Write one line at the end: what the obstacle actually was, and how long the fix took. Most people are surprised by the second number.
:::
