Lessons 5 and 6 leave you holding a good deal. An outline of the chapter. The author's terms, in his sense. The argument, set out in lines. A margin full of marks, including the places where you wrote an X.

This lesson is what those X marks are for, and the first thing it will tell you is that they aren't disagreements yet.

## Rule 9, and why the order isn't politeness

Adler's third stage opens with a rule that reads like manners and isn't.

> Do not say you agree, disagree, or suspend judgment, until you can say "I understand."[1]

Take that literally, because it is meant literally. Until you can state the author's argument in your own words, you have no verdict available to you. Not disagreement, not agreement, and not the third option either. Suspending judgement is also a judgement, and it also has to be earned.

What goes wrong when the order is reversed is not that you are rude. It is that you end up arguing with something nobody wrote. A reader who forms a verdict at page thirty has built it out of the first thirty pages plus their own expectations about what the rest will say, and when the book does something else the verdict does not update, because it was never attached to the book in the first place.

This is also why the effort of lesson 5 is worth the trouble. An outline is the evidence that you have the thing you are about to judge.

## Rules 10 and 11

Two more before the grounds themselves.

**Rule 10: do not disagree disputatiously.**[1] Disagreement is not a contest to be won, and a reader who is reading in order to disagree will find something regardless of what the page says. The practical form of the rule is that you should be able to state the author's position in a way the author would accept before you say what is wrong with it.

That is the same move [Logic and Argument](/courses/logic-and-argument/) teaches from the other side, where it goes by the name of steelmanning. This course does not re-teach it and leans on it. Go and take that course if you have not.

**Rule 11: present good reasons for any critical judgement.**[1] This is the one that separates knowledge from personal opinion, and Adler means the distinction seriously. "I disagree" is a report about you. "I disagree because the second premise is asserted and never argued for" is a claim about the book, and a claim about the book is a thing somebody else can check, agree with, or refute.

The test is simple and it is worth applying to your own verdicts. **Can somebody else disagree with your objection?** If not, you have described a feeling.

## The four grounds, and nothing else

Here is the part of the method that does the work. When you disagree with an author, you are making one of exactly four claims.[1]

**The author is uninformed.** He lacks a piece of knowledge that bears on his conclusion. You owe the reader two things: what the missing knowledge is, and why it changes the conclusion. Notice this is not a criticism of the author's character, and it is often nobody's fault: a writer in 1890 did not have what a writer in 1990 had.

**The author is misinformed.** He asserts something contrary to fact, and the fact was available. The difference from the first ground is the availability, and it matters, because the two objections require different evidence from you.

**The author is illogical.** The conclusion does not follow from what he has given. This is the ground lesson 5 prepared you for: an argument set out in three lines shows you the gap that a paragraph hides.

**The author's account is incomplete.** He has not solved all the problems he set out to solve, or has not seen all the ones his subject raises. This ground is different from the other three and the next section is about why.

Those four are the whole list. An objection that cannot be placed on one of them is usually a report of taste, or of a prior belief you brought with you, and naming the ground is what makes the difference visible.

Why does naming it help? Because each ground tells you what you would have to produce to be right. If you say the author is misinformed, you have to name the fact. If you say he is illogical, you have to point at the step. The demand for evidence is built into the category, which is why the sorting does more work than it looks like it should.

## Three objections to one paragraph

Take the Ruskin paragraph you marked in lesson 6, on looking intensely at words. Here are three objections a reader might make to it, sorted.

**Objection 1: "He offers his own certainty in place of an argument."** The parenthesis reads "(I *know* I am right in this)", and nothing in the paragraph supports the claim that follows it.[2]

That is **illogical**, and the evidence is easy to produce: set the paragraph out as an argument and the premise slot is empty. Ruskin asserts, illustrates with an etymology, and asserts again. The objection is checkable, which is what makes it an objection rather than an impression.

Notice what the objection does not claim. It does not claim Ruskin is wrong. It claims that this paragraph does not establish that he is right, and those are different verdicts with different consequences.

**Objection 2: "The entire difference between education and non-education does not consist in accuracy with words."** Ruskin says it does.[2]

That is **uninformed**, and this course can produce the missing knowledge, because lesson 3 was about it. What makes a passage hard for a particular reader is very largely what that reader already knows about its subject, and a reader can be perfectly accurate with every word on the page and still not know what the page claims. Ruskin in 1865 did not have that literature. He is not being foolish; he is working without something that was not there to be had.

Which is the shape of most good objections on this ground. You are not catching the author out. You are saying what he would have needed.

**Objection 3: "Ruskin is insufferable about this."** He is rather.

And that is not on any of the four grounds, which is why it is worth including. Sort it and it dissolves: it is not that he lacks knowledge, nor that he asserts a falsehood, nor that his conclusion fails to follow, nor that his account is incomplete. It is that his manner is irritating, which is a fact about the reading and not about the argument.

This is the most useful thing the sorting does, and it is why the exercise is worth the four minutes. Most of what a reader wants to say after a difficult page is objection 3 wearing the clothes of objection 1.

:::predict A reader objects to a chapter on the ground that it "does not engage with the obvious counterexample". Before you read on, which of the four grounds is that, and what does the reader owe?
It depends on what they mean, which is the point. If the counterexample shows the author asserted something false, it is misinformed and they owe the fact. If it shows the conclusion does not follow, it is illogical and they owe the step. If the author's argument survives the counterexample but his account does not reach it, it is incomplete, and the next section says what follows from that.

A reader who has not decided which of those three they mean has not yet made an objection. They have noticed something, which is where an objection starts and not where it finishes.
:::

## The wrinkle, and the part nobody quotes

Here is the rule that makes this a method rather than a set of manners, and readers do not like it.

If you cannot show that the author is uninformed, misinformed or illogical, then, in Adler's words, **you must agree, at least in part**.[1] You may still suspend judgement on the whole in light of the fourth ground, incompleteness. But you may not simply decline.

Read that again, because it takes something away from you. The ordinary practice is that a reader is entitled to remain unpersuaded for no stated reason, and this rule says you are not. If you have understood the argument, and you cannot name what is wrong with it, the honest verdict is agreement with the part that holds.

**Incompleteness is not a way out of that.** It is a separate finding, and it produces a different verdict: agreement on what was argued, and suspended judgement on the whole, with the gap named.

Take Ruskin again. Suppose you grant everything he argued. Accuracy with words is worth having, a reader who skims a thousand books may have learned less than one who read ten pages precisely, and the claim survives. Your remaining objection is that he says nothing at all about the reader who is accurate with every word and still cannot say what the paragraph claims, which is the reader lesson 3 spent its whole length on.

That is incompleteness. And what it earns you is not disagreement. It earns you this: *what he argued, I accept; what he did not reach is the larger half of the problem, so I suspend judgement on his claim about the entire difference between education and non-education.*

That verdict is uncomfortable to write because it concedes and withholds in the same breath. It is also more informative than either "I agree" or "I disagree", and it is the verdict most often true of a good book.

:::checkpoint You have read a chapter arguing that a particular teaching method raises test scores. The evidence is well presented and you can find nothing wrong with it. You still think the method would be bad for students, because of something the chapter never discusses, which is what it costs in class time. Write your verdict.
The verdict is agreement in part with suspended judgement on the whole, and the ground is incompleteness. Something like: the chapter establishes that the method raises test scores, and I accept that; it does not weigh that gain against the class time the method consumes, so I suspend judgement on whether it should be adopted.

What you may not do is disagree, because you have not found the author uninformed, misinformed or illogical about the thing he actually argued. The cost objection is real and it is about a different question from the one the chapter answered.

Notice what the discipline bought you. Instead of a verdict, you now have a research question, which is what the class-time cost is. That is a better thing to leave a chapter with.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Criticism means finding fault."** It means judging, and the judgement can go either way. A reader who has worked through the four grounds and found none of them is doing criticism when they write down that the argument holds, and doing it better than a reader who found a fault because they were looking for one.

**"I disagree" is a finished thought.** It is the beginning of one. The finished version names a ground and produces what that ground requires.

**"I can judge it from the summary."** You can judge the summary. Rule 9 is about the book. This is worth saying plainly, because a great deal of what circulates as criticism of a book is criticism of an account of it, and the two are often not the same argument at all.

**"Suspending judgement is a cop-out."** It is the hardest of the three verdicts to earn, because you have to show both that the argument holds as far as it goes and that it does not go far enough. A reader who suspends judgement without doing that is not suspending judgement. They are declining to read.

**"Being unconvinced is a reason."** It is a symptom. Sometimes the cause is one of the four grounds and you have not found it yet, in which case keep looking. Sometimes the cause is that you disagreed before you started, in which case that is what to write down.

## Practice

:::exercise One page, one ground
Take the chapter you worked in lesson 5 and the marks you made on it in lesson 6. Allow 40 minutes.

Write one page with two halves.

The first half is what the author argued. Not what you thought of it. One paragraph, in your own words, and it should be recognisable to somebody who has read the chapter.

The second half is your verdict, on exactly one of the four grounds, with the reason. Name the ground by name. If it is incompleteness, say what is missing and what you are suspending judgement on.

Then the check that makes this worth doing: **if you cannot name the ground, you do not yet have an objection.** Write "no ground found, agreement in part" instead, and mean it. That is a real result and you will get it more often than you expect.
:::

:::exercise Sort your X marks
Go back through the marks you made in lesson 6 and take every X. Allow 20 minutes, in a separate sitting.

Put each one against the four grounds. Count how many land on a ground, how many are objection 3 in disguise, and how many turn out to be places where you had not yet understood the sentence.

That third pile is the interesting one. It is the rule 9 failure caught in your own handwriting.
:::

## Connections

Lesson 5 is the precondition this lesson names. The outline, the terms and the reconstructed argument are what rule 9 means by "I understand", and without them a verdict has nothing to attach to.

Lesson 6's X marks are raw material here rather than conclusions, which is why they are worth making fast and sorting slowly.

[Logic and Argument](/courses/logic-and-argument/) teaches the same discipline from the other direction: how to build an argument that survives this treatment, and what the common failures are called. This lesson names the third ground, illogical, and hands you to that course for the taxonomy under it.

Lesson 10 needs all of this, because reading several authors against each other means judging each of them first, and an unjudged author cannot be compared with anything.
