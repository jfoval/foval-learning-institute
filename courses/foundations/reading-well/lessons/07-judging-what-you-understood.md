Lessons 5 and 6 leave you holding a good deal. An outline of the chapter. The author's terms, in his sense. The argument, set out in lines. A margin full of marks, including the places where you wrote an X.

This lesson is what those X marks are for, and the first thing it will tell you is that they aren't disagreements yet.

## Rule 9, and why the order isn't politeness

Adler's third stage opens with a rule that reads like manners and isn't.

> Do not say you agree, disagree, or suspend judgment, until you can say "I understand."[1]

Take that literally, because it is meant literally. Until you can state the author's argument in your own words, you have no verdict available to you. Not disagreement, not agreement, and not the third option either. Suspending judgement counts as a verdict here too, which is worth saying because readers treat it as the absence of one.

What goes wrong when the order is reversed is not that you are rude. It is that you end up arguing with something nobody wrote. A reader who forms a verdict at page thirty has built it out of the first thirty pages plus their own expectations about what the rest will say, and when the book does something else the verdict often does not update, because it was never attached to the book in the first place.

This is also why the effort of lesson 5 is worth the trouble. An outline is the evidence that you have the thing you are about to judge.

## Rules 10 and 11

Two more before the grounds themselves.

**Rule 10: do not disagree disputatiously.**[1] Disagreement is not a contest to be won, and a reader who is reading in order to disagree will find something regardless of what the page says.

[Logic and Argument](/courses/logic-and-argument/), later in this term, puts a practical test on the same instinct: you should be able to state the author's position in a way the author would accept before you say what is wrong with it. This course leans on that rather than re-teaching it.

**Rule 11: present good reasons for any critical judgement.**[1] This is the one that separates knowledge from personal opinion, and Adler means the distinction seriously: an author's propositions, he says, "are nothing but expressions of personal opinion unless they are supported by reasons", and he asks the same of the reader, who must "distinguish between genuine knowledge and mere opinion".[1]

So "I disagree" is a report about you. "I disagree because the second premise is asserted and never argued for" is a claim about the book, and a claim about the book is a thing somebody else can check, agree with, or refute.

The test is simple and it is worth applying to your own verdicts. **Can somebody else disagree with your objection?** If not, you have described a feeling.

## The four grounds, and nothing else

Here is the part of the method that does the work. When you disagree with an author, you are making one of exactly four claims.[1]

**The author is uninformed.** In Adler's words, "he lacks some piece of knowledge that is relevant to the problem he is trying to solve", and to make the objection stick "you must be able yourself to state the knowledge that the author lacks and show how it is relevant, how it makes a difference to his conclusions".[1] So you owe two things, not one. Notice also that this is not a criticism of anyone's character, and it is usually nobody's fault.

Adler's own example here is the book you worked in lesson 5. Darwin "lacked the knowledge of genetics that the work of Mendel and later experimentalists now provides", and Adler calls that ignorance of the mechanism of inheritance "one of the major defects in *The Origin of Species*".[1] Notice what that objection is and is not. It is a claim about what Darwin had to work with. It is not a verdict on his conclusion, and history settled the gap rather than the conclusion.

**The author is misinformed.** "He asserts what is not the case."[1] The difference from the first ground is that the first is a silence and this is a positive claim, and Adler is explicit that the cause does not matter: the error "may be owing to lack of knowledge, but the error is more than that. Whatever its cause, it consists in making assertions contrary to fact."[1] The two grounds ask different evidence of you. For the first you produce the knowledge he lacked. For the second you produce the fact his sentence gets wrong.

**The author is illogical.** He "has committed a fallacy in reasoning", which Adler splits into the conclusion that does not follow and the two statements that cannot both be true, and either way the reader "must be able to show the precise respect in which" it goes wrong.[1] This is the ground lesson 5 prepared you for: an argument set out in three lines shows you the gap that a paragraph hides.

**The author's account is incomplete.** He has not solved all the problems he set out to solve, or has not seen all the ones his subject raises. This ground is different from the other three and the next section is about why.

Those four are the whole list. An objection that cannot be placed on one of them is usually a report of taste, or of a prior belief you brought with you, and naming the ground is what makes the difference visible.

Why does naming it help? Because each ground tells you what you would have to produce to be right. If you say the author is misinformed, you have to name the fact. If you say he is illogical, you have to point at the step. The demand for evidence is built into the category, which is why the sorting does more work than it looks like it should.

## Three objections to one paragraph

Take the Ruskin paragraph you marked in lesson 6, on looking intensely at words. Here are three objections a reader might make to it, sorted.

**Objection 1: "He offers his own certainty in place of a reason."** You marked this one in lesson 6, and here is what marking it was for. The parenthesis reads "(I *know* I am right in this)", and it is doing work in the sentence that no argument in the paragraph does.[2]

Now sort it honestly, and watch it refuse to go cleanly into a box. The paragraph is not empty of reasons: the British Museum contrast is an argument, and it is the one thing in the paragraph a reader could check. What the parenthesis adds is confidence, and confidence is not a premise. So this is not quite the third ground, because Adler's illogical means a fallacy committed, and no step has gone wrong here.

What survives is a narrow objection, and the narrow form is the useful one: the sentence would be exactly as strong without the parenthesis, and a reader who was persuaded by the parenthesis was persuaded by nothing.

Notice what the objection does not claim. It does not claim Ruskin is wrong. It claims that one part of this paragraph does no work, and those are different verdicts with different consequences.

**Objection 2: "Accuracy with words is not enough on its own."** Ruskin writes that if you read ten pages of a good book "letter by letter,—that is to say, with real accuracy,—you are for evermore in some measure an educated person".[2]

That is **uninformed**, and this course can name what is missing, because lesson 3 was about it. One of the three things that makes a passage hard for a particular reader is what that reader already knows about its subject, and a reader can be perfectly accurate with every word on the page and still not know what the page claims, because the page is leaving the relations between those words implicit. What Ruskin would have needed is the finding that the obstacle is often what the reader brings to the page rather than the care they take over it, and in 1865 it was not there to be had: it was not studied until the twentieth century.

Which is the shape of most good objections on this ground. You are not catching the author out. You are saying what he would have needed, which is what Adler's rule asks of you.

**Objection 3: "Ruskin is insufferable about this."** Plenty of readers put that page down thinking it, and it is worth taking seriously rather than being embarrassed about.

And it is not on any of the four grounds, which is why it is worth including. Sort it and it dissolves: it is not that he lacks knowledge, nor that he asserts a falsehood, nor that his conclusion fails to follow, nor that his account is incomplete. It is that his manner is irritating, which is a fact about the reading and not about the argument.

This is the most useful thing the sorting does, and it is why the exercise is worth the four minutes. My own experience of doing it is that a good deal of what a reader wants to say after a difficult page is objection 3 wearing the clothes of objection 1.

:::predict A reader objects to a chapter on the ground that it "does not engage with the obvious counterexample". Before you read on, which of the four grounds is that, and what does the reader owe?
It depends on what they mean, which is the point. If the counterexample shows the author asserted something false, it is misinformed and they owe the fact. If it shows the conclusion does not follow, it is illogical and they owe the step. If the author's argument survives the counterexample but his account does not reach it, it is incomplete, and the next section says what follows from that.

A reader who has not decided which of those three they mean has not yet made an objection. They have noticed something, which is where an objection starts and not where it finishes.
:::

## The wrinkle, and the part nobody quotes

:::predict You have understood a chapter. You have looked for all four grounds and found none of them. You are still not persuaded. Before you read on, write down what you think the method entitles you to say.
Most readers write something like "I can say I am not convinced". The method does not allow it, and the next paragraph says so in Adler's own words, which are blunter than anything in this lesson.

If you wrote "I have to agree", you have it, and you may be surprised how much it costs to mean it.
:::

Here is the rule that makes this a method rather than a set of manners, and readers do not like it.

Adler puts it about as hard as it can be put. "You have no freedom of will about this. It is not your sacred privilege to decide whether you are going to agree or disagree. If you have not been able to show that the author is uninformed, misinformed, or illogical on relevant matters, you simply cannot disagree. You must agree."[1] And he names the sentence he is forbidding, which you have said and I have said: "I find nothing wrong with your premises, and no errors in reasoning, but I don't agree with your conclusions."[1]

Read that again, because it takes something away from you. The ordinary practice is that a reader is entitled to remain unpersuaded for no stated reason, and this rule says you are not. If you have understood the argument, and you cannot name what is wrong with it, the honest verdict is agreement with the part that holds.

**Incompleteness is not a way out of that**, and Adler is careful about it: the fourth point "is strictly not a basis for disagreement. It is critically adverse only to the extent that it marks the limitations of the author's achievement." What it permits is narrower and stranger. A reader who agrees in part, having found no other ground, "may, nevertheless, suspend judgment on the whole, in the light of this fourth point about the book's incompleteness".[1] So it is a separate finding producing a different verdict: agreement on what was argued, suspended judgement on the whole, and the gap named.

Take Ruskin again. Suppose you grant everything he argued. Accuracy with words is worth having, a reader who skims a thousand books may have learned less than one who read ten pages precisely, and the claim survives. Your remaining objection is that his account never reaches the reader who is accurate with every word and still cannot say what the paragraph claims, which is the reader lesson 3 spent its whole length on.

That is incompleteness. And what it earns you is not disagreement. It earns you this: *what he argued, I accept; what he did not reach is the larger half of the problem, so I suspend judgement on his claim that the entire difference between education and non-education, "as regards the merely intellectual part of it", consists in this accuracy.*

Notice that this is a different objection from the second one above, and the difference is the whole of what this section teaches. That one said a condition he offered is not enough on its own, which is a ground for disagreeing with a claim he made. This one accepts everything he argued and says his account does not reach a case his subject raises, which is a gap in what he covered. Only the second earns a suspension, and it is careful with his own qualifier, which is his and which the objection has to leave in.

That verdict is uncomfortable to write because it concedes and withholds in the same breath. It is also more informative than either "I agree" or "I disagree", and in my experience it is the verdict a good book most often deserves.

:::checkpoint You have read a chapter arguing that a particular teaching method raises test scores. The evidence is well presented and you can find nothing wrong with it. You still think the method would be bad for students, because of something the chapter never discusses, which is what it costs in class time. Write your verdict.
The verdict is agreement in part with suspended judgement on the whole, and the ground is incompleteness. Something like: the chapter establishes that the method raises test scores, and I accept that; it does not weigh that gain against the class time the method consumes, so I suspend judgement on whether it should be adopted.

What you may not do is disagree, because you have not found the author uninformed, misinformed or illogical about the thing he actually argued. The cost objection is real and it is about a different question from the one the chapter answered.

Notice what the discipline bought you. Instead of a verdict, you now have a research question, which is what the class-time cost is. That is a better thing to leave a chapter with.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Criticism means finding fault."** It means judging, and the judgement can go either way. A reader who has worked through the four grounds and found none of them is doing criticism when they write down that the argument holds, and doing it better than a reader who found a fault because they were looking for one.

**"I disagree" is a finished thought.** It is the beginning of one. The finished version names a ground and produces what that ground requires.

**"I can judge it from the summary."** You can judge the summary. Rule 9 is about the book. This is worth saying plainly, because a great deal of what circulates as criticism of a book is criticism of an account of it, and the two are often not the same argument at all.

**"Suspending judgement is a cop-out."** It is the hardest of the three verdicts to earn, because you have to show both that the argument holds as far as it goes and that it does not go far enough. A reader who suspends judgement without doing that is not suspending judgement. They are declining to read.

**"Being unconvinced is a reason."** It is a symptom. Sometimes the cause is one of the four grounds and you have not found it yet, in which case keep looking. Sometimes the cause is that you disagreed before you started, in which case that is what to write down.

## Practice

:::exercise One page, one ground
Take the chapter you worked in lesson 5 and the marks you made on it in lesson 6. Allow 40 minutes.

Write one page with two halves.

The first half is what the author argued. Not what you thought of it. One paragraph, in your own words, and it should be recognisable to somebody who has read the chapter.

The second half is your verdict, on exactly one of the four grounds, with the reason. Name the ground by name. If it is incompleteness, say what is missing and what you are suspending judgement on.

Then the check that makes this worth doing: **if you cannot name the ground, you do not yet have an objection.** Write "no ground found, agreement in part" instead, and mean it. That is a real result and you will get it more often than you expect.
:::

:::exercise Sort your X marks
Go back through the marks you made in lesson 6 and take every X. Allow 20 minutes, in a separate sitting.

Put each one against the four grounds. Count how many land on a ground, how many are objection 3 in disguise, and how many turn out to be places where you had not yet understood the sentence.

That third pile is the interesting one. It is the rule 9 failure caught in your own handwriting.
:::

## Connections

Lesson 5 is the precondition this lesson names. The outline, the terms and the reconstructed argument are what rule 9 means by "I understand", and without them a verdict has nothing to attach to.

Lesson 6's X marks are raw material here rather than conclusions, which is why they are worth making fast and sorting slowly.

[Logic and Argument](/courses/logic-and-argument/) teaches the same discipline from the other direction: how to build an argument that survives this treatment, and what the common failures are called. This lesson names the third ground, illogical, and hands you to that course for the taxonomy under it.

Lesson 10 needs all of this, because reading several authors against each other means judging each of them first, and an unjudged author cannot be compared with anything.
