# Outline: Reading Well

*Stage 2, 2026-09-17. Built from SOURCES.md. Ten lessons, a course-end test, one project.*

**The sequence and why it is this one.** The dependency runs: you cannot choose how to read until you
know reading is several things (1); the cheapest high-value skill is the one that decides whether to
read at all (2); the commonest experience of failure is a page that defeats you, which is a fact
about knowledge rather than effort (3); before teaching method it is worth saying what the machine
can and cannot do, so nobody spends the course chasing a speed that is not available (4); then the
core skill, reconstructing an argument (5), the pen that makes it possible (6), and judgement, which
Adler insists comes last (7); then the two genres a general reader most often meets and most often
mishandles, the paper (8) and the document (9); then the level that turns reading into thinking,
which is reading several books against one question (10).

**Conventions for every lesson.** Open with a real text the reader can see, not a description of one.
Every lesson has the reader do something to a text before it explains the method. Every lesson names
its sources by author and year in the body and lists them under `## Sources`. Where a source was read
at abstract or summary level, SOURCES.md says so and the lesson says so too. Adler's rules are quoted
in his words, from the numbering recorded in SOURCES.md; his canon and his confidence about it are
not taught. Lessons 3 and 6 hand off explicitly to How to Learn Anything rather than re-teaching
rereading, highlighting and retrieval, and lesson 6 hands off to Note-Taking and Personal Knowledge
at Term 2.

**Two standing cautions for the drafter.** Carver's five gears (138 / 200 / 300 / 450 / 650 wpm) sit
on the 300 wpm norm Brysbaert corrects to 238. Use the ordering, not the figures, and say why.
Brysbaert is arguing with Carver rather than confirming him: he writes that Carver "ventured" the
model and "estimated" its speeds, and names him among the authors who promoted the figure the paper
corrects. Attribute the gears as a proposal. And Rayner and colleagues 2016 was read only through the APS public summary and
Brysbaert's account of it, so lesson 4 may not claim more than those support.

## Lesson sequence

### 1. Reading is not one activity (~40 min)
- **Objectives:** explain why a single reading speed applied to everything is a mistake; identify,
  for a given text and purpose, which of the four levels is called for; apply Bacon's three-way
  distinction to five texts the reader met this week.
- **Core ideas:** Bacon, 1625: "Some books are to be tasted, others to be swallowed, and some few to
  be chewed and digested", with his own gloss, some read only in parts, some read but not curiously,
  some read wholly with diligence and attention. Adler's four levels, each containing the one below:
  elementary, inspectional, analytical, syntopical. Carver's five gears as a psychologist proposing
  something similar from the other direction: memorizing, learning, normal reading, skimming,
  scanning, each with its own speed. A proposal, not a measurement, and read through Brysbaert. The decision that precedes reading is which of these you are
  doing, and most readers have never been told there is a decision.
- **Mechanism:** a level is defined by what you are trying to get out, and what you are trying to get
  out determines how much of the text has to be reconstructed in your head. Scanning needs one match.
  Analytical reading needs the author's whole argument rebuilt. The effort is set by the goal, not by
  the page.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: the same 3,000-word article read four ways, with what you end up
  able to say after each. (b) With a wrinkle: a text where the right level changes mid-way. A
  contract skimmed until the indemnity clause, which is then chewed. The lesson is that the level is
  chosen per passage, not per document.
- **Misconceptions:** "reading properly means reading every word"; "skimming is cheating" (it is a
  named level with a procedure); "a summary is as good as the book" (Bacon on reading by deputy and
  "distilled books... flashy things", which lesson 10 returns to); "faster is better" (deferred to 4).
- **Practice:** list the last ten things you read of any length. Mark each with the level it got and
  the level it deserved. The gap is the course.
- **Sources:** Bacon 1625 (read in full, Gutenberg); Adler and Van Doren 1972 (read in full, and not
  linked to learners; see `research/reviews/course-wide.md`); Carver via Brysbaert 2019, attributed
  as a proposal.
- **Connects:** everything. Leads to 2, which is the first level taught as a procedure.

### 2. Surveying a book before you read it (~45 min)
- **Objectives:** apply Adler's six-step systematic skimming to a real book inside an hour; state,
  after surveying, what a book argues and how it is built; decide on evidence whether to read it.
- **Core ideas:** inspectional reading has two sublevels and they answer different questions.
  *Systematic skimming* answers "is this worth my week, and what is in it": title page and preface,
  table of contents "as you would a road map before taking a trip", index with crucial terms looked
  up, publisher's blurb, the pivotal chapters' opening and closing summaries, then dipping in, and
  never failing to read the last two or three pages. Adler's budget: "no more than a few minutes, at
  most an hour". *Superficial reading* is the rule for a hard book on a first pass: read it through
  "without ever stopping to look up or ponder the things you do not understand right away".
- **Mechanism:** a book's argument is redundantly encoded in its apparatus. The contents page is the
  outline the author already made; the index is a frequency table of what the book is about; the last
  pages are where authors cannot resist summarising. Surveying reads the encodings instead of the
  text, which is why it is an order of magnitude faster and not an order of magnitude worse.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a survey of a real book, done in front of the reader, step by step,
  ending in a written verdict. It must be a book the reader can legally open, so it has to be public
  domain or openly licensed. **Not Adler's own text**, which was this outline's first choice and is
  ruled out: the only free copy is an unlicensed scan of an in-copyright book. See
  `research/reviews/course-wide.md`. A Gutenberg book with a real apparatus, a contents page, an
  index and a preface, is the replacement to find. (b) With a wrinkle: a book whose apparatus lies. A trade book with a contents page of
  teasing chapter titles and a blurb that is pure puffery, where the survey has to fall back on step 6
  and the last pages. Adler anticipates this and says a bad blurb "in itself can tell you something".
- **Misconceptions:** "I should not judge a book before reading it" (you are judging whether to read
  it, on evidence from the book); "the contents page is for looking things up"; "if a book is hard I
  should go slower on page one" (the superficial-reading rule says the opposite for a first pass).
- **Practice:** survey a book you own and have not read, in under an hour, with a timer. Write the
  four things: what kind of book, what it argues, how it is built, and whether you will read it.
- **Sources:** Adler and Van Doren 1972, the six steps and both sublevels, verbatim; Harvard Library's
  "preview" habit as the modern short form.
- **Connects:** builds on 1's levels; the survey is the first step of 5 and of 10.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's choice of level, now made with evidence rather than a guess.

### 3. Why the same page is easy for one reader and impossible for another (~40 min)
- **Objectives:** explain why difficulty is a relation between a text and a reader rather than a
  property of the text; identify, in a passage that defeated you, whether the obstacle was vocabulary,
  missing background or syntax; build a route into a text that is currently too hard.
- **Core ideas:** comprehension depends heavily on what the reader already knows. Recht and Leslie
  1988 is the famous demonstration, and the lesson teaches it **with its 2025 critique attached**:
  Reynolds and colleagues reviewed 19 baseball studies and argue the texts demand trivia and
  specialised vocabulary rather than conceptual knowledge, and are "uniquely designed to make
  non-baseball fans appear to be poor comprehenders". The underlying claim is well supported from many
  directions; the flagship study is weaker than its fame, and that gap is itself the lesson in how to
  hold a finding. Practical consequence: the fix for a hard book is often a different, easier book
  first, which is not a retreat but a route.
- **Mechanism:** a text leaves most of its content implicit, because writing everything out is
  impossible. Understanding is filling those gaps from what you already have. A reader with the
  background fills them without noticing; a reader without it is doing a different and much harder
  task on the same page.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: one paragraph of ordinary English about cricket, or about a
  liturgy, or about a bond yield, shown to a reader who lacks the background. Every word is known and
  the meaning is not. (b) With a wrinkle: the reverse case, a passage whose difficulty is syntax
  rather than knowledge, where the fix is rereading the sentence structure, not learning a subject.
  Teaching the reader to tell the two apart is the transferable skill.
- **Misconceptions:** "I am a bad reader" (usually "I lack the background for this text");
  "difficulty is a property of the book", which is the readability-score mistake Writing Clearly
  lesson 1 also treats; "reading it again more slowly will fix it"; "looking things up as I go is the
  responsible approach" (it is, on a second pass; on the first it is how people abandon books).
- **Practice:** take a text that beat you. Diagnose the obstacle in writing. Then find and survey, per
  lesson 2, one easier text that would give you the missing background, and read its first chapter.
- **Sources:** Recht and Leslie 1988 (abstract and secondary level, labelled); Reynolds, Hattan and
  Markham 2025 (abstract level, labelled); Adler on superficial reading; Brysbaert 2019 on rates for
  second-language readers.
- **Connects:** explains why lesson 2's survey sometimes returns "not yet"; sets up 5, where coming to
  terms is how a reader builds the missing vocabulary from inside the book.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 2's superficial-reading rule, now with the reason it works.
- **Hand-off:** How to Learn Anything owns rereading and highlighting. Cite its conclusion, link it,
  do not re-teach the evidence.

### 4. What reading costs: your eyes, the page, and the speed you cannot buy (~40 min)
- **Objectives:** state a realistic reading rate for yourself and calculate how long a given text will
  take; explain why reading rates above roughly 500 wpm cost comprehension; compare skimming and
  speed reading and say what each actually delivers.
- **Core ideas:** Brysbaert's meta-analysis, 190 studies and 18,573 participants: **238 wpm for
  non-fiction, 260 for fiction, 183 read aloud**, with most adults between 175 and 300 wpm on
  non-fiction. The widely quoted 300 wpm is an overestimate with a traceable history. Reading is
  fixations and saccades; the information taken in per fixation is limited and the eyes need time to
  move. Rayner and colleagues 2016: rates above 1000 wpm are not possible without severe comprehension
  loss, and "an increase in reading speed is accompanied by a reduction in comprehension". RSVP, the
  one-word-at-a-time mechanism behind a generation of apps, removes the saccades that "naturally move
  to the parts of text in most need of processing and to move back to material that has not been
  adequately understood", and so "generally impairs the recall accuracy of material presented at
  faster speeds". Peripheral-vision training founders on peripheral vision being fuzzy. **The honest
  concession:** such programmes do appear to train skimming, which is a real skill, and the course has
  already taught it as a level. What actually raises speed is vocabulary and background knowledge,
  which is lesson 3 again. Medium: Delgado and colleagues 2018, 54 studies, screens worse by
  *g* = -0.21 overall, carried by expository text (-0.27) and absent for narrative (0.01), and
  significant only under time pressure.
- **Mechanism:** the eye samples text in fixations of a few characters' useful width; regressions back
  to difficult material are not a defect to be trained away but the repair mechanism of comprehension.
  Remove them and you remove the repair.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: the reader times themselves on a 500-word passage, computes wpm,
  and places it in Brysbaert's distribution. Then computes how long the book they surveyed in lesson 2
  will actually take, which is usually the moment the course becomes real. (b) With a wrinkle: a
  speed-reading claim of 1,500 wpm with comprehension, taken apart with the reader's own arithmetic on
  fixation counts, then the fair question asked: what would the person selling this have to be right
  about, and is there any version of the claim that survives? Answer: skimming, which is worth having.
- **Misconceptions:** "speed reading is a skill I can buy"; "subvocalisation is slowing me down and
  should be suppressed"; "regressions are wasted motion"; "I read at 600 wpm" (usually skimming,
  measured against a comprehension test that was not given); "screens are ruining reading" (the effect
  is small, conditional, and absent for stories).
- **Practice:** measure your own rate on three texts of different difficulty, including one from your
  own field. Then take one text you would normally read on a phone under time pressure and read it on
  paper without a clock, and write down what you noticed.
- **Sources:** Brysbaert 2019 (read in substantial part); Rayner et al. 2016 (**APS summary and
  Brysbaert's account only, and the lesson must say so**); Delgado et al. 2018 (abstract level);
  Tinker's "even with easy material, 500 wpm is very fast reading" via Brysbaert.
- **Connects:** closes the door on the shortcut, so lessons 5 to 10 can teach the method. Uses lesson
  2's surveyed book for the arithmetic.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's levels, now with numbers attached to each gear.
- **Chart:** the two lines of Brysbaert's distribution against the marketed claims, drawn from the
  real figures. One chart, phone-legible, per 4.5.

### 5. Finding the argument: coming to terms with an author (~50 min)
- **Objectives:** apply Adler's four structural rules to a chapter and produce its outline; identify an
  author's key terms and state what each means in that author's usage; reconstruct an argument from the
  sentences that carry it.
- **Core ideas:** Adler's first two stages, in his words. Stage I, what the book is about: classify by
  kind and subject; state the whole book's argument "with the utmost brevity"; enumerate its major
  parts in order and relation and outline them; define the problems the author tried to solve. Stage
  II, interpretation: "come to terms with the author by interpreting his key words"; grasp his leading
  propositions through his most important sentences; know his arguments by finding them in or
  constructing them out of sequences of sentences; determine which problems he solved and which he did
  not, "and of the latter, decide which the author knew he had failed to solve". The phrase to teach is
  **coming to terms**: a term is a word used in one settled sense, and finding the author's sense is
  the reader's job, not the dictionary's.
- **Mechanism:** an argument is not laid out in a paragraph; it is distributed across sentences, and
  often the crucial premise is never stated because the author thought it obvious. Reconstructing it
  forces the gap into the open, which is why this is the step that reveals whether you understood.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: one chapter taken through the four structural rules, ending with a
  one-sentence statement of the whole and a paragraph outline. (b) With a wrinkle: a text where a key
  word shifts sense between chapters, so the reader's first "coming to terms" was wrong and has to be
  revised. That is the common case in real reading and almost never shown.
- **Misconceptions:** "I know what the word means" (the author's usage governs); "the thesis is in the
  introduction" (often it is not, and the last pages are better, per lesson 2); "outlining is
  busywork" (it is the test of whether you found the structure); "if I can quote it, I understood it".
- **Practice:** take the book you surveyed in lesson 2, read one chapter, and produce: the kind of
  book, one sentence on the whole, an outline of the chapter's parts, and a list of its three key
  terms with the author's sense of each.
- **Sources:** Adler and Van Doren 1972, rules 1 to 8, verbatim; Harvard Library's outline-summarize-
  analyze habit, for "the way to determine that you've really gotten the point is to be able to state
  it in your own words".
- **Connects:** builds on 2 (the survey gave you the structure to test); needs 6 to be practical; 7 is
  what you may do once this is done.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 3's diagnosis, since a term you cannot come to is usually missing
  background rather than a hard word.

### 6. Reading with a pen (~35 min)
- **Objectives:** apply a marking system to a chapter that records structure, disagreement and
  questions distinctly; explain why marking works where highlighting does not; build a one-page
  reading record that is still usable a month later.
- **Core ideas:** Adler's 1940 essay: "marking up a book is not an act of mutilation but of love", and
  his practical limit, do not mark a book you do not own. Harvard's annotate habit is blunter and more
  usable: abandon highlighters, write in the margins, develop a personal symbol system, and write down
  the questions you could not resolve so they stay visible. The distinction that makes it work is that
  a mark must record *what kind* of thought it was: this is the structure, this I doubt, this I did not
  understand, this connects to something else. A highlight records only "something here".
- **Mechanism:** marking forces a judgement at the moment of reading, and a judgement is the thing you
  can retrieve later. Colour on a page is a record that your eyes passed over it, which is why it
  feels like work and leaves nothing.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: one page marked in front of the reader, with a five-symbol system
  and every mark justified. (b) With a wrinkle: the same page highlighted instead, then both versions
  revisited a month later with the question "what can you now reconstruct?", showing what each record
  actually preserved.
- **Misconceptions:** "highlighting is studying" (hand off to How to Learn Anything for the evidence);
  "notes should be neat"; "I will remember why I marked it"; "marking a library book is fine".
- **Practice:** mark one chapter with a system you define on paper first. Then close the book and write
  the chapter's argument from your marks alone. The gap tells you whether your system records
  judgements or locations.
- **Sources:** Adler 1940 (**quotation level only; verify against a scan before quoting**); Harvard
  Library's six habits; Dunlosky et al. 2013 by hand-off, not re-taught.
- **Connects:** makes lesson 5 practical and lesson 7 possible.
- **Hand-off:** systems for keeping notes across years belong to Note-Taking and Personal Knowledge,
  Term 2. Say so and stop.

### 7. Judging what you have understood (~45 min)
- **Objectives:** explain why criticism follows interpretation and what goes wrong when it does not;
  identify which of Adler's four grounds a given objection rests on; apply the rule that agreement is
  required where none of the three grounds holds.
- **Core ideas:** Adler's third stage. Rule 9, with his gloss: "Do not say you agree, disagree, or
  suspend judgment, until you can say 'I understand.'" Rule 10, do not disagree disputatiously. Rule
  11, give reasons, which is the difference between knowledge and personal opinion. Then the four
  grounds and nothing else: the author is uninformed, misinformed, illogical, or his account is
  incomplete. **The note nobody quotes:** failing on the first three, "you must agree, at least in
  part", though you may suspend judgement on the whole in light of incompleteness. That is a demanding
  rule and it is the one that makes the method more than etiquette.
- **Mechanism:** an objection that is not tied to one of the four grounds is usually a report of taste
  or of prior belief. Naming the ground forces you to say what would have to be true for you to be
  right, which is the same move Logic and Argument teaches from the other side.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: three objections to one passage, each sorted to its ground, with one
  that turns out to belong to none and dissolves under the sorting. (b) With a wrinkle: a case where
  the reader's real objection is "incomplete", which Adler says is not grounds for disagreement but for
  suspended judgement, so the honest verdict is partial agreement with a stated gap. Readers find this
  uncomfortable and that is the point.
- **Misconceptions:** "criticism means finding fault"; "I disagree" as a finished thought; "I can judge
  it from the summary"; "suspending judgement is a cop-out".
- **Practice:** on the chapter from lesson 5, write one page: what the author argued, and your verdict
  on one of the four grounds, with the reason. If you cannot name the ground, you do not yet have an
  objection.
- **Sources:** Adler and Van Doren 1972, rules 9 to 15 and the note on the last four, verbatim.
- **Connects:** completes analytical reading; needed before 10, where several authors are judged
  against each other.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 5's interpretation, which is the precondition rule 9 names.
- **Cross-course:** Logic and Argument, Term 1, teaches steelmanning and the fallacies; link, and do not
  re-teach.

### 8. Reading a research paper (~45 min)
- **Objectives:** apply a three-pass method to a real open-access paper and state after each pass what
  you now know; identify what a paper's evidence can and cannot support; explain why a paper is not
  read front to back.
- **Core ideas:** Keshav's three passes with his budgets. **First pass, five to ten minutes:** title,
  abstract, introduction; section headings only; conclusions; glance at references. Then the **five Cs**:
  Category, Context, Correctness, Contributions, Clarity, after which you may stop. **Second pass, up
  to an hour:** careful reading, proofs ignored, figures interrogated, asking whether axes are labelled
  and error bars shown, since "common mistakes like these will separate rushed, shoddy work from the
  truly excellent". Afterwards you should be able to "summarize the main thrust of the paper, with
  supporting evidence, to someone else". **Third pass:** "attempt to virtually re-implement the paper".
  Carey, Steiner and Petri 2020 add the general-reader version and two honest notes: there is "no
  correct or incorrect approach" to section order, and "early on, it can take a long time to read one
  article front to back".
- **Mechanism:** a paper is written in a fixed order for archival reasons, not pedagogical ones. The
  claim lives in the abstract and conclusions, the evidence lives in the figures, and the methods
  decide whether the evidence supports the claim. Reading in the archival order postpones every
  question that matters.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a first pass on an open-access paper, live, ending in the five Cs
  answered in five sentences. Use one the course already relies on, Carey et al. 2020 itself or
  Delgado et al. 2018, so the reader can check the course's own sourcing. (b) With a wrinkle: a paper
  whose abstract overstates what the design can show, where the second pass on the methods contradicts
  the first pass's impression. Recht and Leslie 1988 with the Reynolds critique is the ready-made case
  and ties back to lesson 3.
- **Misconceptions:** "I cannot read papers, I am not a scientist"; "the abstract is the paper"; "peer
  reviewed means true"; "I should understand every sentence before going on" (Adler's superficial rule,
  lesson 2, applies here too); "the discussion tells me what was found" (the results do; the discussion
  tells you what the authors make of it).
- **Practice:** first-pass a paper in a field you care about and answer the five Cs. Then second-pass
  it and write the one-paragraph summary you could say aloud to someone else.
- **Sources:** Keshav 2007 (read in full); Carey, Steiner and Petri 2020 (read in full, open access);
  Reynolds et al. 2025 as the worked case.
- **Connects:** applies lesson 2's inspectional idea to a new genre and lesson 7's judgement to
  evidence. Feeds the project.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 3's knowledge gap, which is why a paper outside your field is hard.

### 9. Reading a primary source (~45 min)
- **Objectives:** apply sourcing, contextualization, corroboration and close reading to a document;
  explain why a document's attribution is read before its text; compare two conflicting accounts and
  state what each is evidence of.
- **Core ideas:** Wineburg 1991. Eight historians and eight strong high-school seniors read eight
  documents on the Lexington confrontation of 19 April 1775. **The historians ranked the textbook dead
  last for trustworthiness, below a novel**; three of the eight students put it at or near the top, one
  calling it "the facts", another "straight information" and "objective", though it contradicted
  primary accounts from both sides. The behavioural finding is the lesson: "historians, when first given
  a document, looked immediately to its attribution, whereas students usually began with the first word
  in the upper left and never stopped until they reached the last word on the bottom right." The four
  moves, as the Stanford curriculum teaches them: **sourcing** (who made this, when, for whom, why),
  **contextualization** (what else was happening, what could this author have known), **corroboration**
  (what do other accounts say, and where do they diverge), **close reading** (what does the language
  itself do). Reisman 2012 is the evidence that this transfers: 236 students, five schools, six months,
  gains in historical thinking, factual knowledge, general reasoning **and general reading
  comprehension**.
- **Mechanism:** a document is not a window onto an event; it is an act by a person with a purpose. Read
  from the attribution outward and every sentence changes meaning, because you now know what the sentence
  was for. A textbook hides its author, which reads as objectivity and is the opposite.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: two short conflicting accounts of one small event, sourced and
  corroborated step by step, ending not in "which is true" but in "what each is evidence of". The
  Stanford "Lunchroom Fight" structure, built on our own material rather than copied. (b) With a
  wrinkle: an account that is unreliable about the event and excellent evidence of something else, which
  is the move that separates a historian from a fact-checker.
- **Misconceptions:** "a textbook is more reliable than a document from the period"; "primary sources
  are unbiased because they were there"; "bias means useless"; "contextualizing is background colour"
  (it decides what the author could have known).
- **Practice:** take one document you can reach today, a letter, a minute of a meeting, a photograph
  with a caption, a press release, and write the four moves on it. Then find a second account of the
  same thing and corroborate.
- **Sources:** Wineburg 1991 via his own 1992 AHA account (labelled); Reisman 2012 (abstract level);
  Digital Inquiry Group's free lessons, linked not copied.
- **Connects:** the sourcing move is lesson 7's judgement applied before interpretation rather than
  after, and the lesson should say why the order differs for documents. Feeds the project.
- **Neutrality:** this lesson necessarily uses historical material, so **the neutrality audit is
  mandatory** under 3.4. Choose an event whose factual core is uncontested and whose accounts differ on
  detail, so the teaching point is method rather than a live dispute. Lexington itself is defensible
  because it is the study's own case and both sides' depositions survive, but the drafter must present
  the competing accounts in the terms each side used.

### 10. One question, many books (~50 min)
- **Objectives:** apply the five steps of syntopical reading to one question and four texts; build a
  neutral set of terms in which authors who disagree can be compared; explain why the question governs
  the reading rather than the books.
- **Core ideas:** Adler's fourth level and its five steps: finding the relevant passages, bringing the
  authors to terms, getting the questions clear, defining the issues, analysing the discussion. His
  framing is the whole idea: "In syntopical reading, it is you and your concerns that are primarily to
  be served, not the books that you read." The hard step is the second: authors who disagree usually do
  not share a vocabulary, so the reader must build a neutral one and translate each author into it,
  which frequently reveals that two writers thought they disagreed and did not, or thought they agreed
  and did not. Bacon's warning closes the course: reading "by deputy", with extracts made by others, is
  acceptable only "in the less important arguments, and the meaner sort of books", because "distilled
  books are like common distilled waters, flashy things". That sentence is four hundred years old and it
  is about book summaries, executive briefings, and asking a machine what a book says.
- **Mechanism:** a single book gives you one author's framing along with their answer, and the framing
  is invisible while you are inside it. Reading four gives you the disagreement, and the disagreement is
  where the real question is.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: one narrow question and three short texts, taken through all five
  steps, ending in a stated issue rather than a verdict. (b) With a wrinkle: two authors who appear to
  disagree and turn out to be using one word in two senses, dissolved by step 2. The course has a
  ready-made case in its own sources: whether "speed reading" works depends entirely on whether the
  claim is about comprehension or about skimming, and Rayner and colleagues concede the second while
  refuting the first.
- **Misconceptions:** "I should read the books first and compare afterwards" (the question comes first
  and governs what you read); "syntopical reading means agreeing with everyone"; "a literature review
  is a summary of each book in turn"; "a good summary saves me the reading".
- **Practice:** the project, begun here.
- **Sources:** Adler and Van Doren 1972, the five steps and the framing, verbatim; Bacon 1625.
- **Connects:** uses 2 to select, 5 and 7 to read and judge, 8 and 9 for the genres. It is the level the
  other nine were for.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's levels, closing the arc that opened the course.

Two items must be **cross-lesson**: one pairing lesson 3's knowledge gap with lesson 8's unfamiliar
paper, and one pairing lesson 4's rate arithmetic with lesson 2's survey decision. Distractors are the
misconceptions named in each lesson, not invented wrong answers.

### Project: One question, four texts
**Deliverable.** A reading file of about 1,200 to 1,800 words plus its working papers, on one question
the learner actually wants answered.

**Steps.**
1. State the question in one sentence, and say what you would do differently depending on the answer.
2. Find six candidate texts. Survey each one per lesson 2, in under half an hour each. Record the
   verdict and the reason. At least one must be a research paper and at least one a primary document.
3. Read the two most useful analytically, per lessons 5 to 7. Produce for each: the one-sentence
   statement of the whole, an outline, the key terms in the author's sense, and a verdict naming its
   ground.
4. Three-pass the paper (lesson 8) and apply the four moves to the document (lesson 9).
5. Bring the authors to terms: build the neutral vocabulary and translate each into it.
6. State the issues. Where do these authors actually disagree, and what evidence would settle each
   point? The deliverable ends in stated issues and your own answer with its grounds, not in a summary.

**Rubric.**

| Criterion | What full marks look like |
|---|---|
| The question governs | The reading is visibly driven by the question; irrelevant parts of texts are skipped and the skipping is justified |
| Surveys are real | Six verdicts with reasons drawn from the book's own apparatus, inside the time budget |
| Coming to terms | A neutral vocabulary exists and each author is translated into it, with at least one term where two authors differ in sense |
| Judgement is grounded | Every verdict names one of Adler's four grounds, and at least one reaches "agree in part" honestly |
| Genres handled | The paper's five Cs are answered and its evidence assessed; the document is sourced, contextualized and corroborated |
| Issues, not summary | The file ends in stated issues with what would settle them, not in précis of each text in turn |
| Honesty | Where the learner did not read something fully, the file says so, in the course's own practice |

The last row is deliberate. The course's own research file labels every source by how much of it was
read, and the project asks the learner to do the same thing.

## Neutrality notes

- **Lesson 9 is the only lesson in a 3.4 domain** and its neutrality audit is mandatory. The risk is not
  a false sentence but selection: choosing an event, or a pair of accounts, that makes one side's
  witnesses look credulous. Mitigation: pick an event whose factual core is uncontested, present each
  account in the terms its own side used, and make the teaching point the method rather than the verdict.
- **Lesson 3's example texts** must not make a demographic the butt of the knowledge gap. The cricket,
  liturgy and bond-yield examples are chosen so that most readers lack at least one and no reader lacks
  all three, which is the point being taught.
- **Lesson 10's worked disagreement** is drawn from the course's own subject, not from politics.
- **Adler's canon** is a value question under 3.1. The course teaches his method and says plainly that
  his views about which books are worth reading are his own and are not part of what is being taught.
- **Lesson 4 must be fair to the speed-reading claim.** The strong version is refuted; the weak version,
  that such training teaches skimming, is conceded by the refuters themselves. Report both.

## Concerns to flag

- **Lesson 4 rests on a source read only in summary.** Either fetch the Rayner monograph before drafting
  or keep every claim inside the APS summary and Brysbaert. This is the outline's biggest sourcing risk.
- **Lesson 6 is the thinnest.** If drafting shows it cannot reach depth without straying into the Term 2
  note-taking course, fold it into lesson 5 and make the course nine lessons rather than pad it.
- **The Adler quotations come from an OCR scan.** Spot-check every quoted rule against the page images
  before publishing; OCR of a 1972 print is good but not perfect, and a mis-transcribed rule is exactly
  the defect the fact-check pass exists to catch.
- **Every lesson needs a real text the reader can open.** That constrains examples to public-domain or
  openly licensed material, which is a feature for lessons 2, 5 and 10 and a real difficulty for lesson
  9, where the document has to be short, reachable and genuinely contested in its details.
- **Ten lessons is the upper end** for a Term 1 foundation course. If lessons 5 and 6 merge, nine is
  better than ten with one thin lesson.
