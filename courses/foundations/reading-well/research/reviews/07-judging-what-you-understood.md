# Reading Well, lesson 7: Judging what you have understood

Newest review at the bottom.

## 2026-09-18 — Lesson 07 — depth, fact-check and pedagogy, Tier A
**Reviewer:** fresh-context subagent, against `SOURCES.md`, the Gutenberg text of Ruskin, and the
five sibling lessons. Five critical findings, and the first was the one the review was pointed at:
**the lesson contradicted itself between its two halves on the same Ruskin sentence.**

**Findings:**

1. [critical] Objection 2 disagreed with "the entire difference between education and non-education
   ... consists in this accuracy" on the ground of uninformed. Thirty lines later the incompleteness
   section suspended judgement on that identical sentence using the identical missing knowledge from
   lesson 3. The lesson's own rule forbids the second: suspension is available only where none of the
   first three grounds can be shown. **The lesson stated the demanding rule and then demonstrated
   evading it**, on the lesson's own worked example, against the lesson's own third objective.
2. [critical] **Ruskin's qualifier was stripped, twice.** His sentence is "The entire difference
   between education and non-education (as regards the merely intellectual part of it), consists in
   this accuracy." Lesson 6 prints it with the parenthesis, so the drafter had it on the page, and
   lesson 7 attacked the unqualified universal in a lesson about judging an author only on what he
   actually said, with the primary text linked in its own footnote.
3. [critical] **The uninformed/misinformed distinction was invented**, taught twice as definitional,
   and driving a quiz explain. The lesson made the difference turn on whether the fact "was
   available". Adler's text says the opposite in as many words: a misinformed author "asserts what is
   not the case", and the error "may be owing to lack of knowledge, but the error is more than that.
   Whatever its cause, it consists in making assertions contrary to fact." The invented test came
   from nowhere in `SOURCES.md`.
4. [critical] Quiz item 2 was keyed wrong under the lesson's own invented criterion: an author in
   1890 writing that a disease spreads through bad air is writing after Koch, so the fact *was*
   available and the answer would be misinformed rather than uninformed.
5. [critical] "nothing in the paragraph supports the claim" is false and contradicts lesson 6, which
   says everything else in the paragraph "either supports it, decorates it, or overstates it". The
   British Museum contrast is an argument. And sorting the parenthesis to *illogical* was not earned,
   because Adler's third ground is a fallacy committed and no step has gone wrong.
6. [high] Two load-bearing Adler attributions came from `OUTLINE.md` rather than `SOURCES.md`: the
   knowledge-versus-personal-opinion framing of rule 11, and the suspension clause. **Course-wide
   finding 3 repeating for the fourth time.**
7. [high] Rapoport's rule was presented as "the practical form" of Adler's rule 10; "Go and take that
   course if you have not" reads as a back-reference to a course that comes later in the same term;
   "insufferable ... He is rather" is the course's own voice endorsing a sneer, which standards 3.3
   bars outright; footnote 2's two claims about lesson 6 were both wrong; and objection 2 asserted
   "that literature" and "very largely" beyond what lesson 3 establishes.
8. [medium] Quiz item 1's explain pointed at the wrong lesson; a gloss on rule 9 read as Adler's;
   chapter numbers for the 1972 book that `SOURCES.md` does not record; one predict and one
   checkpoint for three hard ideas; three unsourced generalisations dressed as findings.
9. [low] Three contractions in the file; a garbled sentence; twenty-one bold runs; Open Library not
   linked where three sibling lessons link it; an awkward objective.

**Resolutions:** all applied, and one of them cost more than an edit.

**Finding 6 was fixed at the root.** The Adler scan was re-opened and the third stage re-read, and
`research/SOURCES.md` now records verbatim: both definitions of the first two grounds, the definition
of illogical, the incompleteness passage with its suspension clause, the two sentences behind rule
11, and the demanding rule itself, which turns out to be **much stronger than this course had been
recording**. Adler writes: "You have no freedom of will about this. It is not your sacred privilege
to decide whether you are going to agree or disagree. If you have not been able to show that the
author is uninformed, misinformed, or illogical on relevant matters, you simply cannot disagree. You
must agree." He then names the sentence he is forbidding. The lesson now quotes him rather than
paraphrasing him, and the section is stronger for it.

**That re-reading also handed the lesson its best example.** Adler's own illustration of the
uninformed ground is Darwin, who "lacked the knowledge of genetics that the work of Mendel and later
experimentalists now provides", which Adler calls "one of the major defects in *The Origin of
Species*". The lesson now uses it, and it lands on the book the reader worked in lesson 5.

**Finding 1 was fixed by moving objection 2's target.** It now attacks the sufficiency claim, that
ten pages read accurately make you "for evermore in some measure an educated person", and the
incompleteness case keeps the "entire difference" sentence with its parenthesis intact. A paragraph
now says what separates the two, which is the distinction the lesson's third objective names and
which the draft had collapsed.

**Finding 5 was fixed by letting the objection refuse to sort cleanly**, which is a better lesson
than a tidy one: the paragraph is not empty of reasons, confidence is not a premise, and what
survives is the narrow objection that the sentence would be exactly as strong without the
parenthesis.
