# Reading Well, lesson 5: Coming to terms with an author

## 2026-09-18 — Lesson 05 — depth, fact-check, pedagogy and neutrality, Tier A
**Reviewer:** fresh-context subagent, every Darwin string checked character by character against the
fetched Project Gutenberg #1228 text, and every Adler claim against `SOURCES.md`. Six critical
findings, all of them factual, and all of them in the lesson's own claims *about* the book rather
than in its quotations. Every quotation was verbatim. That is the pattern worth carrying forward:
**this drafter quotes accurately and describes carelessly.**

**Findings:**

1. [critical] "By the end of chapter 3 the same word is doing a different job" over a sentence that
   is in chapter 3's **second** paragraph, before the definition of "struggle for existence" in
   paragraph 4. The section is about the order in which a reader meets things, so the order being
   wrong is fatal to it.
2. [critical] "In chapter 1 ... Selection there means exactly what you think ... There is a chooser,
   the chooser has a purpose, and the purpose explains the result." Chapter 1's second half is
   *Unconscious Selection*, in which Darwin removes the purpose himself and says that version matters
   more to his argument. The whole wrinkle rested on a chapter 1 that is not there.
3. [critical] Footnote 1 attributed to Adler, and cited `SOURCES.md` for, the gloss that a term is a
   word used in one settled sense and that finding it is the reader's work rather than the
   dictionary's. `SOURCES.md` records no such thing. The only file carrying that wording is
   `OUTLINE.md`. **Third lesson in this course to drift off the research file onto the plan.**
4. [critical] "Darwin states it across one paragraph and the two around it", said of three sentences
   that are consecutive in one paragraph, and said immediately after telling the reader that
   arguments are distributed rather than laid out. A reader with the book open sees it at once.
5. [critical] "The rule does not tell you how to find those sentences" is a negative claim about a
   book's contents that `SOURCES.md` cannot support, and is probably false, since Adler's own test is
   that the important sentences are the ones that puzzle you. Labelling the drafter's test as the
   drafter's was honest but not sufficient.
6. [critical] "He thought it too obvious to promote", of the food-and-room premise. Darwin states it
   plainly in a main clause later in the same chapter: "The amount of food for each species of course
   gives the extreme limit to which each can increase." The reconstruction was fair; the diagnosis
   was not.
7. [high] "forty pages on pigeon fanciers and cabbage growers". The page count is about right and the
   subject is wrong. Cabbage occurs twice in chapter 1, both in passing. It is dogs, horses, rabbits,
   ducks, gooseberries, pears, and then pigeons at length.
8. [high] "about ten pages" for chapter 3, which is about six thousand words and nearer twenty pages
   in the first edition.
9. [high] The rule 8 example was in paragraph 3, not "the middle of this chapter", and a promise to
   treat a subject at greater length later is a statement of scope rather than a problem the author
   knows he failed to solve. The real instance was available in the same chapter and unused.
10. [high] Internal inconsistency: three headnote items called load-bearing, with "bears on natural
    selection" put among the illustrations, while the wrinkle section is built entirely on it.
11. [high] No neutrality marker, where lesson 2 carries one, and this lesson goes further into the
    content than lesson 2 did.
12. [medium] Rules 1 to 4 are stated for whole books and applied to a chapter without saying so.
13. [medium] Quiz item 2 was the lesson's own worked example with the names removed.
14. [medium] One predict and one checkpoint in a lesson this long, and the hardest section had
    neither.
15. [medium] Four "here is" openers; exercise 2 had no time budget; zero contractions in the body.
16. [low] Lesson 2 promises "Lesson 5 works all four properly" and this lesson works two of the four
    stages; "the obstacle is almost never the word" flattens lesson 3, which keeps vocabulary as a
    real obstacle; quiz item 3's key was the longest option by twelve characters.

**Resolutions:** 1 to 16 all applied, in the reviewer's own proposed wording where it gave any, and
verified against the Gutenberg text before use. Three are worth recording beyond "fixed".

**Finding 2 produced a better lesson than the one it corrected.** The outline asked for a wrinkle in
which "the reader's first coming to terms was wrong and has to be revised". The draft supplied an
author who changes his usage, which is the rarer case and, here, not what happened. The corrected
version is the commoner and more useful one: **Darwin is consistent throughout, and the reader's
error is settling a term from half a chapter and never going back.** That is worse than an author
shifting, because there is nothing to catch, and the only thing that surfaces it is having written
the sense down. The section is renamed "The wrinkle: a term you settled too early".

**Finding 6 likewise.** "Not missing, misplaced" is both true and a better teaching point than the
draft's "he thought it too obvious to promote", because it shows a reader what setting an argument
out in three lines actually buys: not the discovery that a premise is absent, but the discovery that
the paragraph in front of you is leaning on something that is four pages away.

**Three things the reviewer raised under "what a well-informed critic would say" were added, and
they are among the best paragraphs in the lesson.** That the staging of Adler's rules is in genuine
tension with the sourcing heuristic lesson 9 will teach, where expert readers judge the source before
reading a line, and the lesson now says so and defers the question rather than being caught by it.
That nothing in Adler is measured, which lesson 1 says and this lesson had stopped saying. And that
Darwin's own "In these several senses, which pass into each other" is a counterexample to rule 5,
which the lesson had quoted as candour without noticing: an author who has bundled several senses
under one word is the case where coming to terms returns "he slides between them", and that is the
rule's most valuable output rather than its failure.

**One thing was noted and not changed.** The reviewer judged that one book across lesson 2 and
lesson 5 is continuity rather than monotony, because lesson 2 reads the book from outside and lesson
5 reads one chapter from inside, and because both exercises depend on the book the reader surveyed.
It asked for one line naming a second case so the pattern reads as being about authors rather than
about Darwin, and that line is added.
