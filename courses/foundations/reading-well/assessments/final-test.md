  # --- Levels, and choosing one (L1) ---
  - q: >-
      Adler's four levels are elementary, inspectional, analytical and syntopical. What is the
      relation between them?
    options:
      - They are four techniques, and a reader picks whichever suits their temperament
      - Each contains the one below it, so the higher ones include the lower rather than replacing them
      - They are four stages of a reader's life, from childhood to scholarship
      - They rank from worst to best, so a good reader uses the fourth wherever possible
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The levels are nested, which is why a syntopical reader is still doing inspectional work, so B.
      A makes them a matter of taste, when the choice is made by the text and the purpose. C turns a
      set of skills into a biography. D is the hierarchy-of-worth reading lesson 1 exists to refuse,
      since the syntopical hour is wasted if what you needed was a date.
  - q: >-
      You need one date out of a four-hundred-page history, for a footnote, tonight. What level does
      that call for, and what would be wrong with going higher?
    options:
      - Analytical, because a date taken without understanding the book is a date you cannot trust
      - Syntopical, because one book's date should always be checked against another's
      - Inspectional, and going higher would be thorough rather than wrong
      - Elementary, and going higher would spend hours buying nothing the purpose needed
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The purpose is to find one fact, the index will give it, and an hour spent outlining the book
      buys nothing the footnote needed, so D. A and B both make a virtue of effort the purpose does
      not call for. C is close and gets the level roughly right, but it calls the waste thoroughness,
      which is the judgement the lesson asks you to refuse.
  # --- Surveying (L2) ---
  - q: >-
      You are surveying a book and reach the index. You read it as a list rather than looking
      anything up. What is that step for?
    options:
      - It shows the range of topics and the terms the author treats as central, before you commit
      - It tells you whether the book is well made, since a thin index means a careless publisher
      - It lets you skip the book entirely, since an index contains everything a reader needs
      - It gives you the author's argument in outline, which the contents page cannot do
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Reading the index as a list shows you what range the book covers and which terms carry weight,
      which is a decision aid rather than a lookup, so A. B is a fair impression and not the step's
      purpose. C is the misconception the step exists to prevent. D confuses a list of terms with an
      argument, which is what the contents page is for.
  - q: >-
      A book you are surveying has no index, no publisher's blurb and a contents page of fifty-eight
      one-word topics. Three of the six steps have given you almost nothing. What does that tell you,
      and what do you do?
    options:
      - The survey has failed here, and the book must now be read properly or else not at all
      - The book is badly made, and poor apparatus is a good reason to put a book down
      - It tells you what kind of book it is, and the remaining steps now carry more weight
      - The steps were taken in the wrong order, and starting again from the blurb would help here
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      A book with no apparatus is telling you it is a collection rather than an argument, which is a
      finding rather than a failure, and the dipping and the closing pages now have to do the work,
      so C. A treats an absence of information as an absence of result. B judges the book by its
      packaging. D invents a procedural fix for something that is not a procedural problem.
  # --- Difficulty as a relation (L3) ---
  - q: >-
      What single question separates a vocabulary obstacle from a missing-background one?
    options:
      - Whether the word is technical, since technical words are the vocabulary case by definition
      - Whether a definition fixes it, because a definition gives you a word and not a relation
      - Whether you have read anything else on the subject, since background comes from other books
      - Whether the sentence is long, since long sentences are where unfamiliar words hide
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      A word you can point at is not yet a diagnosis, and the test is whether looking it up leaves
      you where you were, so B. A sorts by the word's register rather than by what you lack. C
      describes where background comes from rather than how to tell it is missing. D is the syntax
      signature attached to the wrong obstacle.
  - q: >-
      A reader gets through a page about a legal settlement without meeting an unfamiliar word, and
      cannot say what either party gave up. Which obstacle, and what should they do?
    options:
      - Missing background, so they need a simpler account of how settlements work
      - Vocabulary, so they should look up the legal terms of art and then reread the page
      - Syntax, so they should take the longest sentences apart into their separate clauses
      - Reading speed, so they should go through the page a second time more slowly
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Reading fluently and still missing the claim is the signature of missing background, and the
      fix is another text rather than more time on this one, so A. B is ruled out by the stem. C is
      a real obstacle with a different signature, which is a sentence you can paraphrase only after
      rearranging it. D is the misconception that rereading repairs a gap, when the second pass has
      the same gaps as the first.
  # --- Rate, speed claims, medium (L4) ---
  - q: >-
      What does the 2019 meta-analysis give as the average rates for adults reading English, and what
      is the band most adults fall in for non-fiction?
    options:
      - 300 silent and 250 aloud, with most adults between 250 and 350
      - 238 for non-fiction and 260 for fiction, with most adults between 100 and 175
      - 400 for non-fiction and 350 for fiction, with most adults between 300 and 500
      - 238 non-fiction and 260 fiction, with most adults between 175 and 300
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The pooled figures are 238 and 260 for silent reading and 183 for reading aloud, and the
      non-fiction band is 175 to 300, so D. A is the inflated norm the paper exists to correct. B has
      the averages right and a band below them, which cannot be. C is the marketed range rather than
      the measured one.
  - q: >-
      A 280-page book runs to roughly 84,000 words. You read non-fiction at about 240 words a minute.
      Your survey says the book has what you need in two of its nine chapters. What does the
      arithmetic tell you, and what follows?
    options:
      - About six hours for the whole book, so the survey should be repeated on a shorter book
      - About nine hours for the whole book, so the book is too long to be worth reading at all
      - About six hours for the whole book and eighty minutes for the two chapters, so read the two
      - About three hours for the whole book, so read it all and skip the survey next time
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      84,000 at 240 is 350 minutes, a little under six hours, and two chapters of nine is roughly
      19,000 words or eighty minutes, so C, and the survey has just saved you four and a half hours.
      A and D get the arithmetic wrong. B turns a length into a verdict, when the survey has already
      told you which part you need.
  - q: >-
      Someone tells you that reading on screens damages comprehension. You have read the meta-analysis
      of 54 studies. What is the accurate reply?
    options:
      - It is small, carried by expository text, absent for narrative, and only under a clock
      - The effect is large and applies to all reading, so serious work should be done on paper
      - There is no effect, and the studies that found one have since been overturned
      - The effect exists but is too small for any reader to act on in practice
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The shape of the result is the finding, and it is conditional in three ways at once, so A. B
      drops every condition and turns a small effect into a rule. C misdescribes the later work,
      which agrees for narrative text. D dismisses an effect that is real exactly where a student
      operates, which is hard expository text against a clock.
  # --- Structural rules and coming to terms (L5) ---
  - q: >-
      An author writes, in chapter 2, "I use the word *interest* throughout in a wide sense, taking in
      not only what a person wants but what would serve them whether they want it or not." In chapter
      9 he argues that a policy can be in someone's interest against their wishes. What has the
      reader in front of them?
    options:
      - An inconsistency, since a want and a benefit cannot both be called by one word
      - A term the author defined, and a chapter 9 argument valid only in that sense
      - A rhetorical trick, since redefining a common word is how an author smuggles a conclusion in
      - A drafting error, which a careful editor would have caught before publication
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The author has pinned one sense down where the reader can see it, and the later argument stands
      or falls under that sense rather than under the ordinary one, so B. A applies the ordinary sense
      after the author has refused it. C is the suspicion the fifth rule exists to replace with
      reading. D treats a deliberate definition as a mistake.
  - q: >-
      You set an author's argument out in three lines and find the conclusion does not follow from the
      two premises as stated. You then find the missing premise four pages earlier, inside an example.
      What has the exercise shown you?
    options:
      - That the author is illogical, since a premise buried in an example is not really a premise
      - That the argument is unsound, because a reader should not have to assemble it
      - That the premise was misplaced rather than missing, which only writing it out would reveal
      - That you outlined the chapter wrongly, and the outline should be done again
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Setting an argument out in lines is what makes a premise that is doing its work from somewhere
      else visible at all, so C. A converts a presentation problem into a fallacy. B makes a
      complaint about the writing into a verdict on the reasoning. D blames the method that just
      worked.
  # --- Marking (L6) ---
  - q: >-
      Reading a chapter, you meet a sentence you cannot restate however many times you read it. Your
      system has marks for the claim, for doubt, for confusion and for a connection. Which do you use,
      and why that one?
    options:
      - Doubt, because a sentence that resists you is usually one the author has not thought through
      - The claim, because a sentence that resists you is usually carrying the argument
      - A connection, and look for the place later where the author says it more plainly
      - Confusion, because not understanding is a different event from disagreeing
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Recording not-understanding as itself is what lets you come back to it once the chapter has
      given you more, so D. A converts confusion into a verdict, which is the error this whole course
      is against. B may turn out to be true and is not what you know yet. C guesses at a resolution
      the text may never offer.
  # --- The four grounds (L7) ---
  - q: >-
      A reader objects that a book's central claim is offered with no reason anywhere in the chapter,
      and that the author's confidence is standing in for the argument. Which of Adler's four grounds
      is that, and what do they owe?
    options:
      - Uninformed, and they owe the knowledge the author lacked and why it bears on the claim
      - None of the four, since it says the support is missing rather than that he is wrong
      - Illogical, and they owe the precise step at which the reasoning goes wrong
      - Incomplete, and they owe the question the author's subject raises and does not answer
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The four grounds are grounds for saying an author is wrong, and this objection does not say he
      is wrong, it says a sentence is unsupported, which is rule 11 turned back on the author, so B.
      A and D each name a ground whose evidence the reader does not have. C is the nearest of the
      four and still misses, since illogical means a fallacy committed and no step has gone wrong.
  - q: >-
      You have understood a chapter on a teaching method, its evidence holds, and you can find nothing
      wrong with it. You still think the method is a bad idea because of something the chapter never
      discusses, which is what it costs in teacher time. What is the honest verdict?
    options:
      - Agreement on what was argued, suspended judgement on the whole, and the gap named
      - Disagreement, since a method whose costs are unexamined has not been shown to be worth adopting
      - Agreement in full, since an objection about what a chapter omits is not an objection to it
      - Suspended judgement on the whole and on the parts, since an incomplete account cannot be assessed
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      You have found no ground against what the author argued, so agreement is owed on that, and the
      omission is the fourth ground, which earns a suspension on the whole with the gap named, so A.
      B converts a scope objection into a verdict the argument has not earned. C drops the gap, which
      is a real finding. D withholds judgement from parts that can be judged perfectly well.
  # --- Reading a paper (L8) ---
  - q: >-
      An abstract reads "In a sample of 31 undergraduates, a five-minute intervention improved recall
      on a next-day test by 14 per cent. These findings suggest that brief interventions can
      substantially improve learning outcomes at scale." Your first pass is over. What goes in the
      Correctness answer?
    options:
      - That the finding is false, since thirty-one undergraduates cannot support a claim of that size
      - That the assumptions cannot be judged at all until the methods section has been read in full
      - That the jump from thirty-one undergraduates to "at scale" is the thing to check
      - That the finding is sound, since the effect is reported with a number and a comparison
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Correctness on a first pass is a judgement about whether the assumptions look sound, and the
      visible strain here is between what was measured and what is claimed, so C. A turns a limit into
      a refutation. B declines the question the pass is for, when the abstract has given you enough to
      name the strain. D treats the presence of a number as evidence.
  - q: >-
      A reader outside the field finds that same paper hard going. Every sentence parses and no word is
      unfamiliar, and they still cannot say why the authors chose the comparison they did. What is the
      obstacle, and what is the fix?
    options:
      - Syntax, and the fix is to take the methods sentences apart clause by clause
      - Vocabulary, and the fix is a glossary of the field's technical terms
      - Reading speed, and the fix is to go through the paper a second time more slowly
      - Missing background, and the fix is a review article or a textbook chapter
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Fluent reading with the point still missing is the signature of missing background, and on a
      paper outside your field what is missing is the relations a specialist supplies without
      noticing, so D. A and B name obstacles the stem has ruled out. C is the misconception that more
      time on this page supplies what the page never says.
  # --- Reading a primary source (L9) ---
  - q: >-
      Two accounts of a disputed meeting. One is a minute written by the chair the same afternoon; the
      other is an email sent that evening by a person who was overruled at it. They agree on who
      attended and what was decided, and disagree about whether an objection was heard. What is the
      useful output?
    options:
      - The agreed material, and each account placed as evidence of what its writer needed on record
      - A judgement about which of the two is more likely to be telling the truth about the objection
      - Nothing, since two interested accounts of the same meeting cannot settle anything between them
      - The chair's minute, since a formal record made the same day outranks an email written in anger
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Two interested accounts agreeing establish a good deal, and each is best evidence of what its
      author needed on the record rather than of the disputed point, so A. B asks a question neither
      document can settle. C throws away the agreed material, which is the stronger half. D applies an
      automatic ranking in place of sourcing.
  - q: >-
      You are sourcing a press release. Which question does the most work, and why?
    options:
      - When was it issued, because timing tells you what the writer was responding to
      - Who was it written for, and what did the writer want that reader to do about it
      - How long is it, because a long release is usually covering something up
      - Is it well written, because a careless release suggests a careless organisation
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      A press release is an act performed on an audience, and knowing the audience and the intended
      effect tells you what every sentence in it is for, so B. A is a real sourcing question and a
      narrower one. C and D read the packaging rather than the purpose, and neither is a claim
      anything supports.
  # --- Syntopical reading (L10) ---
  - q: >-
      Four writers on one question use four different vocabularies. Two of them appear to disagree
      sharply about whether a practice "works". Whose terms should the comparison be conducted in, and
      what should you expect to find?
    options:
      - The clearest writer's, and expect the other three to fit into them with little trouble
      - The earliest writer's, and expect the later three to be answering him whether they say so or not
      - Each writer's in turn, and expect four summaries that can then be set side by side
      - Yours, built for the question, and expect at least one apparent disagreement to dissolve
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The reader establishes the terms and brings the authors to them, and the commonest result of
      doing so is finding that two writers were answering different questions, so D. A and B hand the
      framing to one of the parties, and the framing is the thing under examination. C produces four
      book reports and no comparison.
  - q: >-
      You finish a syntopical project. What should the last page contain, and what would be lost by
      writing a verdict instead?
    options:
      - A summary of each text in turn, since the reader of your file needs to know what you read
      - The issues, with what would settle each, and a verdict discards what the steps built
      - The strongest author's position, since the point of comparing is to find out who is right
      - Your own view alone, since the authors have been read and no longer need to be reported
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The fifth step aims at looking at all sides and taking none, so its product is the issues with
      their alternatives ordered and what evidence would settle each, so B. A is four book reports. C
      hands the conclusion to one of the parties. D throws away the disagreement, which is where the
      real question was.

This test covers all ten lessons. Twenty items, and most of them put a case to you that no lesson
worked, because the point is whether you can use the method rather than whether you remember it.

Two of the items need more than one lesson at once: one pairs the arithmetic of lesson 4 with the
survey decision of lesson 2, and one pairs lesson 3's diagnosis with lesson 8's unfamiliar paper.

The wrong answers are not invented. Every one of them is a misconception named somewhere in the
course, which means that if a distractor looks reasonable to you, the lesson that names it is the one
to go back to.

Pass mark is 80 per cent. If you do not reach it, the useful thing is not the score but the pattern:
look at which lessons your wrong answers came from, and reread those rather than the whole course.
