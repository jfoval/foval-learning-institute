S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Reading Well, lesson seven. Judging what you have understood.

S1: Lessons five and six leave you holding a good deal. An outline of the chapter. The author's terms in his sense. The argument set out in lines. And a margin full of marks, including the places where you wrote a cross.

S2: And this lesson is what those crosses are for.

S1: And the first thing it tells you is that they aren't disagreements yet.

S2: Start with the rule.

S1: Adler's third stage opens with one that reads like manners and isn't. Do not say you agree, disagree, or suspend judgment, until you can say, I understand.

S2: Take that literally?

S1: It's meant literally. Until you can state the author's argument in your own words, you have no verdict available to you. Not disagreement, not agreement, and not the third option either. Suspending judgement counts as a verdict here too, which is worth saying because readers treat it as the absence of one.

S2: And what goes wrong if I judge first?

S1: Not that you're rude. That you end up arguing with something nobody wrote. A reader who forms a verdict at page thirty has built it out of the first thirty pages plus their own expectations about what the rest will say, and when the book does something else the verdict often doesn't update, because it was never attached to the book in the first place.

S2: There are two more rules before the grounds.

S1: Do not disagree disputatiously, which is rule ten. Disagreement isn't a contest to be won, and a reader who's reading in order to disagree will find something regardless of what the page says.

S2: And rule eleven.

S1: Present good reasons for any critical judgement. And Adler means the distinction seriously. An author's propositions, he says, are nothing but expressions of personal opinion unless they are supported by reasons, and he asks the same of the reader, who must distinguish between genuine knowledge and mere opinion.

S2: So I disagree, is a report about me.

S1: And, I disagree because the second premise is asserted and never argued for, is a claim about the book, which is a thing somebody else can check. Here's the test to apply to your own verdicts. Can somebody else disagree with your objection? If not, you've described a feeling.

S2: Now the four grounds.

S1: When you find an author wrong, you're making one of exactly four claims. Three of them are grounds for saying so, and the fourth isn't. First, the author is uninformed. In Adler's words, he lacks some piece of knowledge that is relevant to the problem he is trying to solve. And to make it stick, you must be able yourself to state the knowledge that the author lacks and show how it is relevant, how it makes a difference to his conclusions.

S2: So you owe two things.

S1: Not one. And Adler's own example here is the book lesson five worked. Darwin lacked the knowledge of genetics that the work of Mendel and later experimentalists now provides, and Adler calls that one of the major defects in the Origin of Species.

S2: Which is not a verdict on Darwin's conclusion.

S1: It's a claim about what he had to work with. A reader who turns a missing mechanism into a verdict on the book has left the ground he named.

S2: Second ground.

S1: Misinformed. He asserts what is not the case. The difference from the first is that the first is a silence and this is a positive claim, and Adler is explicit that the cause doesn't matter. The error may be owing to lack of knowledge, but the error is more than that. Whatever its cause, it consists in making assertions contrary to fact.

S2: So the two need different evidence.

S1: For the first you produce the knowledge he lacked. For the second you produce the fact his sentence gets wrong.

S2: Third.

S1: Illogical. He has committed a fallacy in reasoning, and either way the reader must be able to show the precise respect in which it goes wrong.

S2: And fourth.

S1: His account is incomplete. And that one is different from the other three, which we'll come to.

S2: Work one for me.

S1: Take the Ruskin paragraph from lesson six. Objection one. He offers his own certainty in place of a reason, and the parenthesis is doing work in the sentence that no argument in the paragraph does.

S2: Which ground?

S1: Watch it refuse to go cleanly into a box. The paragraph isn't empty of reasons. What the parenthesis adds is confidence, and confidence isn't a premise. So it's not the third ground, because illogical means a fallacy committed and no step has gone wrong. It's not any of the four, and that isn't the taxonomy failing. The four grounds are grounds for saying an author is wrong, and this objection doesn't say he's wrong. It says one sentence of his is unsupported.

S2: Which is rule eleven turned back on him.

S1: And what survives is narrow and useful. The sentence would be exactly as strong without the parenthesis, and a reader who was persuaded by the parenthesis was persuaded by nothing.

S2: Now here's one I want. I've understood a chapter, I've looked for all four grounds, and I've found none of them. And I'm still not persuaded. What am I entitled to say?

S1: This is the rule that makes it a method rather than manners, and readers don't like it. Adler puts it about as hard as it can be put. You have no freedom of will about this. It is not your sacred privilege to decide whether you are going to agree or disagree. If you have not been able to show that the author is uninformed, misinformed, or illogical on relevant matters, you simply cannot disagree. You must agree.

S2: That takes something away from me.

S1: And he names the sentence he's forbidding, which you have said and I have said. I find nothing wrong with your premises, and no errors in reasoning, but I don't agree with your conclusions.

S2: And incompleteness isn't a way out?

S1: He's careful about it. The fourth point is strictly not a basis for disagreement. It's critically adverse only to the extent that it marks the limitations of the author's achievement. What it permits is narrower. A reader who agrees in part, having found no other ground, may nevertheless suspend judgement on the whole, in the light of that incompleteness.

S2: So the verdict is.

S1: Agreement on what was argued, suspended judgement on the whole, and the gap named. Uncomfortable to write, because it concedes and withholds in the same breath, and more informative than either I agree or I disagree.

S2: We've left out two more worked objections, including one on a sentence of Ruskin's about class that turns out to fall on no ground at all, which is a limit of the method worth knowing. And the four things people get wrong. The full lesson has all of it. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. If you can't name the ground, you don't yet have an objection.
