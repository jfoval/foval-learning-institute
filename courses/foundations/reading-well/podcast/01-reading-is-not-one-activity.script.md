S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're starting the course called Reading Well, with its first lesson. Reading is not one activity.

S1: And it opens with a situation. You have a three hundred page book, forty minutes on a train, and a meeting tomorrow where somebody is going to ask what you make of it.

S2: I open it at page one.

S1: Almost everyone does. And you get to about page forty, and then you sit in that meeting knowing the first eighth of a book and nothing about the rest of it, including whether the first eighth was the part that mattered. Mortimer Adler watched people do this and named what it costs. Such readers, he wrote in nineteen seventy two, start a book on page one and plow steadily through it, without even reading the table of contents.

S2: And what does that cost?

S1: In his words, they are thus faced with the task of achieving a superficial knowledge of the book at the same time that they are trying to understand it. Two jobs at once, and the harder one gets no room.

S2: So the problem isn't that I read too slowly.

S1: The problem is that no decision was made, because nobody told you there was one to make. And for the fix, the lesson goes four hundred years back. Francis Bacon, in an essay called Of Studies, about a page long, and there's one sentence in it that everybody quotes and almost everybody cuts in half.

S2: Give me the famous half.

S1: Some books are to be tasted, others to be swallowed, and some few to be chewed and digested.

S2: I've heard that one.

S1: Everyone has. Now the half that gets dropped, which comes after a semicolon. That is, some books are to be read only in parts; others to be read, but not curiously; and some few to be read wholly, and with diligence and attention.

S2: So he glosses his own metaphor.

S1: Which is what makes it usable rather than merely quotable. He isn't being poetic about books. He's telling you three things to do with your eyes and your hands. Read only in parts. Read the whole thing but not closely. Read the whole thing closely.

S2: Three activities out of one word. The lesson asks the reader which of the three they actually do, so let me ask you.

S1: The second one. Swallowed, read through but not closely. And it's the default setting, which means it gets applied to a news article, a contract, a textbook chapter and a novel alike.

S2: Is that wrong, though?

S1: Sometimes it's exactly right, and the lesson is careful here. Nobody has counted how often the default happens to fit. What the lesson claims is narrower. A reader who only ever does one of the three is choosing by habit rather than by need, and the times it fits are luck.

S2: Now, there's a modern version of Bacon's three.

S1: Adler's four levels, and he's careful about the word level. Here's his sentence. There are four levels of reading. They are here called levels rather than kinds because kinds, strictly speaking, are distinct from one another, whereas it is characteristic of levels that higher ones include lower ones.

S2: So they nest.

S1: Like boxes inside boxes, and that matters more than it looks. Take the four in turn, and the thing to keep is each one's question rather than its name.

S1: Elementary reading asks, what does this sentence say. You finished that one in primary school and you're doing it right now without noticing. Inspectional reading asks, what is this about, and Adler ties it to time. It's characterized by its special emphasis on time, and the aim is to get the most out of a book within a given time.

S2: Which is a nice way of saying skimming.

S1: Except he finishes that sentence honestly, and the ending is the part to keep. The time is, in his words, usually a relatively short time, and always, by definition, too short a time to get out of the book everything that can be gotten. So it isn't a cheaper way of doing the full job. He says so himself, and lesson two is entirely about it.

S2: That's two of the four. What's the third?

S1: Analytical reading asks what's being said in detail, and how, and at the very end whether it's true. Judgement comes last, after eight of his fifteen rules. And his pair of definitions is the clearest thing anyone has written about the difference. If inspectional reading is the best and most complete reading that is possible given a limited time, then analytical reading is the best and most complete reading that is possible given unlimited time.

S2: And the fourth?

S1: Syntopical reading asks your question rather than the book's. You read several books against one problem, and the aim is something none of them contains. In his words, the syntopical reader is able to construct an analysis of the subject that may not be in any of the books.

S2: Here's the one I want to put to you. Somebody says, I'm good at close reading but bad at skimming.

S1: It isn't impossible, but they aren't two independent skills, because analytical reading contains inspectional reading. Adler's rules for the analytical level start with classifying the book and outlining its parts, and that's what an inspection hands you. So usually that reader isn't bad at inspection. They're skipping it, and then doing the analytical work without the map.

S1: And the practical version is worth having. If hard books are exhausting you, the fix is often not more effort at the level you're on. It's doing the level below first.

S2: So four names. What decides which one I pick?

S1: This is the lesson's own answer, and it says so, because it's a synthesis of the course's sources rather than any one of them. The effort a text demands is set by what you need to be able to do when you put it down.

S2: Not by the book. Test it for me.

S1: Bacon attaches it to the book, and ranks books by worth. Adler brings in purpose. Put them together and the decision moves off the shelf and onto the reader.

S1: Take what each level leaves you holding. To say what a sentence says, you need that sentence. To say what a book is about, you need its shape. To say whether its argument holds, you need the argument itself, rebuilt well enough that you could run it forwards and find the step you doubt. And to answer your own question across four books, you need all four arguments and a vocabulary you built yourself to compare them in.

S2: Each one's a bigger thing to have made.

S1: Which is why the same twelve pages can honestly take four minutes or four hours, and neither number is wrong.

S2: We've left out a good deal. The psychologist who tried to measure this and how much of it survived, one article read four ways, and the case where the level changes halfway down the page. The full lesson has all of it, with the levels drawn as nested boxes, and the reading audit you're asked to keep for the rest of the course. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Ask what you need to be able to do when you put it down, and then pick the level.
