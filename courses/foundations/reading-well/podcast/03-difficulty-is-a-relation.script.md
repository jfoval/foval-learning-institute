S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Reading Well, lesson three. Why the same page is easy for one reader and impossible for another.

S1: And I want to read you something. It's a real document, four lines of it, and every word is a word you know. The evening is the eighth of June, eighteen seventy five, in the House of Commons.

S2: Set it up for me.

S1: Sir John Hay has risen to ask the Government to open talks with the other countries bound by the existing steering and sailing rules, with a view to amending them, so as to reduce, in the words of his motion, if possible, the lamentable loss of life now arising from collisions at sea.

S2: Ships running into each other.

S1: And then this happens, and this is the whole of the rest of the record of the Commons that evening. The honourable Baronet was proceeding to address the House, when. Notice taken, that forty members were not present. House counted, and forty members not being present. House adjourned at ten minutes after nine o'clock.

S2: So what happened?

S1: If you know how the Commons works, you've just heard a small story with a beginning and an end, and it isn't the story the words appear to tell.

S2: And if I don't?

S1: You've heard routine housekeeping. A man started speaking, there weren't many people in, everybody went home early. Every sentence of that is true. It also misses what happened, which is the more interesting way to be wrong.

S2: Which word tripped me up?

S1: None of them, and that's the point. Present, counted, adjourned, members, House. On the page two things need a gloss and neither is hard. Honourable is written short, as hon., and a Baronet is a title.

S2: So it's not vocabulary.

S1: Two of the phrases are doing more work than they look. Notice taken, and House counted, are the formal names of a procedure rather than descriptions of somebody noticing and somebody counting. But you can't tell that from the words, and nothing on the page flags them, which is the point rather than an exception to it.

S2: So what was I missing?

S1: Six things, none of them on the page. First, forty is the quorum. Below forty the House cannot transact business at all, so that sentence isn't a remark about attendance. It's the meeting ceasing to exist.

S2: Second.

S1: Notice taken is somebody's act rather than a state of affairs. Most often it's simply a member who can see the benches are empty and says so. Sometimes it's a member protecting the House from a vote taken on a thin attendance. And sometimes it's a member who wants this particular debate stopped.

S2: Third.

S1: A count-out ends the whole sitting rather than the item. Whatever else stood on the order paper went with it, and because the record reports only what was reached, you can't see from that page what that was.

S2: Fourth.

S1: Ten past nine is early. That House regularly sat past midnight. In June eighteen seventy five, thirteen of its sittings did, one of them until a quarter past three in the morning.

S2: Fifth.

S1: The House had suspended at seven for dinner and resumed at nine. Ten minutes later it was counted out, which is the ordinary setting for a count. A thin House that hasn't come back.

S2: And sixth.

S1: Was proceeding to address the House means Hay hadn't finished his opening speech. The motion was never debated and never voted on. And a private member had to win a night for a motion in the first place. Hay never brought this one back that session.

S2: Put it together.

S1: A proposal to reduce deaths at sea was stopped dead by a member pointing out that fewer than forty men were in the room, before it was debated or voted on. Whether stopping it was the point, on that particular night, the record doesn't say.

S2: And the record doesn't call it a defeat.

S1: Because there wasn't one to report. Nothing was voted down. The motion lapsed with the sitting, and what the record reports is what actually happened, which is a room too thin to do business. A reader who followed the House knew what that meant, so nothing more had to be written.

S2: So what's the idea?

S1: Difficulty is not a property of a text, the way length is. It's a relation between a text and a particular reader. Those few lines are trivial for one reader and opaque to another, and the lines didn't change.

S2: That sounds obvious.

S1: It does, and almost nobody acts as though it were true. The usual response to a page that won't open is to conclude something about yourself, and the sentence people reach for is, I am a bad reader. Sometimes the honest version is, I lack the background this text assumes, and those two point at completely different actions.

S2: Why does background matter that much?

S1: Because no writer writes everything down. It would be impossible and the result would be unreadable. So every text states some things and leaves the rest to be supplied by the reader. The relations between the things named, the reason one sentence follows another, the significance of a number.

S2: And the Hansard page never says any of my six.

S1: Never says that forty is the quorum, that taking notice is a choice, that a count-out kills the whole sitting, that nine o'clock is early, or that the motion won't come back. It assumes all of it, and a reader who has it fills those gaps without noticing they were gaps. Which is why that reader would tell you the passage was perfectly clear.

S2: So understanding isn't passive reception.

S1: It's the page plus what you bring. And when what you bring is missing, you're doing a different and much harder job than the fluent reader beside you, on the same words.

S2: Now, there are three obstacles in the lesson and this is the useful part for me.

S1: Vocabulary. There are words you don't know and they're load-bearing. The signature is that you can point at them, and the test is whether a definition fixes it. If you look the word up and find yourself exactly where you were, then what you were missing wasn't the word.

S2: Second.

S1: Missing background. You know every word and can't say what the passage claims, or worse, you can say something confident and wrong. The fix isn't in this text. It's another text, easier, on the same subject.

S2: And third.

S1: Syntax. One particular sentence won't resolve, and you can feel yourself rereading it and losing your place. The signature is that you can restate the paragraph around it but not that sentence, and the fix is structural. Find the main verb, find its subject, and put the qualifications to one side.
