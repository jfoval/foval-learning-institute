S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Reading Well, lesson eight. Reading a research paper.

S1: It's the most useful genre this course will teach you to open and the most widely avoided, and it's the one where reading in the order the words are printed does the most damage.

S2: Why does the order hurt?

S1: Because it's archival rather than pedagogical. A paper is laid out so a specialist can find a particular thing in a particular place and so the record is complete. And that order postpones every question you actually have.

S2: Which are?

S1: The claim lives in the abstract and the conclusions. The evidence lives in the figures and the results. And whether the evidence can support the claim is decided in the methods, which sit in the middle and which the abstract compresses to a clause at most.

S2: So front to back.

S1: You meet the claim first with no way to weigh it, then a long stretch you can't yet see the point of, and then the discussion, by which time the claim has had half an hour to settle in your head unopposed. And there's a second cost, which is the one that makes people give up. Reading front to back means deciding whether the paper is worth your time only after you've spent the time.

S2: Give me the method.

S1: It's Keshav's, from a three page note written in two thousand and seven for computer scientists and useful well outside it. Three passes, each with a budget and a defined output, and the first one is where most of the value is.

S2: First pass.

S1: Five to ten minutes. Read the title, the abstract and the introduction carefully. Read the section and sub-section headings and nothing under them. Read the conclusions. Glance over the references, marking any you've already read.

S2: And then?

S1: Then you should be able to answer five questions, which he calls the five Cs. Category, what kind of paper is this. Context, what other work is it standing on. Correctness, do the assumptions look sound. Contributions, what are its main contributions. And Clarity, is it well written.

S2: And then I may stop.

S1: Which is the step that makes the budget worth keeping. Keshav gives three reasons you might. The paper doesn't interest you, you don't know enough to follow it, or the assumptions look invalid. In my own use the answer is usually to stop, and finding that out in eight minutes is the point.

S2: Second pass.

S1: Up to an hour. Read with care, but ignore proofs. Look hard at the figures, and he's specific. Are the axes properly labelled, are error bars shown? Common mistakes like these will separate rushed, shoddy work from the truly excellent. Mark the references you haven't read, for later.

S2: And the output is a test.

S1: Afterwards you should be able to summarize the main thrust of the paper, with supporting evidence, to someone else. Not the topic. The thrust, with the evidence attached.

S2: And the third pass?

S1: Attempt to virtually re-implement the paper. Make the same assumptions the authors made and re-create the work, which is how you find the assumptions they didn't state. It takes hours, it's for papers in your own field, and most readers never need it.

S2: Now, the lesson does a first pass live.

S1: On a paper this course leans on, so you can check our own sourcing while you learn the method. Brysbaert's twenty nineteen meta-analysis of reading rate, which lesson four is built on, and it's a free PDF.

S2: Start.

S1: Title. How many words do we read per minute? A review and meta-analysis of reading rate. That's a question and a method, which is two of the five Cs half answered before you've opened it.

S2: Abstract.

S1: A hundred and ninety studies, eighteen thousand five hundred and seventy three participants, two hundred and thirty eight words a minute for non-fiction and two hundred and sixty for fiction, oral reading a hundred and eighty three. And the estimates are, in his words, lower than the numbers often cited in scientific and popular writings, with the reasons for the overestimates reviewed.

S2: Introduction.

S1: Opens on a review of speed reading and why more than a thousand words a minute is impossible without severe loss of understanding, then narrows to the ordinary question. And it states the article's job in one sentence, which is to discuss how the number came about and how well it is supported by the available data.

S2: Now the headings, and there are a lot of them.

S1: Nineteen before the back matter, and the count is itself information. Two of them begin, a meta-analysis of, so the pooling is the spine. One says, a new fiction book reading study, so the author has run something of his own and set it beside the pooled data. And under the section on the origins of three hundred words a minute sit sub-sections that are just surnames. Tinker. Carver.

S2: What does a surname heading tell you?

S1: That the paper is going to spend a section on what that person claimed, and a paper rarely gives a section to a claim it simply accepts. So there's a theory being tested rather than only a number being estimated.

S2: And that's three kinds of paper in one.

S1: Without reading a word of the body. What the headings won't tell you is how big the new study is. Note the question and carry it.

S2: Conclusions.

S1: There's no section called Conclusions, which is common. The General discussion does that job, and its three sub-headings are written as whole sentences, so they are the conclusions. Normal silent reading rate in English is two hundred and thirty eight words per minute for non-fiction and two hundred and sixty for fiction. There is no evidence for reading gears except for the distinction between reading and scanning. There is no need for different language processes in reading and listening.

S2: That second one is a rejection of a theory.

S1: And lesson four rests on it. Sub-headings written as claims are a gift, and the first pass gets them for nothing.

S2: So give me the five Cs.

S1: Category, a review and meta-analysis with one original experiment attached, whose size I can't answer yet. Context, the psychology of reading rate against a widely repeated figure of three hundred, standing on or arguing with Tinker and Carver, and I'd have to read to find out which. Correctness, the assumptions look sound from here and the obvious risk is in what counts as a study fit to pool, which a first pass can't check. Contributions, a pooled estimate with ranges, a second one for reading aloud, a history of where three hundred came from, and an argument that it's an overestimate. Clarity, the general discussion's headings are whole sentences, which is a good sign.

S2: How long did that take?

S1: Eight minutes. And it's enough to decide three things. Whether to read the paper, what to cite it for, and what you'd have to check before citing it for anything else.

S2: We've left out the second pass worked on a methods section, the case where a second pass contradicts the first, which is this course's own baseball story, and the five things people get wrong, including what peer review actually means. The full lesson has all of it, and the paper is free. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Ten minutes, five questions, and then you may stop.
