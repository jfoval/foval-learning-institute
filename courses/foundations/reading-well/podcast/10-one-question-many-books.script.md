S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Reading Well, lesson ten, the last one. One question, many books.

S1: Everything so far has been one reader and one text. This is the thing people actually want to do. You have a question, nobody has written the book that answers it because the question is yours, and what you have instead is four or six books that touch it, none of which was written to.

S2: Adler calls this the fourth level.

S1: Syntopical reading, and his framing is the whole idea. In syntopical reading, it is you and your concerns that are primarily to be served, not the books that you read.

S2: Which inverts what I've been doing for nine lessons.

S1: Analytical reading serves the book. You work out what the author claimed and whether it holds, and the measure of success is that the author would recognise your account. This serves you. The books are evidence about your question, and much of each book won't bear on it.

S2: So what's the natural mistake?

S1: Read the four books properly, one after another, and compare at the end. And Adler names why that fails. It would be to place the individual books first in the order of your priorities, and your problem second. Do it that way and you finish with four summaries and the question you started with, because you never framed it so all four could answer.

S2: Give me the five steps.

S1: Finding the relevant passages, not the relevant books. Bringing the authors to terms. Getting the questions clear. Defining the issues. And analysing the discussion.

S2: And the second is the hard one.

S1: And it inverts lesson five. There, you come to terms with the author, finding his sense of his key words. Here, in Adler's words, it is you who must establish the terms, and bring your authors to them rather than the other way around. Which comes down to forcing an author to use your language, rather than using his.

S2: That sounds uncomfortable.

S1: He's honest about it. This is probably the most difficult step in syntopical reading, and all of our normal reading habits are opposed to this, because we're used to letting the author set the framing.

S2: Work one.

S1: A narrow question, and four texts this course has already used. When you hit a sentence you cannot understand, should you stop and settle it?

S2: And the four?

S1: Adler, on a first pass through a hard book. Read it through without ever stopping to look up or ponder the things you do not understand right away. Ruskin, from the paragraph you marked in lesson six. You must get into the habit of looking intensely at words, and assuring yourself of their meaning, syllable by syllable, nay, letter by letter. Keshav, whose first pass reads the headings and ignores everything under them. And the Harvard reading habits, which tell you to write down the questions you hear yourself asking, because they are reminders of the unfinished business you still have with a text.

S2: They're not using the same vocabulary.

S1: And two words are doing different jobs in different mouths. Understand. Ruskin's is understanding a word, Adler's is understanding an argument, and Keshav's is narrower still and genre-specific. And stop. Adler forbids stopping during one particular traversal of one particular kind of book. Ruskin isn't describing a traversal at all.

S2: So build the terms.

S1: Two, built for the question rather than taken from anyone. A pass is one traversal of a text with a stated purpose. And to resolve a difficulty is to settle it at the moment you meet it.

S2: Now translate them.

S1: Adler. On a first pass through a hard book, do not resolve. Keshav. Don't even go to the places the difficulties live on the first pass, and on the second, read with care and still defer. Harvard. Record the question when it arises, so whether you settle it now or later it is still there to be settled. And Ruskin. Resolve, at the grain of the word, as a standing habit.

S2: Three of them say keep going and one says stop. That looks like a three to one vote.

S1: And that's what step two is for, because it isn't. Ruskin answers this question, and he answers it the opposite way. Four hundred words before the sentence I quoted he tells you to get good dictionaries and then, whenever you are in doubt about a word, hunt it down patiently, and after that, never let a word escape you that looks suspicious. He adds, honestly, it is severe work.

S2: So he does stop.

S1: What differs is the scope. Adler's prohibition is scoped to one traversal of one kind of book, and he can afford it because a second traversal is coming. Ruskin's carries no scope at all. He does have a second pass, and says a book is not serviceable until it has been read, and re-read, but he never makes the prohibition depend on which pass you're in.

S2: So the vote becomes.

S1: A question about whether the instruction is a property of the first pass or of reading as such, and that question is the thing to take away. And there's a second issue underneath it, about grain, between Ruskin, whose grain is the word, and Adler, whose grain is the argument, with Keshav choosing by genre.

S2: And the fifth step?

S1: The one people skip, because it doesn't feel like an answer. Adler's name for what it's trying to be is dialectical objectivity, and his definition is one sentence. The syntopical reader, in short, tries to look at all sides and to take no sides.

S2: Is that achievable?

S1: He says not. Absolute objectivity is not humanly possible, and the half you'll fail at is looking at all sides rather than taking none. His instruction is to try anyway. So the output is the issues with their alternatives ordered, and not a verdict. Which is better than a verdict, because you now know the question you started with was two questions, that one turns on scope and the other on grain, and which author to go back to for each.

S2: Let me end where the course began. Bacon.

S1: Of Studies, one page, and lesson one opened with it. Here's the close. Some books also may be read by deputy, and extracts made of them by others; but that would be only in the less important arguments, and the meaner sort of books, else distilled books are like common distilled waters, flashy things.

S2: Unpack it.

S1: Read by deputy means read by somebody else on your behalf, and flashy in sixteen twenty five meant insipid, watery, without taste. He's writing about a world where the extract was made by another person, by hand. And the sentence hasn't aged in its reference. A book summary, an executive briefing and a machine's answer about a book are all extracts made by another. But his objection is that the distillate is thin, and this course has a different one. Every one of those hands you somebody else's decisions about what mattered, and in syntopical work those decisions are the thing under examination.

S2: The framing is what I came for.

S1: And a summary is the one part of a book guaranteed not to contain it. He isn't saying never read a summary. He's saying know which kind of book you're doing it to.
