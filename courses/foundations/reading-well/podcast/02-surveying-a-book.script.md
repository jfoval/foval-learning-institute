S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Reading Well, lesson two. Surveying a book, which is how you decide whether to read it. And the claim is that there's a procedure, it takes under an hour, and almost nobody does it. Six steps, from Adler.

S2: Before the steps. What is a survey for?

S1: Deciding. Not for getting a head start on the reading. At the end of it you can say what kind of book this is, what it argues, how it's built, and whether you're going to read it, and you can give your reasons.

S2: All right, work it on something.

S1: The lesson works it on Darwin's Origin of Species, first edition, eighteen fifty nine, which is free and complete at Project Gutenberg. And one note before we start, which is the lesson's own. We're surveying the book. We're not arguing the biology.

S2: Step one.

S1: Title page and preface. And the title page gives you the whole thesis. On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection, or the Preservation of Favoured Races in the Struggle for Life.

S2: That's a title and then the mechanism.

S1: And then the mechanism again in other words. Underneath the title sit two epigraphs, one from Whewell and one from Bacon, both to the effect that God works by general laws rather than by particular interventions.

S2: Which tells you what?

S1: Read that as a surveyor rather than as a theologian. Before a word of the argument, the author has told you who he expected to have to reassure. That's what step one is for.

S2: Is there a preface?

S1: None in this edition, and the Introduction does the work of one. Its last paragraph states the case in his own words. I am fully convinced that species are not immutable. And, natural selection has been the main but not exclusive means of modification.

S2: He hedges it himself.

S1: Two minutes in and you have the book's claim, in the author's words, with his own hedge attached. Step two is the contents, and you read it the way you'd read a road map before a trip.

S2: What's the shape?

S1: Fourteen chapters. Variation, first under domestication and then in nature. Struggle for existence. Natural selection. Laws of variation. Then four chapters of difficulties. Then the fossil record, distribution, affinities, and a recapitulation.

S2: So I can already say where the argument is made and where it's defended.

S1: Having read nothing but a list. And then the wrinkle, which is why this book was chosen. Straight after that contents page comes a second one, headed Detailed Contents, breaking every chapter into its topics in order. Chapter six alone lists transitions, the absence or rarity of transitional varieties, organs of extreme perfection, and eleven more.

S2: Two contents pages.

S1: And the second is the analytical table of contents Adler praises, which he says has nearly disappeared from publishing. Two pages of it are worth more than the first fifty pages of the book.

S2: Step three.

S1: The index, and you read it as a list rather than looking anything up. Scan Darwin's and you're reading his own vocabulary with weights on it. Species, varieties, selection, sterility, instinct, and a very large number of proper names. Agassiz, Lyell, Hooker, Wallace.

S2: What does that tell you?

S1: Before you've read a paragraph, that this is a book engaging with named contemporaries about particular cases, rather than a treatise reasoning from first principles.

S2: Step four is the blurb, and eighteen fifty nine won't have one.

S1: It doesn't. The title page carries his fellowships and his previous book instead, which is what a publisher's pitch looked like then. And absence is still evidence. Nobody was selling this to a general reader.

S2: Step five. Which chapter is pivotal?

S1: The contents nominate chapter four, natural selection, because it's the title's own mechanism and the four chapters after it are headed as difficulties. And the Introduction confirms it. This fundamental subject of natural selection will be treated at some length in the fourth chapter.

S2: So he tells you.

S1: Writers say where the centre of a book is, in the introduction, and readers skip the introduction. Step five is very often done for you.

S2: And step six.

S1: Dip in, and never skip the last pages. Open chapter nine anywhere and you find him stating an objection against himself, and he writes this. Geology assuredly does not reveal any such finely graduated organic chain; and this, perhaps, is the most obvious and gravest objection which can be urged against my theory.

S2: He says that about his own book.

S1: One paragraph, and you know what kind of writer you're dealing with.

S2: So what's the verdict?

S1: Written down, and this is the whole output. What kind of book: a scientific argument for a general educated reader, arguing from accumulated cases rather than from theory. What it argues: that species aren't fixed, and that natural selection is the main but not the only mechanism. How it's built: five chapters to state the mechanism, four to answer the strongest objections, four on the evidence, one to recapitulate. Whether to read it: yes, and chapters four and fourteen carry the case while six to nine carry the defence.

S2: How long did that take?

S1: About thirty minutes. And it's knowledge about the whole book, which reading the first fifty pages could not have given you at any speed.

S2: Now, Darwin is a generous case. What happens on a book with no apparatus at all?

S1: The lesson runs the steps on Bacon's Essays and three of them come back with almost nothing. Fifty eight short pieces, each headed by its topic. Of Truth, Of Death, Of Studies. Every title names a subject and not one tells you what he thinks about it. No index. No publisher's note.

S2: So the method failed.

S1: That's the move I want you to resist. What those three steps told you is that this is not a book with one argument running through it. It's a collection, and the unit is the essay rather than the book. Which is a real finding, and it changes what you do. Step six does the work, and a whole essay here is two pages.

S2: And the modern version?

S1: A paperback with chapter titles chosen to intrigue rather than inform, a blurb of three sentences of praise, and no index. Those three absences have told you it's built to be read once, front to back, quickly, and not to be gone back to.

S2: An apparatus that carries no information is information.

S1: About the book. And the survey still ends in a verdict, and still costs twenty minutes rather than a week.

S2: We've left out the four questions a survey can and can't answer, the executive summary at work, and Adler's rule about a first pass through a hard book, which is the one most readers find counterintuitive. The full lesson has all of it, with the exercise that asks you to survey a book off your own shelf in an hour. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Survey first, and write the verdict down.
