S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Reading Well, lesson four. What reading costs, which is where this course closes a door.

S2: Which door?

S1: There's a shortcut on sale and it doesn't work. But before any of that, the lesson asks you to do something, and I'd ask you to do it too if you're somewhere you can.

S2: Which is?

S1: Take five hundred words of ordinary non-fiction, read it at the speed you'd use if you meant to understand it, and time yourself in seconds. Divide five hundred by the seconds and multiply by sixty. That's your words per minute on that text. Write it down.

S2: Because a rate you've measured beats a rate you've read about.

S1: And you'll want it twice more. Now the measurements. These come from Marc Brysbaert's twenty nineteen meta-analysis, which pooled a hundred and ninety studies of reading rate covering eighteen thousand five hundred and seventy three participants.

S2: Before you give me the number. Put three things in order for me, slowest to fastest. Reading aloud. Reading fiction silently. Reading non-fiction silently.

S1: Aloud is slowest, at a hundred and eighty three words a minute. Then silent non-fiction at two hundred and thirty eight. Then silent fiction at two hundred and sixty.

S2: That's a narrow spread.

S1: Seventy seven words a minute from the slowest of the three to the fastest, and that is the first thing the measurements tell you. Reading rate is not a dial with much travel on it.

S2: And the spread between people?

S1: In the paper's own words. For silent reading of English non-fiction most adults fall in the range of a hundred and seventy five to three hundred words per minute; for fiction the range is two hundred to three hundred and twenty.

S2: So where does three hundred come from? Because that's the number everybody repeats.

S1: The paper traces it, through Huey in nineteen oh one, Tinker's studies, Taylor's sample of twelve thousand one hundred and forty three readers, Carver, and Rayner's later reviews. And the answer is that three hundred is at the top of the measured range rather than in the middle of it. It's the figure this meta-analysis exists to correct.

S2: And Tinker's own view of fast?

S1: Quoted in the same paper. Even with easy material, five hundred words per minute is very fast reading.

S2: All right. Why is there a ceiling at all?

S1: And before I answer, the lesson makes a disclosure here and I'll make it too. Most of what follows rests on a twenty sixteen monograph by Rayner and colleagues, and this course did not open it. Everything attributed to it comes from the Association for Psychological Science's public summary and from Brysbaert's account of it, and the lesson claims nothing beyond those two.

S2: Good. Now the eyes.

S1: Reading isn't a smooth sweep across a line. The eye jumps, stops, and jumps again, and it takes in text only during the stops. The jumps are called saccades and the stops are fixations.

S2: And that limits things how?

S1: Two ways at once. There's a limit on how much can be taken in per stop, and the eye needs time to move between stops. So a rate is roughly a count of fixations multiplied by what each one delivers, and you can't raise either term far without giving something up.

S2: And the movements aren't random.

S1: This is the part that matters most. Rayner and colleagues describe saccades as movements that naturally move to the parts of text in most need of processing and to move back to material that has not been adequately understood.

S2: Move back.

S1: The backward movements that speed-reading courses describe as wasted motion are the mechanism by which comprehension repairs itself.

S2: Let me test that. There are apps that show you one word at a time, in a fixed place, so your eyes never move. Eight hundred words a minute. What does the evidence predict?

S1: Less than you'd get from reading the same text normally, and the gap grows as the speed rises. The technique is called RSVP, and because it removes eye movements entirely it removes the backward ones too. The summary's finding is that eliminating eye movements generally impairs the recall accuracy of material presented at faster speeds.

S2: And notice what the problem isn't.

S1: It isn't that the words go by too fast to see. It's that you've lost the ability to go back, and going back is how you fix a sentence you half understood, which is most sentences in anything hard.

S2: What about training peripheral vision?

S1: That runs into a fact about eyes rather than about training. Peripheral information appears fuzzy and unfocused, which is why you move your eyes in the first place.

S2: Now do the arithmetic on a marketed claim.

S1: Take fifteen hundred words a minute with full comprehension. That's twenty five words every second. Put your own measured rate beside it, and for most readers it's five to eight times what they just recorded on a passage they were actually trying to understand.

S2: So what would have to be true?

S1: One of two things, because there are only two terms in the product. Either the eye is taking in five to eight times more per fixation, which runs into the fuzziness of peripheral vision. Or it's making five to eight times as many fixations per second, which runs into how long a fixation takes and how far the eye can move.

S2: And the evidence?

S1: Says the same thing. Comprehension falls as speed rises, and above roughly a thousand words a minute the fall is severe.

S2: Is there any version of the claim that survives? Because that's the fair question.

S1: There is, and it's worth having. These programmes do appear to train skimming, meaning getting the gist of running text quickly, and that's a real skill. It's a cousin of the survey in lesson two rather than the same thing. The survey reads a book's compressed copies, its contents page and index and blurb, while skimming in this sense reads the text itself fast. Systematic skimming, to give the survey its proper name, is one of the two sublevels of inspectional reading rather than a level of its own.

S2: And what they don't deliver?

S1: The thing being sold, which is analytical reading at five times the speed.

S2: Anything that does raise my rate?

S1: One thing nobody can sell you in a weekend. Vocabulary and practice. A word you know well is processed faster than a word you half know.
