S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Reading Well, lesson six. Reading with a pen, and why a highlighter isn't one.

S1: Lesson five asked you to do a great deal inside one chapter. Classify the book, state its claim in a sentence, outline its parts, find the terms and settle them, gather the argument. Try that with nothing in your hand and you'll lose most of it before the chapter ends.

S2: So what's a mark for?

S1: This is the one distinction the rest of the lesson hangs on. A mark must record what kind of thought you had. This is the structure. This I doubt. This I didn't understand. This connects to something else.

S2: And a highlight?

S1: Records one thing only. Something here. It doesn't say what and it doesn't say why, and a month later you won't know either, because what made the sentence worth colouring was in your head at the time and the colour didn't capture it.

S2: So it's not about effort or neatness.

S1: It's about what each record contains. A judgement is a thing you can retrieve. A location is a thing you have to reconstruct, which means doing the reading again.

S2: Is there a second reason?

S1: And it's the one people miss. Most of a mark's work is done at the moment you make it. To write, this doesn't follow, you have to decide that it doesn't follow. To write, he means something narrower by accuracy here, you have to notice the shift and name it. The note is a by-product of a judgement you were forced to make. Highlighting asks no judgement at all, which is exactly why it feels so restful.

S2: Give me the system.

S1: Five marks, and the symbols don't matter. The five kinds do. A vertical line in the margin for, this is the claim. A question mark for, I don't understand this. A cross for, I doubt this, or it doesn't follow. A circle round a word for, this is a term in the author's own sense. And an arrow for, this connects to something else.

S2: Why those five?

S1: Because they're the five kinds of thought worth telling apart. Not understanding isn't disagreeing, and the two need different actions.

S2: Now, there's a real page in the lesson.

S1: John Ruskin, from a lecture he gave in Manchester in December eighteen sixty four in aid of a library fund, published the next year in Sesame and Lilies. It's about how to read. Here's the passage.

S2: Go on.

S1: And, therefore, first of all, I tell you earnestly and authoritatively, and he puts in brackets here, I know I am right in this, you must get into the habit of looking intensely at words, and assuring yourself of their meaning, syllable by syllable, nay, letter by letter. Then a long clause about letters and sounds, and then this. You might read all the books in the British Museum, if you could live long enough, and remain an utterly illiterate, uneducated person; but if you read ten pages of a good book, letter by letter, that is to say, with real accuracy, you are for evermore in some measure an educated person. The entire difference between education and non-education, as regards the merely intellectual part of it, consists in this accuracy.

S2: And you've cut it.

S1: I have, and the lesson says so, because Ruskin doesn't stop there. The same paragraph runs on for another two hundred and twenty one words. Some of it is still about reading. Most of the rest is class. Words have a peerage, with true descent and ancient blood on one side and modern canaille on the other, and he finishes by saying that a false accent or a mistaken syllable is enough, in the parliament of any civilized nation, to assign to a man a certain degree of inferior standing for ever.

S2: And his view of that?

S1: And this is right, he says. Though the same sentence goes on, but it is a pity that the accuracy insisted on is not greater, and required to a serious purpose. I cut it for room, not to protect him, and it's worth reading before you take him as an authority on anything except accuracy.

S2: All right. Mark it for me.

S1: Six marks. A vertical line beside, you must get into the habit of looking intensely at words. That's the claim, and I want to find it again without rereading the rest.

S2: Second.

S1: A cross beside the bracket, I know I am right in this. He's offering his own certainty as a reason. It isn't one. That's not a complaint about Ruskin, who's entitled to be sure of things. It's a note that this particular sentence gave me nothing to weigh.

S2: Third.

S1: A question mark beside the clause about letters and sounds, which I've read four times and cannot restate. That's lesson three's signature for syntax, so I did lesson three's fix for syntax, and the structure came apart at once and I was still holding nothing. What I'm missing is a fact the sentence assumes and never supplies, and the fix isn't on this page.

S2: Fourth.

S1: A circle round literature and round illiterate. He's using both in a sense of his own, and the text puts both in quotation marks, which is the signal worth learning to spot. For him an illiterate person isn't one who cannot read but one who reads without accuracy.

S2: Fifth.

S1: A cross beside, you are for evermore in some measure an educated person. Ten pages read accurately and you're permanently educated in some measure. That's a much larger claim than anything he's given a reason for, and the hedge, in some measure, is doing a great deal of quiet work.

S2: And sixth.

S1: An arrow beside the last sentence, pointing at my note on lesson five. Ruskin's route to a word is its history. Lesson five's fifth rule takes the other route, into the sense this author pinned down. That's a difference of method rather than of destination, and it's the mark I would certainly have lost.

S2: Now suppose I'd highlighted that paragraph instead.

S1: My guess is three phrases. Looking intensely at words. Letter by letter. An educated person. They're the memorable ones and they're what the eye stops on.

S2: And in a month?

S1: You'd know Ruskin said something about reading words carefully. You wouldn't know that his support was an assertion of his own confidence, that one clause defeated you, that two of his words aren't in their ordinary senses, or what it connected to. Five of those six marks were the useful ones, and not one of them is a phrase.
