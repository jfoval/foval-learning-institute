S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Reading Well, lesson five. Finding the argument, and coming to terms with an author.

S1: Lesson two decided whether to read the book. Lesson three explained why it was hard. This one is what you do once you're inside a chapter and have decided to take it seriously.

S2: And there's a procedure.

S1: Adler's. Fifteen numbered rules in three stages, and this lesson is the first two stages, rules one to eight. The order isn't decoration, and this is the reason. Each stage produces the material the next one needs. You can't say whether an argument holds until you have the argument, and you can't have the argument until you know what the words in it mean in this author's hands. None of it is measured, by the way. Adler is a philosopher writing about a craft rather than a researcher reporting a result. And the lesson says there's a real objection to this ordering, which lesson nine takes up, so hold that question rather than settling it here.

S2: Which chapter are we in?

S1: Darwin's Origin, chapter three, Struggle for Existence, about six thousand words, free at Project Gutenberg. And the same note lesson two carried applies. We're reading the chapter, not arguing the biology.

S2: Why that chapter?

S1: Because its whole argument turns on a phrase Darwin knows you'll misread, and he says so in the fourth paragraph.

S2: Then let me guess first. The chapter is called Struggle for Existence. What do you think he means by struggle?

S1: Most people say animals fighting each other for food. Competition, in the ordinary sense. And that's what the title invites.

S2: And it isn't his sense.

S1: Here's his sentence. I should premise that I use the term Struggle for Existence in a large and metaphorical sense, including dependence of one being on another, and including, which is more important, not only the life of the individual, but success in leaving progeny.

S2: Dependence. That's nearly the opposite of what the word suggests.

S1: He gives the example himself. A plant on the edge of a desert is said to struggle for life against the drought, though more properly it should be said to be dependent on the moisture.

S2: And the parenthesis, which is more important.

S1: That's not politeness. It's telling you which half of the definition the argument is going to lean on, which is reproduction rather than survival. And he admits the whole thing is loose. In these several senses, which pass into each other, I use for convenience sake the general term of struggle for existence.

S2: So he's bundled several things under one word.

S1: And that's worth stopping on, because it's a counterexample to the rule we're using. Rule five assumes an author has one settled sense waiting to be found. Sometimes there isn't one, and Darwin has just said so out loud. When coming to terms returns the answer, he has three senses here and slides between them, that isn't the rule failing. It's the rule's most valuable output, because a place where an author is sliding between senses is a place where the argument is weakest.

S2: Now. Why is any of this more than preliminary politeness?

S1: Because the chapter's argument is valid only under his sense and not under yours. If struggle meant animals fighting, the conclusion that there must be a struggle everywhere would be plainly false, since most organisms don't fight anything. Under the wide sense, it follows.

S2: So a reader who never came to terms.

S1: Would have read the chapter, understood every sentence, and concluded the author was overreaching.

S2: All right, the second rule you wanted. Rule seven.

S1: Know his arguments by finding them in, or constructing them out of, sequences of sentences. And the second half of that is the part that matters. Arguments aren't laid out anywhere, as a rule. They're distributed. A premise in one paragraph, a second four pages later inside an example, the conclusion in a sentence that reads like a summary.

S2: And the job is to gather them.

S1: And write them down, because the gaps only become visible once the argument is on the page in lines.

S2: Do it on Darwin.

S1: His three sentences run one after another, which is unusually tidy of him, and take the tidy case first. Here's the first. A struggle for existence inevitably follows from the high rate at which all organic beings tend to increase. And the second. Every being, which during its natural lifetime produces several eggs or seeds, must suffer destruction during some period of its life, and during some season or occasional year, otherwise, on the principle of geometrical increase, its numbers would quickly become so inordinately great that no country could support the product.

S2: And the third.

S1: Hence, as more individuals are produced than can possibly survive, there must in every case be a struggle for existence, either one individual with another of the same species, or with the individuals of distinct species, or with the physical conditions of life.

S2: Set out as reasoning.

S1: Three steps. Step one. Every kind of organism tends to increase geometrically. Step two. More individuals are produced than can possibly survive. Step three. Therefore there must be a struggle for existence.

S2: Step two doesn't follow from step one on its own.

S1: It does not, and that's the exercise. Something has to be added.

S2: What?

S1: That the supply of food and room doesn't increase geometrically along with the population. Without it, a geometrically increasing population could be fed by a geometrically increasing world and nothing would have to die.

S2: Is it in the text?

S1: Yes, and not where the argument needs it. In the paragraph carrying the argument it appears only inside a subordinate clause, that its numbers would quickly become so inordinately great that no country could support the product, and then as an aside, for the world would not hold them.

S2: So he does say it.

S1: He states the constraint plainly four printed pages on, under the checks to increase. The amount of food for each species of course gives the extreme limit to which each can increase. That's close enough to serve, it's in a main clause, and it's a long way from the three sentences that need it, in a paragraph about something else.

S2: So not missing.

S1: Misplaced. And a reader who never sets the argument out in three lines never notices that the paragraph is leaning on something it doesn't say. That's the normal case, and it's the reason rule seven is worth the effort.

S2: One more thing about that argument.

S1: It closes the loop on rule five. Darwin's third sentence says there must be a struggle in every case, and that's only true under the wide sense he defined earlier, which includes a plant depending on moisture. The argument and the definition are load-bearing for each other, which is why the stages run in the order they do.

S2: We've left out rules one to four, which classify the book and outline the chapter, rule six on finding the important sentences, rule eight on the problems an author knows he hasn't solved, and the wrinkle, which is a term the reader settles too early and never goes back to. The full lesson has all of it, free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Find the terms first, because the argument is built out of them.
