The test measures whether you can evaluate a stranger's argument. This project measures whether you can do it to an argument you actually care about, and then build one of your own that survives the same treatment. That is harder, and it is the thing the course was for.

Budget about five hours, spread over a week or two rather than done in one sitting. The gap matters: you will catch things in your own writing on Tuesday that you could not see on Sunday.

## Choose the argument

Pick one argument you actually encountered. A newspaper column, a podcast segment, a company blog post, a politician's speech, a leaflet through your door, a letter to an editor, a post from someone you follow, a paragraph in a book. It must be:

- **Real, and quotable.** You need the words, the source, and the date. If you cannot link it or cite it, pick something else.
- **Something you have an opinion about.** Not the easiest one to take apart. The one where you already think you know the answer, because that is where the check earns its keep.
- **Long enough to have a structure.** A single sentence will not do. A few paragraphs with more than one reason in them is about right.

You have been collecting these in your folder since lesson 1. Use one of those.

**On subject matter.** Political and religious arguments are allowed here, and so is anything else. You are choosing the argument, and the rubric below rewards the quality of your reconstruction and your evaluation, not the conclusion you reach. A project that steelmans a position you disagree with and then answers it fairly scores full marks whatever side it lands on. A project that treats an opponent's argument carelessly loses marks whatever side it lands on.

## What to produce

Seven parts. Write them in this order, because each one depends on the last.

### 1. The original, quoted

The argument as it was made, quoted accurately, with source and date. Do not paraphrase yet. If it is long, quote the part that carries the argument and summarise the rest, marking clearly which is which.

### 2. The reconstruction

Standard form: numbered premises, a line, "C:" for the conclusion. Unstated premises in square brackets, supplied in the form the arguer would accept, not the form easiest to attack.

Then two diagrams:

- **Linked or convergent.** Which premises work together and which stand alone. This decides what happens if one of them falls.
- **Toulmin's layout**, naming the warrant (the rule licensing the step), the qualifier if there is one, and the rebuttal if there is one. If the argument has no qualifier and no rebuttal, say so; that is itself a finding.

### 3. The steelman

One paragraph putting the argument at its strongest, following Rapoport's rules. The test is not whether it sounds generous. The test is whether someone who holds the position would read your paragraph and say yes, that is what I think.

If you can actually show it to such a person, do, and write down what they said. That is the only real check on this part, and it is worth the awkwardness.

### 4. The evaluation

- **What kind of link is claimed?** Deductive, or which inductive family, or conductive.
- **The verdict on the link.** If deductive, valid or invalid, and if invalid, produce the counterexample: same form, true premises, false conclusion. If inductive, strong or weak, and name the weakness precisely: sample size, unrepresentative sample, an analogy whose shared feature is not the relevant one, a causal claim with the other three readings unexcluded.
- **The ARG conditions on each premise.** Acceptable to the argument's audience, relevant to the conclusion, and together adequate grounds for it. Say which premise fails which condition. "The premises are weak" is not an evaluation.
- **Any appeal to authority** gets the six critical questions, and you say which one it fails or passes.
- **Any probability claim** gets counted in natural frequencies. If the argument gives you a percentage, convert it and show the working.

### 5. The fallacy check

List every named pattern you can find in the argument. For each one, run its critical questions and say whether they convict it or acquit it.

This part is marked on the testing, not the naming. An argument that contains a slippery slope with a documented mechanism at each step is not thereby defective, and saying "slippery slope" and stopping scores nothing. Neither does hunting for fallacies that are not there.

### 6. Your own argument

One page, on the same question, arguing your own position. Whether that is against the original or in agreement with it is up to you.

Written by Weston's rules: the claim early and definite; the argument as the outline; each basic premise defended by its own short argument or a source; the strongest objection to *your* position detailed and met; a fair summary claiming no more than you have shown.

The objection to your own position goes in Rapoport form, the same standard you held the original to. Then either answer it, or concede the part that is right and rewrite your conclusion with a qualifier and a rebuttal that say exactly how far your claim now reaches.

### 7. The note

Half a page. What would you have concluded about this argument before the course? Did that change? If it did, say which tool changed it. If it did not, say whether you now hold the same position for different reasons, and what those are.

This is not a reflection exercise for its own sake. An argument you evaluated honestly and still disagree with is a normal outcome, and saying so precisely is worth more than a conversion story.

## How it is marked

Twelve points. Nine to pass.

| What is assessed | Points |
|---|---|
| The reconstruction is faithful, and the missing premises are ones the arguer would accept | 2 |
| The steelman would satisfy someone who holds the position | 2 |
| The link is correctly classified and correctly judged, with the counterexample or the weakness actually shown rather than asserted | 2 |
| The ARG and critical-question evaluation is specific to these premises, not generic | 2 |
| The fallacy check names patterns and tests them, rather than labelling | 1 |
| Your own argument has acceptable premises, a link you can name, and its strongest objection honestly stated | 2 |
| The qualifier, if you used one, matches the strength of what you actually showed | 1 |

The two-point items are the ones where most projects lose marks, and they lose them the same way: a reconstruction that quietly weakens the original, and a steelman written to be refuted rather than to be recognised. Both are easy to spot and both come from the same habit, which is arguing to win. The whole course is against that habit.

## Marking your own work

There is no grader here yet. Mark it yourself, a week after you finish, against the table above, and be harder on yourself than you want to be. Two checks make self-marking honest:

- For part 3, the steelman: would the person you are answering sign it? If you cannot ask them, find the best written statement of their position you can and compare.
- For part 6, your own argument: hand it to someone who disagrees with you and ask them where they would stop reading. That is Weston's rule 38, and it is the one people skip.

Keep the project. When you take a course in history, ethics, economics or apologetics here, you will be asked to argue, and this is the standard.
