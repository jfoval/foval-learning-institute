  - q: >-
      A council report states, "Attendance at the leisure centre fell 12% last year, because the
      pool was closed for eight weeks of resurfacing." Is this an argument, and how do you tell?
    options:
      - >-
        No, an explanation. Both parties already accept that attendance fell; the closure is
        offered to account for it, not to persuade anyone that it happened
      - >-
        Yes, an argument. It has a "because", which is a premise indicator, so the closure is
        the premise and the fall in attendance is the conclusion it supports
      - >-
        Yes, an argument, but a weak one, since eight weeks of closure is not on its own enough
        to establish a fall of that size right across a whole year
      - >-
        No, a bare assertion. Nothing here is offered in support of anything, so there is
        neither a premise nor a conclusion here to be identified at all
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The test is direction. An argument moves from what is offered to what is claimed; an
      explanation starts from something already granted and says why it happened. Nobody is
      being persuaded that attendance fell, because the report states it as a fact both it and
      its reader accept. So B. Option B treats "because" as decisive, and the lesson 1 warning
      is that indicator words are not failsafe: "because" appears in explanations as often as in
      arguments. Option C evaluates an argument that was never made. Option D is wrong because
      something is being offered, just not as support.
  - q: >-
      A neighbour says, "You should get the boiler serviced this month. The warranty runs out in
      November." What unstated premise does the argument need, in the form the neighbour would
      accept?
    options:
      - >-
        That every boiler needs an annual service, which is the general rule the specific advice
        about this boiler is an instance of
      - >-
        That the boiler is likely to fail soon, since otherwise there would be no reason to
        raise the question of servicing it at all
      - >-
        That a service is cheaper, or free, while the warranty is still running, so doing it
        before November costs less than doing it after
      - >-
        That warranties on domestic appliances are generally worth having, which is what makes
        the November date relevant to the decision here
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Supply the premise the arguer would sign, and among those take the strongest. The
      neighbour's two sentences only connect if the warranty makes a difference to what the
      service costs or covers, so B. Option A is a premise the neighbour might well accept, but
      it makes the warranty date irrelevant, and the date is half the argument. Option B
      smuggles in a claim about this boiler that the neighbour never made and might reject.
      Option D is true and too weak to carry the step; it explains why warranties matter in
      general without linking to the timing.
  - q: >-
      Consider, "All mammals lay eggs. All whales are mammals. So all whales lay eggs." Which
      verdict is correct?
    options:
      - >-
        Invalid, because the conclusion is false, and an argument cannot be valid when the thing
        it concludes is not actually the case
      - >-
        Valid but unsound, because the form rules out true premises with a false conclusion,
        while the first premise is in fact false
      - >-
        Invalid, because a false premise cannot support a conclusion, so nothing whatever
        follows from an argument that contains one
      - >-
        Sound, because the reasoning is impeccable, and soundness is a property of the reasoning
        rather than of the premises it happens to use
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Validity is a property of the link, not of the sentences. There is no possible case where
      "all mammals lay eggs" and "all whales are mammals" are both true and "all whales lay
      eggs" is false, so the argument is valid; the first premise is false, so it is unsound.
      That is A. Options A and C are belief bias, the effect Evans, Barston and Pollard measured
      in 1983, where a conclusion you know to be false pulls the validity judgement with it.
      Option D reverses the definition; soundness is validity plus true premises.
  - q: An argument has all true premises and a true conclusion. What follows about its validity?
    options:
      - >-
        It is valid, since the one combination validity rules out is true premises with a false
        conclusion, and that combination has plainly not occurred in this case
      - >-
        It is sound, and soundness entails validity, so the question of the link has already
        been settled for us by the truth of the parts themselves
      - >-
        It is probably valid, and the more premises turn out true the more confident you can
        reasonably be that the link between them genuinely holds
      - >-
        Nothing follows. True premises and a true conclusion are consistent with a valid
        argument and with an invalid one, so the link still has to be tested
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Validity is about whether the conclusion could be false while the premises are true, which
      is a question about possible cases, not about the actual ones. "Paris is in France; grass
      is green; so 2 + 2 = 4" has true premises and a true conclusion and no link at all. So C.
      Option A confuses "has not occurred" with "could not occur". Option B assumes what is
      being asked, since soundness is defined as validity plus true premises. Option C treats a
      link as something evidence about the premises could support, which it is not.
  - q: >-
      To show that "Some councillors are landlords; some landlords are magistrates; so some
      councillors are magistrates" is invalid, what do you actually have to produce?
    options:
      - >-
        An argument of the same form whose premises are true and whose conclusion is false,
        which need not describe any real council at all
      - >-
        A real council where it happens that no councillor is a magistrate, which shows the
        conclusion false and so shows the argument to be invalid
      - >-
        A proof that the two "some" premises cannot both be true at once, since that is
        precisely what makes the inference break down here
      - >-
        A demonstration that the conclusion is false in most cases, which is enough to show that
        the premises here do not guarantee it
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      A counterexample is a possible case, not an actual one, and it targets the form. "Some
      cats are black things; some black things are cars; so some cats are cars" has true
      premises and a false conclusion in the same shape, which settles it. So B. Option B would
      show the conclusion false in one case without showing the form defective, and the
      conclusion might be true in your council while the argument is still invalid. Option C
      misreads the premises, which are perfectly consistent. Option D confuses invalidity with
      unreliability; one possible case is enough.
  - q: >-
      A gym's rule reads, "You may use the pool only if you have completed the induction."
      Vikram has completed the induction. What follows?
    options:
      - >-
        That Vikram may use the pool, since the induction is the one condition the rule sets for
        being allowed into the water
      - >-
        That Vikram may use the pool, provided also that he is a member, a thing the rule leaves
        unstated but plainly assumes
      - >-
        Nothing about whether Vikram may use the pool. The rule makes the induction necessary
        for pool use, not sufficient for it
      - >-
        That the induction is sufficient for pool use, so anyone at all who completes it has
        thereby acquired the right to swim there
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      "P only if Q" means Q is required for P, which translates as "if P then Q", so "if you may
      use the pool, then you have completed the induction." Affirming Q tells you nothing about
      P; there may be other requirements, such as membership or opening hours. So C. Options A
      and D read the rule backwards, turning a necessary condition into a sufficient one, which
      is the single most common conditional error. Option B gets the right verdict for the wrong
      reason and then adds a premise the rule never states.
  - q: >-
      "If the parcel was posted first class, it arrives within two days. The parcel did not
      arrive within two days." Which conclusion is valid, and what is the form called?
    options:
      - >-
        That the parcel was not posted first class, by denying the antecedent, which is one of
        the two invalid look-alikes
      - >-
        That the parcel was posted second class, by modus tollens, since the two classes exhaust
        the available options
      - >-
        Nothing follows, because denying the consequent of a conditional leaves the antecedent
        entirely open
      - >-
        That the parcel was not posted first class, by modus tollens, which denies the
        consequent to deny the antecedent
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Denying the consequent is modus tollens and it is valid: if first class guaranteed two
      days, and two days did not happen, then first class did not happen. So D. Option A reaches
      the right conclusion but names the wrong form; denying the antecedent would be "it was not
      posted first class, so it did not arrive in two days". Option B adds a premise about there
      being only two classes, which the argument does not contain. Option C confuses denying the
      consequent, which is valid, with affirming it, which is not.
  - q: >-
      "The match is on unless the pitch is frozen." The pitch was not frozen. A supporter
      concludes the match was on. Is that supported by the sentence alone?
    options:
      - >-
        No. "Unless" is too vague in ordinary English to license any inference at all, so
        nothing can be drawn from a sentence containing it
      - >-
        Yes. "P unless Q" is read as "if not Q then P", so with the pitch unfrozen the sentence
        delivers exactly that conclusion on its own
      - >-
        Yes, but chiefly because the speaker plainly also meant that a frozen pitch would cancel
        the match, which is the reverse direction
      - >-
        No. The sentence gives only the reverse direction, that a frozen pitch would cancel the
        match, and says nothing about an unfrozen one
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The safe reading of "P unless Q" is "if not Q then P", which is the part nobody would
      deny, and that is precisely the inference the supporter draws. So A. Option D has the
      direction backwards; the reverse reading is the one the sentence does not strictly
      license. Option C gets the verdict right by appealing to the direction that is not needed
      here. Option A overstates the difficulty: "unless" carries a defensible reading, and the
      lesson's advice is to translate it as "if not" and write any reverse direction down
      separately.
  - q: >-
      "All the diagrams in the report are hand drawn. Some hand drawn diagrams are inaccurate.
      So some of the diagrams in the report are inaccurate." What does a Venn check show?
    options:
      - >-
        Invalid, because the overlap between hand drawn and inaccurate can fall entirely outside
        the region belonging to the report's diagrams
      - >-
        Valid, because the report's diagrams fall inside the hand drawn circle, and part of that
        circle can be seen to overlap the inaccurate one
      - >-
        Valid, but only on the traditional reading, which assumes that the categories involved
        are not empty of members in the first place
      - >-
        Invalid, because the first premise is universal while the conclusion is particular, and
        no particular conclusion follows from a universal one
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Draw it. The report's diagrams sit inside "hand drawn". The "some" premise puts an X in
      the overlap of hand drawn and inaccurate, but nothing forces that X into the report's
      region, so the premises can be true with the conclusion false. So B. Option B assumes the
      X lands where it is needed, which is the undistributed middle. Option C invokes
      existential import, which does not rescue this form. Option D states a rule that is not
      general; some valid forms do draw particular conclusions where a particular premise is
      present.
  - q: >-
      A magazine surveys its own readers about reading habits, gets 40,000 replies, and reports
      that 71% of people read more than twenty books a year. What is the main problem?
    options:
      - >-
        The sample is too small to support a claim about the whole population, and a survey of
        this kind would need to be substantially larger before it could be relied on
      - >-
        Nothing much. With 40,000 replies the margin of error is tiny, so the figure can safely
        be taken as accurate to within a fraction of a percentage point
      - >-
        The survey measures what people say rather than what they do, which is really the only
        reason to doubt a figure gathered from this many respondents
      - >-
        The result is probably right about readers but is reported as a claim about people, and
        a magazine's readers are not a representative sample of anyone else
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Representativeness beats size, and a self-selected sample of one magazine's readers is
      drawn from exactly the people most likely to read a lot. So B. Option A gets the diagnosis
      backwards by blaming size; 40,000 is ample and does not fix the bias. Option B makes the
      same mistake and dresses it in a margin of error, which measures sampling noise and not
      sampling bias. Option C names a real limitation of self-report, but it is not what turns
      this into a claim about the wrong population.
  - q: >-
      An argument runs, "Our neighbouring town cut its speed limit to 20 and casualties fell
      18%. Our town should do the same and expect a similar fall." What most weakens it?
    options:
      - >-
        That the two towns differ in population, since a smaller or larger town will not produce
        the same percentage change in casualties
      - >-
        That the neighbouring town has a bypass carrying most of its through traffic, while this
        town's main road runs through the centre
      - >-
        That an 18% fall is a single result, and no policy conclusion should be drawn from one
        town's experience however similar it is
      - >-
        That the two towns have different names, histories and councils, so any analogy between
        them is bound to break down somewhere
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      An analogy is only as good as the relevance of the feature that carries the inference.
      Here the mechanism is traffic on residential roads, and a bypass changes exactly that, so
      A. Option A names a difference that is real but not obviously relevant to a percentage
      change. Option C is too strong; a single well documented case is evidence, just not
      conclusive. Option D lists differences that every analogy has and that no analogy needs to
      survive, which would rule out all analogical reasoning.
  - q: >-
      A study of office workers finds that those who take a lunch break away from their desk
      report lower stress. Which set of readings must be considered before concluding that
      breaks reduce stress?
    options:
      - >-
        That breaks reduce stress, or that the finding is a statistical artefact, since with a
        large enough sample those are the only two possibilities left live
      - >-
        That breaks reduce stress, or that the effect is smaller than reported, or that it
        applies only to office workers rather than to working people generally
      - >-
        That breaks reduce stress, that lower stress makes taking a break easier, that something
        such as workload drives both, or that the association is chance
      - >-
        That breaks reduce stress, or that the workers who take breaks were less stressed to
        begin with, which is the one alternative a correlation leaves open
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      A correlation between A and B admits four readings: A causes B, B causes A, a third thing
      causes both, or coincidence. Here the reverse direction is live, because someone under
      less pressure finds it easier to leave their desk, and workload is an obvious common
      cause. So C. Option D names only the reverse direction and calls it the only one. Options
      A and B skip the causal alternatives entirely and treat the question as one about effect
      size or scope.
  - q: >-
      A description of a man mentions his interest in birdsong, hedgerows and early mornings.
      Which is more probable, and why?
    options:
      - >-
        That he is a farmer, because the set of farmers includes every farmer who also watches
        birds, so it cannot be less probable than a subset of itself
      - >-
        That he is a birdwatcher who volunteers for a conservation charity, because the
        description matches that combination better than it matches a bare occupation
      - >-
        Neither, because a description of someone's interests carries no information at all
        about how probable any particular occupation is for him
      - >-
        That he is a birdwatcher, because the base rate of birdwatchers in the general
        population is higher than the base rate of working farmers
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      A conjunction can never be more probable than either of its conjuncts, because every case
      of "farmer and birdwatcher" is already a case of "farmer". Representativeness pulls hard
      the other way, which is what Tversky and Kahneman found. So B. Option B is the conjunction
      fallacy stated in full. Option C overcorrects; the description does carry information, it
      just cannot make a conjunction beat its own conjunct. Option D asserts a base rate that
      happens to run the wrong way and is not needed for the answer.
  - q: >-
      A condition affects 1 in 200 people. A screening test flags 90% of those who have it and
      8% of those who do not. Someone tests positive. Counted in natural frequencies, roughly
      what is the chance they have it?
    options:
      - >-
        About 90%, since that is the proportion of people with the condition that the test
        correctly identifies as positive
      - >-
        About 50%, since a positive result roughly balances the chance of having the condition
        against the chance of a false alarm
      - >-
        About 8%, since that is the false positive rate, and that rate fixes how often a
        positive result is wrong
      - >-
        About 5%, because in a group of 10,000 people about 45 true positives sit alongside
        about 796 false positives
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Take 10,000 people. About 50 have the condition and the test flags about 45 of them. Of
      the 9,950 who do not, 8% is about 796 flagged. So about 45 out of 841 positives are real,
      which is close to 5%. That is C. Option A is the base rate neglect the format is designed
      to expose; 90% is the chance of a positive given the condition, not the reverse. Option C
      confuses the false positive rate with the chance the result is wrong. Option B splits the
      difference without doing the count.
  - q: >-
      In court, an expert says the chance of this fibre match arising if the defendant is
      innocent is 1 in 5,000. Counsel concludes there is a 1 in 5,000 chance the defendant is
      innocent. What is wrong?
    options:
      - >-
        Nothing, provided the 1 in 5,000 figure is itself correct, since the two statements are
        just two different ways of putting the very same probability
      - >-
        The expert has given the chance of the evidence given innocence, and counsel has
        restated it as the chance of innocence given the evidence
      - >-
        The figure is too small to be reliable, and a match statistic of that order should never
        be presented to a jury without a margin of error
      - >-
        The expert should have given the chance of a match if the defendant is guilty, which is
        the figure that actually bears on the verdict
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      These are different quantities, and swapping them is the prosecutor's fallacy. How likely
      is the evidence if the defendant is innocent, against how likely is innocence given the
      evidence: the second depends on how many other people could have left that fibre, which
      the statistic alone does not say. So C. Option A asserts the very identity that fails.
      Option C raises a real evidential concern that is not the logical error here. Option D
      asks for a figure that is usually close to 1 and would not repair the reversal.
  - q: >-
      A colleague argues, "We shouldn't move the standup to 9am. Half the team starts at 9.30."
      Which missing premise is the most charitable reconstruction?
    options:
      - >-
        That no meeting should ever be scheduled at a time when any member of the team is unable
        to attend it, whatever the circumstances happen to be
      - >-
        That the standup is worthless unless every single member of the team is present for the
        whole of it, right from the very first minute
      - >-
        That a standup works only if the people it coordinates are present, and a meeting half
        the team misses will not do the job it exists for
      - >-
        That people who start at 9.30 are unwilling to come in earlier, which is the real
        objection the colleague is evidently reluctant to state
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Charity means supplying the premise the arguer would sign, and among those the strongest.
      Option C is defensible and does the work. So D. Options A and B are easy to refute and the
      colleague would reject both, which makes supplying them a straw man rather than a
      reconstruction. Option D replaces the stated reason with a motive, which is not a
      reconstruction of the argument at all.
  - q: >-
      You reconstruct an argument and find it valid. Its second premise is one you judge a
      reasonable member of the audience would want evidence for, and none is offered. Which ARG
      condition does it fail?
    options:
      - >-
        Acceptability, because the premise is not something the audience will grant without
        support, and no support has been given
      - >-
        Relevance, because a premise nobody has reason to believe cannot bear on the conclusion
        in the way the argument requires
      - >-
        Grounds, because the premises taken together stop providing enough support once the
        doubtful one has been set aside
      - >-
        None of them. The argument is valid, and the ARG conditions apply only to arguments
        whose link is inductive rather than deductive
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Acceptability asks whether the audience can reasonably grant the premise. A premise that
      needs evidence and has none fails there, whatever the link is doing. So B. Option B
      misdescribes relevance, which asks whether the premise bears on the conclusion, and this
      one does. Option C describes what happens after you drop the premise, not the condition it
      fails while it stands. Option D is wrong because ARG applies to the premises of any
      argument; validity settles the link and leaves the premises untested.
  - q: >-
      Two arguments appeal to authority. One, a GP cites the national clinical guideline for
      prescribing a statin. Two, a celebrity chef cites their own recovery to recommend a diet
      for diabetes. Which is cogent, and which critical question separates them?
    options:
      - >-
        Both are fallacious, since an appeal to authority substitutes someone's say-so for
        evidence and is never a legitimate move in an argument of any kind
      - >-
        The second, and the question is trustworthiness, since a personal recovery is direct
        first-hand experience while a guideline is merely a document written by a committee
      - >-
        Both are cogent, since each cites a source with real standing in its own domain, and the
        reader is free to weigh the two accounts against each other
      - >-
        The first, and the question is field. A clinical guideline is the consensus of the
        relevant field, while the chef is not qualified in the subject they advise on
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      An appeal to authority is a legitimate argument form with critical questions attached, and
      here the field question does the work: expertise in cooking does not carry across to
      metabolic medicine. So B. Option A convicts by form, which Massey's asymmetry says you
      cannot do. Option B mistakes vividness for reliability; one recovery is a sample of one.
      Option C declines to ask any of the questions.
  - q: >-
      Two slippery slope arguments. One, a safety officer argues that accepting one uninspected
      weld will reset the standard for the next batch, citing two previous cases where it did.
      Two, a columnist argues that allowing a bike lane on one street will end car ownership in
      the city. Which is stronger, and why?
    options:
      - >-
        The second, because it names a larger and more serious outcome, and the size of what is
        at stake is what makes any slope argument worth taking seriously at all
      - >-
        The first, because each link has a mechanism and documented precedent, while the second
        asserts a chain of steps without giving a reason for any of them
      - >-
        Neither, because every slippery slope argument treats a first step as though it forced a
        last one, which is exactly what makes the whole pattern a fallacy
      - >-
        Both equally, since each describes a chain of consequences and neither one can be shown
        false until the events in question have actually happened
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      A slippery slope is an inductive argument about a chain, and its strength depends on
      whether each link has a mechanism and evidence. The safety officer supplies both; the
      columnist supplies neither. So C. Option C convicts by form. Option A confuses the stakes
      with the support. Option D treats unfalsifiability as parity, when the absence of a stated
      mechanism is itself the weakness.
  - q: >-
      A headline reads, "New drug cuts risk of stroke by 40%." The trial reports 5 strokes per
      1,000 on the drug against 8 per 1,000 on placebo. What has the headline done?
    options:
      - >-
        Misreported the result, since a fall from 8 to 5 is a reduction of 37.5% rather than the
        40% the headline claims
      - >-
        Reported the figure correctly and completely, since 40% is the reduction in risk and no
        other number is needed to understand it
      - >-
        Reported the relative reduction and left out the absolute one, which is 3 fewer strokes
        per 1,000 people treated
      - >-
        Confused relative and absolute risk in a way that overstates the benefit by exactly the
        base rate, which here is 8 per 1,000
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Three fewer strokes out of eight is a relative reduction of about 40%, so the headline is
      not arithmetically wrong; it is incomplete. The absolute reduction is 3 per 1,000, which
      is what a reader needs to weigh the drug against its costs and harms. So A. Option A
      disputes a rounding that is fine. Option B treats the relative figure as sufficient, which
      is the habit the lesson exists to break. Option D describes a relationship between the two
      figures that is not what "overstating by the base rate" would mean.

## What this test measures

Twenty items, thirty minutes, and you need 80% to pass. Fourteen of the twenty use cases that appear nowhere in the lessons, because the point is not whether you remember the examples. It is whether you can take an argument you have never seen and say what kind of link it claims, whether the link holds, and whether its premises will bear the weight.

The distractors are the misconceptions the lessons named, one by one. Several of them are the answer a learner gives when they have understood the vocabulary and not the test behind it: naming a fallacy instead of running its critical questions, treating a large sample as a representative one, reading a necessary condition as a sufficient one, or letting a false conclusion decide a validity judgement.

## What a pass means

Passing means you can do the six steps on a stranger's argument without the lesson in front of you. It does not mean you do them on your own arguments, which is harder and is what the project is for. Do the project.

## After the test

Read every explanation, including for the items you got right. Two of the items are matched pairs where the same pattern appears in a cogent argument and a bad one, and the explanations say which critical question separates them. That distinction is the one most worth carrying out of the course, because it is the difference between having a vocabulary of fallacy names and being able to use it.
