In January 2007 the UK's Advertising Standards Authority ruled on a poster you may have seen a version of. It said "More than 80% of dentists recommend Colgate". Two members of the public complained, the ASA opened its own inquiry, and the ruling went against the advertiser. The survey behind the poster had let each dentist recommend several brands at once. The ruling noted that another competitor's brand was recommended by dentists almost as much as Colgate was. Nobody had asked the dentists to pick one.[1]

Notice what the ruling did not say. It did not say that dentists are not experts on toothpaste, or that you shouldn't listen to them. It said that the poster claimed the dentists asserted something they hadn't. The argument was "experts say X, so X", and the trouble was with what the experts actually said.

That is the whole of this lesson in one case. You are about to learn the standard names for bad arguments, and one of those names, appeal to authority, fits the Colgate poster. But "appeal to authority" also fits every time you take a doctor's word about a dose or a mechanic's word about a brake pad, and those arguments are good. The name tells you the shape. It doesn't tell you the verdict. People who study fallacies have been making that distinction since Hamblin's book in 1970, and it is the part that usually falls off in the retelling. By the end you should be able to use the names the way an expert does: as a hypothesis about what might be wrong, to be tested with the six-step check you already own.

## Where the list comes from

Aristotle wrote the first one. In *On Sophistical Refutations* he set out thirteen ways of "producing the false appearance of an argument". Six "depend on language": ambiguity, amphiboly, combination, division of words, accent, form of expression. Seven don't: accident, the use of an expression absolutely rather than with a qualification (what the medieval logicians called *secundum quid*), "ignorance of what refutation is", the consequent, "assuming the original conclusion", "stating as cause what is not the cause", and "making of more than one question into one".[2][3] You'll recognise several. Assuming the original conclusion is begging the question. Stating as cause what is not the cause is false cause. The consequent is affirming the consequent from lesson 3.

Two things about that list matter for how you use its descendants. First, it is nearly twenty-four centuries old, and the lists you meet online are its descendants. Second, it was built for a game, the game of dialectical refutation, in which one speaker tried to force another into a contradiction. A "sophistical refutation" was a move that looked like a winning move in that game and wasn't. The list was never designed to catalogue everything that goes wrong when a newspaper columnist or a planning committee reasons badly, which is part of why it fits real life awkwardly.

The list grew piecemeal, and it is still growing. Locke named three kinds of "ad" argument that have stuck: *ad verecundiam*, now called appeal to authority; *ad ignorantiam*, to ignorance; and *ad hominem*, to the person. Whately and Mill later added their own sortings.[3]

Below I sort twenty-two of the commonest names into five families: relevance, weak induction, presumption, ambiguity, and illicit transference. That sort is a convenience, not a discovery. It is the arrangement most introductory courses use, and its merit is that it groups the patterns by what has gone wrong rather than by their Latin names, which is the right question to be holding. Later in this lesson you will meet two rival sorts, and one of them may be better.

The Stanford Encyclopedia's working definition is the one to hold on to: a fallacy is "an argument that seems to be better than it really is".[3] Keep the word *seems*. A fallacy isn't a bad argument. It's a bad argument with a good disguise, and the disguise is usually that it looks like a good argument you've seen before.

## The five groups, with the schema and the twin

Here is the list, compressed. For each I give the schema, in the two-line style of the appendix to a Lander University logic course,[4] and then the cogent twin: the good argument with the same surface shape. The twin is the important half. If you learn only the names, you'll spend the rest of your life convicting good arguments. The documented cases come later, and not for every name: for six of these I could not find an instance I could present fairly, and I say so where they come up.

**Fallacies of relevance.** The premises are true and beside the point.

- *Appeal to force.* Accept p, or something bad happens to you. Twin: a genuine warning ("if you sign this, you'll be liable"), which is a premise about consequences, not a threat about belief.
- *Appeal to pity.* Someone suffers; therefore p. Twin: an argument in which suffering is the relevant fact, as in a claim for damages.
- *Appeal to the people.* Everyone believes p; therefore p. Twin: a claim about what many independent, informed observers report, which is evidence (a thousand sailors who saw the same rock).
- *Argument against the person* (ad hominem), in three forms: abusive (he's a bad person, so p is false), circumstantial (she'd benefit from p, so p is false), and tu quoque (you do it too, so your argument fails). Twin: a credibility challenge when the claim rests on the person's word. We'll spend a whole section on this one.
- *Accident.* A general rule, applied to a case it was never meant for. Twin: the rule, applied to a case it does cover.
- *Straw man.* Refute a distorted version of p; conclude that p is refuted. Twin: refuting the strongest version. This is lesson 7's charity, failing.
- *Missing the point* and *red herring.* The premises support some conclusion, just not this one. Twin: a genuine change of subject, announced as such.

**Fallacies of weak induction.** The premises are relevant but too weak.

- *Appeal to unqualified authority.* E says p; therefore p, where E isn't in a position to know. Twin: E says p, where E is a qualified, unbiased expert in the field, and the experts agree.
- *Appeal to ignorance.* The Lander appendix gives it as "p is unproved. Therefore not-p is true."[4] Set out in the two lines this lesson will keep asking you for:

```
1. p is unproved.
------------------------------------------------------------------------
C: Not-p.
```

  Twin: p is unproved after a search that would have found it, so probably not-p.
- *Hasty generalisation.* A few cases have property F; therefore all do. Twin: a large, representative sample has F. Lesson 5's rules apply.
- *False cause.* A came before B, or with B; therefore A caused B. Twin: a method-of-difference comparison, or a mechanism plus a dose-response pattern.
- *Slippery slope.* If we take step one, then two, then three, then disaster; therefore not step one. Twin: the same chain with a mechanism and evidence for each link.
- *Weak analogy.* A is like B in some respects; B has F; therefore A has F, where the shared respects aren't the ones that matter. Twin: an analogy on the relevant features.

**Fallacies of presumption.** The premises quietly assume what's at issue.

- *Begging the question.* p, because q, where q is p in other words. Twin: none, and it is the exception that proves the mechanism below, because a circle is bad in every context rather than in the wrong one. A valid argument whose premise and conclusion are both obviously true can look circular without being so.
- *Complex question.* "Have you stopped doing X?" bundles two questions into one so that any answer concedes the first. Twin: a question whose presupposition has already been agreed ("when did you last service it?", asked of someone who has said they service it).
- *False dichotomy.* Either A or B; not A; therefore B, where C was available. Twin: a real either/or, where the options really are exhaustive.
- *Suppressed evidence.* The premises are true, and the fact that would have changed the verdict is left out. Twin: a summary that omits what is genuinely irrelevant, which is what every summary must do; the fault is in which fact went.

**Fallacies of ambiguity.** A word or a sentence changes meaning in transit.

- *Equivocation.* A term used in one sense in a premise and another in the conclusion. Twin: a term used in one sense throughout, which is the rule lesson 10 will hold you to in your own writing.
- *Amphiboly.* A sentence whose grammar allows two readings, with the argument using both. Twin: a sentence with one grammatical reading, which is what asking "which did you mean?" produces.

**Fallacies of illicit transference.** A property moves between part and whole.

- *Composition.* Each part has F; therefore the whole has F. Twin: the same move where F adds up (each brick weighs two kilos, so a hundred bricks weigh two hundred).
- *Division.* The whole has F; therefore each part has F. Twin: the same move where F distributes (the whole shipment is steel, so each crate holds steel).

That's the list, and it is a decent index of the ways arguments go wrong. Now the part that gets less space than the list.

:::checkpoint A friend says "the argument for the new timetable is fallacious: it's an appeal to authority, because it just cites the head of department." Which fact about the head of department would you need before you could agree?
Whether the claim rests on her being in a position to know. If the argument is "the head says the timetable will fit the rooms, and she's the one who allocates rooms", the appeal is to a qualified authority on exactly the question, and it's fine. It becomes the fallacy only if she is being cited on something outside her competence, or against what other qualified people say, or for an opinion she didn't actually express. "It cites an authority" is not a diagnosis; it's the start of one.
:::

## Nearly every fallacy is a good move in the wrong place

The idea that makes the list usable is this. Nearly every pattern on it is also the shape of a legitimate argument. Appealing to experts is how most of what you know arrived; you did not personally verify the boiling point of water or the existence of Australia. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy says it plainly: "Most reasoning of this kind is not fallacious, and much of our knowledge properly comes from listening to authorities."[5] Attacking the person is a standard move in cross-examination, and the US Supreme Court has held that the right to make it is part of a constitutionally protected right, as you'll see. Slippery slope and appeal to ignorance are inductive arguments about consequences and about searches, and lesson 6 gave you the tool that measures them.

So the name can't be the verdict. What decides is whether *this* instance is the good version or the bad one, and that is a question about content and context, which is exactly what the six-step check tests. Walton, whose books set out the method this lesson uses, called each pattern an *argumentation scheme* and attached to each a short list of *critical questions*. Ask the questions; if the argument survives them, it's cogent; if it fails one, you've found the flaw and you can say exactly which it is, which is a far more useful thing to be able to say than a Latin name.[6]

For appeal to expert opinion the scheme is:

```
1. E is an expert in field S.
2. A is a claim in S.
3. E asserts A.
------------------------------------------------------------------------
C: A.
```

And Walton's six critical questions:

1. **Expertise.** How credible is E as an expert?
2. **Field.** Is E an expert in the field that A is in?
3. **Opinion.** What did E actually assert?
4. **Trustworthiness.** Is E personally reliable, or is there a reason to doubt it (a payment, a stake)?
5. **Consistency.** Is A consistent with what other experts say?
6. **Backup evidence.** Is E's assertion based on evidence?[6]

Run the Colgate poster through them. Expertise: dentists, fine. Field: toothpaste, fine. Opinion: here it falls over. The dentists asserted "I'd recommend Colgate, among others"; the poster asserted "dentists recommend Colgate over other brands". Trustworthiness: the survey was commissioned by Colgate, and the ruling criticised the poster for not saying so, which is a reason to look closely rather than a conviction. Consistency: another brand scored almost as high, so the experts were not lined up behind one answer. Backup evidence: not really at issue. Verdict: weak here, and it fails on question 3.[1] Notice how much more you now know than "it's an appeal to authority". You know which question it fails, which means you know what a fixed version of the poster would have to say.

## Worked example 1: the expert who was in the wrong field

Lesson 6 gave you the Sally Clark case for the prosecutor's fallacy. It also contains the cleanest failed appeal to authority I know of, and this time you'll run the questions before I do.

You met the case in lesson 6. Here it is from a different angle, and this time you run the questions before I do. The Royal Statistical Society set out what happened in a statement in October 2001. "A medical expert witness drew on published studies"[7] to get a figure for how often cot death strikes a family like the defendant's, and then, in the Society's words, "went on to square this figure to obtain a value of 1 in 73 million for the frequency of two cases of SIDS in such a family."[7]

The Society's judgement: "This approach is, in general, statistically invalid. It would only be valid if SIDS cases arose independently within families."[7] It does not leave that hanging. It says there are "very strong a priori reasons for supposing that the assumption will be false", which is the whole of the objection: the multiplication needed a fact about families that nobody had established and that the statisticians thought was probably untrue.[7]

The jury heard that figure from an expert. Take the six questions and run them yourself before reading on. Expertise, field, opinion, trustworthiness, consistency, backup evidence. Which one fails?

:::predict Which of Walton's six critical questions does the 1 in 73 million figure fail? Decide before you open this.
Question 2, the field. The witness was a medical expert. The claim was a statistical one about the independence of two events within a family, and on that question he was not an expert at all. The RSS statement ends by making exactly this point: "Although many scientists have some familiarity with statistical methods, statistics remains a specialised area. The Society urges the Courts to ensure that statistical evidence is presented only by appropriately qualified statistical experts, as would be the case for any other form of expert evidence."[7] Questions 1 (a real expert), 3 (he did assert it), and 4 (no reason to doubt his honesty) all pass. Question 6 partly fails too: the independence assumption had no evidence behind it, which is what the Society said in the sentence you have just read.
:::

That last quotation is worth a second look, because it is itself an appeal to authority, and a cogent one. The RSS is arguing: statistical claims should come from statisticians. Run the questions on the RSS. Expertise, yes. Field, yes: it's a statistical society talking about statistics. Opinion: quoted in full. Trustworthiness: a learned society with no stake in the verdict. Consistency: I know of no statistician who disagreed. Backup evidence: the statement gives its reasons. So the same shape, "an authority says so", is fallacious in the witness box and cogent in the press release, and the difference is visible only when you ask the questions.

The law, incidentally, has its own version of the six questions. In *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals* (1993) the US Supreme Court told trial judges that when deciding whether scientific expert testimony is admissible they may consider "whether it can be (and has been) tested", whether it has been through peer review and publication, "the known or potential rate of error", and whether it has "general acceptance" in the relevant community. And it said something a logic teacher would underline: "The focus, of course, must be solely on principles and methodology, not on the conclusions that they generate."[8] Test the link, not the conclusion. That is step 4 of the check, arriving in a Supreme Court judgment by another route.

:::checkpoint An economist with a good record on inflation is quoted on a poster saying a new bridge design is unsafe. Which critical question fails, and which pass?
Question 2, the field, fails: inflation and bridge engineering are different fields, and a record in one earns no credibility in the other. Question 1 passes (a real expert), question 3 passes if the quotation is accurate, and 4, 5 and 6 can't be judged from the poster. The argument is weak on the field question alone, which is enough.
:::

## Worked example 2: attacking the person, twice

Here are two arguments with the same shape: don't accept what this person says, because of something about the person. One of them is a fallacy. Decide which before you read the verdicts.

**Case A.** Richard Green was the witness who identified the defendant, Davis, near a stolen safe in an Alaska burglary trial. At the time, Green was himself on juvenile probation for burglary. The defence wanted the jury to hear that, to argue that a witness on probation for burglary, questioned about a burglary, had reasons of his own for being helpful, and that his identification should be weighed with that in view. The trial judge barred any mention of it.[9]

**Case B.** This one is hypothetical, because I could not find a documented instance I could present fairly, and the outline for this course says not to invent one and pass it off. A plumber tells you your boiler's heat exchanger is cracked and needs replacing. You later learn that she supports a football club you can't stand and that she once got a parking fine. You conclude the boiler is fine.

:::predict Which of these is the fallacy? And what is the difference between them?
Case B is the fallacy, and the difference is what the claim rests on. In case A the claim ("that's the man I saw") rests entirely on Green's word. There is no other route to it. So anything bearing on whether his word is reliable is relevant to the claim. The defence's argument was that a witness in his position might have had a reason to say what the police wanted to hear, true or not, and that the jury should weigh the identification with that in view. Whether he in fact had such a reason was never decided; what the Supreme Court decided was that the jury was entitled to hear the question asked. In case B the claim ("the heat exchanger is cracked") rests on a physical fact you can check, and on her expertise, not her tastes. A football allegiance and a parking fine bear on neither. Attacking the person is relevant exactly when the claim stands on the person, and irrelevant when it doesn't.
:::

The US Supreme Court agreed with case A, over two dissents, in *Davis v. Alaska* (1974). Chief Justice Burger wrote: "We have recognized that the exposure of a witness' motivation in testifying is a proper and important function of the constitutionally protected right of cross-examination." The jurors, he said, "were entitled to have the benefit of the defense theory before them so that they could make an informed judgment". And he drew the line that this lesson turns on. A general attack on a witness's character is one thing; "a more particular attack on the witness' credibility is effected by means of cross-examination directed toward revealing possible biases, prejudices, or ulterior motives".[9] That is the good ad hominem, and a court thought it important enough to overturn a conviction for.

Walton's thesis, after a book-length study, is that no ad hominem argument is inherently fallacious.[10] The IEP gives the everyday test in a sentence: "attacks on a person for their immoral sexual conduct are irrelevant to the quality of the person's reasoning about Kepler's astronomy, but they are relevant to arguments promoting the person for a leadership position in a church or mosque or city council."[5] Ask what the claim rests on. If it rests on the person, the person is fair game.

### The harder version: a conflict of interest

Now the harder version, the *circumstantial* form: don't trust this because of who paid for it. In 2016 three researchers published an analysis of internal documents of the Sugar Research Foundation, the sugar industry's research organisation. They found that in 1965 the Foundation had commissioned a review of the dietary causes of heart disease from a group of academic researchers, paying them $6,500 (about $48,900 in 2016 dollars). The review, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 1967, "singled out fat and cholesterol as the dietary causes of CHD and downplayed evidence that sucrose consumption was also a risk factor." The Foundation "set the review's objective, contributed articles for inclusion, and received drafts. The SRF's funding and role was not disclosed."[11]

:::predict Does the payment show that the 1967 review's conclusions were false? Decide, and write your reason, before you open this.
No, and the reason is the whole of this section. A funded review can be right, and this one's conclusions have to be judged against the evidence like anyone else's. What the payment does is fail question 4, trustworthiness, and a failed question is an instruction rather than a verdict: it tells you to reopen the review and read it. That is what the 2016 researchers did, and what they set out from the documents, that the funder set the objective, contributed articles for inclusion and received drafts, is what carries the verdict. Not the cheque. Then look at how they phrased their own recommendation: policy committees "should consider giving less weight to food industry-funded studies".[11] Less weight. Not zero.
:::

That is the calibrated version of the circumstantial ad hominem, and it is a good argument. The fallacious version would be "it was industry-funded, therefore false", and the researchers didn't make it.

:::checkpoint A friend dismisses a study because its lead author has a patent on the treatment it tested. Is that relevant, and is it decisive?
Relevant: yes, to question 4. A financial stake is a reason to doubt personal reliability and to check the study's methods with extra care. Decisive: no. The study's premises are its data and methods, and they are what settle whether its conclusion holds. Give it less weight, look for independent replication, and read the methods. "Patent, therefore false" is the fallacy; "patent, therefore check" is the check.
:::

## The same words, weak and then strong

Lesson 6 gave you "there is no evidence the drug is harmful". Here it gets its name, appeal to ignorance, and here is why the name settles nothing.

:::predict Two cases, the same eight words. Case one: the drug was licensed last month and nobody has run a safety study. Case two: the same sentence after fifteen years and several large studies. Which is the stronger argument, and what exactly is the difference? Write it before you open this.
Case two, and the difference is entirely in what the search could have found. Ask lesson 6's question of each: how much more likely is "no evidence of harm" if the drug is safe than if it is harmful? When nobody has looked, about equally likely either way, so the likelihood ratio sits near 1 and the evidence barely moves you; the argument is weak. After studies large enough to have found harm if it were there, "nothing found" is much more likely under safety than under harm, the ratio is well above 1, and the same words carry a strong argument. The schema "p is unproved, therefore not-p" fits both cases exactly. It convicts neither.
:::

The documented case of the weak version is five pages that repay reading in full. After the loss of the space shuttle *Challenger* in 1986, Feynman sat on the presidential commission and wrote his own appendix to its report. On the solid rocket booster seals, which had shown erosion on earlier flights:

> "The phenomenon of accepting for flight, seals that had shown erosion and blow-by in previous flights, is very clear. The Challenger flight is an excellent example. There are several references to flights that had gone before. The acceptance and success of these flights is taken as evidence of safety. But erosion and blow-by are not what the design expected. They are warnings that something is wrong ... The fact that this danger did not lead to a catastrophe before is no guarantee that it will not the next time, unless it is completely understood. When playing Russian roulette the fact that the first shot got off safely is little comfort for the next."[12]

Look at what makes the argument from past flights weak, in lesson 6's terms. Take a run of twenty flights, which is a round number of mine rather than a count from the appendix. If the joint fails, say, one flight in a hundred, then twenty successes in a row have probability about 0.82; if it is as safe as management believed, the probability is about 1. The ratio is about 1.2. Twenty safe flights should barely have moved anyone, and Feynman's point is sharper still: the flights were not even a test of the thing that worried the engineers, because the erosion had already shown the joint was operating outside its design. Feynman recorded estimates of the risk ranging from roughly 1 in 100 to 1 in 100,000, with the higher figures coming from the working engineers and the very low ones from management, and he noted that the former "would imply that one could put a Shuttle up each day for 300 years expecting to lose only one".[12]

And the strong version, documented. In 2019 a Danish team followed 657,461 children born between 1999 and 2010, comparing those who had received the MMR vaccine with those who hadn't, and following them to August 2013. Over five million person-years, 6,517 autism diagnoses were made. The adjusted hazard ratio for autism was 0.93, with a 95% confidence interval running from 0.85 to 1.02, which means the data are consistent with a small reduction in risk, no change, or a very small increase, and rule out anything larger. No increased risk appeared in any subgroup or any period after vaccination. The authors' conclusion: the study "strongly supports that MMR vaccination does not increase the risk for autism".[13] That is "no evidence of harm" after a search that would have found it. Same words as the drug licensed last month; a strong argument this time, and the difference is entirely in what the search could have found.

### Slippery slope, with and without a mechanism

The same Feynman appendix contains a slippery slope that was strong, which is the half of the pattern nobody shows you. He wrote that "certification criteria used in Flight Readiness Reviews often develop a gradually decreasing strictness. The argument that the same risk was flown before without failure is often accepted as an argument for the safety of accepting it again."[12] That is a chain: accept a little erosion, and the accepted erosion becomes the new baseline, and the next review measures against the baseline rather than the design. Each link has a mechanism (the comparison point moves) and evidence (the review records). The slope is real. Feynman's own example: when deciding whether flight 51-L was safe in the face of ring erosion on flight 51-C, "the erosion depth was only one-third of the radius", and from that "it was asserted, there was 'a safety factor of three.'"[12] Hold on to this passage. It comes back in the practice wearing the other hat.

Now a hypothetical slope with no mechanism at all: "if we let one student hand in an essay late, soon nobody in the school will meet a deadline." Nothing is offered that would move the school from one extension to universal lateness. Ask for the mechanism, and if none comes, the argument is weak. That question, not the name, is the test.

## The straw man, in a courtroom

Lesson 7 taught you charity: reconstruct the version the other person would sign. The straw man is what happens when you don't, and judges have a phrase for it.

In 2015 the Wisconsin Court of Appeals decided *Wolf v. American Family Mutual Insurance*, a dispute about whether a change in state law reached into a policy written before the change took effect. The policy contained what the court calls an "elasticity clause". The brief filed for Wolf cited an earlier case to establish that elasticity clauses are enforceable. The court's reply:

> "Wolf ostensibly cites *Roehl* for the proposition that elasticity clauses are enforceable. Of course they are. However, her contention attacks a straw man. American Family does not contend the elasticity clause is unenforceable; rather, it argues that the elasticity clause does not implement changes which are not 'in conflict with' the terms of the policy because these legislative changes have not become effective until a policy is renewed or issued."[14]

See the move. The insurer's actual argument was narrow: this clause doesn't apply to this change, for this reason. The reply answered a broad claim the insurer never made, that such clauses never apply. The answer was correct and irrelevant. Rapoport's first rule from lesson 7, restate the opponent's position so well they'd thank you, would have caught it in a sentence: "American Family says the clause is enforceable but doesn't reach this change." Once you've written that down, you can't argue against something else without noticing.

:::checkpoint You're about to reply to a colleague's proposal. What one thing do you write before your reply, and what fallacy does it prevent?
Their position, in their terms, in a form they would sign. Then you argue with that. It prevents the straw man, and it also does something the name doesn't: it often shows you that the position is stronger, or narrower, than you'd assumed, so your reply is aimed at the right target.
:::

## The rest of the list, in documented cases

**Hasty generalisation and false cause.** In 1998 the *Lancet* published a paper by Wakefield and colleagues describing "12 children (mean age 6 years [range 3-10], 11 boys)" referred to a gastroenterology unit. Its findings section reported: "Onset of behavioural symptoms was associated, by the parents, with measles, mumps, and rubella vaccination in eight of the 12 children." Its interpretation was that the condition "was generally associated in time with possible environmental triggers".[15] Twelve children, selected because they had already been referred with these problems, is the schema of hasty generalisation. And "associated, by the parents", in time, is the schema of post hoc: A came before B, therefore A caused B. The paper itself was cautious in its wording; the public argument built on it was not.

In February 2010 the *Lancet* retracted the paper. The retraction says that, following the UK General Medical Council's findings, "it has become clear that several elements of the 1998 paper by Wakefield et al are incorrect ... In particular, the claims in the original paper that children were 'consecutively referred' and that investigations were 'approved' by the local ethics committee have been proven to be false. Therefore we fully retract this paper from the published record."[16] Hold that thought for the fallacy fallacy below.

**Division.** In 1950 W. S. Robinson took the 1930 US census and computed, for the nine census divisions, the correlation between the percentage of residents who were foreign-born and the percentage who were illiterate. It was 0.619. Then he computed the same correlation across individuals: is a foreign-born person more likely to be illiterate? That was 0.118. On a state-by-state basis the group figure was 0.526; the individual figure hadn't changed.[17]

:::predict Robinson ran the same pair of questions on a second variable in the same census: colour and illiteracy. Across the nine divisions that correlation was 0.946, which is nearly perfect. Before you read on, guess the individual figure.
0.203.[17] So the gap is wider on the second pair than on the first, and the size of the group figure gave no warning of it: a divisional correlation of 0.946 sat on top of an individual one of 0.203. That is the finding, and it is worth being careful about what it is. The paper does not tell you why the two numbers differ, and I am not going to invent a story. The point is the gap itself. A figure computed across regions is a fact about regions; a figure computed across people is a fact about people; they are different numbers about different things, and the first will not tell you the second, in either direction or at any size.
:::

Concluding from "regions with F have G" to "people with F have G" is division, and epidemiologists call it the ecological fallacy.

**Composition** is the same error the other way, and I'll give it hypothetically: every player on the team is the best in her position, so the team is the best. Whether that follows depends on whether the property adds up, and "best" often doesn't.

**Equivocation.** Two documented cases. In March 2016 the ASA ruled against a broadband provider's advertisement for a "TOTALLY UNLIMITED" service. The provider ran traffic prioritisation that, when a line was busy, could hold peer-to-peer traffic down to 5% of the line's capacity. The ASA's reasoning, as reported: a "'totally unlimited' claim was stronger, and ... consumers would understand it to mean that the service was free from any provider-imposed limitations."[18] Two senses of one word were doing business with each other. In the trade, "unlimited" means no data cap, and that was true. To a reader, "totally unlimited" means no limits at all, which is what the ASA held consumers would take it to say, and the traffic policy was a limit. The argument "it's unlimited, so buy it" ran across the gap between the two. The textbook form has the shift inside one argument; the advertising form has it between writer and reader; the ASA's job is to police that gap, and the ruling names the two senses.

The second is Feynman's again. That "safety factor of three" claim: in engineering, a safety factor is the designed margin between what a structure can take and the load it is meant to carry, so that an unexpected load or a flaw in the material still leaves the thing standing. Erosion of a seal that was designed to have no erosion is not a margin of that kind; it's a failure that hasn't finished. Feynman: "This is a strange use of the engineer's term, 'safety factor.'"[12] One phrase, two meanings, and the argument for flying rode across from one to the other.

**Begging the question.** Circular reasoning: the conclusion is smuggled into a premise. A hypothetical: "this policy is the fairest option, because no other option treats people as fairly." The second half is the first half rearranged. Two notes. In ordinary English "that begs the question" now usually means "that raises the question", and you should know both usages and keep them apart; in logic the phrase means the circle. And a circle can be hard to see when the premise is a long way from the conclusion in the text; lesson 7's reconstruction, which puts the premises next to the conclusion, is what exposes it.

**False dichotomy and complex question** I'll leave hypothetical and short. "Either we cancel the away fixtures or the club folds" is a false dichotomy if there's a third option (a subscription rise, a cheaper coach), and a true one if there isn't; you check by looking for the third option, not by spotting the "either". "Have you stopped skipping the safety checks?" is a complex question; the answer is to separate the two questions and answer each.

That's the list, and it's what most people mean by "learning the fallacies". If you're reading in one sitting and it has been an hour, stop here and come back for the rest. What follows is the half that decides whether the list is any use, and it deserves a fresh head. Before you start again, see how many of the five families you can name without looking.

## Now the honest part: why the experts warn you about the names

You have the list. Before I tell you what the people who study fallacies think of it, make a guess.

:::predict Think of the last few real arguments you read or heard: a news column, a work email, a debate. How often do you think the patterns above appeared in their fallacious form? Once a paragraph? Once a week? Almost never? The people who study this disagree with each other.
Here is the range. Charles Hamblin, who started modern fallacy theory in 1970, thought the textbook treatment was so muddled it couldn't answer the question. Maurice Finocchiaro, in 1981, argued that textbook fallacies are rare in real discourse and that we should talk about "fallacious arguments" case by case instead. Boudry and colleagues, in 2015, argued that whichever answer you give, you're in trouble: define the patterns strictly and they almost never occur; define them loosely enough to occur and they're often cogent. The defenders reply that the names index real patterns and that with the right critical questions attached they work. That disagreement is the rest of this section.
:::

In 1970 Hamblin published *Fallacies*, and it is the reason a lesson like this one exists. He looked at what he called the standard treatment, "The typical or average account as it appears in the typical short chapter or appendix of the average modern textbook", and wrote: "And what we find in most cases, I think it should be admitted, is as debased, worn-out and dogmatic a treatment as could be imagined: incredibly tradition bound, yet lacking in logic and in historical sense alike, and almost without connection to anything else in modern Logic at all."[3][22] His complaint was that textbooks defined a fallacy as "an argument that seems valid but is not", and then listed items that aren't invalid at all; appeal to pity and begging the question are two of them. The definition and the list had been sitting side by side without being reconciled, and Hamblin's book is where somebody finally said so at length.

That is why the definition at the top of this lesson is the one it is. "An argument that seems to be *better* than it really is" is the repair. It keeps appeal to pity and begging the question on the list, because both can be perfectly valid and still no good, and it moves the fault from the form of the argument to the gap between how good the argument looks and how good it is.

:::predict Begging the question is on every fallacy list. Is it invalid?
No. "p, therefore p" is as valid as an argument gets: there is no possible case in which the premise is true and the conclusion false, which is lesson 2's definition of validity. That is Hamblin's point in one line. The textbooks said a fallacy was an argument that seems valid but isn't, and then put on the list an argument that is valid and is still no good. Notice what follows for you rather than for the textbooks: if a pattern can be valid and bad at the same time, then testing an argument's form was never going to be enough, and everything this lesson does with critical questions is what you do instead.
:::

Three objections followed that you should be able to state.

**Finocchiaro (1981):** textbook fallacies are rare in real discourse. Real arguments go wrong in ways that don't fit the categories, and the categories, when you look for instances, are hard to find. He preferred to talk of "fallacious arguments" and to analyse each on its own terms.[3][21]

**Massey (1981):** there is an asymmetry between proving validity and proving invalidity. To show an argument valid, you show it has a valid form. But showing it has an invalid form proves nothing, because every argument has many forms and one of the others might be valid. Massey's conclusion: "no argument can be convicted of being a fallacy on logical grounds."[3][21] That sentence is the reason this lesson is built the way it is. You cannot convict an argument by matching it to a pattern; you have to go into the content, which is what the critical questions make you do.

:::checkpoint Someone shows you an argument, points at its shape, and says "that's affirming the consequent, so it fails". On Massey's account, what is the one thing you can ask them?
Which form are you holding it to, and why that one? Every argument instantiates more than one form, and showing that it fits an invalid shape does not show that there is no valid shape it also fits. One valid form settles validity for good; a match to an invalid form settles nothing. That is the asymmetry. It is lesson 2's counterexample method seen from the other side: to show an argument invalid you have to produce the case, in its content, where the premises hold and the conclusion fails, and nothing about the argument's silhouette does that for you.
:::

Put Massey together with the fallacy fallacy, which is coming, and you have this lesson's rule. Massey says the shape cannot convict: whatever pattern you have matched, the argument in front of you may have another shape that is fine, so a name gets you as far as "worth a look" and no further. The fallacy fallacy says that even a successful conviction does not reach the conclusion: you have shown that this argument does not establish p, and p is exactly where it was. So a name can neither settle the argument nor settle the claim. What it can do is tell you which questions to ask, and that is all a hypothesis ever does.

**Boudry, Paglieri and Pigliucci (2015):** the Fallacy Fork. In their words: "if fallacies are construed as demonstrably invalid form of reasoning, then they have very limited applicability in real life (few actual instances). On the other hand, if our definitions of fallacies are sophisticated enough to capture real-life complexities, they can no longer be held up as an effective tool for discriminating good and bad forms of reasoning." And: "almost every known type of fallacy is a close neighbor to sound inferences or acceptable moves in a debate."[19] Every twin in the list above is an instance of that sentence.

Read the two middle boxes as a pair: whichever definition of a fallacy you choose, one of them is what you get. Then ask which of the two your own use of the names has been sitting on. Boudry and colleagues conclude that fallacy theory should give way to case-by-case evaluation, and that is one live position in the field.

**The defenders and reformers** don't dispute the fork so much as build around it. Walton keeps the names and redefines a fallacy as a misuse of an argumentation scheme in a particular kind of dialogue, judged by the critical questions; the same move can be legitimate in one kind of exchange and a fallacy in another, so which kind of exchange you are in is part of the evaluation.[6][3]

The pragma-dialectical school in Amsterdam, van Eemeren and Grootendorst, rebuilt the subject around what an argument is *for*. On their account, arguing is a procedure two people run to resolve a difference of opinion, and it works only if both keep to certain rules, among them a freedom rule and a burden-of-proof rule. A fallacy is then any move that blocks the resolution. That covers the traditional list, explains why its items are faults rather than merely listing them, and catches moves the list has no name for, such as refusing to let an opponent put their case at all.[3][23]

Johnson and Blair, at Windsor in Ontario, where the informal-logic movement took shape, sort fallacies as failures of one of three conditions on a cogent argument: acceptability, relevance, sufficiency.[3][23] That is the sort you met in lesson 7, where Govier's third letter is G for grounds and asks the same question. Their claim is that you never needed the list: three questions are quicker to run than twenty-two names, and every name is a familiar way of failing one of the three. On that view the list is a set of common ways to fail one of three tests, and the tests are what you actually run.

**What would settle it.** Corpus studies: take a large body of real argument, count how often each pattern occurs, and count how often the instances are actually defective. Instruction studies: teach one group the names, one the critical questions, one the rules for a critical discussion, and one nothing but practice at evaluating cases, and see who evaluates real arguments better at a delay. We have the second kind only in outline. A 2015 meta-analysis of 341 effect sizes found that critical-thinking instruction works overall (a weighted mean effect of g+ = 0.30), with dialogue, authentic problems and mentoring associated with the larger effects. Those are associations across a body of studies rather than a horse race the reviewers ran, and the review did not isolate fallacy labels as an ingredient at all.[20] So the honest position is that the disagreement is open.

**This course's line, said plainly.** Teach the names, because you will meet them and because they are a good index of the ways arguments fail. Teach every name with its critical questions and its cogent twin, never alone. And treat a name as a hypothesis: "this might be an appeal to ignorance" means "go and check whether anyone looked", not "case closed". Walton would sign that. Boudry would not, and it is worth saying so rather than pretending the disagreement away: his conclusion is that the names should go, and be replaced by evaluation of the argument in front of you. My reason for keeping them is not that he is wrong about how much they can do. It is that you will meet these names whether or not they are any good. They arrive in comment threads and in meeting rooms, aimed at you, as though they settled things, and you need to be able to say what the name would have to show and whether it shows it. That requires knowing the name. If Boudry is right, this is a lesson in disarming a bad tool rather than in using a good one, and it is still worth the hour. The IEP, which lists 231 fallacy names, puts the warning on the tin: "Simply applying the fallacy name to a passage cannot substitute for a detailed examination of the passage and its context or circumstances."[5]

## The fallacy fallacy

One more pattern, and it's the one that the names create. Suppose you show that someone's argument for p is fallacious. What do you now know about p?

:::checkpoint You've shown that a colleague's argument for the new rota is circular: her premise was the conclusion in other words. What do you now know about whether the new rota is a good idea?
Nothing you didn't know before. A bad argument for p tells you that this argument doesn't establish p. It leaves p exactly where it was, and there may be a good argument for it that nobody has offered yet. To learn anything about the rota you would need a different argument: what happened on teams that ran it, what the people on the shifts say, what it costs. Concluding "the argument is fallacious, so p is false" is itself a fallacy, usually called the fallacy fallacy, and it is the mistake the names make easy.
:::

The *Lancet* retraction is the clean case. The retraction established that the 1998 paper's argument stood on false premises: the children were not "consecutively referred" and the investigations were not "approved" by an ethics committee.[16] That destroys the argument. It says nothing, by itself, about whether MMR causes autism; a paper with honest premises could in principle have found the same thing. What settles the claim is the evidence gathered when people looked properly, which is where the Danish cohort of 657,461 children comes in, with its hazard ratio of 0.93.[13] The claim is false because the search that would have found it found nothing, not because one argument for it collapsed. Keep those two verdicts apart and the fallacy fallacy has nowhere to get in. It's the same discipline as lesson 2's: test the link, then test the premises, and only then say something about the conclusion.

## What people get wrong

:::checkpoint Before you read the list, close the page and write out, from memory, the six critical questions for an appeal to expert opinion, in any order. Beside each one, write a case from this lesson that failed it.
Expertise, field, opinion, trustworthiness, consistency, backup evidence. Field is the one the Sally Clark evidence failed: a medical expert on a question about statistical independence. Opinion is the one the Colgate poster failed, since the dentists did not assert what the poster said they had. Trustworthiness is the one the 1967 sugar review failed, and it is the one to handle most carefully, because failing it is an instruction to read the methods rather than a licence to reject the conclusion. Backup evidence half-failed in the Clark case too. If you could name fewer than four of the six, reread the Colgate run-through before you start the practice: the practice is twelve cases and these are what you run on them.
:::

**"Any appeal to authority is a fallacy."** Most of what you know came from authorities and you were right to accept it. The fallacy is the appeal to an authority who is out of field, or against the consensus, or misquoted, or compromised. Run the six questions.[5][6]

**"Ad hominem is always a fallacy."** *Davis v. Alaska* is the answer. When a claim rests on someone's word, their motives are the evidence.[9][10]

**"Slippery slope and appeal to ignorance are always fallacies."** They are inductive arguments. Feynman's Flight Readiness Reviews were a real slope with a mechanism; the Danish cohort is a real absence of evidence that is strong evidence of absence. So what decides is the same thing that decided in lesson 6: how much more likely the evidence is under one story than under the other.[12][13]

**"Begs the question means raises the question."** In everyday English it now does. In logic it means the circle. Know which one you're using.

**"Spotting the name settles it."** Three people, from three directions, say no. Massey: an argument has more than one form, so matching it to an invalid one convicts nothing. Boudry: nearly every name has a cogent twin, so the name does not sort the good from the bad. Dowden, who maintains the longest list of them, puts the warning on his own tin: the name "cannot substitute for a detailed examination".[3][19][5]

**"A fallacious argument has a false conclusion."** The fallacy fallacy. The retraction destroyed an argument; the cohort study settled the claim.

**"The list is complete, coherent, and ancient in its current form."** It began as thirteen items for a debating game, grew by accretion, and the definition textbooks give it still does not fit half the items on it, and the field disagrees about whether to keep it at all.[2][3]

**"I spot fallacies quickly, so I'm good at this."** You spot them quickly on the other side. Lesson 2's belief bias predicts that you'll check the link on arguments whose conclusions you dislike and check the conclusion on arguments you like. If a fallacy diagnosis arrives before you have finished reading the argument, that speed is information about you, and the right response is to run the check on your own side's argument too.

:::checkpoint Answers to the twelve. Open this only when all twelve are written down.
Each answer names the question that decided it. If yours names only a pattern and a verdict, you matched rather than tested; go back and write the question.

1. Appeal to expert opinion, run through all six. Expertise: yes. Field: yes, a statistical society on statistics. Opinion: quoted in full. Trustworthiness: no stake in the verdict. Consistency: no statistician dissented. Backup evidence: the statement gives its reasons. Cogent here, on all six. It would flip if the Society were speaking about, say, the medical evidence in the same case.

2. Circumstantial ad hominem. The test: what is the conclusion about, the study or its weight? It is about weight, and the documents supply the reason. Cogent here. "Less weight" is a claim the evidence supports; "therefore false" would not have been, and that is the change that would flip it.

3. Division. The test: does the unit in the premise match the unit in the conclusion? Premise is census divisions, conclusion is individuals. The individual correlation in the same data was 0.203. Fallacious here, and note how little warning the group figure gave: 0.946 is nearly perfect and told you almost nothing about people. It would flip only if the conclusion were about divisions.

4. Appeal to ignorance, strong form. The test: would the search have found the harm if it were there? 657,461 children and a hazard ratio of 0.93 say yes. Cogent here. It would flip if the cohort had been a few hundred children, or if nobody had looked for autism specifically.

5. Equivocation. The test: does the term keep one sense from premise to conclusion? "Safety factor" means a designed margin in engineering and is used here of unplanned damage. Fallacious here. It would flip if the seals had been designed to erode by up to a third and had eroded by a ninth.

6. Circumstantial ad hominem. The test: what does the claim rest on? The identification rests entirely on the witness's word, so a reason to shade it is evidence about the claim. Cogent here, and the Supreme Court agreed. It would flip if there were CCTV of the same identification, because then the claim would no longer rest on him.

7. Appeal to ignorance, weak form, and fallacious: the earlier flights were not a test that could have revealed the danger, because the erosion had already shown the joint working outside its design, so the likelihood ratio sits near 1. Feynman's own argument, that each acceptance resets the baseline for the next review, is the strong slippery slope from the body. Two patterns in one sentence, opposite verdicts, and the lesson gave them different verdicts on purpose. If you named only one, go back and ask which question you were answering. That is Massey's asymmetry in miniature: an argument has more than one form, and which form you hold it to is a choice you are making.

8. Hasty generalisation and post hoc. Two tests. Is the sample large and representative? Twelve children already referred with these problems, so no. Is there anything beyond temporal order? Parental recall of timing, and no comparison group, so no. Fallacious here on both. Item 4 is what the non-fallacious version of this question looks like.

9. Satisfies general acceptance; fails testability, peer review and publication, and known error rate. Three of four against it, and the Court's own answer to evidence that is shaky but admissible is "vigorous cross examination, presentation of contrary evidence, and careful instruction on the burden of proof". Note what the Court said to focus on: principles and methodology, not the conclusions they generate. That is step 4 of the check.

10. Equivocation. The test: two senses of one word carrying the argument. "Unlimited" as no data cap, against "unlimited" as no provider-imposed limitation. Fallacious here. It would flip if the advertisement had said "unlimited data" and the traffic policy had touched only speed at peak times, with that said plainly.

11. Straw man, and it is yours. The test: is the position you refuted the one your colleague holds? No; the objection was about this estate and the bus route, and you answered a claim about moving at all. Fallacious here, and the fix is lesson 7's rule 1: write their position in a form they would sign before you reply to it.

12. Two arguments, two verdicts. The retraction is not a fallacy: it establishes that two premises were false, which is a sound reason to withdraw a paper. The further step, "therefore the hypothesis is false", is the fallacy fallacy. The test: does the argument's collapse bear on the claim? No. What settles the claim is item 4.

## Connections

The names are shortcuts for the check you already own. Every critical question in this lesson is one of lesson 7's ARG conditions in a specific dress: the six expert questions test acceptability, which is Govier's A; the ad hominem test is a relevance test, her R; the likelihood ratio from lesson 6 is how you measure grounds for the inductive patterns, and it's what decides slope and ignorance. Massey's asymmetry is lesson 2's lesson again: you show an argument invalid by finding a counterexample in its content, not by matching its shape. And belief bias, from lesson 2, is why you'll find fallacies faster on the other side; the discipline is to run the questions symmetrically.

Lesson 9 takes the names and the six questions to the news, where the arguments come compressed and the source does the work of the warrant. Lesson 10 turns the check around on your own writing.
