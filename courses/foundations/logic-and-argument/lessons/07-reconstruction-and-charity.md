        On the conclusion, because the old bins may have been withdrawn for reasons the neighbour hasn't mentioned
      - >-
        On the second premise, because "I don't think" is a guarding term and guarded claims are where writers hide their doubts
      - >-

"I don't think" tells you how far the writer will go, which is information you can hold them to, and attacking the guarded claim means attacking the part they were careful about.       - >-

      - >-

    explain: >-

In the second, a closing time and a finishing time give you nothing apart; together they give you an answer. That is linked support, and losing either one leaves nothing behind. Option C has the word "linked" backwards: linked premises do not share the load, they carry it jointly, so losing one is fatal rather than survivable. Option B applies the acceptability condition to the argument as a whole and treats a false premise as the argument's verdict; acceptability is applied premise by premise, and then you read the damage off the diagram. Someone you know posts an argument you think is wrong. You read it once, fast, and start typing. Twenty minutes later they reply that you've answered something they never said, and they're right. You had a good objection to a bad argument. It wasn't their argument.

Here is the sort of thing that does it, from a neighbourhood noticeboard:

> Obviously we should keep the gate locked at night. Half the flats have had something taken from the bike store this year, and although the lock is a nuisance for late shifts, that's a small price.

Read it twice. Before you can answer it you have to settle what it actually says, which of its sentences is doing the work, and what it needs to be true that it hasn't said. Most replies to a passage like that get all three wrong and then argue for twenty minutes with something nobody wrote.

This happens to careful people, and it happens for a reason. An argument in the wild is a compressed thing. The premises are half-stated, the general rule that carries the step is left out, and the words around the reasons ("obviously", "probably", "although") are doing work that is easy to miss. Before you can test an argument you have to rebuild it, and the rebuilding is where an evaluation can go wrong before it has properly started.

This lesson is about rebuilding well. It rewrites step 3 of the six-step check you've been carrying since lesson 1, and it fills in step 5, which is the last one missing: find the conclusion, find the premises, supply what's missing, test the link, test the premises, and only then consult your opinion of the conclusion. Everything here serves one rule: evaluate the strongest version of what the other person actually holds, because that's the only version worth refuting.

## Read the small words first

Before you rewrite an argument, read it once for the words around the reasons. The terms come from *Understanding Arguments*, where "close analysis" of those words is the pass that comes before the "deep analysis" of putting an argument in standard form. This course meets them through [Van Cleave's open textbook](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Philosophy/Introduction_to_Logic_and_Critical_Thinking_2e_%28van_Cleave%29/01%3A_Reconstructing_and_Analyzing_Arguments), which follows the same method, rather than through Sinnott-Armstrong and Fogelin's book, which we haven't read.[4][5] Van Cleave sorts the words into three kinds.[4]

Assuring terms tell you that reasons exist without giving them. "Clearly", "obviously", "everyone agrees", "studies show", "I can assure you". Van Cleave's definition: assuring is "informing someone that there are further reasons although one is not giving them now."[4] One of his exercise sentences shows the shape: "Most pediatricians agree that the single most important factor in childhood obesity is eating sugary, processed foods, which have become all too common in our day and age."[4] Notice there are two assurances in one sentence, not one, and neither is a study. Van Cleave's advice is that assurances aren't necessarily illegitimate, "but a critical thinker will always regard them as somewhat suspect."[4] His reason is that an assurance goes where a writer expects resistance. That makes it useful to you whichever way it cuts: the assured claim is the one the writer thinks needs help, so it's the one worth an extra minute, whether the help that's missing is evidence or a reader who won't like the answer.

:::predict Two sentences from two different writers. (a) "Obviously the road is more dangerous since they widened it." (b) "The road is probably a bit more dangerous since they widened it." Which writer has told you more, and which claim would you check first?
(b) has told you more. "Probably" and "a bit" are measurements: the writer has decided how far they'll go and said so, and you can hold them to exactly that and no further. (a) has told you nothing except that the writer expects no argument. "Obviously" isn't evidence; it's a request to skip the evidence, and the request is worth refusing. So check (a) first. That's the reverse of the obvious move, which is to go after the bolder claim because it looks like the bigger target. The bolder claim is the one that's been measured. The confident one is the one that hasn't.
:::

Guarding terms weaken a claim so it's harder to refute. "Probably", "in most cases", "some", "it may be that". Van Cleave: guarding is "weakening one's claims so that it is harder to show that the claims are false."[4] He gives a ladder, from "All U.S. Presidents were monogamous" down to "Some U.S. Presidents were monogamous".[4] The first is easy to test and the second is nearly impossible to deny.

A guard cuts both ways, and the sentence won't tell you which. "Probably" can be a measurement, in which case hold the writer to it and don't let them cash it later as the bold claim. It can also be a retreat to a claim so weak that nobody could deny it, which is what the bottom of Van Cleave's ladder is showing you. The test is whether the guarded claim still supports the conclusion. If it does, the writer measured. If the conclusion needs the bold version, the guard is cover. Either way, "probably stale" does not license "throw it away without looking."

Discounting terms anticipate an objection and wave it through. "Although", "even though", "but", "nevertheless", "still". Van Cleave: discounting is "anticipating objections that might be raised to one's claim or argument as a way of dismissing those objections."[4] His example is "The worker was inefficient, but honest."[4] The point after "but" is the one the writer wants you to keep; the point before it is the one they've decided you'll set aside. Sometimes that's fair. Sometimes the objection they've discounted is the strongest thing in the passage, and the "although" is the only sign it was ever there.

None of this is evaluation yet. It's a map of where the writer feels strong and where they feel weak, drawn by the writer.

:::checkpoint "Everyone knows the trains here are unreliable, and although the new train company has promised improvements, they'll probably be late again on Monday." Sort the three marked terms, and say which claim you'd check first.
"Everyone knows" is assuring; it sits on the claim that the trains are unreliable, which is offered with no figures. "Although the new train company has promised improvements" is discounting; the promise is mentioned so it can be set aside. "Probably" is guarding; the writer is only committing to a likelihood. Check the assured claim first: what is the punctuality figure, and is it actually bad? The guarded conclusion is the part the writer has measured, and the discounted promise is the objection they'd rather you didn't weigh.
:::

## Linked or convergent: does the argument survive a lost premise?

Take two everyday arguments, both plainly made up.

"The flat is ten minutes from her office, it's under budget, and it has a garden. She should take it."

"The last train leaves at 22:10 and the trip takes fifty minutes. She'll be home by 23:00."

Now suppose in each case that the second premise is false. The flat is over budget. The trip takes ninety minutes, not fifty.

:::predict Which of the two arguments is still standing after its second premise falls?
The flat argument survives, weaker. Two reasons remain: it's near work and it has a garden. The train argument is gone. A departure time on its own tells you nothing about an arrival time; it needed the trip time to say anything at all. The difference isn't in the number of premises. It's in how they support the conclusion.
:::

The flat argument has convergent premises: each one is a separate reason for the conclusion, and each would count for something on its own. The train argument has linked premises: they support the conclusion only together, and neither does anything alone.

The distinction is Govier's chapter 2, "Pinning Down Argument Structure", and the Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on informal logic uses it as the basic move in drawing an argument out.[2][3] Convergent premises are separate reasons, each with its own line to the conclusion. Linked premises are joined with a plus sign, to show "that they support the argument's conclusion (only) when combined."[3]

That is what the two shapes look like on paper, and it is the notation the practice will ask you for. Notice what the lower panel has that the passage didn't: a third box, "she catches it". Nothing follows from a departure time and a journey time unless she is on the train, and the writer left that out because it went without saying. Write it in and all three premises are linked, because losing any one of them leaves the other two saying nothing about when she gets home.

The reason to tell the two shapes apart is that the whole point of evaluating premises one at a time, which is what step 5 does, is to know what happens to the argument when one of them fails. If the premises are linked, a single false premise sinks the conclusion. If they're convergent, a single false premise costs the argument one reason and leaves the rest. So a diagram is a prediction about damage. It tells you, before you've tested anything, which premises are load-bearing and which are extra weight.

**The test, and it takes ten seconds.** Cover one premise. Ask whether what's left still gives you any reason at all for the conclusion. Any reason, not a good one. If yes, that premise was convergent. If the rest of the argument goes silent, it was linked to something. Run it on every premise and you have the diagram.

Plenty of real arguments mix the two. The flat argument, written out fully, would probably have a linked pair hiding inside it: "it's under budget" only counts because "[she can't afford anything over budget]", and those two go together. You'll see a mixed case in the boiler message below.

:::checkpoint An argument has three premises and you've found that one of them is false. When does the argument survive?
When the false premise is a convergent one, giving its own separate support that the other two don't depend on. Then the argument loses a reason and keeps the rest. If the false premise is linked to another, the pair fails together, and if it's linked to all the others, the argument is finished. So the question "does one false premise sink it?" has no answer until you've drawn the structure.
:::

## Toulmin's layout: the argument seen from the arguer's side

In lesson 1 you met Harry. "Harry was born in Bermuda, so Harry is a British subject." You found the missing premise, a general rule about people born in Bermuda, and you saw that writing it down made its exceptions visible.

:::checkpoint You met that rule in lesson 1 under a different name. What did you call it there, and what did writing it down do for you?
You called it the missing premise: the general claim the arguer needs and hasn't stated, which you supply in brackets. Writing it down did two things. It made the argument's real commitment visible, since the arguer is relying on a rule about everyone born in Bermuda and not just a fact about Harry. And it made the exceptions findable, because once a rule is on the page you can ask who it doesn't cover. Toulmin's name for it is the warrant, and his layout gives those exceptions a box of their own.
:::

Toulmin, in *The Uses of Argument* (1958), proposed six slots in place of premises and a conclusion, on the ground that the two-part layout leaves the most interesting part of an argument unwritten. This course reads him through [Hitchcock's paper on his warrants](https://humanities.mcmaster.ca/~hitchckd/Toulminswarrants.pdf).[1] Laid out on Harry:

- **Claim:** Harry is a British subject.
- **Data**, sometimes called grounds: Harry was born in Bermuda.
- **Warrant:** "A man born in Bermuda will generally be a British subject." This is the rule that licenses the step from the data to the claim. Toulmin, Rieke and Janik later called such a rule "a general, step-authorizing statement."[7]
- **Backing:** the statutes on British nationality as they stood in 1958, which are why the warrant held. They have been rewritten since, so this particular warrant has expired, and that is worth a moment: a warrant is a rule about the world, and rules about the world stop being true. Lesson 1 has the statute that replaced them.
- **Qualifier:** "presumably". The warrant says "generally", so the claim can only be made with that strength.
- **Rebuttal:** "unless both his parents were aliens" (the legal term, then and now, for citizens of another state), or unless he has since been naturalised elsewhere. The circumstances under which the warrant doesn't apply.[1]

:::predict Before you read on: "Harry was born in Bermuda, so Harry is a British subject" has two parts on the page and at least two more that nobody said. Name one of the unsaid parts, and say what would have to go wrong for the argument to fail.
One unsaid part is the rule: a man born in Bermuda will generally be a British subject. The other is what "generally" is holding back, which is a list of exceptions the rule tolerates. So the argument can fail in two quite different ways. Either the rule is wrong, or the rule is right and Harry is one of its exceptions. Those need two different replies, and a premise list gives you one slot for both of them. That's the whole reason to learn a second layout for an argument you can already write out.
:::

Now write the same argument in the standard form you've been using since lesson 1:

```
1. Harry was born in Bermuda.
2. [A man born in Bermuda will generally be a British subject.]
------------------------------------------------------------------------
C: Harry is (presumably) a British subject.
```

Look at what happened. The warrant is the bracketed premise. The two layouts are one argument viewed twice, so the second view has to earn its place.

Hitchcock, in a careful paper on Toulmin's warrants, gives the answer. A warrant is not a premise; it's an inference-licence, a rule the arguer is relying on rather than a fact they're asserting.[1] Seeing it as a rule does two things a premise list doesn't. First, a rule has a scope, so it invites the question "does this rule apply here?", which is the rebuttal slot. Second, rules belong to fields. Hitchcock stresses that warrants are field-dependent: the rule that licenses a step in law ("born in Bermuda, so a British subject") is nothing like the rule that licenses a step in structural engineering ("the beam is rated for twice this load, so the floor will hold"), and a step that's fine in one field can be worthless in another.[1] When someone argues from a courtroom rule to a medical conclusion, the layout shows the mismatch at once.

This is the reason the layout finds weaknesses a premise list hides. The rebuttal slot has to be filled. Writing "unless" and then stopping to think is where the exceptions come from. You won't find them by staring at "Harry was born in Bermuda, so Harry is British". You find them by writing the rule down and asking who it doesn't cover.

:::checkpoint Name the warrant in "She's a doctor, so she'll know."
Something like: a doctor generally knows the answer to a medical question. Once you've written it you can see the rebuttal slot fill itself. Unless the question isn't medical. Unless it's outside her specialty. Unless it's a question the profession hasn't settled. The data ("she's a doctor") is fine; the claim ("she'll know") is only as good as the warrant's reach, and the warrant reaches a good deal less far than the sentence sounds.
:::

## The boiler message

Here is a message of the kind a person might send to the family group chat. It's made up, so nothing in it needs to be true, and that's the point: you're testing the argument, not the boiler.

> Obviously the boiler needs replacing. It's fourteen years old, and every engineer will tell you they don't last past fifteen. It's been serviced twice this year already, which is probably more than a new one would need in five. And although a new one costs about £2,500, the savings on gas alone would pay that back within a few years. So we should replace it this autumn rather than wait for it to go.

**Close analysis first.** "Obviously" is an assuring term on the conclusion. "Every engineer will tell you" is an assuring term on the fifteen-year rule, and that rule is the premise with no evidence attached. "Probably", "about", and "within a few years" are guards, and they look honest: the writer is measuring what they're claiming about the servicing and the payback. "Although a new one costs about £2,500" is a discount: the cost is mentioned so it can be set aside.

**Now the reconstruction.** The conclusion is the last sentence. The stated premises are the age, the fifteen-year rule, the servicing, and the payback. Put them in standard form:

```
1. The boiler is fourteen years old.
2. Boilers don't last past fifteen years.
   ---------------------------------------------------------------------
   C1: The boiler will fail within about a year.
3. It has been serviced twice this year, which is probably more than a
   new one would need in five.
4. A new boiler costs about £2,500 and would pay that back in gas
   savings within a few years.
------------------------------------------------------------------------
C: We should replace the boiler this autumn rather than wait for it to
   fail.
```

C1 is an intermediate conclusion: something an argument proves on the way to what it wants. Real arguments are full of them, and marking them is how you find which half of an argument a bad premise takes down.

Now run the removal test rather than taking my word for the structure. Cover premise 3. "Fourteen years old, boilers die at fifteen, so replace it before it fails" still gives a reason, so 3 was convergent. Cover premise 2. "The boiler is fourteen years old" gives nothing at all, because fourteen is only alarming if you already believe something about fifteen, so 1 and 2 are linked to each other.

Now the gap. Something is missing between "it'll fail within a year" and "replace it this autumn rather than wait." Write down what the writer needs to be true that they haven't said. Do it before you open the box.

:::predict What does this writer need to be true that they haven't said?
Something like: "[Replacing a boiler before it fails is cheaper, or less disruptive, than replacing it after.]" The whole "rather than wait for it to go" depends on that. Notice the writer would sign it happily; it's obvious to them, which is exactly why it went unsaid. Notice also that it is a checkable claim. If an emergency replacement costs about the same as a planned one and the boiler has a fair chance of lasting another two winters, the argument loses most of its force even if every stated premise is true.
:::

So the whole thing, written out:

```
1. The boiler is fourteen years old.
2. Boilers don't last past fifteen years.
5. [Replacing a boiler before it fails is cheaper, or less disruptive,
   than replacing it after.]
   ---------------------------------------------------------------------
   C1: We should replace it before it fails rather than after.
3. It has been serviced twice this year, which is probably more than a
   new one would need in five.
4. A new boiler costs about £2,500 and would pay that back in gas
   savings within a few years.
------------------------------------------------------------------------
C: We should replace the boiler this autumn rather than wait for it to
   fail.
```

## Charity: reconstruct the version they'd sign

The principle of charity is old and plain. When a passage can be read more than one way, read it the way the arguer would accept. When you supply a missing premise, supply the one they'd agree to, not the one that's easiest to knock down and not the one that makes the argument valid at the price of being absurd.

That last case is the one worth slowing down for, because it feels like generosity. Take the café: "It's closed on Mondays, so let's go Tuesday." You could make that valid by adding "[Tuesday is the only day it opens]". Nobody believes that. You could add "[Tuesday is the next day that suits us both]", which makes the argument strong rather than valid and is exactly what the speaker meant. Charity says take the second, and if you reconstruct an argument nobody holds, your refutation refutes nobody.

The rule has two halves and lesson 1 gave you both: the most plausible premise the arguer would accept, and among those, the one that makes their argument strongest. The order matters. When no premise they'd sign makes the argument work, you haven't failed to be charitable. You've found the problem, and now you can say what it is.

:::predict Someone says: "The bus is cancelled, so we'll have to drive." Two different bracketed premises would carry that step. One of them makes the argument valid. Write both, then say which one charity picks and what you gain by picking it.
The valid one is something like "[The only ways to get there are the bus and the car]". It gets you to the conclusion with a guarantee, and it's plainly false: there's a train, a taxi, a bicycle, and not going. The charitable one is "[Driving is the only other way that gets us there in time]", which the speaker would sign and which makes the argument strong rather than valid. Charity takes the second, and notice what you gain by taking the weaker premise. "Is driving really the only other option that arrives in time?" is a question that can be answered, and answering it settles the argument. "Is the bus really the only way anyone can travel?" settles nothing, because nobody was arguing it. The stronger premise gave you an easier target and a worthless one.
:::

So charity isn't politeness. It's accuracy about the object you're evaluating. A reconstruction that the arguer wouldn't sign is a wrong answer to the question "what is this person's argument?", in the same way that a wrong sum is a wrong answer, and the fact that it was uncharitable is just the way it went wrong.

Internet usage has a word for the charitable version: the steelman, by contrast with the straw man, which is the version of someone's argument that is easy to knock down because they never made it. "Steelman" appeared in the early 2010s and has no canonical source; the older academic name is the principle of charity. Use either. Because the word has no settled definition, this course fixes one: a steelman is the version the best defender of the view actually holds. Some writers use it more loosely, for the strongest case anyone could make for a position, and it's worth keeping the two apart, because only the first is a reconstruction of somebody's argument. If you build a magnificent argument for a position and no one who holds the position would recognise it, you haven't steelmanned them; you've built your own argument and put their name on it, which is a straw man with better manners.

:::checkpoint You are arguing against a colleague who says the team should stop the Monday stand-up. You write: "Their case is that meetings crowd out the deep work the team is measured on, and that anything urgent already reaches people faster by message." They read it and say "that's not quite it". Is what you wrote a steelman?
No. A steelman is the version the person actually holds, and the test is theirs, not yours. You may have written a better argument than the one they hold; you may have written one they think misses their point entirely. Either way it isn't their argument yet, so refuting it refutes nobody. "That's not quite it" is the most useful sentence you can get, and the next move is to ask what's missing and write it again. The version that ends this exchange is the one where they say "yes, and I wish I'd put it that way".
:::

## Rapoport's rules

The best-known statement of how to do this comes from the philosopher Dennett, in *Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking* (2013). He credits Rapoport, whom he calls a legendary social psychologist and game theorist, and he presents the rules as the antidote to the habit of caricaturing an opponent. Under the heading "How to compose a successful critical commentary," Dennett writes:[6]

1. "You should attempt to re-express your target's position so clearly, vividly, and fairly that your target says, 'Thanks, I wish I'd thought of putting it that way.'"
2. "You should list any points of agreement (especially if they are not matters of general or widespread agreement)."
3. "You should mention anything you have learned from your target."
4. "Only then are you permitted to say so much as a word of rebuttal or criticism."

Two of the four assume something you may not have. If you can find no point of agreement and learned nothing, don't manufacture either; rules 2 and 3 are there to make you look, and looking honestly and finding nothing is the rule being followed. Rule 1 is the one with no exceptions, because it's the only one that changes what you're arguing with.

Dennett's stated reason is practical: following the rules turns your opponent into a more receptive audience for your criticism.[6] There's a second reason that matters more for this course. You can't do rule 1 without doing the reconstruction properly. To restate someone's position so well that they'd thank you, you have to have found their conclusion, their premises, the rule they're relying on, and the strength they're claiming. Which means rule 1 is this whole lesson, put to the one person who can tell you whether you got it right.

:::checkpoint Two openings to a reply, both polite. Only one passes rule 1. (a) "I understand you feel strongly about later school starts, and I respect that." (b) "Your case is that teenagers get more sleep when the day starts later, that the extra sleep shows up in attendance, and that rescheduling the buses is a one-off cost against a permanent gain." Which passes, and what exactly is wrong with the other?
(b) passes. It states the case in the form its holder would state it, with the reasons in the order they carry weight, and nothing in it is conceded or mocked. Show it to the other person and they say yes, that's what I think. (a) passes no test at all, because it describes the other person's feelings rather than their argument, and you can't refute a feeling. It's the easiest thing to write when you haven't done the reconstruction, which is why rule 1 is worth checking against rather than assuming you've met. If you can't write a paragraph like (b), you don't yet know what the argument is, and every objection you write next will be aimed at something you invented.
:::

Let me model it on one of the disagreements you'll pick from in the practice, the family dog. Suppose I'm against getting one and my sister is for it. Her case, restated by me under rule 1:

> A dog would get the children outdoors every day, which nothing else we've tried has managed. It would give the youngest, who's anxious, something to care for that depends on her. We've both said we want the house to feel more like a home than a timetable, and a dog does that in a way a plan can't. The costs are real, but they're known and steady, which is the kind of cost we handle well. And if we don't do it while the children are this age, we won't do it at all.

Rule 2: I agree that the children are indoors too much and that our previous fixes have failed. Rule 3: I hadn't thought about the youngest and responsibility; that's a real point and I learned it from her. Rule 4, only now: two of those five reasons need the children to do the work themselves, the daily outdoors and the youngest's responsibility, and I think the walking will land on one adult by the second month. That leaves the cost point and the now-or-never point standing, so what I've got is an objection that narrows her case rather than one that sinks it, and I should say so in those words.

Compare what I'd have said without the rules. "Dogs are expensive, and you haven't thought about the cost." She had. She answered it in her second-to-last sentence, and she answered it well: the costs are known and steady, which is the kind we handle. Without the restatement I'd have fired at the one part of her case that was already armoured, and she'd have spent her reply saying so instead of answering the objection that actually bites.

:::callout Charity is not agreement
Rapoport's rules end with rebuttal. Restating a position fairly commits you to nothing about whether it's right. What it commits you to is that when you say it's wrong, you'll be talking about the thing itself. The test of a steelman is whether the other person recognises it, not whether you've come round to it.
:::

## Step 5: the acceptability, relevance, and grounds conditions

You now have an argument reconstructed in the version its author would sign, diagrammed, with its warrant found. Time to test it.

:::predict Suppose an argument is valid: the conclusion really does follow from the premises. Can it still be a bad argument, and if so what would be wrong with it?
Yes, and the usual trouble is with the premises rather than the link. Validity says the premises, if true, give the conclusion all the support there is. It says nothing about whether the arguer has earned them. "Every boiler over fifteen years old should be replaced; this one is sixteen; so it should be replaced" is valid, and its first premise is the round rule the family message leaned on and couldn't support. So an argument can pass on its link and fail on its premises, and the three names below exist so you can say which of those happened.
:::

Lessons 2 through 6 gave you the tests for the link. The ARG conditions add the tests for the premises and put the whole thing in one frame. The letters are Govier's, from a textbook this course has not read; everything below is taken from the Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on informal logic, which was.[2][3] An argument is cogent if its premises are:

**(A) Acceptable.** The Stanford entry's short version is that premises are acceptable "when they are true or acceptable in some other way."[3] The version this lesson will use is the tighter one Govier's letters point at: a premise is acceptable when a reasonable person in the arguer's audience could grant it without being given an argument for it first. The two come apart in one interesting case, and it's the case that matters here. A premise can be true and still not have been earned for this audience, and when that happens the argument has work left to do even though nothing in it is false. Where does a premise get earned? The CSUN critical-reasoning syllabus names the three usual places, and they're the ones you'll meet: common knowledge, testimony you have no reason to doubt, and an authority who is actually an authority on the point.[8] Note what acceptability is not. It isn't "I accept it", and it isn't "it's true".

**(R) Relevant.** Each premise gives some support to the conclusion. The Stanford entry's wording, following Johnson and Blair, is that premises are "relevant when they provide some (i.e. any) support for the conclusion of the argument."[3] That sounds like a low bar and it is one, which is exactly why a failure on it is easy to miss: an irrelevant premise is usually true, usually about the same subject, and often the reason the passage felt persuasive. "He's been late three times this month" is relevant to whether he should keep his job and irrelevant to whether his budget figures are right, and the sentence carries no marker telling you which question is on the table. Showing the failure takes work, because you have to state the conclusion precisely first and then ask what this premise adds to it.

**(G) Adequate grounds.** The premises, taken together, give the conclusion enough support: the link is valid, if a deductive link was claimed, or strong enough, if an inductive one was. This is the test you already own.

:::checkpoint The grounds condition asks whether the premises give the conclusion enough support. For each of these, say what "enough" means and which lesson gave you the test. (a) "The last train leaves at 22:10 and the trip takes fifty minutes, so she'll be home by 23:00." (b) "Eleven of the twelve flats in this block have damp, so flat 7 probably does." (c) "The witness picked him out, and only one coat in a thousand carries that fibre, so it was him."
(a) claims a guarantee, so enough means valid, and the test is lesson 2's: could the premises be true and the conclusion false? (b) claims only probability, so enough means strong, and the tests are lesson 5's: is the sample big enough, and does it cover the case in hand? (c) claims probability too, and lesson 6 gives you the way to say how much: the likelihood ratio asks how much likelier that evidence is if he did it than if he didn't, which is a measure of how far a premise should move you rather than a verdict. All three are the grounds condition. Only the standard for "enough" changes.
:::

One warning about the word. In lesson 5 you met "cogent" for an inductive argument that's strong and whose premises are in fact true. The ARG frame uses the same word more widely: any argument, deductive or inductive, whose premises are acceptable, relevant and adequate. The difference that matters is the first condition. Lesson 5 asked whether the premises were true; this asks whether the arguer has earned them for this audience, which is a question you can actually answer from the page. And notice what none of it does. Deciding a premise is unacceptable tells you the arguer hasn't earned it; it doesn't tell you it's false. Finding out whether it's true is evidence work, and it will belong to Statistics for Citizens and The Scientific Method rather than here.

Johnson and Blair's original version calls the third condition sufficiency, and the Stanford entry uses their letters, ARS.[3] "Grounds" is the same idea, and the letters don't matter. What matters is that the three conditions are separate, so that an argument can fail exactly one and you can say which.

### The boiler, judged

Now the verdict we held back. **The link:** this is an inductive argument, and you can tell from the guards. Given the premises it's reasonably strong, so grounds passes. **Relevance:** every premise bears on whether to replace the boiler now, so relevance passes throughout. **Acceptability** is where it gives. Premise 1, the age, is a matter of record. Premises 3 and 4 are guarded and plausible, and a reasonable person in this family would grant them provisionally, though "can we see the bills?" is a fair request.

Premise 2 is the problem, and notice how you know. It's the premise under "every engineer will tell you", which is an assurance and not a figure, and it's stated as a hard cut-off at a round number. Neither of those makes it false. Both of them make it the premise you'd have to see evidence for before granting it, which is the acceptability condition doing its work. Ask what the writer would say if you pressed, and you'll get something weaker and more defensible: older boilers fail more often and cost more to repair. Grant that instead and the linked chain still runs, but it runs weakly, and premise 5 is now carrying more weight than the writer noticed.

The verdict is not "wrong". The argument is alive on its convergent legs (3 and 4) even if the linked chain (1, 2, 5) is weaker than it looked. A fair reply is "the fifteen-year rule isn't a rule, but the servicing and the savings might be enough on their own; can we see the bills?" That reply refutes the version the writer actually holds, keeps the parts that work, and moves the conversation forward. The reply "boilers don't have expiry dates, so no" refutes one premise and pretends the other three don't exist. And naming which condition failed is what turns "I disagree" into something the writer can answer.

:::checkpoint You now have every step. Close the page and write out the six-step check in order, and against each step write the lesson that filled it in.
1. Find the conclusion (lesson 1). 2. Find the premises (lesson 1). 3. Supply what's missing, in the form the arguer would accept (lesson 1, and charity today). 4. Test the link: valid, or strong? (lessons 2 to 4 for deduction, 5 and 6 for induction; this is the grounds condition). 5. Test the premises: acceptable, and relevant to this conclusion? (today). 6. Only now consult your opinion of the conclusion (lesson 2, where belief bias is waiting). If step 6 moved up the list while you were writing, that's the thing the whole check exists to stop.
:::

Lesson 8 will hand you a list of fallacy names. On Johnson and Blair's sort, and on this course's, most of them are shortcuts for a failure of one of these three conditions, and you'll be able to say which, which is more than the name tells you. Lesson 8 also gives you the rivals to that sort, because there are good ones: Walton reads a fallacy as an argument scheme misused in a particular kind of exchange, pragma-dialectics as a rule broken in a critical discussion, and Boudry, Paglieri and Pigliucci argue the whole list should go. You'll meet all three there and decide for yourself.

## What people get wrong

**"Reconstruction means putting it in my words."** It means putting it in theirs, made explicit. Your words are fine for the bracketed premises, as long as the arguer would sign them. The moment the reconstruction contains something they'd deny, it's no longer a reconstruction of their argument.

**"Charity means agreeing."** It means evaluating the right object. You can restate someone's case so well that they thank you and then show, on the acceptability condition, that its central premise has no support. That's not a contradiction; it's the order Dennett's fourth rule requires.

**"A steelman is the strongest argument I can imagine for the position."** It's the strongest argument the position's best defenders actually make. If nobody holds your improved version, you're arguing with yourself. Ask the question the first rule asks: would they say "I wish I'd put it that way"? If they'd say "that's not what I think", start again.

**"Relevance is obvious."** It's the failure that's hardest to see and hardest to demonstrate, because an irrelevant premise can be perfectly true and squarely on the same subject. The test is to state the conclusion precisely and then ask what this premise adds to it. If the honest answer is "nothing, but it's true", you've found the failure.

**"Acceptable means I accept it."** It means a reasonable member of the audience the arguer is addressing could accept it without more argument. Your acceptance is neither necessary nor sufficient. This is also where your opinion of the conclusion sneaks back in early: you'll find yourself calling premises unacceptable because you dislike where they lead. Step 6 exists to stop that; keep it in sixth place.

**"Guarding is weaselling."** A guard tells you the size of the claim rather than hiding it. Hold the arguer to the guarded claim and don't let them cash it later as the bold one. But check whether the guarded version still supports the conclusion, because a guard can also be a retreat to something too weak to do any work.

Then set the qualifier: read your warrant back and write the one word the claim is entitled to, "certainly", "presumably", or "sometimes". If the warrant says "generally" and the conclusion says "so", you've found something the arguer owes you.

**3.** Pick a disagreement from this list where you have a view: whether children should have homework; whether a family should get a dog; whether a team is better off meeting in person or by video; whether it's better to live in a city or a small town. Write the other side's case under rule 1, in a paragraph they'd thank you for. Then rules 2 and 3 in a sentence each. Then, and only then, your best objection. If you can, show the first paragraph to someone who holds the view and write down their reaction. "That's not quite it" is the useful answer; ask what's missing and rewrite.

**4.** Go to the folder argument you most disagree with. Write its steelman: the reconstruction the arguer would sign, in standard form, with a diagram and its warrant named. Don't evaluate it yet. That paragraph is the first draft of the course project, and the evaluation comes after lesson 8. If that argument is about politics or religion, that's fine, and it's what the project expects. You'll be judged on whether the person who made it would sign your version, not on which side you end up on.

:::checkpoint Model answers for the three passages in item 1. Open this only when all three are written out. Items 2, 3 and 4 use your own material, so there's no key; the test there is whether the arguer would sign what you wrote.
**(a) The school start time.** Assuring: "Clearly" on the conclusion, and "are known to" on the sleep claim, which is the premise with no figure attached and the one to check first. Guarding: "probably" on the attendance claim and "most" on the absences claim, both honest. Discounting: "although the buses would need rescheduling".

```
1. Teenagers sleep badly before eight.
2. [A school should set its start time to fit when its pupils can sleep.]
3. Most absences are first-period absences.
4. [A later first period would turn some of those absences into attendance.]
------------------------------------------------------------------------
C: The school should move the start time later.
```

Premises 1 and 2 are linked, and so are 3 and 4; the two pairs are convergent with each other, since either would be a reason on its own. The bus cost is not a premise for the conclusion at all. It's the objection the writer discounted, and disposing of an objection removes a reason against rather than adding a reason for. Marking it as a further premise is the commonest error on this passage. ARG: relevance passes, grounds is reasonable for an inductive argument, and acceptability fails on premise 1, which is exactly where the assuring term sits. "Sleep badly before eight" isn't a claim a governor could grant without seeing something, and it isn't precise enough to check.

**(b) The printer.** No assuring, guarding or discounting terms at all, which is worth noticing: a passage can be free of marker words and still fail. Two arguments stacked, the first feeding the second.

```
1. If the printer were out of toner, the pages would come out faint.
2. The pages are coming out faint.
   ---------------------------------------------------------------------
   C1: The printer is out of toner.
3. [We have no toner in the cupboard.]
------------------------------------------------------------------------
C: We should order toner today.
```

Both stated premises are acceptable and both are relevant. It gives on grounds, and it gives at the first step: this is affirming the consequent, from lesson 3. Faint pages are what an empty cartridge produces, and they're also what a worn drum produces, and the conditional never said otherwise. Write the counterexample down: worn drum, full cartridge, faint pages, all three premises true and the conclusion false. Acceptability and relevance are the new conditions; grounds is the one you've been testing since lesson 2, and here it's the one that fails.

**(c) The review site.** Assuring: none, and the absence tells you something, because this writer gives reasons instead of assurances. Guarding: "nearly always", which is honest, and you should hold them to it rather than to "always". Discounting: "although the reviews look thorough", five words disposing of the strongest objection to the conclusion.

```
1. The site is owned by a company that sells the products it reviews.
2. It nearly always rates the parent company's products highest.
3. [An owner with a stake in the outcome whose ratings track that stake
   is not an independent guide.]
------------------------------------------------------------------------
C: You can't trust that review site.
```

Premises 1 and 2 are linked through 3. Ownership alone would be a reason for suspicion and no more; the pattern alone could be a coincidence, or could be true because the products are better. Together they're much stronger than either. This one gives on grounds rather than acceptability: both stated premises are checkable, but "you can't trust it" reaches further than they support. A conflict of interest plus a favourable pattern shows the ratings aren't independent evidence, not that any particular review is false. And that is what the "although" was hiding, because thoroughness is exactly what would make individual reviews useful even from an interested source.

Three passages, three different conditions. That's the point of keeping them apart.

## Connections

This lesson completes the six-step check. The missing premise you learned to bracket in lesson 1 is what Toulmin calls a warrant, seen from the arguer's side as a rule rather than a fact. Step 4 is the grounds condition, which is lessons 2 to 4 for deductive links and lessons 5 and 6 for inductive ones. Step 5 is the acceptability and relevance conditions you met today. Step 6 is where belief bias, from lesson 2, waits for you, and the only defence is to keep it sixth.

Lesson 8 gives you the fallacy names. It comes after this one on purpose: on the sort this course uses, a fallacy name is a hypothesis that an argument fails one of the ARG conditions, and you now have the tests that decide the hypothesis. Lesson 9 turns the method on the news, where the assuring terms come from sources and the missing premises come from editors. Lesson 10 turns Rapoport's rules round: you'll steelman your own opposition before you write, so that your argument has already met its best objection by the time a reader does.

One thing this lesson didn't settle, and you're owed the reason. Everything here has evaluated arguments as *products*: a set of premises, a conclusion, a link, judged on the page. That's one school's choice, and the Stanford entry on informal logic maps the other two.[3]

**Dialectic.** Pragma-dialectics, worked out at Amsterdam by van Eemeren and Grootendorst, holds that an argument is a move in a procedure for resolving a difference of opinion, so you can't tell whether a move is any good by looking at it alone. Their ten rules for a critical discussion cover moves the product view can't see at all: refusing your opponent the right to advance a standpoint, or shifting the burden of proof onto them. On their account a straw man is bad not because it fails a premise test but because it makes resolution impossible.

**Rhetoric.** Perelman and Olbrechts-Tyteca, writing in 1958, the same year as Toulmin, and more recently Tindale, hold that argument is addressed to an audience and is assessed by the adherence it wins from one. Their point lands on this very lesson. The acceptability condition you just learned says a premise is acceptable if a reasonable person *in the arguer's audience* would grant it, so on their reading the question "is this a good argument?" has no answer until you say "good for whom?"

**Logic**, this course's choice, answers that both of those are true and neither removes the question a reader actually has in front of them: given this passage, in my hands, right now, is there a good reason here? Acceptability, relevance and sufficiency can be run by one person alone with a pen, which is the whole reason this course teaches them. Walton's dialogue theory tries to hold all three together, and the Stanford entry describes other attempts to combine them.

People who study argument for a living disagree about which is basic. The "Go deeper" list points at the map, and lesson 8 comes back to it, because the three schools also disagree about what a fallacy is.
