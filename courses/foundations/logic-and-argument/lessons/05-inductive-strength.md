    options:

      - The argument was never a real argument, because a passage that gives reasons on both sides of a question is a discussion rather than an argument

Suppose a friend of yours is thinking about buying a car and says this: "My last three cars from that maker all had gearbox trouble before 80,000 kilometres. I'm not buying another one." Three bad experiences, a conclusion about the fourth. That shape turns up wherever people generalise from what has happened to them.

Run the check you've been building since lesson 1. Conclusion: the next car from that maker will probably have gearbox trouble too. Premises: three cars, three gearboxes, three failures. Missing premise, in brackets, written as she would actually sign it: [three failures out of three is a sign of how this maker builds gearboxes]. Now step 4, test the link. In lessons 2 to 4 that meant asking whether there's any possible case where the premises are true and the conclusion false. Here there plainly is: the maker could have changed gearbox supplier two years ago, so the sign points at a car that no longer exists. So the argument is invalid.

And yet it isn't a bad argument. Three out of three is real evidence. If you told her "that's invalid" she'd rightly say "I never claimed it was certain; I claimed it was likely." She's making a different kind of claim about the link, and it needs a different test. That test is this lesson, and it's the one you reach for whenever the claim is about what will probably happen rather than what must. That covers most of what you believe about the world outside your own front door, from whether the bus will come to whether the bridge will hold.

## Strong, weak, and cogent

An argument is deductive when the premises are claimed to guarantee the conclusion, and inductive when they're claimed only to make it probable. That is the whole of the distinction, and it follows from the definitions themselves: a deductive argument is one whose conclusion "is supposed to follow from its premises with absolute certainty", and an inductive one is an argument whose conclusion is supposed to follow "with a high level of probability" ([Van Cleave 1.8](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Philosophy/Introduction_to_Logic_and_Critical_Thinking_2e_%28van_Cleave%29/01%3A_Reconstructing_and_Analyzing_Arguments/1.08%3A_Deductive_vs._Inductive_Arguments)).[1] Neither definition mentions direction of travel.

Lesson 2 left you a promise to collect here, because this is the first place the two kinds sit side by side. The myth is that deduction runs from the general to the particular and induction from the particular to the general. All four combinations happen, and here they are:

- **General to particular, deductive.** All humans are mortal, so Socrates is.
- **Particular to particular, inductive.** This coin came up heads a thousand times, so the next toss will probably be heads.
- **General to particular, inductive.** Ninety-seven of every hundred bolts in this batch are sound, so the bolt in my hand is probably sound. The premise is about the batch and the conclusion about one bolt, and nothing is guaranteed.
- **Particular to general, deductive.** This bolt is faulty, so it's false that all the bolts in the batch are sound. One case, a claim about every case, and it follows with certainty.

Direction tells you nothing. What picks the test is what the arguer claims the link does.

:::checkpoint Which test does each of these need? (a) "Nine of the ten flats in this block have damp, so flat 7 probably does." (b) "Flat 7 has no damp, so it's false that every flat in the block has damp."
(a) is inductive: a general premise, a particular conclusion, and "probably" tells you what is being claimed. Ask whether it's strong, and it is, unless you know something that makes flat 7 unlike the others, a new roof for instance. (b) is deductive: a particular premise, a general conclusion, and valid. One case refutes an "all". If you sorted them by direction of travel you got both wrong, which is why lesson 2 warned you off it.
:::

For a deductive argument, step 4 asks: is it valid? For an inductive one, step 4 asks: is it strong? An inductive argument is strong when, if the premises were true, the conclusion would probably be true. It's weak when the premises, even if true, don't make the conclusion probable. And, matching "sound" from lesson 2, an inductive argument is called **cogent** when it's strong and its premises are in fact true. The Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on inductive logic puts the same idea in terms of degree: the premises of a good inductive argument "support the truth of its conclusion to some appropriate degree".[2] Degree is the word to hold on to. Validity is all or nothing; strength comes in amounts.

### How to tell which kind you're looking at

The distinction turns on what the arguer claims, and real arguers almost never say. Three tests, in order.

**What they said.** "Must", "it follows", "therefore certainly", "there's no way round it" claim a guarantee. "Probably", "likely", "suggests", "I'd expect" claim strength. Most arguments give you one of these words if you look.

**The shrug test.** Describe a case where the premises hold and the conclusion fails, and imagine saying it to them. If they'd retract, they were claiming certainty and you've found a counterexample. If they'd shrug and say "I said probably", they were claiming strength and you've found nothing yet. That's what your friend with the cars does, and it's why "invalid" bounced off her.

**Charity.** If a passage is invalid read one way and strong read the other, read it the other way, because that's the version the arguer would sign. Lesson 7 makes this a rule.

:::checkpoint An argument has true premises and a strong link. What can still go wrong?
The conclusion can still be false. That's not a defect in the argument; it's what "probably" means. A strong, cogent argument for "the next toss will be heads" is exactly the argument you should accept, and the next toss can still be tails. The argument was good and the world didn't cooperate. Deductive arguments never do this to you. Sound deductive arguments guarantee their conclusions. Cogent inductive arguments make them probable, and probable things sometimes don't happen.
:::

### Why adding evidence can hurt

One property separates inductive arguments from deductive ones outright. Take the coin:

```
1. This coin has come up heads on all of the last 1,000 tosses.
------------------------------------------------------------------------
C: The next toss will probably be heads.
```

That's strong. A thousand heads in a row is about as good a reason as you could want to expect another.

:::predict Can adding a true premise ever make an argument worse?
For a valid deductive argument, never. If the conclusion follows from the premises, it still follows when you add more premises; you can't un-follow something by learning more. For an inductive argument, yes, easily. Watch what one extra line does to the coin.
:::

Now add a premise that you've just learned, and that's true:

```
1. This coin has come up heads on all of the last 1,000 tosses.
2. The coin has just been checked by a lab and is a fair coin.
------------------------------------------------------------------------
C: The next toss will probably be heads.
```

Premise 2 doesn't contradict premise 1. Both are true. But the argument is now weaker, because a fair coin has no memory, and the thousand heads, which looked like evidence about the coin, now look like an astonishing run of luck that says nothing about the next toss. (If you want to say "I'd want to re-check that lab", good, that's the right instinct, and it's step 5, testing the premises. Take the premise as given for now.)

Logicians call this defeasibility: an inductive argument can be defeated by new information without any of its original premises being false. A valid argument can't. Whatever you add to a valid argument, the conclusion still follows. This is why "the argument was strong at the time" is a perfectly coherent thing to say, and why every conclusion you reach inductively is held on condition that nothing relevant turns up. It's also why, in real inquiry, "we found out more" is not an embarrassment but the thing working as designed.

### Why strength comes in degrees

Why does strength come in degrees, and why can it drop? Because of what an inductive link is. A valid deductive link holds in every possible case; there's no way to make the premises true and the conclusion false. An inductive link only holds in most of the cases the premises describe. Your conclusion is about one case: the next toss, the fourth car, the patient in front of you. So the argument is strong to the degree that the premises cover that case, which means the degree to which the cases they describe resemble it in the ways that matter.

That's a picture rather than a theory of how support works, and it's the picture I'd hold on to, because the rest of the lesson follows from it. A sample supports a generalisation to the degree that it covers the population. An analogy supports its conclusion to the degree that the shared features are the ones that matter. A causal argument is strong to the degree that it isolates the one thing that varies. And a best explanation is best only until a better one covers more. Lesson 6 gives the same idea in probabilities, which is where anyone who wants it precise has to go.

The informal-logic literature sorts arguments into four kinds by the link they claim: deductive, inductive, conductive, and abductive (reasons weighed on both sides, and picking the explanation that fits best, respectively).[3] You've met the first. The five sections that follow take the rest in turn: the three inductive families a general reader needs, then the abductive kind under its plainer name, then the conductive one.

:::callout One word, two sizes
"Inductive" gets used in a narrow sense (generalising from cases, as in the coin and the cars) and a broad sense (any argument whose premises are claimed to make the conclusion probable rather than certain). **This lesson uses the broad one**, because the test is the same in every case: strong or weak, by coverage. Groarke's taxonomy above keeps abduction separate, because it classifies by the pattern of inference rather than by the test. When someone tells you induction is "just" generalising, they're using the narrow sense.
:::

## Family one: generalising from a sample

Start with a version of the argument anyone testing a new product has to make. Say you've made a hot sauce and want to know whether people would buy it. You give a bottle to twelve friends. Ten say they'd buy it. So:

```
1. Ten of twelve people who tried the sauce said they'd buy it.
------------------------------------------------------------------------
C: Most people who try the sauce would buy it.
```

Weak, and it's worth being precise about why, because it's weak in two separate ways and fixing one doesn't fix the other.

First, size. Twelve is few enough that ten out of twelve barely narrows anything down. If the real rate were 70%, you'd get ten or more about a quarter of the time; if it were 85%, about three quarters of the time. Twelve people can't tell those two worlds apart, and they point to different business decisions. Suppose you give it to a hundred people instead. Now eighty say yes. Better: the premise now covers more of the ground the conclusion is about.

Second, and this is the one that survives the fix, representativeness. Who were the hundred? If they're still your friends, they like you, they know you made it, and several of them probably share your taste in food, which is why they're your friends. The conclusion is about "people who try the sauce" and the sample is "people who know the maker and were handed a free bottle by them". The premises cover a small and unusual corner of the population the conclusion is about, and no increase in size fixes that. A thousand friends is still friends.

:::predict You fix representativeness too. You set up a stall at a Saturday market, strangers try the sauce, and 160 of 200 say they'd buy it. What weakness is still there?
"Said they'd buy it" is not "bought it". The premise is about a stated intention with a free taste in their mouth and the maker smiling at them; the conclusion you actually care about is what people do at a shelf with a price on it. The sample can be large and representative of market-goers and still not cover the case you're arguing about, because it measures the wrong thing.

And if you also said that Saturday market-goers aren't everyone, you've got the idea twice over: every sample is representative of something, and the question is always whether that something is the population in the conclusion.
:::

Weston's chapter on arguments by example sets three rules for any generalisation: "Use more than one example" (rule 7), "Use representative examples" (rule 8), and "Background rates are often crucial" (rule 9).[4] When you meet a survey in the news, the questions to ask are the ones you just asked: how many, chosen how, asked what, and compared with what. Statistics for Citizens, when that course is published, will teach the arithmetic of sampling properly; this course only needs you to see that the arithmetic is answering the coverage question.

Lesson 4 again, in new clothes. A conclusion of the form "all A are B" is refuted by one case, because it's a universal claim and one case falsifies it. "Most A are B" isn't refuted by one case; that would be like treating a "most" as an "all". If someone answers your well-sampled "most customers prefer the new label" with "well, my cousin doesn't", they've brought a counterexample to an argument that never claimed there were none. The right reply isn't to defend the cousin's taste; it's to point out which kind of claim you made.

## Family two: analogy

An argument from analogy says: this case is like that case in several ways, and that case had a certain feature, so this case probably has it too. Made-up case, but a common one: "Our neighbours' extension took four months and came in on budget. Ours is the same size, the same builder, the same kind of house. It'll take about four months." That's a decent argument. What makes it one is worth being slow about.

The extension's duration depends on its size, on the builder, and on the house it's attached to (foundations, access, the age of the walls). The similarities named are the ones that bear on the conclusion.

:::predict Same shape, different similarities: "Their extension came in on budget. Ours has the same colour front door and the same number of bedrooms. So ours will come in on budget too." Two similarities, same as before. Is it as strong an argument? Say why not before you open this.
It's worthless. The first version named the size, the builder and the kind of house, and those are the things that decide what an extension costs. A front door and a bedroom count decide nothing about it. The number of similarities hasn't changed. What's changed is whether they bear on the conclusion, and that is the whole test for an analogy. It's the coverage idea again: the shared features have to be the ones that produce the feature you're arguing about.
:::

An analogy is strong to the degree that the shared features are the ones that produce the feature in the conclusion. Irrelevant similarities add nothing however many there are. And one relevant difference can sink an analogy that has a dozen similarities going for it. If the neighbours' extension was built in a dry summer and yours is starting in November, that one difference may matter more than everything the two projects share.

:::checkpoint Two cases share ten features and differ in one. Can the argument from analogy still be weak?
Yes, in two ways. If the ten shared features are irrelevant to the conclusion, they give no support however many there are. And if the one difference is the thing that produces the feature you're arguing about, it outweighs all ten. Count relevance, not features. The question is never "how similar are they?" but "are they similar in the way that matters for this conclusion?"
:::

One more thing to ask of any analogy: what job is it doing? Analogies are often given to make an unfamiliar case vivid rather than to prove anything, and you should decide which before you attack it. If someone says a proposed change is "like rearranging the deckchairs", they're not offering an argument, they're offering a picture, and the reply is to ask what the picture is claiming.

The inductive arguments that decide things claim that one thing causes another. This drug lowers blood pressure. The new timetable reduced lateness. Eating late gives me bad sleep. The tools for checking a causal claim were written down in 1843 by John Stuart Mill, and they're still the bones of experimental design. Mill states them as canons, his word for the rules themselves. I'll give you two of them, in Jonathan Bennett's modernised wording of Mill's text, because those two are the ones you'll use. (The chapter is titled for four methods and states five canons; the fifth is the one at the end of this section.)

### The method of difference

Mill's own example is a killing. "When a man is shot through the heart, the Method of Difference shows us that it was the gunshot that killed him: he was in the fullness of life immediately before, all circumstances being the same [as after] except the wound".[6] The square brackets there are Bennett's own insertion, marked because he is rewriting Mill's sentences into modern English rather than reprinting them. Mill introduces it as a logical process that gives us "almost all the inductive conclusions we draw in daily life".[6] The canon itself says that where an instance in which the phenomenon occurs and an instance in which it does not "have every circumstance in common except for one x that occurs only in the former", that x "is the effect or the cause or an indispensable part of the cause".[6]

Read the canon slowly, because everything in it earns its place. Two instances. Everything in common but one thing. The phenomenon in one and not the other. Then that one thing is the cause, or part of it. In the man's case: alive and then dead, and the only thing that changed was the wound. So the wound did it.

Notice the shape of the reasoning. "If the wound weren't the cause, he'd still be alive, since nothing else changed. He isn't alive. So the wound is the cause." That's modus tollens, lesson 3's search for the case that breaks the rule, and it's the same move as a counterexample in lesson 2. The method of difference is the hunt for the one thing whose presence or absence makes the difference, and it works by ruling out everything else.

This is why Mill says the method of difference "is more particularly a method of artificial experiment", where the method of agreement (the first canon, which looks for the one thing that instances of the phenomenon share) is "more especially what we use when experimentation is impossible".[6] A controlled experiment is the method of difference built on purpose: two groups alike in everything you can arrange, one gets the treatment, and any difference in outcome is laid at the treatment's door. The modern addition Mill didn't have is randomising who gets the treatment, which is how you handle the differences you can't even name. The Scientific Method course, when it is published, will own the design of experiments; the point here is that you already understand the logic, because you've been using it since lesson 2.

Mill's canon is demanding, and that's the part people miss. "Every circumstance in common except for one" is almost never true of two real cases. Two patients, two mornings, two cars are alike in most things and differ in many small ones. So a real method-of-difference argument is strong to the degree that the other differences have been ruled out or are known not to matter, and weak to the degree that some other difference could be the one doing the work. When you read "we tried X and Y happened", ask: what else changed?

### Concomitant variation, and what the slogan leaves out

Mill's fifth canon covers the case where you can't remove the suspected cause and can only watch it vary. "If any phenomenon x varies in some specific way whenever another phenomenon y varies in some specific way, x is either a cause or an effect of y, or is causally connected with it in some other manner".[6] "Concomitant variation" is Mill's phrase for two things that go up and down together, which is what we now call a correlation.

Now look at the hedge at the end, "or is causally connected with it in some other manner". Mill explains it immediately: "I add that last clause because when two phenomena match each other in their variations it doesn't follow one is cause and the other effect. If they were two effects of a common cause, they would exhibit concomitant variation; and this method alone can't tell us whether they're related in that way rather than as cause and effect."[6]

That, in 1843, is the whole content of the slogan "correlation isn't causation". As an objection, the slogan is a good one: someone has inferred a cause from a pattern, and the pattern alone doesn't get them there. What it isn't is an answer. Mill's version says that when two things really do vary together, they are causally connected in some manner, and that this method alone can't tell you which.

Notice the "really do". Mill was writing before anyone had a theory of sampling error, so his "vary together" means a real pattern rather than a wobble in a small set of figures. That's why the modern list has four readings where Mill's canon has three: the fourth is that the pattern is a fluke of the data you happen to hold, which is a possibility Mill's wording doesn't leave room for and which any statistician would put at the top of the list. So, four readings of any correlation between A and B:

4. It's chance: the two happened to move together in the data you have.

A causal argument from a correlation is strong to the degree that readings 2, 3 and 4 have been ruled out, and weak to the degree that any of them is still live. Rule out chance first, because until you have, Mill's canon doesn't even apply.

Keep readings 2 and 3 apart, because they are easy to blur. Reading 2 says your effect is really the cause. Reading 3 says a third thing produced both. And a rival cause that has nothing to do with A is none of the four: it's the loose "every other circumstance in common" clause of the method of difference, and it's the commonest way a real causal argument leaks. Four readings, and lesson 9 adds a fifth that the returning-aircraft case makes vivid: the pattern can be an artefact of how the cases got into your data in the first place.

### The café, the pharmacy, and a joke about sunburn

An invented case, to keep the arithmetic clean. A café owner keeps daily sales figures. The pharmacy next door, run by a friend, keeps daily figures too. Comparing notes, they find that on days when the café sells a lot of iced drinks, the pharmacy sells a lot of after-sun lotion. The correlation is strong and holds across the whole summer. The café owner jokes that her iced coffee is giving people sunburn.

:::predict Write all four readings for this case, and say for each whether it's live and why.
Reading 3, a common cause, is the one to land on, and it's right: hot, sunny days push up both. But saying why the other three fail is the actual argument. Reading 1 (iced drinks cause sunburn) has no mechanism. Reading 2 (people buying after-sun then buy a cold drink) has a thin story at best; the drink sales rise on the same day as the lotion sales, which is too fast for that story. Reading 4 (chance) is unlikely given a whole summer of data. Reading 3 explains all of it with one obvious cause.
:::

:::predict Using the method of difference, what single comparison would test reading 3 against reading 1? Write it down before you open this.
Find pairs of days that were equally hot and sunny but on which iced-drink sales differed for some other reason, say a morning when the coffee machine broke. If after-sun sales track the weather and not the drinks, reading 1 is dead and reading 3 stands. You've held the suspected common cause fixed and let the suspected cause vary. A good trial of a drug or a policy does the same thing at a far larger scale: hold the other differences steady, vary the one. Those other differences have a name in research, confounders: anything that changes alongside your suspected cause and could be doing its work.

If you wrote "compare a hot day with a cold one", look again. That varies the weather, which is the thing you were trying to hold still.
:::

One more pattern to name, because it's the one people reach for without noticing. Post hoc reasoning ("after this, therefore because of this") is a causal argument from a single sequence: I did X, then Y happened, so X caused Y. It's the method of difference with the "every other circumstance in common" clause quietly dropped. You started the new supplement on Monday and slept badly on Tuesday. Also on Monday: a stressful meeting, a late dinner, a change in the weather. It has a fallacy name, false cause, and lesson 8 takes it up under that name. The fix is always the same question: what else changed?

:::checkpoint Someone says "the new school timetable has cut lateness; the figures dropped the month it came in". Which reading of the correlation is the argument assuming, which readings has it not ruled out, and what one comparison would help most?
It assumes reading 1, timetable causes the drop. It hasn't ruled out reading 3, a genuine common cause: the new timetable came in at the start of a term, and lateness may drop at the start of every term anyway. Nor reading 4, since one month is a small sample of months. Reading 2 is far-fetched here. It also hasn't ruled out a second cause acting in the same month, such as a new attendance officer, which isn't one of the four readings but is the other thing the method of difference hunts: another circumstance that changed at the same time.

The most useful single comparison: lateness in the same month last year, when there was no new timetable. If it dropped then too, the timetable has lost its evidence.
:::

## Inference to the best explanation

Now a kind of argument that doesn't quite fit any of the three families, though it's inductive in the broad sense. You come home. There are muddy paw prints on the kitchen floor, the pie you left cooling on the counter is gone, and the dog is asleep in his basket looking, you'd swear, guilty. You conclude the dog ate the pie.

Nothing here is a sample. Nothing is an analogy. It isn't a correlation. What you've done is take a set of facts and pick the hypothesis that best explains them. The philosopher Gilbert Harman gave this pattern its usual name in 1965: inference to the best explanation, and he named and defended it as a basic form of non-deductive inference.[7] Groarke's survey lists it, under the name abduction, as the fourth kind of argument alongside deductive, inductive, and conductive.[3]

:::predict Before you accept "the dog ate the pie", name two other explanations that fit those same facts, and say what each one needs you to believe.
A burglar with muddy shoes who took only the pie: that covers the prints and the pie, but it needs a burglar who ignored everything else in the house. Or your flatmate came home, ate the pie, and the dog brought mud in from the garden: that covers everything, but it needs two separate events where the dog story needs one, and your flatmate is away.

Both fit. Neither fits as much of the evidence, as simply, or as well with what else you know. That comparison is the argument. The dog is only its conclusion.
:::

The word to underline is *best*. Not "an explanation", not "the explanation that fits", but the best of the rivals. The dog wins because it explains more of the evidence, with fewer separate assumptions, and fits what else you know: the flatmate's away, and the dog has form.

The guilty look is worth nothing, by the way. Dogs produce it for a person who comes home cross, whether or not they did anything, so it fits every rival equally and discriminates between none of them. Strike it out and the dog still wins on the prints, the pie and the flatmate's absence. Evidence that every explanation predicts is evidence for none of them.

Those are the three questions this lesson will use of any explanation offered as an argument, and they are the lesson's own statement of the test rather than Harman's: does it explain more of the evidence than its rivals, does it do so more simply (fewer extra assumptions, fewer coincidences), and does it fit what else you already know? Fitting the facts is not one of them, because an explanation that fits is always available. So the first move against a proposed best explanation is to ask what else would explain the same facts, and the second is to find the observation that one explanation predicts and the other doesn't. The dog story predicts pie crumbs in the basket. Go and look.

:::checkpoint You've concluded the dog ate the pie. Name one thing you could learn tomorrow that would defeat that conclusion without making any of your three facts false.
There are plenty, and being able to name one is the test of whether you understood the argument. Your flatmate's flight was cancelled and she came home after all. A neighbour says she found the back door open. The dog hasn't been able to get onto the counter since his hip went. None of those touches the prints, the missing pie or the look on the dog. All three of your facts stay true and the conclusion goes anyway.

That's defeasibility again, and it's why an inference to the best explanation is inductive: best of what you thought of is not best of all, and a new rival can arrive at any time. Diagnosis of any kind runs on this argument, and what separates the good diagnosticians is how long they keep asking "what else would produce this?" after an answer has arrived.

Whether inference to the best explanation is really a form of its own, or a compressed way of doing what lesson 6 does with probabilities, is argued over. What nobody disputes is that the pattern is everywhere and that it has tests.
:::

## The conductive argument: reasons on both sides

One more shape, and it's the one a decision usually arrives in: reasons on both sides, and a judgement. The informal-logic literature calls it the conductive argument, the third of Groarke's four kinds: several independent reasons support a conclusion and are weighed against counter-considerations that pull the other way.[3] Govier's textbook has the standard chapter on it.[5]

Say you're deciding whether to take a job in another city. For: the pay is a third higher; the work is what you trained for; the city has better transport. Against: you'd be four hours from your parents; your partner would need to find work; you like where you live.

Now do the weighing where it can be seen, because that's the only part anyone else can check. The pay and the training pull the same way and don't depend on each other, so they add. Against them, your partner's work is the heaviest counter-consideration, because it's the one that can turn a third more pay into less money coming in. The distance from your parents is real, and how heavy it is depends on how often you'd actually travel. Liking where you live is genuine and it's the one that fades. My verdict on the reasons as stated: take the job if your partner can work there, and don't if they can't. That's what a conductive verdict looks like. Not a score, but a statement of which reason is carrying the weight and what would flip it.

Two things make this different from the arguments earlier in the lesson. First, the reasons are independent. In the deductive arguments of lessons 2 to 4 the premises work together (lesson 7 will call this linked support), and if one falls the argument can collapse. Here, if you learn the pay is only a fifth higher, the other reasons still stand and the weighing shifts a little. Second, the counter-considerations are part of the argument. Stating them isn't a concession of defeat; it's what an honest weighing looks like. An argument for the move that pretends there's nothing on the other side is a weaker argument to a careful reader, not a stronger one, because the reader can see the other side and will conclude you either couldn't see it or hoped they wouldn't.

:::checkpoint One friend argues for taking the city job and gives you four reasons for it and nothing against. A second gives three reasons for it and two against, and still says take it. Whose argument should you trust more, and what does that tell you about counter-considerations?
The second one's. The first friend has either not looked at the other side or has decided not to show it to you, and since you can see the other side yourself, the omission costs them your trust instead of winning your agreement. The second has told you what the decision costs and thinks it's worth paying, which is a claim you can check reason by reason. Stating the counter-considerations isn't a concession in a conductive argument. It's the part that makes the weighing something a reader can examine.
:::

How do you evaluate a conductive argument? You check each reason on its own (is it true, and does it bear on the conclusion?), you check that the counter-considerations are stated at their real weight, and then you ask whether the weighing is one a reasonable person could make. There's no formula that settles that last step for you. Decision theory has ways of making a weighing explicit, and Probability and Decisions will teach them; they help most when the reasons can be put in comparable units, and most practical reasons can't be. What you can always do is the weighing in the open, where the reasons and the counter-considerations can each be checked. Lesson 10 will have you write an argument in this form.

## What people get wrong

**"Induction is the weak kind of reasoning."** It's the kind that reaches beyond what you already have. A valid deductive argument adds no premises: everything it delivers was already entailed by what you put in, though nobody thinks that makes it uninformative, and a great deal of mathematics consists of finding out what one's own assumptions entailed all along. What deduction can't do is take you to a claim the premises don't already entail. That's the move induction makes, and the risk it runs. Every forecast, every diagnosis, every "the bridge will hold" is inductive. Calling it weak is like calling your legs weak because they're not wheels.

**"A strong argument that turned out wrong was a bad argument."** No. Strength is about the premises and the link at the time, not the outcome. A doctor who prescribes on the best evidence and sees the patient react badly did not reason badly; the argument was strong and defeasible, and it was defeated. You'll want to say the same about your own past decisions, and you're entitled to, provided the argument really was strong on what you knew.

**"Correlation isn't causation" as the end of the discussion.** It's the beginning. Mill's fifth canon says that when two things really vary together they're causally connected in some manner; the work is ruling out chance and then deciding which of the other three readings it is.[6] The slogan does the first half of the job. The four readings are the second half.

**"More similarities make a better analogy."** Relevant similarities do. Irrelevant ones are decoration, and a single relevant difference outweighs any number of them.

**"A single case can't prove anything."** One well-chosen comparison can. Mill's man shot through the heart is one comparison, not one case, and it settles the question without a sample of shootings, because everything else was held constant. What a single case can't do is support a generalisation, which is a different job. Ask which job the case is being asked to do.

**"If it's not deductive, anything goes."** The whole lesson is the reply. Inductive arguments have tests as definite as validity, just not as tidy: coverage, relevance, the four readings, the rivals. "It's only probable" is not a licence; it's a claim about degree, and the degree can be checked.

:::callout The older question underneath
There's a question under all of this, and I'm not going to pretend it's settled. Hume argued that any defence of induction has to use induction, so the whole practice rests on something we can't argue for without going in a circle. Popper's answer was that there's no inductive logic at all, and that science works by conjecture and refutation rather than by piling up confirmations; others think the probability machinery of lesson 6 is the answer. *forall x* puts the practical half of it well: to rebut an argument "you can show that (one or more of) the premises are not true, or you can show that the argument is not valid. Logic, however, will only help you with the latter!"[8] The tests in this lesson are the ones working scientists, doctors and juries actually apply, and they're worth having whichever way that older argument goes.
:::

## Practice

Nine arguments. For each, decide first what kind of link is being claimed (deductive, or one of the inductive families, or conductive), then give a verdict: valid or invalid for the deductive ones, strong or middling or weak for the rest, with the reason named. Three of the nine are deductive look-alikes from lessons 2 to 4, and none of them announces itself, so don't reach for "strong or weak" until you've asked which kind of claim is being made. For every one you judge weak or invalid, write the reason in a line. Write your nine verdicts before opening the answers.

1. Every swan anyone in Europe had ever seen was white. So all swans are white.
2. The council hasn't closed the footbridge, and they close any bridge they find unsafe. So it's safe.
3. My sister and I both got food poisoning after Tuesday's dinner. We ate the same fish and nothing else in common that day; nobody else at the table had the fish, and nobody else was ill. So the fish did it.
4. This flat is like the one I rented last year: same landlord, same street, same size. That one had damp. So this one probably has damp.
5. We shortlisted every applicant who had a degree. Of the people we shortlisted, some were interviewed. So some applicants with degrees were interviewed.
6. Since the new manager arrived, the shop's takings have fallen for three months. So the new manager is the cause.
7. The only thing that drops the pressure gauge below one bar is a failure in the boiler itself. The gauge is below one bar this morning. So the boiler has failed.
8. The engine warning light is on, the car is losing power on hills, and there's a faint smell of burning. The best explanation is a slipping clutch, so that's what it is.
9. I should take the evening course: it's what I need for promotion, it's free through work, and it runs near home. On the other hand, it's two nights a week for a year and I'd miss the children's bedtime. On balance, I'll take it.

:::checkpoint Check your nine verdicts.
1. Inductive generalisation, and the verdict turns on something not stated. The sample is enormous and it covers one continent, while the conclusion is about swans everywhere, so the question is Weston's third rule: what would you expect about how much this property varies? If you had reason to think plumage doesn't vary by continent, the argument was strong and was then defeated, which is what defeasibility looks like from the inside. If you had no such reason, the sample was never representative of the population in the conclusion and the argument was weak all along, and no number of further European swans would have fixed it. Europeans mostly hadn't asked. Note the "all" as well: the conclusion is refuted by one case, because it's an "all" claim, even though the argument for it was inductive.

2. Deductive look-alike, and valid: it's modus tollens (if unsafe then closed; not closed; so not unsafe). Step 5 is where it fails if it fails: is the second premise true? Councils don't always close unsafe bridges. Valid, probably unsound.

3. Causal, and strong. It's really Mill's third canon, the joint method: the two who were ill had only the fish in common, and the ones who skipped the fish were fine, so both halves are doing work. Not certain (they could share a stomach bug from elsewhere), but the design is right.

4. Analogy. Middling. Same landlord and same street are relevant to damp (maintenance habits; the local building stock); same size isn't. It's strong only if damp in the old flat was a landlord-and-building problem rather than a one-off. Ask about the cause of the old damp before trusting the analogy.

5. Deductive look-alike, and invalid. Draw the circles from lesson 4: the shortlisted people who were interviewed might all be shortlisted applicants without degrees. Undistributed middle.

6. Causal, post hoc, and weak as stated. Three months is a short series, and nothing else has been ruled out: the season, a new competitor, roadworks outside. Reading 2 is live and is the one people miss here: the manager may have been sent in *because* takings were already falling, so the fall caused the manager rather than the other way round. Reading 3 is live too: a new shopping centre a street away would pull customers off and prompt the owner to bring someone new, and would show up as both. What else changed?

7. Deductive look-alike, and valid. It reads like a diagnosis, which is why it sits next to item 8, but the first premise claims that nothing else can drop the gauge, so the conclusion is guaranteed rather than made probable. The link holds. Everything here rides on step 5: is that first premise true? A leaking radiator drops the pressure too, and if it does, the argument is unsound, not invalid. Notice what one word changes. If the arguer had said "usually the only thing", the claim about the link changes with it, and you'd judge it as an inference to the best explanation, by the rivals, which is item 8's job.

8. Inference to the best explanation, and weak as stated, because no rivals were considered. A slipping clutch explains the power loss and the smell; does it explain the warning light? Several other faults explain all three. It's a fair hypothesis and a poor argument until the rivals are named and something tells them apart.

9. Conductive, and reasonably strong: three independent reasons, two counter-considerations stated at their real weight, and a weighing in the open. You can disagree with the weighing; you can't say the argument hid anything.
:::

:::exercise On paper, with a causal claim you met this week
Go to the folder of arguments you've been keeping since lesson 1, or to something you read or heard this week, and find one causal claim: X causes Y, X improved Y, X is why Y happened. Then:

1. Write it in standard form, with the missing premise in brackets. In a causal argument the missing premise is the "nothing else changed" clause; write it down as the arguer would have to accept it.
2. Write the four readings: X causes Y; Y causes X; some C causes both; chance. For each, write one sentence on whether it's live in this case, and if it is, what C might be.
3. Name the single observation or comparison that would rule out the most. In most cases that will be a method-of-difference comparison: a case with everything the same and X absent. Say what that case would be, and whether anyone could actually get it.
4. Give your verdict: strong, weak, or "can't tell without the comparison in step 3", and put the sheet in your folder.
5. Last, write one thing you could learn tomorrow that would weaken the argument without making any of its premises false. That's its defeater. If you can't name one, either the argument is deductive after all or you haven't finished understanding it.
:::

Aim for what makes an inductive argument strong and what makes it cogent, what defeasibility means and the coin example in one line, the two separate things that can be wrong with a sample, Weston's three rules, the one test for an analogy, Mill's two canons in your own words, the four readings of a correlation, the three questions to ask of a best explanation, and what makes a conductive argument strong, including why stating the reasons on the other side makes it stronger rather than weaker. ## Connections

Step 4 of the check now has two halves. If the link is claimed to be certain, ask whether it's valid, with the tools from lessons 2 to 4. If the link is claimed to be probable, ask whether it's strong, with the tools from this lesson. The first question you now ask of any argument is which kind of claim it's making, because the wrong test gives a confident wrong answer: run a strong inductive argument through the validity test and it comes back invalid every time, which tells you nothing about the argument and everything about the test.

Lesson 6 gives strength a number. When you asked how much a thousand heads should move you, or how well a sample covers a population, you were asking a question probability answers, and the base rates, conjunction rule and likelihood ratio of the next lesson are the arithmetic behind "coverage".

Lesson 7 returns to conductive arguments when it separates linked from convergent support, and lesson 10 has you write one. Lesson 8 takes up the causal patterns under their fallacy names (false cause, hasty generalisation, weak analogy) and the reason those names are hypotheses, not verdicts: as you've seen, each one has a strong cousin with the same shape. And lesson 9 puts the sampling idea to work on the news, including the case that adds the fifth reading: a pattern that is an artefact of how the cases got into your data.

Two other courses will own parts of this ground properly when they are published. Statistics for Citizens will teach sampling, margins, and how large "large enough" is. The Scientific Method will teach the design of experiments, which is Mill's method of difference made rigorous. This lesson is meant to let you read both with the logic already in hand.

## Sources

1. Van Cleave, M. J., *Introduction to Logic and Critical Thinking*, 2nd ed. (Lansing Community College; CC BY 4.0), chapter 1, section 1.8 "Deductive vs. inductive arguments", on LibreTexts. A deductive argument's conclusion "is supposed to follow from its premises with absolute certainty, thus leaving no possibility that the conclusion doesn't follow from the premises"; an inductive argument's "with a high level of probability"; inductive arguments are defeasible, since adding a premise can overturn the verdict. See the note in `research/SOURCES.md`: these pages could not be fetched from the drafting session and the wording was taken from cross-checked search extracts, so this entry is one grade weaker than "read from the source". The word "cogent" is not recorded from Van Cleave and is used here as field vocabulary.
2. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Inductive Logic" (rev. Feb 2025). A good inductive argument's premises "support the truth of its conclusion to some appropriate degree"; degree of support as the core notion; the report that Carnap's programme of a purely logical confirmation function is now generally regarded as unsuccessful.
3. Groarke, L., "Informal Logic", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*. The four argument kinds: deductive, inductive, conductive, and abductive.
4. Weston, A., *A Rulebook for Arguments*, 5th ed. (Hackett, 2018), chapter II, "Arguments by Example", rules 7 to 9: "Use more than one example", "Use representative examples", "Background rates are often crucial". The rule numbers and titles were read from the publisher's contents and rule pages; the chapter itself has not been read, and no claim is made here about its argument beyond the three rule titles.
5. Govier, T., *A Practical Study of Argument*, 7th ed. enhanced (Cengage, 2013), chapter 12, "Conductive Arguments and Counterconsiderations". Cited for the existence of the standard chapter only. No page of Govier has been read by this course, so nothing is attributed to its contents; the description of conductive arguments in this lesson comes from source 3.
6. Mill, J. S., *A System of Logic* (1843), Book III, ch. 8, "Of the Four Methods of Experimental Inquiry", in the modernised text by Jonathan Bennett (earlymoderntexts.com, 2017), pp. 191 to 199 of that edition. Read from the PDF at draft time. The man shot through the heart, including Bennett's bracketed insertion; "almost all the inductive conclusions we draw in daily life"; the method of difference as "more particularly a method of artificial experiment" and agreement as "more especially what we use when experimentation is impossible"; the fifth canon on concomitant variation as quoted in full; and the sentence following it, beginning "I add that last clause because", on two effects of a common cause. The two fragments of the second canon are quoted as `SOURCES.md` records them; the connecting words are this lesson's, because the full sentence has not been re-keyed from the PDF. Mill's own example for concomitant variation is not recorded and none is given here.
7. Harman, G., "The Inference to the Best Explanation", *Philosophical Review* 74(1), 1965, 88 to 95. Names and defends the inference: from a set of facts, infer the hypothesis that would best explain them, as a basic form of non-deductive reasoning. Cited for the name and for that defence. How Harman relates this inference to enumerative induction is the argument of the paper and is not summarised here. The three criteria the lesson uses are its own statement of the test and are not taken from Harman.
8. Magnus, P. D., Button, T., Trueman, R., Zach, R. et al., *forall x: Calgary* (Fall 2025, CC BY 4.0), chapter 2. To rebut an argument "you can show that (one or more of) the premises are not true, or you can show that the argument is not valid. Logic, however, will only help you with the latter!"
