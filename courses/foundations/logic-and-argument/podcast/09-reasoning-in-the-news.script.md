S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Logic and Argument, lesson nine. Bad reasoning in the news and everyday life.

S1: It opens in October twenty fifteen, when the World Health Organization's cancer agency put out a press release. It classified processed meat as carcinogenic to humans, group one, put red meat a rung down at probably carcinogenic, and said each fifty gram portion of processed meat eaten daily increases the risk of colorectal cancer by eighteen percent.

S2: Of those four statements, one travelled.

S1: The release said one more thing in the same breath, quoting the head of its monographs programme. For an individual, the risk of developing colorectal cancer because of their consumption of processed meat remains small, but this risk increases with the amount of meat consumed.

S2: Group one is the same group as tobacco, though.

S1: And look at what that pair of sentences tells you about the label. A classification that puts something in the top group and calls the individual risk small cannot be a statement about how much harm the thing does. It's a statement about how sure the evidence is that the thing causes cancer at all. Nobody was saying a rasher is a cigarette.

S1: Now that eighteen percent. It's a relative risk. A ratio between two rates. What you want is the absolute risk: how many people in a group this happens to.

S2: So how many more cases per thousand?

S1: Cancer Research UK worked it out the same day. About sixty one people in every thousand in the UK develop bowel cancer. Among a thousand who eat the least processed meat, about fifty six do, and among a thousand who eat the most, about sixty six.

S2: Ten more per thousand. Real, and not the risk from smoking.

S2: So how should I read a news story?

S1: As an argument with the premises squeezed out. Bacon causes cancer is a conclusion. The premises are a study, a statistic and a source, and the source stands in for the warrant, the unstated step from lesson seven that licenses you to move from, the agency says, to, it's true. Often you never see the study.

S2: And the six step check from lesson one still applies.

S1: Still applies, in the same order. This lesson adds five questions that fit the compressed form. Three for the source: who says, how do they know, who disagrees. Two for the number: share of what, compared with what.

S2: Start with the source. Was the bacon argument bad?

S1: The argument was fine. The agency is an expert body in cancer research, whether processed meat causes cancer is a claim in cancer research, and the agency asserts that it does. That's an appeal to authority of the good kind. What went wrong happened after it: the conclusion got repeated with a size attached that nobody had claimed.

S2:

S1: Which is the shape of most news failures. The source is sound and the sentence about the source isn't.

S2: Where do the three questions come from?

S1: Anthony Weston's Rulebook for Arguments. Rule thirteen, cite your sources, because a reader has to be able to go and check. Rule fourteen, seek informed sources: sources must be qualified to make the statements they make. For the best information about global climate change, go to climatologists, not politicians. Rule fifteen, seek impartial sources: people who have the most at stake in a dispute are usually not the best sources of information about the issues involved. And he adds the part people miss, that honesty isn't enough, because the truth as one honestly sees it can still be biased. We tend to see what we expect to see.

S2: So what does an interested source actually do?

S1: It doesn't usually lie. It notices and passes on what fits what it expects. So the question to ask of a manufacturer's press release is rarely, is this false. It's, what is this not telling me.

S2: Take a case. A drug company reports its own trial and every number in it is true.

S1: Then rule sixteen, cross check. And rule fifteen licenses, what is this not telling me, not, is this false. Look for the missing half: the trials that didn't report, the harms given in a different unit from the benefits.

S2: And in the news, which of lesson eight's Walton questions does the work?

S1: The field question and the opinion question. A physicist on diet, a surgeon on economics: the qualification is real and it's in something else. And run the bacon argument through all six and it passes every one, which is the uncomfortable part. An appeal to authority can pass all six and still be repeated as something the authority never said.

S2: There's one source you haven't mentioned.

S1: The one you trust most. Your own experience, which nobody cross checks. A sample of one. Run the same three questions on it.

S2: Now the numbers. Share of what.

S1: In October nineteen ninety five the UK Committee on Safety of Medicines warned that third generation oral contraceptive pills raised the risk of blood clots twofold, that is, by a hundred percent.

S2: How many extra cases was that?

S1: Per seven thousand women, one. The studies had shown that of every seven thousand women on the earlier, second generation pills, about one had a thrombosis, and this increased to two among women on third generation pills.

S2: So the absolute increase was one in seven thousand, and the relative increase really was a hundred percent. Both true, and only one of them tells a woman what she needs to know.

S1: And the consequences are documented. Women stopped taking the pill. Gigerenzer and colleagues report an estimate of thirteen thousand additional abortions in the following year in England and Wales, one extra birth for every extra abortion, and about eight hundred additional conceptions among girls under sixteen. Their conclusion: had the committee and the media reported the absolute risks, few women would have panicked and stopped taking the pill.

S2: Is the lesson saying the warning was wrong?

S1: It says explicitly that it isn't. The studies did find a difference between the two generations of pill, and nineteen ninety five is where these figures come from and where they stay. And it isn't advice about any medicine. As lesson six said for the whole course, this is education, not personal medical advice.

S2: So why does a relative risk hide the base?

S1: Because a ratio throws the base away, and that isn't a trick, it's arithmetic. Divide two in seven thousand by one in seven thousand and the seven thousand cancels, which is why a relative risk is so easy to headline. One number, no denominator. But doubles the risk of something that happens to one in seven thousand means one extra case in seven thousand, and doubles the risk of something that happens to one in ten means one extra case in ten. The word doubles can't tell you which.

S2: So how do I put the base back?

S1: By counting, which is lesson six's method. A round number of people, apply the base rate, apply the change, compare the counts. Counting forces the denominator back onto the page.

S2: Do the bacon one that way.

S1: Fifty six in a thousand at the bottom of the range. Raise fifty six by eighteen percent and you get about sixty six, which is what Cancer Research UK published the same day. Ten more in a thousand. So the honest sentence is, ten more people in every thousand at the top of the range, not, bacon causes cancer. And their sixty six came from their own data, not from that sum.

S1: From fifty six and sixty six you can always recover the ratio. From the ratio alone you can never recover fifty six and sixty six. Which is why the ratio is the number that gets printed and the counts are the numbers you have to go and find.
