S1: The medical form, posed to physicians by David Eddy in nineteen eighty two. A woman has a routine mammogram. In the population being screened, one percent of women have breast cancer. What is the probability she has cancer?

S2: I want to say something near eighty percent.

S1: Gigerenzer and Hoffrage report that ninety five out of a hundred physicians estimated it at between seventy and eighty percent. The answer is about eight in a hundred, so they were off by a factor of ten, in the direction that turns a positive screen into a diagnosis.

S1: Take a thousand screened. One percent have cancer, so ten women, and nine hundred and ninety do not. Of the ten with cancer, the test is positive for eighty percent, so eight. Of the nine hundred and ninety without, it's positive for nine point six percent, so about ninety five.

S1: A hundred and three positives in all, of which eight have cancer. Eight over a hundred and three is seven point eight percent.

Seventy to eighty percent is what you get if you look only at the test.

S1: Because the evidence has to be judged against every explanation, and this number judges it against one. Here's the society's own framing, and it's the sentence to memorise. The jury needs to weigh up two competing explanations for the babies' deaths: cot death or murder. Two deaths by cot death or two murders are each quite unlikely, but one has apparently happened in this case. What matters is the relative likelihood of the deaths under each explanation, not just how unlikely they are under one explanation.

S1: A number on its own can't be unlikely. It can only be unlikely compared with something. The Court of Appeal put it plainly. The one in seventy three million figure is no help. It is merely a distraction.

S2: Last thing. Is there a measure of how much evidence should move me?

S1: The likelihood ratio, and you've now seen the comparison twice. How likely is this evidence if the claim is true, against how likely if it's false. Go back to the mammogram.
