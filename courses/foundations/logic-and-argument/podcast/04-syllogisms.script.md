S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Logic and Argument, lesson four. All, no, some: checking a syllogism in two minutes.

S1: And it opens in a lift. Someone at work says, everyone who gets promoted here has done the leadership course. Everyone on my team has done the course. So everyone on my team is going to get promoted.

S2: Something's wrong with that.

S1: You can probably feel it. But feeling it isn't checking it, and if the conclusion had been one you liked, you might not have felt it at all. Lesson two gave you the counterexample method, which takes imagination, and imagination runs out when the argument is about something you care about. This lesson gives you the same test done with a pencil. You draw three circles, draw the premises, and look. If the conclusion is already on the page, the argument is valid. If not, the gap on the page is your counterexample.

S2: Which is the part I can't do in an episode.

S1: The pictures are in the lesson. What we can do here is everything that has to happen before you pick the pencil up.

S2: Start with the translation, then.

S1: Everything in this lesson is built from four sentence shapes. All A are B. No A are B. Some A are B. Some A are not B. Aristotle worked out the logic of those four in the Prior Analytics in the fourth century BC, and the circles are a much later picture of his system.

S2: And everyday sentences don't arrive in those shapes.

S1: So the first skill is translation, and there are five traps. Bare plurals mean all. Dogs are mammals is, all dogs are mammals. Ground floor flats flood is a claim about all of them, and the speaker will retreat to, well, most of them, the moment you find one that didn't.

S2: And that retreat matters?

S1: Most isn't one of the four forms. An argument built on most is an inductive argument, which is lesson five's business.

S2: Second trap.

S1: Not all is not none. Not all the trains were late means, some trains were not late. It does not mean, no trains were late. That one starts more bad rebuttals than any other.

S2: Third.

S1: All A are not B is ambiguous in English. All the shops are not open usually means some are shut, but sometimes means none are open. Ask which. Don't guess. Fourth, only reverses the direction. Only members may park here means, all people who may park here are members. It does not mean all members may park here.

S2: That's the only if point from lesson three.

S1: Only tells you what's necessary, not what's sufficient. And fifth, some means at least one.

S2: Try me. Only ticket holders will be admitted.

S1: All people who will be admitted are ticket holders. And it does not tell you that every ticket holder will be admitted. The venue could still turn one away for being drunk, and the sign would stand.

S2: Now, some. Because I think that's the one that starts fights.

S1: In logic, some A are B means at least one A is B. That's all. So, some of the students passed, is true if one passed, true if half did, and true if every single one did.

S2: That last one still feels wrong.

S1: And the lesson explains why. In conversation, if you knew everyone passed you'd say so, and saying some when you could have said all invites the listener to conclude you couldn't say all. Linguists call that a conversational implicature. Something the listener infers from your choice of words, not something the words assert.

S2: How do you know it isn't part of the meaning?

S1: It's cancellable. Some passed, in fact all of them did, is not a contradiction. That's the test. And for checking arguments you use the logical reading, because if an argument only works when some is read as some but not all, it's leaning on something the premise never said.

S2: Give me the case where that bites.

S1: Minutes of a meeting record, some members of the committee voted for the proposal. A year later a member insists the vote wasn't unanimous and points at that line as proof.

S2: And it isn't proof.

S1: The line is true if three voted for and just as true if every member did. The non unanimity may well be real, but it needs its own evidence. A recorded vote against, an abstention. That sentence doesn't carry it.

S2: Right. How do I deny each of the four? Because I suspect that's where I go wrong.

S1: Every argument you check will eventually be argued about, and most of those go wrong in the first minute because someone denies the wrong thing. Two sentences are contradictories when exactly one is true: they can't both be true and can't both be false. All A are B is contradicted by, some A are not B. No A are B is contradicted by, some A are B.

S2: And read backwards.

S1: Some A are B is contradicted by, no A are B. Some A are not B by, all A are B. And notice what isn't on that list. All A are B and no A are B are not contradictories. They can both be false at once, whenever some As are B and some aren't. Logicians call that pair contraries.

S2: So if someone says all the trains were late and I say, no they weren't, none of them were.

S1: You've taken on a much bigger claim than you needed, and you're probably wrong too. The practical rule is this. To knock down an all, find one exception. To knock down a no, find one case. To knock down a some, you have to show there are none at all.

S2: Which is why some claims are easy to make and hard to refute.

S1: And there's a trap there. A colleague says, not all the invoices went out late, and you think they're wrong. What would you have to show?

S2: That every invoice went out late.

S1: Every one. Because not all went out late is, some invoices did not go out late, and the contradictory of a some not is the all. One counterexample won't do. A single late invoice is exactly what their claim allows.

S2: All right. The dog argument.

S1: Every logic teacher uses it because it catches everyone. All dogs are mammals. All cats are mammals. Therefore all dogs are cats.

S2: Obviously wrong. But say why in a way that would still be wrong if I believed the conclusion.

S1: Both premises put things inside the mammal circle. Neither says anything about the relationship between dogs and cats directly. Two groups that both sit inside a third can overlap completely, partly, or not at all, and the premises don't say which.

S2: And drawn?

S1: You shade the part of Dogs outside Mammals, and the part of Cats outside Mammals. Then look at what the conclusion needs, which is the whole of Dogs outside Cats shaded. Half of it is. The other half, dogs that are mammals and aren't cats, is untouched. Sitting there, open.

S2: And that open region is the counterexample.

S1: It's a dog that's a mammal and isn't a cat. Which is to say, a dog. Textbooks call this the fallacy of the undistributed middle: the middle term, mammals, is never used to say something about all mammals, so it can't connect the other two.

S2: And the lift argument is the same shape.

S1: Everyone promoted did the course, everyone on my team did the course, so everyone on my team gets promoted. Same three circles, same two shadings, same open region: a person who did the course and isn't promoted. Invalid in exactly the way the dog argument is, and it takes thirty seconds.

S2: What if the colleague says, but everyone promoted here really has done the course, I checked?

S1: They've defended a premise, which is step five. The fault was at step four. Even with both premises true, the conclusion doesn't follow.

It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Go and draw one.
