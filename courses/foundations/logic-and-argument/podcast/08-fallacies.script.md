S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Logic and Argument, lesson eight. Fallacies: the names, the patterns, and why the experts warn you about the names.

S1: It opens in January two thousand and seven, with a ruling by the UK's Advertising Standards Authority on a poster. More than eighty percent of dentists recommend Colgate.

S2: And the ruling went against them.

S1: Two members of the public complained, and the survey behind the poster turned out to have let each dentist recommend several brands at once. Enough did that a competitor's brand was recommended almost as much, and nobody had asked them to pick one.

S2: So dentists aren't experts on toothpaste?

S1: That's exactly what the ruling did not say. The argument was, experts say X, so X, and the trouble was with what the experts said.

S2: It said the poster claimed the dentists asserted something they hadn't. And that's the lesson in one case.

S1: You're about to learn the standard names for bad arguments, and one of them, appeal to authority, fits that poster. But appeal to authority also fits every time you take a doctor's word about a dose or a mechanic's word about a brake pad, and those arguments are good. The name tells you the shape. It doesn't tell you the verdict.

S2: Where does the list come from? And give me a definition of fallacy I can hold.

S1: Aristotle wrote the first list, in On Sophistical Refutations, and most of what you'll meet online is a lightly edited version of it. Which matters, because it was built for the formal debating contests of his Athens. As for the definition, take the Stanford Encyclopedia's, and keep the word seems. A fallacy is an argument that seems to be better than it is. So a fallacy isn't a bad argument. It's a bad argument with a good disguise, and the disguise is usually that it looks like a good argument you've seen before.

S2: The lesson sorts them into five groups, and for each one it gives a twin.

S1: The cogent twin. The good argument with the same surface shape, and it's the important half.

S2: Learn only the names and you'll spend your life convicting good arguments. Work one of the twins.

S1: Appeal to ignorance is, it's unproved, therefore not true. Its twin is, it's unproved after a search that would have found it, so probably not.

S2: So what's the general point?

S1: Nearly every pattern on that list is also the shape of a legitimate argument. Appealing to experts is how nearly all your knowledge arrived. You didn't personally verify the boiling point of water. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy says it plainly: most reasoning of this kind is not fallacious, and much of our knowledge properly comes from listening to authorities.

S2: So the name can't be the verdict.

S1: What decides is whether this instance is the good version or the bad one, and that's a question about content and context, which is what the six step check tests. Douglas Walton did more than anyone to make this precise. He called each pattern an argumentation scheme and attached to each a short list of critical questions.

S2: Give me the questions for appeal to expert opinion.

S1: Six. Expertise: how credible is the expert? Field: are they an expert in the field the claim is in? Opinion: what did they assert? Trustworthiness: is the expert personally reliable, or is there a payment or a stake? Consistency: is this consistent with what other experts say? And backup evidence: is the assertion based on evidence?

S2: Run the poster through them.

S1: Expertise, dentists, fine. Field, toothpaste, fine. Opinion is where it falls over. The dentists asserted, I'd recommend Colgate among others, and the poster asserted, dentists recommend Colgate over other brands. Trustworthiness: the survey was commissioned by Colgate, and the ruling found the survey's scripts hadn't made clear to the dentists that the research was for Colgate and could be used in advertising, which is a reason to look closely rather than a conviction. Consistency: another brand scored almost as high.

S2: So it fails on question three.

S1: Which is more than, it's an appeal to authority, tells you. You know which question it fails, so you know what a fixed poster would have to say.

S2: Lesson six gave us Sally Clark.

S1: And that case contains the cleanest failed appeal to authority I know of. The Royal Statistical Society's statement says a medical expert witness drew on published studies to obtain a figure for the frequency of cot death in families with some of the characteristics of the defendant's, and squared it to get one in seventy three million for two cot deaths in one family.

S2: Run the six questions. Expertise, he was a real expert.

S1: Question one passes. Question three passes: he did assert it.

S2: So it's the field.

S1: He was a medical expert. The claim was a statistical one, about whether two events within a family are independent, and on that question he wasn't an expert at all. The Society's statement ends by making exactly that point: statistics remains a specialised area, and it urges the courts to ensure that statistical evidence is presented only by appropriately qualified statistical experts, as would be the case for any other form of expert evidence.

S2: And question six?

S1: Partly fails too. The independence assumption had no evidence behind it, and the Society noted very strong a priori reasons for supposing it false.

S2: Here's what I like about that. The Society's own statement is an appeal to authority.

S1: And a cogent one. Run the questions on them. Expertise, yes. Field, yes, a statistical society talking about statistics. Opinion, quoted in full. Same shape, fallacious in the witness box and cogent in the press release, and the difference is visible only when you ask the questions.

S2: Last thing. Ad hominem, which everybody thinks they know.

S1: Two arguments with the same shape: don't accept what this person says, because of something about the person. Richard Green was the main prosecution witness in an Alaska burglary trial. He identified the defendant, Davis, near a stolen safe. At the time Green was himself on juvenile probation for burgling two cabins, and the defence wanted to tell the jury that, to argue he had a motive to point at someone else quickly. The trial judge barred any mention of it.

S2: And the second?

S1: Hypothetical, and the lesson says so rather than inventing a case. A plumber tells you your boiler's heat exchanger is cracked. You later learn she was rude to a neighbour and once had a parking fine, and you conclude the boiler is fine.

S2: The plumber one is the fallacy.

S1: And the difference is what the claim rests on. Green's claim, that's the man I saw, rests entirely on his word. There's no other route to it. So anything bearing on whether his word is reliable is relevant. The heat exchanger rests on a fact you can check, and on her expertise, not her manners.

S2: What happened in the Alaska case?

S1: The US Supreme Court agreed with the defence, seven to two, in Davis against Alaska in nineteen seventy four. Chief Justice Burger wrote that the exposure of a witness's motivation in testifying is a proper and important function of the constitutionally protected right of cross examination, and drew the line this lesson turns on. A general attack on a witness's character is one thing. A more particular attack on the witness's credibility is effected by means of cross examination directed toward revealing possible biases, prejudices, or ulterior motives.

S2: So the good ad hominem is a court's idea of a constitutional right.

S1: And Walton's thesis, after a book length study, is that no ad hominem argument is inherently fallacious. Ask what the claim rests on. If it rests on the person, the person is fair game.
