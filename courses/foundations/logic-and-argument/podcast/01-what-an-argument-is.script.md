S1: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm John.

S2: And I'm Haley. Today we're starting the Logic and Argument course, on the first lesson. What an argument is, and how to find the missing step.

S1: And it starts with a text message. Sunday night, a friend texts you: take the train tomorrow, the M6 is shut at junction ten. Two sentences, and you're already deciding whether to agree.

S2: That's an argument? It's a text about traffic.

S1: It's an argument, and here's the interesting part. You just did something without noticing. You supplied a step your friend never wrote. Something like, if the M6 is shut, driving will take much longer.

S2: Which they didn't say.

S1: Which they didn't say. And if that step is wrong, the advice is wrong. Suppose the closure is northbound and you're going south. Your friend never said the thing that was wrong.

S2: Okay. So what counts as an argument, properly?

S1: A set of statements where some of them, the premises, are offered as reasons for another one, the conclusion. Not a quarrel. Just reasons offered for a claim, however calmly.

S2: How old is that definition?

S1: Aristotle, fourth century BC. He wrote that a syllogism, which is his word for a deduction, is discourse in which, certain things being stated, something other than what is stated follows of necessity from their being so.

S2: Say that again slowly, because I lost it.

S1: Certain things stated, those are your premises. Something other, that's the conclusion. Follows from their being so, that's the link. Nearly twenty four centuries later, same three parts.

S2: And the lesson gives you a method.

S1: Six steps. Find the conclusion. Find the premises. Supply what's missing. Test the link. Test the premises. And then, only then, step six, consult your opinion of the conclusion.

S2: Step six is last.

S1: On purpose. Most people start there. They decide whether they like the conclusion, and then go looking for reasons to have been right.

S2: Ouch.

S1: The check makes you earn your opinion. This lesson only does the first three steps.

S2: Then let's do step one. How do I find the conclusion?

S1: Ask what the passage is trying to get you to accept. That's the whole test. Often it's the sentence you'd push back on if you disagreed.

S2: I was taught it comes at the end.

S1: It does in textbook examples, which is where the habit comes from. In real writing it's often first. People state the claim and then defend it.

S2: Alright, I want to push on something. You said an argument is reasons offered for a claim. What about this. The train was late because a tree came down on the line near Crewe.

S1: That's the one that fools nearly everyone. That's an explanation, not an argument.

S2: Because of the word because?

S1: Never because of the word. The test is direction. An argument runs from what's offered to what's claimed. Here are reasons, therefore accept this. An explanation runs the other way. This is already accepted, and here's why it happened.

S2: So nobody's trying to convince me the train was late.

S1: You were on it. Compare it with, you should go to bed early because the exam is at nine. That's trying to establish something you might disagree with.

S2: Give me a hard one.

S1: Our sales fell in March because the new website is harder to use.

S2: That could be either.

S1: It could, and that's the lesson. If everyone agrees sales fell and the speaker is telling you why, explanation. If the speaker is trying to convince you the website is at fault, then sales falling is a premise, the website claim is the conclusion, and it's an argument with the conclusion tucked inside the because clause.

S2: Same words, different job.

S1: The job depends on what's taken as given.

S2: Right. Step three, then. The missing step. You said that's the one that pays.

S1: Nearly every argument outside a textbook leaves something out, and it's left out because the arguer thought it was too obvious to say. Most of the time they were right, and writing it down changes nothing.

S2: But?

S1: But too obvious to say is also an exact description of a claim nobody checked. Write a premise down and you look at it, and your reader looks at it. Leave it out and neither of you does. From the outside, the harmless ones and the weak ones look identical.

S2: So you write it down every time to find out which you've got.

S1: Every time. And there's a rule for what goes in the brackets. Not the premise that makes the argument work. The premise the arguer is actually relying on, in the form they'd accept if you read it back to them.

S2: Why does that distinction matter so much?

S1: Because if you add a premise they'd reject, you're not evaluating their argument any more. You built a new one and knocked it down.

S2: Okay. So what does writing it down actually buy you?

S1: There's an example from Stephen Toulmin, from a book called The Uses of Argument, published in nineteen fifty eight. Harry was born in Bermuda, so Harry is a British subject.

S2: Seems fine.

S1: Seems fine. Now write down the rule it leans on, the way Toulmin words it. A man born in Bermuda will generally be a British subject.

S2: Generally.

S1: And the moment that word is sitting on the page in front of you, every reader asks the same question. Generally, except when?

S2: Which nobody asks about the one line version.

S1: Nobody. Reading born in Bermuda, so British, you don't pause to think about Harry's parents. Toulmin names two, and leaves the list open. Unless both his parents were aliens, which is the legal term for citizens of another state. Or unless he has since become a naturalised American.

S2: And those were invisible a second ago.

S1: Completely. That's what step three is for. Writing the rule down shows you how far the argument reaches, because a general rule has exceptions, and the exceptions are the counter cases.

S2: Does that mean the Harry argument is bad?

S1: No, and the lesson is firm about this. It's a perfectly good argument. The exceptions don't sink it, they qualify it. What it earns is, Harry is presumably a British subject unless one of those things applies. An honest arguer would say so.

S2: So finding a missing premise isn't a gotcha.

S1: Almost every real argument has one. Finding it is where evaluation begins, not where it ends. Usually it tells you how much weight the argument can bear, not that it can't bear any.

S2: One thing I noticed. That was the law as Toulmin described it in nineteen fifty eight.

S1: The lesson flags that. British nationality law has been rewritten since, so the rule itself has gone out of date. Which is worth a moment, because a bracketed premise is a claim about the world, and claims about the world can stop being true.

S2: And none of that works until you can write the argument down.

S1: Including the part nobody said.
