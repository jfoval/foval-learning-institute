S1: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm John.

S2: And I'm Haley. Logic and Argument, lesson three. Conditionals.

S1: Two people look round the same flat, and on the way out they each make an argument. The first one says, if this place had damp, you'd see stains on the ceiling. There aren't any stains. So there's no damp.

S2: Fine.

S1: The second one says, if this place had damp, you'd see stains on the ceiling. Look, there are stains. So it's got damp.

S2: Also fine?

S1: Same first premise. Same words. Same confident so. One of them is valid and one of them isn't.

S2: Go on then.

S1: The first one holds. The rule promised stains wherever there's damp. So no stains means no damp, or the rule would have been broken.

S2: And the second?

S1: The rule never promised that stains have to come from damp. A leak that was fixed years ago leaves stains in a perfectly dry flat. Both premises true, conclusion false.

S2: Ah. So the second person read the promise as going both ways.

S1: And it doesn't. That's the whole lesson, really. So look at what an if then sentence actually promises. If P then Q. There are four possible situations, and the sentence rules out exactly one of them.

S2: Which one?

S1: P true, Q false. Damp and no stains. That's the only case that makes the promise false. Damp with stains, fine. No damp with stains, fine, the promise never applied. No damp, no stains, fine.

S2: So a dry flat with stains doesn't break the rule at all.

S1: Doesn't touch it. The sentence is silent about dry flats. And there are two words worth having for this. Damp is sufficient for stains. Damp on its own guarantees them.

S2: And stains?

S1: Stains are necessary for damp. You can't have damp without them. But necessary isn't sufficient. Stains being required for damp doesn't make stains a guarantee of damp.

S2: And that gap is where the second person fell in.

S1: That gap is where both of the famous mistakes come from. So here's the thing to memorise. There are four one step moves you can make on if P then Q, and two of them work.

S2: Which two?

S1: Start from P. You've got damp, so you've got stains. That's called modus ponens, and nobody argues with it. Or start from not Q. No stains, so no damp. That's modus tollens, and it's the first flat viewer.

S2: And the two that fail?

S1: Start from Q, or start from not P. Stains, so damp, is called affirming the consequent, and it's the second flat viewer. No damp, so no stains, is called denying the antecedent, and the old fixed leak sinks that one too.

S2: So the rule of thumb is about which end you start from.

S1: If you remember nothing else, remember that. You're allowed to start from the if end, or from the negation of the then end. The other two starts are where people go wrong.

S2: Alright, quick test me.

S1: If the tap's dripping, the washer's gone. The washer's gone. So the tap's dripping.

S2: That's starting from the then end. Affirming the consequent. Invalid.

S1: And a washer can be gone in a tap that's turned off. One more. If she'd caught the earlier bus, she'd be here by now. She isn't here. So she didn't catch it.

S2: Starting from not Q. That's modus tollens. Valid.

S1: Good. Now here's the part I actually wanted to get to, because knowing the shapes and using them turn out to be different skills.

S2: This is the card thing.

S1: Peter Wason, nineteen sixty six. Four cards on a table. Each one has a letter on one side and a number on the other. Face up you can see E, K, four, and seven.

S2: Okay.

S1: The rule under test is, if there is a vowel on one side of the card, then there is an even number on the other side. Which cards must you turn over to find out whether the rule is true or false? Turn as few as you can.

S2: E, obviously. And... the four.

S1: That is what most people say, and it's the wrong pair. The answer is E and seven.

S2: Why not the four?

S1: Go back to the four situations. The one case that breaks this rule is a vowel with an odd number on the back. So ask of each card whether turning it could reveal that case. The E is a vowel. If the back is odd, the rule's broken. Turn it.

S2: And the K?

S1: Consonant. The rule says nothing about consonants. Turning it would be denying the antecedent.

S2: And the four is the even number.

S1: If the back is a vowel, the rule is kept. If it's a consonant, the rule didn't apply. Nothing can break. Turning it is affirming the consequent.

S2: And the seven.

S1: Odd number. If there's a vowel on the back, that's a vowel with an odd number. Broken. That's modus tollens, and it's the card almost nobody turns.

S2: So the four cards are literally the four moves laid out on a table.

S1: That's exactly what they are. And the pair most people pick is one valid move and one invalid one. Wason summarised what was known by nineteen sixty eight like this. Nearly all subjects select P, from sixty to seventy five per cent select Q, only a minority select not Q, and hardly any select not P.

S2: Do we have better numbers now?

S1: We do. Ragni, Kola and Johnson-Laird pooled two hundred and twenty eight of these experiments in twenty seventeen. On abstract rules like the vowel one, nineteen percent get the correct pair.

S2: Nineteen.

S1: But here's the second half. Griggs and Cox ran the same four card format with a regulation instead of a description. If a person is drinking beer, then the person must be over nineteen years of age. The cards are someone drinking beer, someone drinking a soft drink, someone aged twenty two, and someone aged sixteen.

S2: And you check the beer drinker and the sixteen year old.

S1: You do, and most people do. On those permission and obligation rules the pooled rate is sixty four percent.

S2: From nineteen percent to sixty four percent. Same structure.

S1: Same four moves. And notice the twenty two year old is exactly the four card, the one that gets turned so often on the vowel rule and gets left alone here.

S2: So what does that actually prove? That people can't do logic?

S1: No, and the lesson is careful about this, because it's easy to draw the wrong lesson. Given a rule someone could be breaking, most people go straight to the case that would break it. That's the move the abstract version couldn't get out of them. Why the content helps that much is still argued over, and there are four live explanations that disagree about what the task even measures.

S2: Fine. But what about the failure itself? Is that settled?

S1: Also not. Calling the abstract result a confirmation reflex is Wason's own reading, and it's one reading. Evans says the selections track the items actually named in the rule rather than confirming cases, which predicts people do better on rules with a not in them, and they do. Oaksford and Chater argue the common answer is a reasonable way to gather information if you read the rule as a claim about probability.

S2: So what survives all of them?

S1: One sentence. The card people leave face down is the one that could break the rule.

S2: So when I meet an if then claim and want to know whether to believe it.

S1: Your instinct will send you hunting for examples where P and Q both hold. The rule can survive a thousand of those. Go and look for a P and not Q case instead. And if you can attach a purpose to the rule, or imagine somebody cheating, you'll find that move easier, which is worth doing to yourself on purpose.
