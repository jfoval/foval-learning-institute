S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Logic and Argument, lesson seven. Reconstruction and charity: the strongest version first.

S1: It opens with something that happens to careful people. Someone posts an argument you think is wrong. You read it once, fast, and start typing. Twenty minutes later they reply that you've answered something they never said, and they're right.

S2: Why does that keep happening?

S1: Because an argument in the wild is a compressed thing. The premises are half stated, the general rule that carries the step is left out, and the words around the reasons, obviously, probably, although, are doing work that's easy to miss.

S2: So you had a good objection to a bad argument, and it wasn't their argument. You have to rebuild it before you can test it. Where do you start?

S1: With the small words. Sinnott Armstrong and Fogelin call this close analysis, and it comes before standard form. Van Cleave sorts them into three kinds.

S2: Go.

S1: Assuring terms tell you that reasons exist without giving them. Clearly, obviously, everyone agrees, studies show. His definition is, informing someone that there are further reasons although one is not giving them now.

S2: Give me one in the wild.

S1: One of his own examples. Most pediatricians agree that the single most important factor in childhood obesity is eating sugary, processed foods.

S2: So the sentence gives you a report that agreement exists, not a study. And that's not automatically bad?

S1: He says assurances aren't necessarily illegitimate, but a critical thinker will always regard them as somewhat suspect. And the practical version is sharper. Writers reach for an assurance exactly where they have no evidence to hand, so the premise under obviously is the one to check first.

S2: Second kind.

S1: Guarding terms weaken a claim so it's harder to refute. Probably, in most cases, some, it may be that.

S2: All US Presidents were monogamous is easy to test. Some US Presidents were monogamous is nearly impossible to deny. So a guard is a sign of weakness?

S1: That's the thing to notice. A guarded claim is usually the honest one. A writer who says probably is telling you exactly how far they'll go. Hold them to the guarded claim, not the bold one you'd have preferred to attack.

S2: And not to let it drift the other way.

S1: Don't let the conclusion outrun the guard either. Probably stale does not license, throw it away without looking.

S2: Third kind.

S1: Discounting terms anticipate an objection and wave it through. Although, even though, but, nevertheless, still. His example is, the worker was inefficient, but honest. The point after the but is the one the writer wants you to keep. The point before it is the one they've decided you'll set aside.

S2: And sometimes they shouldn't get to.

S1: Sometimes the objection they've discounted is the strongest thing in the passage, and the although is the only sign it was ever there.

S2: So what does close analysis leave you with?

S1: A map of where the writer feels strong and where they feel weak, drawn by the writer. Assurances mark weak spots. Guards mark what they've measured. Discounts mark the objections they know about.

S2: Read the map, then rebuild. Try me on one.

S1: Everyone knows the trains here are unreliable, and although the new operator has promised improvements, they'll probably be late again on Monday.

S2: Everyone knows is assuring. Although is discounting. Probably is guarding.

S2: The assured one. What's the punctuality figure, and is it actually bad?

S1: The guarded conclusion is the honest part, and the discounted promise is the objection the writer would rather you didn't weigh.

S2: Right. Next idea.

S1: Two everyday arguments, both made up. The flat is ten minutes from her office, it's under budget, and it has a garden. She should take it. And: the last train leaves at ten past ten and the trip takes fifty minutes, so she'll be home by eleven.

S2: All right.

S1: Now suppose the second premise in each is false. The flat is over budget. The trip takes ninety minutes, not fifty.

S2: The flat argument still stands, weaker. It's near work and it has a garden. The train argument is gone.

S1: Gone. A departure time on its own tells you nothing about an arrival time. The difference isn't the number of premises, it's how they support the conclusion.

S2: Name them.

S1: The flat argument has convergent premises. Each one is a separate reason, and each would count for something on its own. The train argument has linked premises. They support the conclusion only together, and neither does anything alone. The Stanford encyclopedia draws it this way: convergent premises are separate arrows into the conclusion, and linked premises are joined with a plus sign to show that they support the conclusion only when combined.

S2: And why does telling them apart matter?

S1: Because the whole point of testing premises one at a time is to know what happens when one fails. If the premises are linked, a single false premise sinks the conclusion. If they're convergent, a false premise costs the argument one reason and leaves the rest.

S2: So the diagram is a prediction.

S1: A prediction about damage. It tells you which premises are load bearing before you test anything.

S2: And most real arguments mix the two, I assume. You also said in lesson five that conductive arguments weigh reasons on both sides.

S1: Those are convergent support with the other side of the ledger written in.

S2: Last piece. There's a layout you keep promising me.

S1: From lesson one. Harry was born in Bermuda, so Harry is a British subject. Stephen Toulmin, in The Uses of Argument, nineteen fifty eight, thought the premise and conclusion layout hid too much and proposed six slots instead.

S2: On Harry.

S1: Claim: Harry is a British subject. Data, or grounds in his later textbook: Harry was born in Bermuda. Warrant: a man born in Bermuda will generally be a British subject. Backing: the statutes on British nationality. Qualifier: presumably, because the warrant says generally. And rebuttal: unless both his parents were aliens, or unless he has since become a naturalised American.

S2: But the warrant is just the bracketed premise, isn't it. Why do I need a second layout?

S1: That's the right question, and the lesson takes it seriously. David Hitchcock's answer is that a warrant is an inference licensing rule, not a premise. A rule the arguer is relying on, not a fact they're asserting.

S2: And seeing it as a rule buys you what?

S1: Two things a premise list doesn't. A rule has a scope, so it invites the question, does this rule apply here, which is the rebuttal slot. And rules can belong to fields. Toulmin held that warrants are field dependent: the rule that licenses a step in law is nothing like the rule that licenses a step in medicine, and a step that's fine in one field can be worthless in another.

S2: Is that the whole picture?

S1: Hitchcock adds a caution it needs. Plenty of everyday warrants belong to no organised field at all. They're rough generalisations from common experience, and for those there's no professional body to check the rule against. There's only the work of writing it down and asking how far it reaches.

S2: And the rebuttal slot has to be filled.

S1: Which is the reason the layout finds weaknesses a premise list hides. Writing unless, and then stopping to think, is how you find that Harry's parents might have been diplomats, or that the doctor might be a dermatologist being asked about a heart.

S2: Do that one. She's a doctor, so she'll know.

S1: The warrant is something like, a doctor generally knows the answer to a medical question. Write it down and the rebuttal slot fills itself. Unless the question isn't medical. Unless it's outside her specialty. Unless it's a question the profession hasn't settled. The data is fine. The claim is only as good as the warrant's reach, and the warrant reaches a good deal less far than the sentence sounds.
