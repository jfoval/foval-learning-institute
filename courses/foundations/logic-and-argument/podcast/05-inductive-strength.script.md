S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Logic and Argument, lesson five. Inductive strength.

S1: It opens with a friend thinking about buying a car. She says, my last three cars from that maker all had gearbox trouble before eighty thousand kilometres, I'm not buying another one.

S2: Run the check on it.

S1: Conclusion: the next car will probably have gearbox trouble too. Premises: three cars, three failures. Missing premise, in brackets: what happened to three of them will happen to the next. Now step four. Is there a possible case where the premises are true and the conclusion false?

S2: Obviously. The three could all have failed and the fourth be fine.

S1: So the argument is invalid. And yet it isn't a bad argument: three out of three is real evidence. If you told her that's invalid she'd rightly say, I never claimed it was certain, I claimed it was likely.

S2: So she's making a different claim about the link.

S1: An argument is deductive when the premises are claimed to guarantee the conclusion,.

S2: So for a deductive argument step four asks, is it valid. And here?

S1: Is it strong. A strong inductive argument is one whose premises render the conclusion likely, and a cogent argument is a strong argument with true premises. Cogent is to inductive arguments what sound was to deductive ones. And validity is all or nothing, where strength comes in amounts.

S2: So if an argument has true premises and a strong link, what can still go wrong?

S1: The conclusion can be false, which isn't a defect in the argument. It's what probably means. A strong, cogent argument for, the next toss will be heads, is exactly the argument you should accept, and the next toss can still be tails.

S2: All right, the coin. A thousand heads.

S1: This coin has come up heads on all of the last thousand tosses, so the next toss will probably be heads. That's strong. Now, can adding a true premise ever make an argument worse?

S2: For a valid deductive argument, no. If the conclusion follows, it still follows when you learn more.

S1: For an inductive one, easily. Add a second premise. The coin has just been checked by a lab and is a fair coin.

S2: That doesn't contradict the first.

S1: Both are true. But the argument is now weaker, because a fair coin has no memory, and the thousand heads now look like an astonishing run of luck that says nothing about the next toss. Logicians call this defeasibility: further information can defeat the verdict that the conclusion is well supported. A valid argument can't be defeated that way.

S2: Which is why, the argument was strong at the time, is a coherent thing to say.

S1: And why every conclusion you reach inductively is held on condition that nothing relevant turns up.

S2: There's a sting in that lab example, though, isn't there.

S1: Suppose someone who knows about the lab report argues from the first premise alone. Every word is true, and the link from their one premise is strong. The argument still shouldn't move you, because it leaves out evidence its maker has, and the left out evidence changes the verdict. So cogency, in any version worth having, requires the premises to take in the relevant evidence available, not just to be true.

S2: Which gives me a question to ask.

S1: Of anyone with an interest in the conclusion. Not only, is this true, but, what do they know that isn't in here.

S2: Why does strength come in degrees at all?

S1: A valid deductive link holds in every possible case. An inductive link holds in most of the cases the premises describe. The conclusion is about one case, the next toss, the fourth car, and the argument is strong to the degree the premises resemble that case in the ways that matter.

S2: Coverage.

S1: Hold that one idea and the rest follows. A sample supports a generalisation to the degree that it covers the population. An analogy supports its conclusion to the degree that the shared features are the ones that matter.

S2: The lesson works four families through that idea. Take cause.

S1: The tools were written down in eighteen forty three by John Stuart Mill. Here's his own example of the method of difference, in Jonathan Bennett's modern wording. When a man is shot through the heart, the method of difference shows us that it was the gunshot that killed him: he was in the fullness of life immediately before, all circumstances being the same as after except the wound.

S1: Mill says that's the logical process behind almost all the inductive conclusions we draw in daily life.

S2: And notice the shape of it. If the wound weren't the cause, he'd still be alive, since nothing else changed. That's modus tollens from lesson three. So a controlled experiment is this built on purpose.

S1: Two groups alike in everything you can arrange, one gets the treatment, and any difference in outcome is laid at its door. But the canon is demanding. Every circumstance in common except for one is almost never true of two real cases. So an argument is strong to the degree the other differences have been ruled out. When you read, we tried X and Y happened, ask what else changed.

S2: And there's a warning he adds.

S1: It's not true, he says, that the same phenomenon is always produced by the same cause. The plurality of causes. The wound explains this death and says nothing about deaths in general. Finding the cause in one case closes that case, not the question.

S2: What about correlation?

S1: His fifth canon covers the case where you can't remove the suspected cause and can only watch it vary. Two things that vary together, it says, are cause and effect, or causally connected in some other manner. Then the hedge. When two phenomena match each other in their variations, it doesn't follow one is cause and the other effect. If they were two effects of a common cause, they would exhibit concomitant variation, and this method alone can't tell us which.

S2: That's correlation isn't causation, in eighteen forty three.

S1: And notice what he actually says. Not that a correlation tells you nothing. That it tells you the two are causally connected in some manner, and this method alone can't tell you which. So the slogan has two uses. As a demand, name the other readings and show me how you ruled them out, it's doing the work. As a conversation ender, as if any correlation were worthless, it claims more than the canon allows.

S2: And the other readings are?

S1: Four. A causes B. B causes A. Something else, C, causes both. Or chance: the two happened to move together in the data you have. A causal argument from a correlation is strong to the degree that readings two, three and four have been ruled out.

S2: Work one.

S1: Hypothetically. A café owner finds that on days when she sells a lot of iced drinks, the pharmacy next door sells a lot of after sun lotion, all summer. She jokes that her iced coffee is giving people sunburn.

S2: Common cause. Hot sunny days push up both.

S1: Right, and the argument is saying why the other three fail. Iced drinks causing sunburn has no mechanism. Sunburn causing iced drink sales has a thin one at best, and the drink sales rise on the same day as the burns, which is too fast for that story. And chance is unlikely across a whole summer.

S2: And you'd test it how?

S1: Find pairs of days equally hot and sunny where iced drink sales differed for some other reason, a coffee machine breakdown say. If after sun sales track the weather and not the drinks, the first reading is dead. You've held the suspected common cause fixed and let the suspected cause vary. That's Mill's canon applied to a café.
