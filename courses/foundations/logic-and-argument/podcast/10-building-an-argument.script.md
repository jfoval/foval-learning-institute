S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Logic and Argument, lesson ten, the last one. Building an argument that holds up.

S1: It opens with an email you could have written last week. We should move the team meeting to Monday morning. Fridays are dead anyway, half the team is already thinking about the weekend, and Monday sets the week up properly.

S2: A perfectly normal email. I'd nod and move on.

S1: Most people would. Now read it as someone who's done the first nine lessons. There's an unstated premise, that a meeting works better when people are fresh, and nobody has checked it. Half the team is a sample of one office on a couple of Fridays. Sets the week up properly is overtone doing the work of a reason.

S2: And the objection?

S1: The strongest one isn't there at all. Monday morning is when the people who actually run the week are busiest.

S2: And a reader who has to supply your objections for you trusts the rest a little less. So this is the course turned round on me.

S1: For nine lessons you've been the reader with the checklist. Now you're the writer and the checklist is coming for you. Every tool in the course is a place where a reader can stop you. Lesson one taught them to find your missing premise. Five, to ask how big your sample was. Six, to look for the base rate you left out. Eight gave them the names and the critical questions.

S2: So what's the mechanism?

S1: An argument survives when its best opponent has already been through it. Run those checks on your own draft and you find the stops before your reader does, and then you fix them or say them out loud. What's left is an argument the reader can't stop, or can only stop where you've already marked.

S2: And there's a rulebook.

S1: Anthony Weston's Rulebook for Arguments, the short book most often handed to students who need to write arguments rather than classify them. The first six are for short arguments. Know what your premises and your conclusion are before you start. Unfold your ideas in a natural order. Start from reliable premises. Be concrete and concise. Build on substance, not overtone. And use consistent terms.

S2: Run those on the meeting email.

S1: It fails rule three, because the half the team premise isn't reliable. It fails rule five, because sets the week up properly is overtone. And it never states the premise the whole thing rests on. Three of six, in three sentences. The lesson says that's typical of a first draft, including its own.

S2: Give me the worked one.

S1: You're deciding whether to buy a used car or lease a new one, and you want to write the case for buying so a friend can check it. Hypothetical, and the numbers are made up. First draft. Buy the used car. Leasing is just renting, and you never own anything at the end. Everyone knows leasing is a rip off.

S2: Start with rule one.

S1: Premises and conclusion. Conclusion: buy the used car. Premises: leasing is renting, you own nothing at the end, and everyone knows leasing is a rip off. Written out like that, the third premise isn't a reason. It's the conclusion in a different coat. Delete it.

S2: And rule three, reliable premises?

S1: Leasing is renting is roughly true, but it's an analogy, and lesson five taught you an analogy is only as good as the relevance of the shared feature. What feature is doing the work here? Cost over the period you'll have the car. So replace the analogy with the thing it was standing in for.

S2: Which means writing a premise about cost.

S1: And not, leasing costs more. Something a friend could check. Over the five years I expect to keep a car, buying this used car and running it costs less in total than leasing a new one for the same period.

S2: And to make that acceptable?

S1: Two totals. Purchase price plus running costs, less expected resale, on one side. Lease payments plus fees and any end of term charges on the other. And if you can't get those numbers, you don't have the premise. Weston's advice is to go and find them, not to write around the gap.

S2: What do the other rules do to it?

S1: Rule five, substance not overtone: rip off goes. Rule six, consistent terms: cost means total cost over five years everywhere, not monthly cost in one line and total in the next.

S2: Why does that last one matter so much?

S1: Because the standard argument for leasing is that the monthly payment is lower, and it's true. Your argument has to meet it with the same word meaning the same thing.

S2: And the second draft?

S1: Four premises. What buying costs in total, less resale. What leasing costs in total. That the first is less than the second. And in brackets, the unstated one: other things being roughly equal, I should choose the option that costs less over the period I'll have the car. Conclusion, buy the used car.

S2: So the link is now valid.

S1: If the four are true, the conclusion follows. Every premise is something a friend can check, and the unstated one is written down in brackets where the reader can see it. And look at what it does. It says, other things being roughly equal, and that's the door the objection walks through. A short argument that holds up doesn't hide that door. It marks it.

S2: Which brings back the refrain.

S1: The one from lesson two. Forall x put it this way. To rebut an argument you can show a premise is false or show the argument is invalid, and logic only helps with the second. When you're the writer, that sentence tells you where your work is. Making the link valid is the easy part. The premises are where the friend will push, and each one needs a number, a source, or a reason it counts as common knowledge.

S2: Now, longer arguments.

S1: Weston has rules for those too. Explore the issue. Spell out your basic ideas as arguments. Defend your basic premises with arguments of their own. Reckon with objections. Explore alternatives. And for an essay: state your claim early and make it definite, let the argument be the outline, detail objections and meet them, seek feedback and use it. And his last rule for essays, which nobody expects. Modesty, please.

S2: Work one.

S1: Hypothetical again. You want to argue that you should learn Spanish this year. You explore the issue: a work trip to Madrid in ten months, your partner's family speaks Spanish, and you've wanted to for years. You spell those out as arguments and defend the premises. The trip is booked, the family is real, and the practice claim has evidence behind it.

S2: So far so good.

S1: Now reckon with the objection, in Rapoport's form from lesson seven. State the other side so well that a holder of it would accept your statement. And the best objection isn't, languages are hard.

S2: What is it?

S1: You tried this two years ago with an app, did it daily for six weeks, then stopped when work got busy, and you never got past ordering food. Nothing about your time has changed. The problem was never motivation. It was that twenty minutes a day doesn't survive a busy month.

S2: That's uncomfortably good.

S1: And it's partly right. The history is accurate and the time hasn't changed.

S2: So what do I do?

S1: Three options. Abandon the claim, which is what people do when they've been taught that conceding is losing. Ignore the objection and hope the reader doesn't notice, which a reader who's done this course will notice. Or qualify the claim: keep what the objection didn't touch, concede what it did, and write the concession into the conclusion.

S2: And the course recommends the third.

S1: Using Toulmin's two slots from lesson seven. A qualifier, presumably, or in most cases. And a rebuttal, the unless.

S2: The full lesson has the qualified conclusion written out, the rest of Weston's rules for extended arguments, the ten line self check, and the course project, which is an argument of your own with its objections in it. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: That's the end of Logic and Argument. Thanks for listening, and go and write the objection down first.
