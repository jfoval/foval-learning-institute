S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. We're in the Logic and Argument course, lesson two. Valid and sound.

S1: Start with something you could have heard from a neighbour. Every flat in this building that's had damp has had its windows painted shut. Your windows are painted shut. So you're going to get damp.

S2: That sounds fine. I'd nod along to that.

S1: Most people do. Now hold the shape and change the words. Every cat is a mammal. Your dog is a mammal. So your dog is a cat.

S2: Oh. That's the same argument.

S1: Same skeleton, and the second one is obviously broken, which means the first one was too. Nothing about the damp argument guaranteed its conclusion. It just had a conclusion you were ready to believe.

S2: So what's the test?

S1: One question. Could every premise be true and the conclusion still be false? If yes, the argument is invalid. If that's impossible, it's valid.

S2: And with the dog, both premises are true and the conclusion is false.

S1: A real case, so the shape is dead. Logicians call that a counterexample, and one settles it.

S2: But hang on. That definition doesn't mention whether anything is actually true.

S1: It doesn't, and that's the part people fight. Try this. All fish fly. All whales are fish. So all whales fly.

S2: Every single thing in that is false.

S1: And the argument is valid. Because if every whale were a fish, and every fish did fly, the whales would fly. The link holds, and validity is about the link and nothing else. The lesson quotes the open textbook the course uses, which says validity is not about the actual truth or falsity of the sentences in the argument, and then adds, it is often said that logic doesn't care about feelings, actually it doesn't care about facts either.

S2: Surely an argument with false premises is a bad argument, though.

S1: It might be. But bad is a bigger word than invalid. Validity is one narrow property, and the reason to keep it narrow is that you can check it without knowing anything about the world.

S2: So what's the word for the one I actually want?

S1: Sound. An argument is sound if it's valid and all of its premises are true. The bridge that's well built and also starts from the right bank.

S2: And if someone shows me a sound argument for something I don't like?

S1: Then the conclusion is true and your dislike is what has to move. Which is why sound is such a high bar. You have to establish the link, which logic can do, and the truth of every premise, which logic can't. The lesson is blunt about it. To rebut an argument you can show a premise isn't true, or show the argument isn't valid, and logic will only help you with the second.

S2: Is there anything I should just memorise?

S1: One thing. Picture four boxes. Valid or invalid down the side, all premises true or at least one false along the top. Every combination happens except one. Valid, all premises true, conclusion false. That cannot exist. Not rare. Impossible, by definition.

S2: And is that useful, or just tidy?

S1: Here's the trick. If you know an argument is valid and you know its conclusion is false, you've learned something about the premises without reading them.

S2: At least one of them is false.

S1: At least one. You don't know which, and you don't know that all of them are. That's the shape of every if that were true, then this would follow, and it doesn't, that you've ever said.

S2: So the definition isn't complicated. Why does the lesson say it's harder than it sounds?

S1: Because of what happens when you use it. In nineteen eighty three, Jonathan Evans, Julie Barston and Paul Pollard at Plymouth Polytechnic ran three experiments on exactly these two premise arguments. They built four kinds. Valid with a believable conclusion, valid with an unbelievable one, invalid with a believable one, invalid with an unbelievable one. And they told undergraduates, in writing, to treat each as a logic problem, and to accept a conclusion only if it necessarily followed from the premises.

S2: So they were warned.

S1: They were warned. Now, two of the arguments. No addictive things are inexpensive. Some cigarettes are inexpensive. Therefore some addictive things are not cigarettes.

S2: Sure. Fine.

S1: And, no millionaires are hard workers. Some rich people are hard workers. Therefore some millionaires are not rich people.

S2: That one's nonsense.

S1: Same shape. Both invalid. From no A are B, some C are B, what actually follows is some C are not A. Some cigarettes are not addictive. Some rich people are not millionaires. The conclusion offered runs the other way round in each case.

S2: So how did people do?

S1: Across the three experiments, seventy one percent accepted the cigarettes conclusion as valid. Ten percent accepted the millionaires one.

S2: Sixty points, on the same skeleton. And the only difference is whether the conclusion is something people already believe.

S1: That's it.

S2: Does that mean logic did nothing?

S1: No, and the lesson is careful here. The paper reports substantial belief biases alongside equally substantial effects of logic. People did respond to the link, but the bias was bigger on the invalid arguments. And in the second experiment, with sixty four students, subjects were right eighty seven percent of the time when logic and belief pointed the same way, and forty eight percent when they conflicted.

S2: Where the two disagreed, judgement fell to a coin toss.

S1: And these were intelligent adults told in writing to judge the logic. They weren't careless. They were doing what minds do.

S2: Which is what, exactly?

S1: The account the lesson uses is that checking a conclusion against what you already believe is fast, and you've done it before you finish reading. Checking whether the premises force the conclusion is slow. So if the fast check says yes, that's true, a lot of people stop there. If it says no, that's absurd, they go looking for the flaw, and they find it.

S2: So the flaw was findable the whole time.

S1: In both arguments. Many people only went looking in one of them. Evans and his colleagues called that selective scrutiny.

S2: Is that the accepted explanation?

S1: No, and the lesson refuses to pretend otherwise. In twenty ten, Chad Dube, Caren Rotello and Evan Heit made the strongest challenge to it. Using methods borrowed from the study of memory, built to separate accuracy from willingness to say yes, they argue belief doesn't degrade your reasoning at all. You scrutinise a believable argument just as hard. What belief shifts is your threshold for saying it follows.

S2: So you accept the same quality of evidence more easily. And if they're right?

S1: The fast check story is wrong, and the classic experiments were measuring a response habit rather than a reasoning failure. The lesson says plainly that it isn't resolved.

S2: Then does the practical advice survive either way?

S1: That's the good news, and it's why the lesson bothers with the disagreement. Every account shares this much. A conclusion you already believe is more likely to get your yes, whether or not the argument earned it. Maybe you check less, as Evans's group read it. Maybe you check just as hard and say yes more easily, as Dube, Rotello and Heit read it. Either way, an invalid argument for something you believe gets past you more often than one for something you doubt.

S2: And this is why step six of the six step check is last.

S1: Exactly why. The lesson says the order matters more than any single step. Skip to six and you'll do step four badly, and worst on the arguments that flatter you. And the defence doesn't ask you to check harder, which is lucky, because nobody knows how to do that on demand. It asks you to check in a fixed order, before your opinion of the conclusion gets a vote.
