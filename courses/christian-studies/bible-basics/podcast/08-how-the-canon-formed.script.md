S1: It opens inside the New Testament. Second Peter, talking about Paul's letters. Some things in these letters are hard to understand, things the ignorant and unstable twist to their own destruction, as they also do to the rest of the scriptures.

S2: The rest of the scriptures.

S1: One set of Christian writings ranked with Scripture by another, inside the New Testament itself. Nobody voted. Nobody convened. A letter was read in a church, kept, copied, and read again, and at some point people were calling it Scripture and arguing about what it meant.

S2: Is there a catch in using that verse?

S1: There is, and the lesson says it up front rather than letting you find it later. Second Peter is itself one of the books that took longest to be received, and scholars argue about when it was written. If it's late, that passage shows a second century church treating Paul's letters as Scripture rather than a first century one. Either way it shows the process, which is what the lesson is about.

S2: So what is a canon?

S1: It comes from a Greek word for a measuring rod, and came to mean the list of books a community treats as its rule.

S2: And how does one form?

S1: Here the lesson is careful, because there's more than one answer and it tells you which it's using. On the account this course works with, a canon isn't made by a vote. It's the shape a community's worship has already taken, written down when somebody asks. Churches read certain books aloud on Sunday, copied them, taught from them, and defended them when challenged. When a bishop finally wrote a list, he was describing what his churches did, the way a catalogue describes shelves that were already full.

S2: And that's checkable?

S1: It makes a prediction you can check. If canons formed by decree you'd expect one document, one date, and silence before it. If by use, local lists that mostly agree, a few books argued over for a long time, and councils that confirm rather than choose.

S2: Right. Now the claim everybody has heard.

S1: That the Emperor Constantine, or the Council of Nicaea, picked the books in three twenty five. Most people have it from a novel. The lesson's answer is to walk the dated evidence in order and see whether there's room for it.

S2: Start.

S1: Around the year one eighty, Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, names the four Gospels and argues there can be neither more nor fewer than four. That's a hundred and forty five years before the council. So if Constantine picked the Gospels, he picked ones that had already been the only four for a century and a half.

S2: What about people who were actually at Nicaea?

S1: Two of them. Eusebius sat at the council. He sorted the books in a history he had substantially written before it, in editions of about three thirteen and three sixteen, and brought to final form as late as three twenty four or three twenty five. He puts James, Jude, Second Peter, and Second and Third John among the disputed, and hedges on Revelation.

S2: And in that final pass?

S1: He had every opportunity to add a line saying, as the great council decreed. He didn't. He left the disputed list standing.

S2: And the second man?

S1: Athanasius, who was at Nicaea as a young deacon. In three sixty seven, forty two years later, he gives the first surviving list of exactly our twenty seven. If the council had listed the books, the obvious thing to write would be a line saying, as the great council decreed. He writes nothing of the kind. He tells the churches not to add or subtract, as if the list is something they already have.

S2: Anything later?

S1: In three ninety three and three ninety seven, two councils in North Africa list the books and ask, in a line that survives in only some copies, that Rome confirm it. That's nearly seventy years after Nicaea. And if that line is original, a body that thought it was choosing the books wouldn't have asked somebody else to check its work.

S2: And Nicaea itself?

S1: We have its creed and its twenty canons, and none of the surviving records of its business mention the canon of Scripture. The council was about whether the Son is of the same substance as the Father, and about the date of Easter, and some church discipline.

S2: So the story fails.

S1: It fails on the evidence. But the lesson says something better than that. It fails as the wrong kind of story. Once you've seen how the lists were made, an emperor chose the books is like saying a librarian decided which books people had been reading for two hundred years. It gets the direction of causation backwards. The lists exist because the reading came first.

S2: You said earlier there's more than one account of what all this was.

S1: Three, and they all read the same evidence and they all reject the Nicaea story. The recognition model, which is Bruce's and Michael Kruger's, says the church recognised a canon it didn't create: the books carried their own authority, and the fourth century lists are the church catching up with a fact. The community model, which is Lee Martin McDonald's, says the canon was community determined: recognition was slow and local, different regions read different collections for centuries, and those lists represent choices churches made under the pressures of their time.

S2: And the third?

S1: The ecclesial model, which belongs to the Catholic and Orthodox traditions. It says a canon presupposes an authority competent to recognise one. The question which books can't be answered by the books themselves, because a reader has to know already which books to consult. The Church that received, read and transmitted them is what makes the answer knowable, and a decree like Trent's, or a council's reception in the East, is that authority doing in public what it had been doing all along.

S2: Is there a sharp form of that?

S1: There's a question put to Protestants, and the lesson puts it before the answer. If the canon is a list, and no inspired list appears anywhere in Scripture, then on your own principles you have a fallible collection of infallible books, and you got it from the very Church whose judgement you otherwise decline.

S2: And the answer?

S1: Kruger's, and it's that God's providence and the Spirit's witness do the work an institution would otherwise have to do. Whether that reply succeeds is exactly where a thoughtful Catholic and a thoughtful Protestant stop agreeing.

S2: So where does this course stand?

S1: On the recognition model, and it says so where you can see it. We believe the church received these books because they were God's word, not that they became God's word by being received. That's a faith commitment stated as one. And the disagreement between the three isn't a question a new manuscript could settle, because it's a question about what kind of thing a canon is.
