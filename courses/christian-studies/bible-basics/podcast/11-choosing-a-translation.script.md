S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in the course called The Bible: What It Is and How to Read It, lesson eleven. Choosing a translation.

S1: One verse, two ways. Amos four, six. The King James: and I also have given you cleanness of teeth in all your cities, and want of bread in all your places. The NIV: I gave you empty stomachs in every city and lack of bread in every town.

S2: Same Hebrew?

S1: Same Hebrew, both translated by scholars who could read it. And most people ask which is right, which is the wrong question with the right one just behind it.

S2: So which is right?

S1: Both. The Hebrew says, word for word, cleanness of teeth. A grim picture of teeth with nothing on them. The King James keeps the picture and leaves you to work out what it means. The NIV works it out for you.

S2: So what's the real question?

S1: Not which translation is accurate, but which kind of accuracy you want for the reading you're about to do. There are two axes, and keeping them apart makes most translation arguments shorter. Which text it came from, and how it was translated.

S2: Start with the text.

S1: For the Old Testament, almost every modern Protestant and Catholic translation works from one printed Hebrew edition, whose text is the Leningrad Codex of about ten oh eight, the oldest complete Hebrew Bible. For the New Testament, the standard edition weighs the manuscripts wherever they differ and prints what the editors judge the earliest recoverable reading. Nearly every English translation since eighteen eighty one follows a critical Greek edition.

S2: And the King James doesn't.

S1: Here's why. In fifteen sixteen Erasmus published the first printed Greek New Testament to reach the public, in Basel, at speed. His own verdict, in a letter written within weeks, was that it was thrown together rather than edited, and in the same breath he claimed it beat everything before.

S2: How many manuscripts did he have?

S1: Seven, most from the twelfth century, and only one of Revelation, missing its last verses, which he filled by translating the Latin Vulgate back into Greek. His text was revised by others through the sixteenth century, and in sixteen thirty three an edition printed at Leiden carried a sentence calling it the text now received by all. From the Latin of that phrase, textum receptum, comes the name Textus Receptus. And the King James translators of sixteen eleven worked from that family, because those were the editions there were.

S2: So the King James differs where the two texts differ.

S1: The three famous passages from lesson nine are the clearest cases. The Received Text has the longer ending of Mark, the woman caught in adultery, and the heavenly witnesses of First John five, seven, without brackets. The first two because the manuscripts Erasmus had contained them, and First John five, seven because his third edition of fifteen twenty two added the sentence. And the lesson is careful to say the King James isn't mistranslating. It's an excellent formal translation of its own text.

S2: Is that settled or argued?

S1: Sort it as this course does. History establishes how each printed text was made. Scholars argue which form stands closest to what the apostles wrote, with the great majority holding it's the critical text, a minority arguing for the priority of the Byzantine text on text critical grounds, and a smaller group holding the Received Text specifically, on grounds that include a doctrine of preservation. And Christians on both sides hold by faith that God has preserved his word. Where they differ is where to look for it.

S2: Right. The second axis.

S1: Two translations can start from the identical Greek and still read as differently as those two versions of Amos, because the difference is a decision about what to keep. Fee and Stuart describe three approaches. A formal equivalence translation keeps the form: the words, the word order where English allows, the idioms and pictures. A functional one keeps the meaning, and will change the form to do it. And a free translation restates the idea in the translator's own words.

S2: Where does the NIV sit?

S1: In the middle, on Fee and Stuart's placement. Most people who own one guess literal, because it reads so smoothly they assume nothing was reworked. The smoothness is the reworking.

S2: Why can't a translation sit at both ends?

S1: Because translation is a chain of decisions, one per word, phrase and sentence. At every link the translator holds the source language's form in one hand and the target language's sense in the other, and at a surprising number of links the two pull apart. Keep the form and you lose some sense. Give the sense and you lose the form. You can protect a link. You can't protect both hands at every link, because English and Hebrew aren't the same shape.

S2: And what follows from that?

S1: Three things you can use. First, every translation interprets. A formal one makes its decisions quietly, by choosing which English word to line up with which Hebrew one. A functional one makes them out loud. So, I want a translation that doesn't interpret, is asking for something that cannot exist.

S2: Second.

S1: That which translation is most accurate is a malformed question until you finish it. Accurate to what, the words or the meaning? For what, reading Isaiah on the train, or tracing a Hebrew word through the Psalms?

S2: And third.

S1: That the trade offs are honest and predictable. A formal translation preserves wordplay, repetition and ambiguity, and pays in idioms that don't land. A functional one keeps a first time reader moving, and pays by hiding the form. A free one gives freshness, and pays with the most translator between you and the text.

S2: Show me the whole spectrum on that one verse.

S1: Amos four, six, keeping the phrase that matters. King James and the English Standard Version both have cleanness of teeth in all your cities. The NIV has empty stomachs in every city. The Christian Standard Bible has absolutely nothing to eat in all your cities. The NET has, I gave you no food to eat in all your cities, and puts the Hebrew idiom in a note. The New Living Translation has, I brought hunger to every city. And The Message: I'm the One who emptied your pantries and cleaned out your cupboards.

S2: You can watch the form drain out.

S1: And the sense fill in. The first two keep the picture and trust you. The middle ones give you the meaning in an image of their own. The New Living Translation drops the image and states the fact. And The Message invents two images no Israelite farmer had, so the sentence lands on a modern reader the way Amos's landed on his. None has made a mistake. They've made choices, and a choice hides something.

S2: So how do I choose?

S1: The lesson takes Fee and Stuart's advice as its own, as three Bibles. One for reading, a functional translation you can read a whole book of at a sitting. One for study, a formal one that shows you the form, which you open when a word repeats or an image puzzles you. And one for freshness, optional, for passages you know so well you no longer hear them. Never alone, and never for settling what a text says. Then a fourth: the NET, free, for the days you want to see the decisions made. It's the version this course quotes.

S2: We've left out the gender question, which the lesson gives in each side's words because it's the one that makes people angry. The King James question, in its own terms. What First John five, seven looks like from the translation side. Byzantine priority, which isn't the Received Text. What changes if your shelf is Catholic or Orthodox. How to find all this out for a Bible the chart doesn't list, from its preface, copyright page and footnotes. What people get wrong. And the argument that a functional translation is the more accurate method, not the less. And how few of the world's languages have a Bible at all, which is a number most people get badly wrong. The full lesson has all of it, free, at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Ask which text it came from, and what it chose to keep.
