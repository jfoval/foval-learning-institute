S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're starting the course called The Bible: What It Is and How to Read It, with its first lesson. Finding your way around: what the Bible is and how it is laid out.

S1: And it opens with a sentence of Jesus, from Luke twenty four. Everything written about me in the law of Moses and the prophets and the psalms must be fulfilled.

S2: Most people hear one thing in that.

S1: That Jesus says the whole Bible is about him, which is in it. But look at how he names the whole Bible. He doesn't say the Scriptures and leave it there. He gives three parts. The law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms. Scholars argue about how standard that three part description was, because the New Testament more often says just the Law and the Prophets. Either way, he's describing a collection with a shape.

S2: Before we go further, say where this course stands.

S1: It's a School of Christian Studies course and the label on it says so. We believe the Bible is God's Word. That in these books God speaks, and speaks truly, about himself and about us. That is a faith claim and we hold it by faith. We won't dress it up as a neutral fact, and we won't pretend it's the only view a thoughtful person can hold.

S2: And the other view?

S1: A critical scholar reads the same texts as ancient documents, asks what each meant in its own setting, and holds that the question of God's speech is not one the historical method can settle. That's a serious position held by serious people, and the course says so every time it matters. And critical is a word about a method, not a belief. Critical scholars include believing Jews, Catholics, Protestants, and people of no faith.

S2: So how does the course keep those apart?

S1: With three phrases it uses over and over, in its own words. History establishes something when the documents and the digs settle it and scholars of every conviction agree. Scholars argue about something when serious, informed people disagree and the evidence doesn't yet settle it, and there you get each side in its own terms. And we hold by faith something when it's ours as Christians, and evidence bears on it without deciding it.

S2: Sort three for me. One. The Tanakh, which is the Jewish name for the same books, counts the twelve short prophets as one book, and a Christian Bible counts them as twelve.

S1: History establishes that. Count them either way and everybody gets the same answer, whatever they believe about God.

S2: Two. Moses wrote the five books of the Law.

S1: Scholars argue about it, seriously, with the critical mainstream and the evangelical mainstream reaching different conclusions from the same text.

S2: And the last one. These books are God's word to us.

S1: We hold that by faith, and the course says so in its own voice rather than slipping it in as a fact. Three sentences about the same book, three different boxes. And the middle one is the box people empty by mistake.

S2: Right. How many books are in the Bible?

S1: Depends whose Bible is on your table. A Protestant Bible has sixty six. Thirty nine in the Old Testament, twenty seven in the New. A Catholic Bible has seventy three, because it counts seven more books, and some longer forms of Esther and Daniel, in the Old Testament. An Orthodox Bible has more still.

S2: And the New Testament?

S1: The same in all three, with one exception. The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo church has the largest canon of any, and its broader New Testament runs past twenty seven. This course uses sixty six and says so now, so that nothing is hidden. Why the difference exists, and what each tradition says for itself, is a whole lesson later on.

S2: Now the shelves. Old Testament first.

S1: Four groups, and say them out loud once because you'll use them every time you open the book. Law, five books, Genesis to Deuteronomy. History, twelve, Joshua to Esther. Poetry and Wisdom, five: Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs. And Prophets, seventeen, Isaiah to Malachi.

S2: Five, twelve, five, seventeen.

S1: Thirty nine.

S2: A word about names, before we get to the Jewish arrangement.

S1: This course says Old Testament, because that's the Christian name for the first and larger part of the Bible. Jews call the same books the Tanakh, and for them it isn't an old anything. It's Scripture, read on its own terms, in its own order, in synagogues this week, and not a prelude to anything. When this course talks about how Jews read these books, it uses their words.

S2: Right. The Jewish arrangement.

S1: The Tanakh, which is what Jesus was describing in Luke twenty four. Tanakh is an acronym. Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim. Law, Prophets, Writings. Torah is the same five. Nevi'im, the Prophets, is eight, and it includes Joshua, Judges, Samuel and Kings, which a Christian Bible files under History. Ketuvim, the Writings, is eleven.

S2: Five, eight, eleven. Twenty four.

S1: The same books. Samuel, Kings and Chronicles are each one book instead of two, the twelve short prophets are one, and Ezra and Nehemiah are one. Twenty four, or thirty nine. One library, two ways of counting the shelves.

S2: Here's the question I'd never have thought to ask. The Christian Old Testament ends with Malachi. What ends the Tanakh?

S1: Chronicles. With one honest wrinkle: the two oldest complete Masoretic manuscripts put Chronicles at the front of the Writings rather than the end, so the arrangement was never quite uniform. But every printed Jewish Bible you can buy puts it last. So read the two last words side by side. A Jewish reader closes the Bible on Cyrus. Whosoever there is among you of all his people, the Lord his God be with him, let him go up. A Christian reader closes the Old Testament four books earlier in the same collection. Look, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the great and terrible day of the Lord arrives.

S2: One sends you to Jerusalem to build.

S1: And the other tells you to watch for someone coming. Same books, two endings, two different next steps.

S2: And the New Testament shelves?

S1: Four again, and simpler. Gospels, four. Acts, one. Letters, twenty one. Revelation, one. Four, one, twenty one, one. Twenty seven. And nothing there is in date order. The order is by kind. It's a library, not a timeline, and the shelf tells you what kind of thing you're holding before you open it.

S2: Last thing, and it's the one that surprised me most.

S1: The numbers in the margins. Nobody who wrote the Bible ever saw one. The chapter divisions are usually credited to Stephen Langton, later Archbishop of Canterbury, working in Paris in the early thirteenth century. Verse numbers came about three and a half centuries after that, from a Paris printer called Robert Estienne, in his Greek New Testament of fifteen fifty one. The Geneva Bible of fifteen sixty was the first complete English Bible to carry them.

S2: So they're a finding aid.

S1: And a very good one. But they aren't units of meaning. A verse often isn't a sentence. A chapter often cuts across a section. Treat the numbers as the address of a line, not as a claim about where a thought begins and ends.
