S2: So a genre is?

S1: A contract between a writer and a reader about what the sentences are for. The writer signs it by choosing a form, and you sign it by recognising the form and switching your expectations. The first readers had that reflex for free. We have to learn it on purpose, as a set of rules.

S2: You said rules. Start with the main one.

S1: Fee and Stuart call it the basic rule, and their book has been the standard first book on this in evangelical colleges and seminaries for forty years. A text cannot mean what it never could have meant to its author or readers.

S2: Say what that does and doesn't do.

S1: It doesn't say a text can only mean one thing, and it doesn't say a text has nothing to say to you. It sets a boundary. Whatever you take from a passage, it must be something the writer and the first readers could have recognised. If Paul could not have meant it, and the Corinthians could not have heard it, it isn't there.

S2: Test it on something.

S1: Someone reads, I can do all things through Christ who strengthens me, as a promise that God will help them win their match. Put that in front of the Philippians. Paul is in prison, thanking a poor church for money, and the sentence just before it is about knowing how to be full and how to be hungry.

S2: So could they have heard, you will win?

S1: No. Could they have heard, I have learned to cope with either? Yes, because that's the sentence he just wrote. So the sports reading fails the test and the coping reading passes it. And notice what the rule did and did not do. It ruled a meaning out. It didn't say the verse has nothing to say to somebody about to play a match.

S2: Before we go on, is that rule itself contested?

S1: And the lesson says so, which I think is the right thing to do. The three textbooks it leans on for this, Osborne, Vanhoozer, and Duvall and Hays, all come off one shelf. That meaning is what an author did with words, and that we can get at it, is a position rather than a fact. The philosophers Gadamer and Ricoeur, and the reader response critics after them, say meaning happens in the meeting between a text and a reader and can't be pinned to a dead man's intention. A Catholic reader will say authorial intention is necessary and not sufficient, because the Second Vatican Council's constitution on divine revelation also requires it to be read within the living tradition of the whole church. A critical scholar will say that for most Old Testament narrative there's no single author to recover, only sources and editors.

S2: And the course's own position?

S1: Is the one those three books take, because we think a text that can mean anything protects nobody. And you should know we hold it, rather than found it.

S2: And the rules for reading one?

S1: There are ten, and three of them add up to the habit that matters. Narratives record what happened, not necessarily what should have happened. What people do in a narrative is not necessarily a good example, and frequently it's the opposite. And we aren't always told at the end whether what happened was good or bad, so we're expected to judge from what God has taught plainly elsewhere.
