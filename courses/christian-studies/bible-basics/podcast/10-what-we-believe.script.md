S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in the course called The Bible: What It Is and How to Read It, lesson ten. What we believe about this book, and what others make of it.

S1: And this is where the course names the tool it's used since lesson one. Every claim about the Bible is one of three kinds.

S2: Give me them.

S1: Established by history: things the evidence has settled and experts accept whatever their religion. No record of the Council of Nicaea mentions the canon. A Muslim, an atheist and a Baptist give you the same answer.

S2: Second.

S1: Argued from evidence: questions where serious, informed people disagree because the evidence is real but doesn't settle it. Who wrote the Pentateuch, and when. There the honest move is to give each side's case and say what would settle it.

S2: And third.

S1: Held by faith: claims that evidence bears on but cannot decide. That the Bible is God's Word. A Christian holds those, says so, and doesn't pretend they came from a dig.

S2: Why does the sort help?

S1: Because most quarrels about the Bible are between someone asserting a faith claim as if it were history, the Bible is God's Word so Moses wrote it, and someone denying a history claim because of a faith disagreement, the Bible is a human book so David never existed. Both skipped the sort. Name which kind is on the table, and the first has to make an argument instead of an assertion, and the second has to go and look at the stele.

S2: So what does this course believe?

S1: This is the one place in the course where it says so in full, in the first person, and builds it as an argument rather than an assertion. We believe the Bible is God's Word: that in these books God speaks, and that what they teach is true. We hold that by faith. Here's why.

S1: First, because Jesus did. Every Gospel shows him treating the Scriptures of Israel as God's own word. In an argument with opponents he says, in passing, that the scripture cannot be broken. So if you follow Jesus, you have a reason to receive the book he received, the way he received it. That's the argument, and the lesson says it finds it strong.

S2: And the objections to that?

S1: Two standard ones, and you should have both. The first is circularity: we know Jesus' view of Scripture from the Gospels, so the argument leans on the books it's used to justify. The reply is that it needs only the Gospels' general reliability as sources, a far weaker claim than inspiration, and one many non Christian historians grant.

S2: And the second?

S1: Accommodation. That Jesus spoke of the book of Moses the way his hearers spoke, without that settling who wrote it, as he spoke of the sun rising. The reply is that some of his uses aren't incidental but load bearing, where an argument turns on what the text says. The lesson says neither objection is silly, and that this course does not treat Jesus saying the book of Moses as settling who wrote it.

S2: What are the other reasons?

S1: The apostles treated their own writings the same way. You met Second Peter in lesson eight, ranking Paul's letters with the rest of the scriptures. The same letter says of the prophets that no prophecy was ever borne of human impulse, rather, men carried along by the Holy Spirit spoke from God. That the church has always received it so, which is one of the few things here that's established rather than believed. And last, and least transferable, that people who have read this book for years and lived by it report that it does what it says it does. That's testimony, not proof, and the lesson includes it because leaving it out would misdescribe why most Christians believe as they do.

S2: Christians don't all mean the same thing by that.

S1: They don't, and the lesson sets out six positions, mostly quoted from their own reference texts rather than from their opponents. What's worth saying here is what they share. Every one holds that Scripture is inspired by God, and was written by human authors using the conventions of their day. Every one says you must read it by its genre. And every one holds that it's authoritative for what Christians believe and do. The disagreement is narrower than the noise suggests, and it's usually about scope: what without error ranges over.

S2: Last thing. The view from outside.

S1: The course promised you'd hear that at full strength from its own teachers. Christine Hayes, who taught Yale's introduction to the Hebrew Bible, opens by saying religious faith simply isn't the topic of this course. And: no one in this course wishes to undermine or malign religious faith any more than they wish to promote or proselytize for it.

S2: So what's the method?

S1: That the Bible is a library of ancient documents, written and edited over centuries by many hands, and can be read the way any ancient library is read. Whether God speaks in it is a question the method neither asks nor answers, because it's built to give a Jew, a Christian, a Muslim and an atheist a common ground. That is not a claim that the Bible is false. It's a decision about which questions a classroom can answer.

S2: And the results?

S1: It would be dishonest, the lesson says, to leave you with the disclaimers and none of the results. So. That Isaiah has at least two authors writing centuries apart. That the Pastoral letters and Second Peter were written in the names of men already dead. That Daniel was written in the second century before Christ and not the sixth. That the Gospels were published anonymously. That the exodus and the conquest did not happen as Joshua describes them. Those are argued rather than established, and this course takes the other side of several. But they are the conclusions, and some scholars hold they count decisively against any doctrine that calls this book God's Word. Bart Ehrman is the best known of them, and the lesson says that if you want that case you should take it from him at full length, rather than from a summary written by people who disagree with him.

S2: And the thing people get wrong about the people who hold them?

S1: Three things follow, and the lesson says its misconception list depends on all three. First, that critical scholars are atheists is wrong as a description. Raymond Brown, whose introduction is the Catholic critical standard, was a priest. Enns writes from inside evangelicalism. Hayes is a scholar of Judaism. The method brackets faith. It doesn't require its absence.

S2: Second.

S1: They disagree with each other at least as much as with the church, and say so. Hayes on the documentary hypothesis, the method's most famous result: it needs to be remembered that the documentary hypothesis is only a hypothesis. An important and a useful one. But none of the sources posited by critical scholars has been found independently. And she adds that most biblical scholars today accept some version of it, though not with tremendous confidence in the finer details.

S2: And third.

S1: That the results sort into the same three columns as everything else. That Paul's letters are first century is established. That the Pentateuch is built from four written sources is argued. And whether God speaks in the book is the question the method has declined.

S2: We've left out two traditions outside Christianity that hold a doctrine of these same books, Judaism and Islam, each in its own terms. That the three categories don't stay put, and what moves a claim between them. The scholarly fight over whether the church always held inerrancy under other words. The four words the positions turn on, the six positions in their own reference texts, and where this course places itself among them. The whole list of what people get wrong. Two worked examples, one on the mustard seed, where every position reads the passage the same way, and one on the death of Moses. What archaeology has and hasn't done. And a practice set. The full lesson has all of it, free, at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Name which kind of claim is on the table, and the argument gets better.
