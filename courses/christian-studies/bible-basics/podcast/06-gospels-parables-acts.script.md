S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in the course called The Bible: What It Is and How to Read It, lesson six. Reading the Gospels, the parables, and Acts.

S1: It opens with two verses most people who know the prodigal son have never read. Luke fifteen. Now all the tax collectors and sinners were coming to hear him. But the Pharisees and the experts in the law were complaining, this man welcomes sinners and eats with them.

S2: And then the story.

S1: Most people know it as a picture of a sinner coming home to a forgiving father, and that isn't wrong. But it stops two thirds of the way through, at the party, and never asks who Jesus was talking to. Luke tells you. Read it with those people in front of you and the last character to appear, the older brother who won't come in, turns out to be the one the story was built to reach.

S2: A word about those people.

S1: Worth pausing on, because centuries of Christian preaching have flattened them into hypocrites. The Pharisees were a renewal movement. They took the holiness the Torah asked of priests at the temple and tried to live it at ordinary tables, which is why who you ate with mattered so much to them. Their heirs are the rabbis, and so rabbinic Judaism, and so every Jew alive today. When the lesson says a parable was aimed at them, it means aimed at serious people with a serious reason for their objection, which is the only way a trap of that kind works.

S2: So what's the claim?

S1: That the Gospels are the part of the Bible most people think they already understand, because the stories are familiar, and familiarity is the problem. Three habits fix most of it. Knowing what kind of book a Gospel is. Hearing who a parable was aimed at. And telling what Acts reports from what it commands.

S2: Start with the kind of book.

S1: Which means starting with what the writers say about their own work. John ends his Gospel by telling you he selected, and gives his reason: so that you may believe. Luke opens with a formal preface naming his sources and his reader.

S2: So none of the four is a transcript?

S1: None of the four claims to be one. The best answer to what they are, and the majority view since Richard Burridge's study in nineteen ninety two, is that they're ancient biographies with a theological aim.

S2: What did an ancient biography do?

S1: It told the life of a significant person to show his character and commend a way of living. It didn't have to run in strict chronological order, and it was happy to arrange material by topic. That's the genre contract from lesson three, and it matters, because a reader who brings the contract of a modern documentary to one of these will find contradictions the first readers would never have noticed.

S2: Give me one.

S1: John puts the cleansing of the Temple at the start of Jesus' ministry. Matthew, Mark and Luke put it in the last week. A reader with the documentary contract sees an error and has to choose. Force the two into one, or decide the Bible has a mistake in it.

S2: And genre dissolves it?

S1: Ancient biography arranged by topic, and John has reasons of his own for opening with the Temple. And here's the part to have if somebody tells you this is a concession to critics. The evangelical inerrantists who wrote the Chicago Statement in nineteen seventy eight said so in their own document. Inerrancy is not negated by the topical arrangement of materials, or by variant selections of material in parallel accounts.

S2: So reading by genre isn't a concession.

S1: It's what the inerrantists say they do. And the same signatories drew a line, which it's fair to give. Their separate statement on interpretation, four years later, denies that generic categories, meaning categories of genre, which negate historicity may be imposed on narratives that present themselves as factual. So on their own account genre latitude isn't a licence to reclassify a narrative as something that never claimed to have happened. Where ancient biography's conventions sit against that line is argued among evangelicals, sometimes sharply.

S2: Right. Acts.

S1: Acts is narrative, so everything from lesson three applies. It reports what happened rather than what should have happened. But it raises a question the Old Testament stories don't, because Acts is about the church and you are in the church. If the first Christians did something, must we?

S2: And the answer?

S1: Fee and Stuart's, whose handbook this course follows. What Acts narrates or describes does not function as a command unless Scripture elsewhere commands it, or unless Luke can be shown to have meant it as a pattern. So the test has two parts. Is this practice commanded elsewhere? And is it consistent across the book, or does it change from story to story?

S2: Run it on something.

S1: Acts two says the first believers held everything in common and sold property to give to anyone in need. Is that commanded elsewhere? Generosity to the poor is, everywhere, from Deuteronomy to the letters, so the principle is binding. Selling all property into a common fund is commanded nowhere. And in Acts five Peter tells Ananias the land was his own before he sold it, and the money at his disposal afterwards, which only makes sense if it was voluntary.

S2: And consistent across the book?

S1: Paul's churches in the letters own property and are told to give freely and proportionately, rather than to pool everything. So the generosity is a rule, and the common fund is a description of one church's response.

S2: So a reader who turned every report into a rule.

S1: Would build a church that pools all its property. The lesson runs the test on a second case, choosing leaders by lot, which comes out the same way and leaves that reader explaining why Acts itself stops doing both.

S2: Does the test settle everything?

S1: No, and here is where it doesn't. In Acts eight the Samaritans believe and are baptised, and the Spirit comes on them only later, when the apostles arrive. In Acts ten Cornelius and his household receive the Spirit while Peter is still preaching, before baptism. In Acts nineteen some disciples at Ephesus are baptised and receive the Spirit when Paul lays hands on them.

S2: Three sequences.

S1: Pentecostal and charismatic Christians read these as showing that receiving the Spirit can be a distinct event after conversion, and they have a serious case, because Luke plainly records it happening three times. Most Reformed, Lutheran, Catholic and Orthodox readers hold that these were unique moments as the gospel crossed each new boundary, to Samaritans, to Gentiles, to disciples of John, and that the letters teach the Spirit is given at conversion. They also have a serious case.

S2: So what does the test give?

S1: That Luke isn't laying down a sequence, since he gives three. What he is showing you is argued, at full strength, on both sides. That's a real disagreement between Christians who love the same book, and a lesson on reading Acts should say so rather than pick a side and pretend the test settled it.

S2: We've left out whether the Temple was cleansed once or twice, and why the lesson says the choice isn't a test of faith. Whether a lot is still a legitimate way to choose, which one church still does and another did for two hundred years. Why the four Gospels are not four independent witnesses, which is in the section on what people get wrong, and the question of who used whom among the first three. The whole middle of the lesson: how a parable works, the trap inside one, the hard verse about why Jesus spoke in them, what allegorising does to them, and the prodigal son read twice. Also who wrote the Gospels and when, with the critical mainstream and the conservative case each in its own words and what both sides agree on. And an exercise on Mark four, three storms, and Acts six. The full lesson has all of it, free, at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Ask what kind of book you're in, and don't turn a report into a rule.
