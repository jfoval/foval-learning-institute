S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in the course called The Bible: What It Is and How to Read It, lesson twelve, the last one. A way of reading you can keep: method, tools, and a plan.

S1: Two scenes, about five hundred years apart. In the first, Jerusalem's returned exiles stand in a square from early morning until midday while Ezra reads the Law aloud, and thirteen named Levites explain it, so that the people gained understanding from what was read.

S2: And the second?

S1: A synagogue in Berea hears Paul preach and then, rather than taking his word for it, opens the Scriptures to check. These Jews were more open minded than those in Thessalonica, for they eagerly received the message, examining the scriptures carefully every day to see if these things were so.

S2: Put them together.

S1: And you have the whole lesson. Reading with the sense given, so what you read is what the writer meant. And reading daily, on a schedule, with other people, so it keeps happening once the enthusiasm has gone.

S2: So what do I carry away from eleven lessons?

S1: Four questions, some free tools, and a plan. You've been asking the questions since lesson two. First: what kind of writing is this? Story, law, poem, proverb, oracle, gospel, letter, vision. That's lesson three's rule, genre first, because genres are contracts and the wrong contract misreads every sentence while feeling perfectly clear.

S2: Second.

S1: Where in the story is it? Which act, which covenant is in force, who is speaking to whom. Meaning depends on position: a promise to exiles is not a promise to graduates.

S2: Third.

S1: What did it mean then? To the person who wrote it and the people who first heard it. Fee and Stuart's basic rule: a text cannot mean what it never could have meant to its author or readers.

S2: And fourth.

S1: What does it mean now? Where our situation matches theirs, the word applies as written. Where it doesn't, find the principle the particular carries, and be honest about which is which.

S2: Why four and not forty?

S1: Look back at every misreading this course has shown you. Jeremiah twenty nine, eleven on the card skipped question two. Proverbs twenty two, six as a guarantee skipped question one. Philippians four, thirteen on the gym wall skipped question three. Head coverings, applied or dismissed without argument, skipped question four. The lesson says it hasn't found a misreading in this course that didn't skip at least one of them, and hasn't found one that survived all four.

S2: How do I actually answer them?

S1: Read outward from the verse in circles. Take Philippians four, thirteen. The sentence says, I am able to do all things through the one who strengthens me. The paragraph says the all things are hunger and plenty, abundance and need. The book is a letter from prison to a church that sent money. The testament tells you what in Christ means across Paul's letters. And the whole Bible tells you where contentment sits in the story.

S2: And by which circle is the gym wall gone?

S1: By the third, and nothing outside the page was needed to remove it. Now the hard one. Psalm one thirty seven, nine, which is a wish for the death of Babylon's children.

S2: Go.

S1: The sentence is that wish. The paragraph is a lament sung by people who had watched it done to theirs. The book is Israel's prayer book, so this is spoken to God, not commanded by him. The testament supplies the covenant curses it echoes. And the whole Bible supplies the line about leaving vengeance to God, which is what the psalmist is doing.

S2: Does that make it easy?

S1: No, and the lesson says so. Five circles, and the verse is still terrible, and it's no longer an endorsement. The circles aren't a formula for making hard verses easy. They're a way of making sure you've read the whole of what's there before you decide what it means. Most of the misreadings this course has named came from stopping at circle one.

S2: Tools.

S1: You don't need Hebrew or Greek to read the Bible well. You need the four questions and a few free tools, and you need to know which tool answers which question, because the commonest mistake is reaching for a Greek lexicon to answer a question the paragraph would have answered.

S2: The mapping.

S1: For the shape of a book, an animated overview, free, watched after your first read as a check. For the date and setting, a study Bible, read knowingly, because its notes are one scholar's help, dated, written from a position, and not Scripture. For seeing where translations differ, a site that puts dozens side by side, because a difference is a place where a decision was made. For the decision behind the words, the NET Bible, which shows its working in more than sixty thousand notes. And for the word under the English, free sites that put the tagged Hebrew and Greek beneath it.

S2: Last thing. The plan.

S1: One figure first. How long does it take to read the whole Bible at an ordinary pace?

S2: I'd guess a couple of hundred hours.

S1: About seventy. Crossway surveyed more than eleven thousand readers and worked out that in just twelve minutes per day you could read the whole Bible in a year, which comes to about seventy three hours.

S2: And seventy hours once is a lot.

S1: Twelve minutes a day is not, and the difference between those two ways of spending seventy hours is why a plan exists. A little on a schedule beats a lot once, mostly because it happens at all, and because a book read across two weeks accumulates context that a book read in one evening does not.

S2: Which plan?

S1: The one you'll actually keep, and the man who wrote the most demanding plan in common use said so himself. M'Cheyne drew up a calendar for his congregation in eighteen forty two: four chapters a day, the Old Testament once and the New Testament and Psalms twice in the year. Before the calendar he listed the dangers of using it, and the fourth was a yoke too heavy to bear. If this be the case with any, he wrote, throw aside the fetter, and feed at liberty in the sweet garden of God.

S2: That's the author telling you to drop it.

S1: The moment it becomes a burden. Which is the measure: a plan you admire and don't keep is worth less than a chapter a day you do. And if you're new, or coming back, the lesson would start you on one Gospel, a chapter a day, then the next.

S2: And the other half of those opening scenes.

S1: Reading together. M'Cheyne wanted that all might be feeding in the same portion of the green pasture at the same time, and among the advantages he listed was that friends would have a subject for profitable conversation in the portions read that day. Private reading was rare for most of the church's history, because copies were expensive and few could read. That's a fact about literacy and cost rather than a rule, and Acts has a man reading Isaiah by himself in a chariot. But the practice the Bible mostly shows is shared. Find one other person and read the same book on the same days.
