S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in the course called The Bible: What It Is and How to Read It, lesson five. Reading poetry, the Psalms, and wisdom.

S1: And it opens with two verses that sit side by side in the same paragraph of Proverbs twenty six. Do not answer a fool according to his folly, lest you yourself also be like him. And then: answer a fool according to his folly, lest he be wise in his own opinion.

S2: One after the other. So which is correct?

S1: They're both correct, and the placement is the point. Whoever put Proverbs together set two opposite instructions next to each other on purpose. A proverb isn't a rule that covers every case. It's a compressed observation about how things usually go, and it leaves you to judge which case you're in.

S2: And if I read either one alone, as a command?

S1: Then you'd be doing the thing the book is built to teach you not to do. That pair is the lesson in miniature.

S2: So start with how a Hebrew poem is built.

S1: Because everything follows from it. Hebrew poetry doesn't rhyme sounds. It rhymes ideas. A poet says something in one line, then says it again in a second line that repeats, sharpens, contrasts or completes it. Psalm nineteen: the heavens declare the glory of God, the sky displays his handiwork. Day after day it speaks out, night after night it reveals his greatness.

S2: Heavens and sky. Declare and display.

S1: Glory and handiwork. The second line isn't adding a new fact about a different object, and the second pair adds that the speech goes on day and night, without pause. Fee and Stuart, whose textbook this course follows, put it plainly. Those four lines aren't trying to say four different things, and the poet isn't saying the heavens do one thing and the skies another. Together the two lines speak of one glorious reality.

S2: Does the pairing have a name?

S1: Parallelism, and the name comes from Robert Lowth, Professor of Poetry at Oxford and later Bishop of London, who published his lectures on Hebrew poetry in seventeen fifty three. He sorted the pairs into three kinds, by whether the second line restates the first, contrasts with it, or carries the thought forward. Synonymous, antithetic, synthetic.

S2: Are those labels safe to use?

S1: Useful and a little misleading. In nineteen eighty one James Kugel argued that synonymous lines are almost never simply synonymous. The second line seconds the first, and his shorthand for it is: A, and what's more, B.

S2: Test it on the psalm.

S1: Declare the glory of God is general. Displays his handiwork makes it specific, the sky as evidence of a maker. The second line is usually where the emphasis lands. So the habit is this. Whenever you meet a pair of lines, ask what the second one adds. Not what line B means on its own, and not, these two say the same thing so I can skip one. But: what's more.

S2: The second thing.

S1: Is that the vocabulary is, in Fee and Stuart's phrase, purposefully metaphorical. In the Psalms the mountains skip like rams, and God is a shepherd, a rock, a fortress, a shield. None of that is decoration on top of a plain statement. It's how the statement is made.

S2: And the mistake?

S1: Two directions. One is to press the picture and read Psalm twenty three as a recommendation of rural life. The other is to strip the picture out and keep a proposition. Fee and Stuart offer, God is revealed in his creation, as a prose rendering of those four lines of Psalm nineteen, and call it totally colorless. It's shorter, and that's the point.

S2: Say what the poem does that the sentence doesn't.

S1: It makes you supply what the sentence hands over. That the sky is evidence, because declare and display are what a witness does. That the evidence is continuous, because day follows night follows day. And that the one being testified to is a maker, because handiwork is a word about hands. The prose sentence asserts all of that and shows none of it.

S2: And the supplying is the reading.

S1: Which is why poetry compresses. A prose writer spells out the connections; a poet leaves them for you. Read a psalm as a list of propositions and you strip out the part where the meaning lives.

S2: The other half. Wisdom.

S1: Wisdom, in the Bible's sense, is the skill of making good choices in a world God has ordered. Proverbs is its plainest form: short sayings about how life usually goes. Fee and Stuart draw the consequence: the briefer a statement is, the less likely it is to be totally precise and universally applicable.

S1: Which is how English proverbs work. Look before you leap isn't about jumping, and everyone handles that correctly by instinct. The trouble starts when the same kind of sentence appears in the Bible and the instinct switches off, because if it's in the Bible it must be a promise.

S2: The first rule?

S1: Fee and Stuart give four guidelines, and the first is the one to memorise. Proverbs are not legal guarantees from God. The blessings in the book are likely to follow wise choices, but nowhere does Proverbs teach automatic success.

S2: The verse that's done most damage.

S1: Proverbs twenty two, six. Train a child in the way that he should go, and when he is old he will not turn from it.

S2: Read as a guarantee to parents.

S1: It produces two kinds of damage. Parents whose grown children have left the faith conclude they failed, or that God did. And parents whose children stayed conclude it was their doing.

S2: And read well?

S1: As a proverb. A child shaped early in a good direction usually keeps to it, and that's a good reason to do the shaping. It isn't a mechanism that overrides the grown child's own choices. And the same book puts those two opposite instructions about the fool side by side, which tells you its sayings are situational.

S2: Now someone will say that's a convenient way to explain away the verses that don't come true.

S1: And it's worth asking whether anything in the Bible itself treats proverbs this way, or whether it's a modern reader's escape hatch. There is, and it's the sharpest thing in the wisdom books. Job's three friends spend most of that book quoting wisdom at him. Suffering follows sin. Much of it could be lifted straight out of Proverbs.

S2: And at the end?

S1: God speaks, and says to Eliphaz: my anger is stirred up against you and your two friends, because you have not spoken about me what is right, as my servant Job has. The friends took true generalisations and applied them as guarantees to a case they didn't fit, and God says they haven't spoken rightly of him.

S2: So the rule isn't a modern escape hatch.

S1: That's how the lesson reads Job forty two, seven, and the textbook reads it the same way. One fair caution, which the lesson makes itself: the verse says they haven't spoken rightly about God, and readers differ on whether the fault was mainly their theology or mainly their misuse of a form. The two are hard to separate, because the form they misused was a form about how God runs the world.

S2: We've left out the Psalms as a shelf, and their types. Two psalms read badly and then well, and the second is the hardest thing in the lesson: the psalms that ask God to do terrible things to enemies. The lesson gives the case for leaving them out of public prayer at full strength, says this course keeps them and prays them, and says you should know both before deciding. The Jewish readings, in their own terms, including where Ecclesiastes is read at a feast of joy. Job's frame, and the one place where the lesson's own rule goes quiet. Ecclesiastes, and a real disagreement between two commentators who are close friends, where the course says which side it holds. And the one thing in Hebrew poetry that doesn't survive translation. The full lesson has all of it, free, at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Ask what the second line adds, and don't turn a pattern into a promise.
