S1: And I'm John. Today we're in the course called The Bible: What It Is and How to Read It, lesson nine. How the text reached us, and how reliable it is.

S1: Open Mark at the end. Chapter sixteen, verse eight, where the women run from the tomb and say nothing to anyone. Now look at what your Bible prints next.

S2: There's a break in the page, and a note.

S1: Usually in square brackets, telling you that some of the earliest manuscripts don't include verses nine to twenty. Most readers skip past it. But stop and think what it's saying. The translators are telling you that the next twelve verses, which include the risen Jesus appearing to Mary and the ascension, are in some manuscripts and not others, and that the ones missing them are among the oldest we have.

S2: And that's the lesson.

S1: Lesson eight told you which books are in the Bible. It said nothing about whether the words in them are the words their authors wrote. Between a letter Paul dictated in the fifties and the Bible on your table lie about fourteen centuries of copying by hand.

S2: Start with what everybody says. It's been translated so many times you can't trust it.

S1: The picture behind it is a chain. Hebrew to Greek to Latin to English, each link copying the last, errors piling up like a game of telephone. That is not how a modern Bible is made. The translators sat down with a printed Hebrew text and a printed Greek text and translated directly from them.

S2: How many links?

S1: Two. Manuscripts to edition, and edition to your Bible.

S2: Is there anything true in the myth?

S1: One thing. The King James translators in sixteen eleven worked from a Greek text resting on a handful of late manuscripts, which is why your Bible and a King James differ at First John five, seven. That's about the manuscripts, not about translation.

S2: How does hand copying go wrong?

S1: The standard textbook, by Bruce Metzger and Bart Ehrman together, sorts the mistakes into four kinds. Errors of the eye, when two lines end with the same word and the scribe's eye jumps from one to the other, dropping everything between. Errors of the ear, and errors of memory. And errors of judgement, when a scribe thought the text in front of him was wrong and fixed it.

S2: And the ending of Mark is which?

S1: Judgement. A copyist thought the book stopped too abruptly and supplied a finish. And here's the sentence to keep, because it answers nearly every objection you'll hear. Many independent copies are better than one perfect one.

S2: Say why.

S1: When a scribe in Alexandria makes a mistake, it travels down to the copies made from his copy. It doesn't appear in a manuscript copied in Rome from a different exemplar, unless it was already in the text both were copying. So when copies diverge, the divergence tells you where an error entered. Telephone is one chain and you only hear the last whisper. This is a tree with branches you can compare. More manuscripts help because there's more to compare. Older ones help because fewer generations separate them from the source. And widely spread ones help because an error made in one place is exposed by copies made elsewhere.

S2: And when the evidence is evenly matched?

S1: One criterion does most of the work, and it's worth learning by name. Prefer the harder reading. Copying has a direction. A scribe who meets rough grammar or a saying that sounds wrong tends to smooth it, and almost nobody roughens a smooth text on purpose. So when one reading is difficult and the other comfortable, the difficult one is usually older, and the comfortable one is the repair.

S2: Now the number people quote. How many variants are there?

S1: About four hundred thousand. A variant is any place where one manuscript differs from another, and that looseness is why it gets misused.

S2: Whose number is it?

S1: Daniel Wallace's, and Ehrman gives a range of two hundred thousand to four hundred thousand. They count the same way: a difference counts once, however many manuscripts carry it.

S2: And what does that imply?

S1: That the more manuscripts you have, the more differences you'll find, because every fresh copy brings its own slips. A text with thousands of manuscripts will have vastly more variants than a text with ten. A large count of variants is what abundant evidence looks like, not what corruption looks like.

S2: Say the sharp version.

S1: If we had one manuscript, we'd have zero variants and no way to know whether it was any good. A text with no variants is a text with no evidence.

S2: So sort them.

S1: Wallace's categories, in his own words. Spelling and nonsense readings are the vast majority, accounting for at least seventy five percent of all variants. Then differences that don't change the meaning in translation, like Greek word order. Then differences that would change it but have no chance of being original, because they sit in one late manuscript against everything else. And last, in his words: the smallest category by far is the last category, meaningful and viable variants. These comprise less than one percent of all textual variants.

S2: Less than one percent of four hundred thousand is still thousands of places.

S1: Which is why the lesson says don't let the small percentage do too much work either. Mark sixteen is one.

S2: And the two of them disagree about what all this means.

S1: Publicly, and both their names are on that textbook. So take each in his own terms. Ehrman, the agnostic, writing on his own blog to his own readers: none of your cherished doctrines appears to be in real danger because of variations in our surviving manuscripts, at least the variations that we know about. The parenthesis is his.

S1: Wallace, the evangelical, on the meaningful and viable variants: even here, no cardinal belief is at stake. These variants do affect what a particular passage teaches, and thus what the Bible says in that place, but they do not jeopardize essential beliefs.

S2: So where's the argument?

S1: Ehrman's second point, which the lesson takes at full strength. He asks why doctrines should be the measure at all. Why is it, he writes, that threats to significant doctrines are taken to be the only standard of what is important to the Christian religion? In my view, he says, this idea is completely wrong headed.

S2: Give me one of his examples.

S1: At Mark chapter one, verse forty one, the manuscripts have Jesus either moved with compassion or angry as he heals the leper, and that decides what kind of figure Mark is drawing. That doesn't overturn a creed. It changes what one book teaches.

S2: And where does the course land?

S1: It says it agrees with Ehrman about that, and asks you to hold both men's points together, because they don't conflict. The text is recoverable to a very high degree. No article of the creeds rests on a disputed reading. And there are a few thousand places where it matters to a careful reader which reading is right. All three are true. A Christian who says only the first two has left out something the reader deserves to know, and so has a sceptic who says only the third.
