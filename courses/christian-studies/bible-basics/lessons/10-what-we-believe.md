> "All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness, that the man of God may be complete, equipped for every good work." (2 Timothy 3:16–17, ESV)[1]

This is the verse most Christians reach for when someone asks what they believe about the Bible. It's a good verse to reach for. But look at how much it leaves open. "All Scripture": which Scripture? Paul is writing to Timothy about "the sacred writings" Timothy knew from childhood, which means the Hebrew Bible, decades before anyone had bound the Gospels with the letters. "Breathed out by God": breathed how? Dictated? Prompted? Superintended? The verse doesn't say. And then, having made the claim, it turns immediately to what the book is *for*: teaching, correcting, training. It's a verse about origin and purpose, and it's silent on almost every question people fight about.

That silence is where this lesson lives. Lessons 8 and 9 gave you the history: which books, and which words. This lesson asks what to make of it. It has three jobs. First, to say what this course believes about the Bible, once, plainly, and why. Second, to lay out the range of what Christians believe, in each tradition's own words, so that an inerrantist, a Catholic, an Orthodox Christian, a Presbyterian, and a scholar who teaches the Bible at Yale could each read their paragraph and say "yes, that's mine". Third, to hand you the sorting tool the course has used since lesson 1 and let you use it on your own.

## The tool: three kinds of claim

You've watched this course say *history establishes*, *scholars argue*, and *we hold by faith* in every lesson since the first. Time to name it as a method, because it's the single most useful habit you can take from this course into any argument about the Bible.

Every claim about the Bible is one of three kinds.

**Established by history.** Things the evidence has settled and that experts accept whatever their religion. Paul's letters were written in the first century. A ninth-century inscription from Tel Dan names a "House of David". No record of the Council of Nicaea mentions the canon. You can look these up, and a Muslim, an atheist, and a Baptist will give you the same answer.

**Argued from evidence.** Questions where serious, informed people disagree because the evidence is real but doesn't settle it. Who wrote the Pentateuch, and when. Whether Mark was written before or after the Temple fell. How big David's kingdom was. Here the honest move is to give each side's case and say what would settle it.

**Held by faith.** Claims that evidence bears on but cannot decide, held on religious grounds. That the Bible is God's Word. That Isaiah 7:14 finds its fullest meaning in Jesus. That God acts in history at all. A Christian holds these, says so, and doesn't pretend they came out of a dig.

Here's the mechanism, and why the sort ends arguments rather than starting them. Most quarrels about the Bible are between someone asserting a faith claim as if it were history ("the Bible is God's Word, so Moses wrote it") and someone denying a history claim because of a faith disagreement ("the Bible is a human book, so David never existed"). Both have skipped the sort. Name which kind of claim is on the table, and the first person has to make an argument instead of an assertion, and the second has to look at the stele. The quarrels that survive the sort are the real ones, and they're fewer than you'd think.

One caution before you use it. The categories aren't fixed for ever. A claim can move from argued to established when a stele turns up, as "David existed" did in 1993. It can move the other way when a consensus cracks. And the boundary between argued and faith is itself sometimes argued: a Christian who thinks the resurrection is a historical claim with evidence behind it and a historian who thinks it lies outside what history can assess are disagreeing about which column it goes in. The sort doesn't settle that. It makes you say it out loud.

:::checkpoint Someone says, "The Gospels were written by eyewitnesses." Which kind of claim is that, and what would a critical scholar, a conservative scholar, and this course each say about it?
It's argued, not established and not faith. There's real evidence on both sides: Papias and Irenaeus in the second century naming the authors, the manuscript titles, the early dates some scholars defend, against the anonymity of the texts themselves, the later dates most critical scholars hold, and the question of what "eyewitness" means for Mark, whom nobody claims was one. A critical scholar (Ehrman's summary in lesson 6) says the names were attached in the second century and the writers were not eyewitnesses. A conservative scholar says the attributions are early and unanimous and that Mark and Luke wrote from apostolic sources. This course says it holds the traditional authorship as the better historical case, and that its trust in the Gospels doesn't depend on winning that argument (lesson 6). Notice what the sort did: it stopped anyone from saying "the Bible says so" or "scholars have shown" as if either ended the matter.
:::

## What we believe, and why

This is the one place in the course where we state our own view of the Bible in full. It's labelled, it's in the first person plural, and it's built as an argument rather than dropped as an assertion, because a faith claim is still a claim someone can ask you to defend.

We believe the Bible is God's Word: that in these books God speaks, and that what they teach is true. We hold that by faith. Here is why we hold it.

First, because Jesus did. Every Gospel shows him treating the Scriptures of Israel as God's own word and as authoritative for what he did and taught. On the road to Emmaus he says "everything written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled" (Luke 24:44).[2] In an argument with opponents he says, in passing, "Scripture cannot be broken" (John 10:35).[3] In the Sermon on the Mount: "until heaven and earth pass away, not an iota, not a dot, will pass from the Law until all is accomplished" (Matthew 5:18).[4] John Wenham's *Christ and the Bible*, the classic statement of this argument, puts it simply: a Christian's view of Scripture should start from Christ's view of Scripture, and Christ's view, on the Gospel evidence, was that it was true, authoritative, and from God.[5] If you follow Jesus, you have a reason to receive the book he received, in the way he received it. That's the whole argument, and we find it strong.

Second, because the apostles treated their own writings the same way. You met 2 Peter in lesson 8, ranking Paul's letters with "the other Scriptures" (2 Peter 3:16).[6] The same letter says of the prophets that "no prophecy was ever produced by the will of man, but men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit" (2 Peter 1:21).[7] And Paul, in the verse this lesson opened with, says Scripture is "breathed out by God".[1] These are the earliest Christian statements of what the Bible is, made from inside it.

Third, because the church has always received it so. This is one of the few things in this lesson that is established rather than believed: from Irenaeus to Athanasius to Aquinas to Luther to Trent, Christians of every tradition have called Scripture inspired by God and authoritative for faith. What they've meant in detail has varied, and we'll get to that. That they meant it has not.

Fourth, because we have found it true. This is the least transferable reason and the most honest one. People who have read this book for years, and lived by it, report that it does what 2 Timothy says it does: teaches, corrects, trains. That's testimony, not proof. We include it because leaving it out would misdescribe why most Christians actually believe what they believe.

That's our view. You'll not hear it repeated as a refrain; you've heard it now. What follows is the range of what Christians mean by it, and then what people outside the faith make of the same book.

## Four words, as the tradition uses them

Before the positions, the vocabulary, because the positions differ mostly in how they use these words.

**Inspiration** is the oldest of the four and the one every Christian tradition shares. It translates the Greek of 2 Timothy 3:16, *theopneustos*, "God-breathed". It says where Scripture comes from: God, through human writers. It doesn't by itself say how (dictation is one theory, and a minority one; most traditions say God worked through the writers' own minds, styles, and situations) or what follows for accuracy.

**Authority** says what Scripture is for: it's the rule. Christians disagree about whether it's the only rule (the Reformation's *sola scriptura*) or the supreme written rule alongside Tradition (the Catholic and Orthodox view), but every tradition says the church sits under it, not over it.

**Infallibility** says Scripture cannot fail. Fail at what? Here the word splits. In older usage it meant "cannot mislead", full stop. Since the 1970s it has often meant something narrower: Scripture cannot fail in its purpose, which is to teach faith and practice, and whether every incidental detail is accurate is a separate question. Fuller Seminary's statement of faith, adopted in 1972, says the Scriptures are "the written Word of God, the only infallible rule of faith and practice".[8] The Lausanne Covenant of 1974 says Scripture is "without error in all that it affirms, and the only infallible rule of faith and practice".[9] Read those two side by side and you'll see why the word needs a footnote: Lausanne's "without error in all that it affirms" is read by inerrantists as their own position and by others as a deliberate softening, and both readings have been defended by people who were in the room.

**Inerrancy** says Scripture contains no error. It's the strongest word and the most recent as a slogan. The Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy, drafted in 1978 by the International Council on Biblical Inerrancy, is its reference text.[10]

Which of these the church "always" held is itself a scholarly fight, and it's worth knowing because you'll meet both sides. In 1979 Jack Rogers and Donald McKim published *The Authority and Interpretation of the Bible: An Historical Approach*, arguing that the central Christian tradition, from Augustine through Calvin, held Scripture infallible in matters of faith and conduct while allowing that its historical and scientific details might not be precise, and that strict inerrancy was a later development.[11] In 1982 John Woodbridge answered with *Biblical Authority: A Critique of the Rogers/McKim Proposal*, arguing that the historical record shows the older writers held the Bible wholly true, details included, and that the Rogers and McKim reading imposed a modern distinction on them.[12] The Chicago Statement itself takes Woodbridge's side in advance: Article XVI affirms "that the doctrine of inerrancy has been integral to the Church's faith throughout its history" and denies that it "is a doctrine invented by scholastic Protestantism".[10]

What can be said as established? Two things. The church has always called Scripture inspired and authoritative and trusted it as true. And the word "inerrancy" as a banner, with a council and nineteen articles behind it, dates from the twentieth century. "The church invented inerrancy in 1978" gets the second half right and the first half wrong; "the church always used the word" gets the first half right and the second wrong. Whether the older writers held the *doctrine* under other words is what Rogers, McKim, and Woodbridge were arguing about, and the argument is argued.

## The range within Christianity, in each position's own words

Now the positions. For each, I've gone to the tradition's own statement and quoted it, so that you're reading what they say rather than what their opponents say they say. This course's own view, stated above, sits closest to the first two.

### Inerrancy: the Chicago Statement (1978)

:::predict The Chicago Statement is the strictest of the positions you'll read. Before you see its text, guess: does it allow the Bible to use round numbers, to describe the sun as rising, to arrange events out of chronological order, or to quote loosely? Or does strict inerrancy rule those out?
It allows all of them, by name. Article XIII is below. Most people who have only heard inerrancy described by its critics guess the other way.
:::

The Statement's Short Statement, point 4, gives the claim at full strength: "Being wholly and verbally God-given, Scripture is without error or fault in all its teaching, no less in what it states about God's acts in creation, about the events of world history, and about its own literary origins under God, than in its witness to God's saving grace in individual lives."[10] Article XII makes the scope explicit: it denies "that Biblical infallibility and inerrancy are limited to spiritual, religious, or redemptive themes, exclusive of assertions in the fields of history and science".[10] That is the sentence that separates inerrancy from the positions below. It says the "without error" ranges over everything the Bible asserts, history and science included.

Two articles keep this from being what its critics imagine. Article X locates inerrancy in the original text: "inspiration, strictly speaking, applies only to the autographic text of Scripture, which in the providence of God can be ascertained from available manuscripts with great accuracy", and copies and translations "are the Word of God to the extent that they faithfully represent the original".[10] So an inerrantist has no quarrel with lesson 9; the variants are in the copies, and the position never claimed the copies were perfect.

And Article XIII is the one to memorise. It denies "that it is proper to evaluate Scripture according to standards of truth and error that are alien to its usage or purpose", and it further denies "that inerrancy is negated by Biblical phenomena such as a lack of modern technical precision, irregularities of grammar or spelling, observational descriptions of nature, the reporting of falsehoods, the use of hyperbole and round numbers, the topical arrangement of material, variant selections of material in parallel accounts, or the use of free citations".[10] The Exposition spells out what that means for reading: "So history must be treated as history, poetry as poetry, hyperbole and metaphor as hyperbole and metaphor, generalization and approximation as what they are, and so forth."[10] Article XVIII requires "grammatico-historical exegesis, taking account of its literary forms and devices".[10]

So the inerrantist position, in its own words, is: everything Scripture asserts is true, *and* what Scripture asserts is determined by its genre, its conventions, and its purpose, not by a modern standard of precision it never aimed at. Genre first, the rule of lesson 3, is written into the inerrantists' own charter. An inerrantist who reads this paragraph should find nothing conceded and nothing softened.

One more article matters for later in this lesson. Article XVIII also denies "the legitimacy of any treatment of the text or quest for sources lying behind it that leads to relativizing, dehistoricizing, or discounting its teaching, or rejecting its claims to authorship".[10] Hold that; it's the sentence that decides how an inerrantist reads Deuteronomy 34.

### Infallibility: Fuller and Lausanne

The infallibilist position, as it's been called since Fuller Seminary revised its statement of faith in 1972, says Scripture is "the only infallible rule of faith and practice".[8] Its holders would put it like this: the Bible cannot fail in what God gave it to do, which is to teach us who God is, what he has done, and how to live. It is entirely trustworthy for that. Whether an incidental number in Kings matches the number in Chronicles is a different kind of question, which they are content to leave to the historians, and they think the older tradition (the Rogers and McKim reading) was content to leave it there too. Many in this camp accept Lausanne's "without error in all that it affirms" and simply read "affirms" more narrowly than Chicago does: the Bible affirms theology and ethics; it uses, rather than affirms, the cosmology and conventions of its day.[9]

Inerrantists reply that "faith and practice" can't be walled off from history, because the faith is about things that happened, and that once you allow errors in the details you have no principled place to stop. Infallibilists reply that Chicago has to spend Article XIII doing exactly the sorting they're accused of. That exchange has run for fifty years, and both sides are made of people who pray over the same book.

### Catholic: Dei Verbum (1965)

The Second Vatican Council's constitution on revelation, *Dei Verbum*, promulgated on 18 November 1965, is the Catholic reference text. Paragraph 11 first states inspiration in full: the books of both Testaments "in their entirety, with all their parts, are sacred and canonical because written under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, they have God as their author", and the human writers, "as true authors, consigned to writing everything and only those things which He wanted".[13] Then the sentence everyone argues about: "since everything asserted by the inspired authors or sacred writers must be held to be asserted by the Holy Spirit, it follows that the books of Scripture must be acknowledged as teaching solidly, faithfully and without error that truth which God wanted put into sacred writings for the sake of salvation."[13]

Read that last clause twice. Does "for the sake of salvation" limit *which* truth is without error (the truth that concerns salvation, leaving history and science open), or does it say *why* God put the truth there (his purpose was our salvation, and everything asserted is without error)? Catholics have argued this for sixty years. The restrictive reading was held by Raymond Brown and Joseph Fitzmyer, two of the century's leading Catholic biblical scholars; the purposive reading was argued by Cardinal Augustin Bea, who helped draft the text, on the grounds that "salvation" in the Latin attaches to the verb "wanted put into the writings", not to the noun "truth". Robert Miller's 2016 survey in the *Journal of Scriptural Reasoning*, "For the Sake of Our Salvation", fifty years on, lays out both readings and shows the question still open.[14] A Catholic reading this should find both of their scholars' positions here, and no verdict from a Protestant course on which is the true Catholic one.

Two more things a Catholic would want said. *Dei Verbum* 12 requires attention to "literary forms": "truth is set forth and expressed differently in texts which are variously historical, prophetic, poetic, or of other forms of discourse", and the interpreter must find "what meaning the sacred writer intended to express".[13] Genre first, again, this time from Rome. And paragraph 9 places Scripture within a larger whole: "it is not from Sacred Scripture alone that the Church draws her certainty about everything which has been revealed", so that "both sacred tradition and Sacred Scripture are to be accepted and venerated with the same sense of loyalty and reverence".[13] Paragraph 21 says the Church "has always venerated the divine Scriptures just as she venerates the body of the Lord".[13] That's the Catholic position: full inspiration, without error in what God intended to teach (with the scope debated in-house), read by genre, and read within the Church's Tradition and teaching office.

### Orthodox: Scripture within Tradition

The Orthodox would begin somewhere else, and it's important not to fold them into the Catholic paragraph. For Orthodoxy the question "is the Bible inerrant?" is already framed in a Western way; the prior fact is that the Bible is the Church's book, and it lives there. Fr Thomas Hopko's *The Orthodox Faith*, the standard catechetical series of the Orthodox Church in America, puts it this way: "For the Orthodox, the Bible is the main written source of divine doctrine since God Himself inspired its writing by His Holy Spirit", and "All parts of the Bible are interpreted in the Orthodox Church in the light of Christ since everything in the Bible leads up to Christ and speaks about Him."[15] On interpretation: "The Bible lives in the Church. It comes alive in the Church and has the most profound divine meaning for those who are members of the community which God has established." And: "It is only within the living Tradition of the Church under the direct inspiration of Christ's Spirit that the proper interpretation of the Bible can be made."[15] Hopko is careful to add that scholars "can offer much light to the words of the scriptures", and equally careful to say that "by themselves and by their academic work alone, no men can produce the proper interpretation of the Bible".[15]

So the Orthodox reader holds Scripture fully inspired and fully true, and would say the question of its truth can't be answered by a private reader with a critical apparatus, or by a statement with nineteen articles; it's answered in the liturgy, the Fathers, and the Church's continuous life, of which Scripture is the heart. Metropolitan Kallistos Ware's *The Orthodox Church* is the standard English account.[16]

### Mainline: the Confession of 1967

The Presbyterian Church (USA)'s Confession of 1967 is the clearest short statement of what the mainline Protestant churches hold, and it's often summarised by people who haven't read it. Here is what it says. "The one sufficient revelation of God is Jesus Christ, the Word of God incarnate, to whom the Holy Spirit bears unique and authoritative witness through the Holy Scriptures, which are received and obeyed as the word of God written. The Scriptures are not a witness among others, but the witness without parallel."[17] Then: "The Scriptures, given under the guidance of the Holy Spirit, are nevertheless the words of men, conditioned by the language, thought forms, and literary fashions of the places and times at which they were written. They reflect views of life, history, and the cosmos which were then current. The church, therefore, has an obligation to approach the Scriptures with literary and historical understanding."[17]

Notice what that does. The Word of God, in the strict sense, is Christ. Scripture is the "witness without parallel" to him, "received and obeyed as the word of God written", and read with the critical tools because it's also human writing from particular times. Someone in this tradition would say they believe the Bible; what they decline to say is that its human authors' views of "the cosmos which were then current" are part of what it teaches. Inerrantists read "witness to the Word" as a step down from "is the Word" (Chicago's Article III denies "that the Bible is merely a witness to revelation").[10] The Confession's defenders read it as putting Christ where he belongs. That's the disagreement, stated by both.

### An evangelical alternative: Enns's incarnational model

Peter Enns's *Inspiration and Incarnation* (2005; second edition, Baker Academic, 2015) is worth a paragraph because it comes from inside evangelicalism and accepts most of the critical conclusions you'll read below.[18] His model is an analogy, and he insists it's only that: as Christ is fully God and fully human, so Scripture is fully God's and fully human, and its human marks are not embarrassments to be explained away but part of what God chose. Those marks, in his account, include Genesis sharing the form of other ancient Near Eastern creation stories, real theological diversity between biblical books, and the New Testament writers reading the Old in ways a modern historian wouldn't. Inerrantists have replied that the analogy proves too much (Christ was fully human and without sin, so the analogy doesn't by itself license error). Enns's reply is in the second edition's postscript. Both are on the shelf.

### What all of them share

Now step back and look at the six together, because this is the part that surprises people. Every position above holds that Scripture is inspired by God. Every one holds that it was written by human authors using the forms and conventions of their day. Every one says you must read it by its genre: Chicago's Article XVIII, *Dei Verbum* 12, the Confession of 1967's "literary fashions", Hopko's Church-read Bible, Enns's human marks. Every one holds that it is authoritative for what Christians believe and do.

The disagreement is narrower than the noise suggests. It's about what "without error" ranges over: every assertion, history and science included (Chicago); what God intended to teach for our salvation, with the scope debated (Rome); faith and practice (Fuller); the witness to Christ (the Confession); or a question that only makes sense inside the Church's Tradition (Orthodoxy). Nearly every Christian argument about the Bible that isn't about a specific passage is an argument about that one word "ranges".

| Position | Its own reference text | What "without error" ranges over, in its own terms |
|---|---|---|
| Inerrantist | Chicago Statement (1978) | Everything Scripture asserts, history and science included, read by genre and by the autographs |
| Infallibilist | Fuller statement (1972); Lausanne (1974) as many read it | Faith and practice; the Bible cannot fail in its purpose |
| Catholic | *Dei Verbum* 11 (1965) | "That truth which God wanted put into sacred writings for the sake of salvation"; whether that limits or explains is argued in-house |
| Orthodox | Hopko, *The Orthodox Faith*; Ware | The Church's book, true and inspired, read within Tradition; the question is framed differently |
| Mainline | Confession of 1967 | "The witness without parallel" to Christ; "the words of men", read with literary and historical understanding |
| Enns | *Inspiration and Incarnation* (2015) | Fully divine and fully human; the human marks include ancient cosmology and diverse voices |

:::checkpoint A friend says, "Either you believe the Bible is inerrant or you don't believe the Bible." Using the table, what's the flaw in that sentence, and what's the true thing it's reaching for?
The flaw is that it collapses six positions into two and puts five of them in the "don't believe" box. Every row in the table says it believes the Bible is God's inspired, authoritative Word; they differ on what "without error" ranges over. The true thing the sentence is reaching for is that the scope question matters, and that inerrantists think the other scopes leave no principled place to stop. That's a real argument, and it deserves to be made as an argument, not as a definition of who counts as a believer.

## The critical view, in its own terms

Now the view from outside the faith, or rather from outside the question of faith, which is a different place. This course promised you'd hear it at full strength from its own teachers. Here it is.

:::predict Christine Hayes teaches Yale's introduction to the Hebrew Bible, free online. In her third lecture she reads Genesis 1 alongside *Enuma Elish*, the Babylonian creation epic. Before you read on, guess what she asks her students to conclude about whether Genesis is true.
Nothing. She doesn't ask them to conclude anything about its truth, in either direction. Her words are below, and they're the key to understanding what critical scholarship is and isn't.
:::

Hayes opens her course by saying what it is. "The Bible is not a book with all that that implies, that it has a uniform style and a message and a single author... It's a library."[19] Then the stance: "Religious faith simply isn't the topic of this course." "No one in this course wishes to undermine or malign religious faith any more than they wish to promote or proselytize for religious faith." "Our approach is going to be necessarily academic; and especially given the diversity of people in this room, that's really all that it can be, so that we have a common ground and common goals."[19] If a student raises a question that rests on a belief, she says, "I'll most likely respond by inviting you to consider the article of faith that lies behind that question... I'm not going to be drawn into a philosophical or theological debate over the merits of that belief."[19] Her interest, she says, "will be centered on the culture and the history and the literature and the religious thought of ancient Israel in all of its diversity rather than questions of faith and theology".[19]

Dale Martin's New Testament course at Yale says the same thing in its description: "Although theological themes will occupy much of our attention, the course does not attempt a theological appropriation of the New Testament as scripture."[20] It treats the documents "as ancient literature and as sources for historical study".[20]

So the critical view, stated as its holders state it, is this. The Bible is a library of ancient documents, written and edited over centuries by many hands, and it can be read the way any ancient library is read: for its sources, its seams, its development, its setting among the other literatures of its world. Whether God speaks in it is a question the method neither asks nor answers, because the method is built to give a Jew, a Christian, a Muslim, and an atheist in the same lecture hall "a common ground". That's the whole of it. It is not a claim that the Bible is false. It's a decision about which questions a university classroom can answer.

Three things follow, and the misconception list below depends on all three.

First, "critical scholars are atheists" is simply wrong as a description. Raymond Brown, whose *Introduction to the New Testament* (1997) is the Catholic critical standard, was a priest.[21] Enns is an evangelical. Hayes is a scholar of Judaism. The method brackets faith; it doesn't require its absence.

Second, critical scholars disagree with each other at least as much as they disagree with the church, and they say so. Hayes, on the documentary hypothesis that is the method's most famous result: "It needs to be remembered that the documentary hypothesis is only a hypothesis. An important and a useful one... But none of the sources posited by critical scholars has been found independently." And: "Most biblical scholars today do accept some version of Wellhausen's theory... though... not... tremendous confidence in some of the finer details."[22] That's the critical position describing its own limits, which is what a good position does.

Third, the results the method produces sort into the same three columns as everything else. That Paul's letters are first-century is established, and Hayes and Ehrman would say so with any evangelical. That the Pentateuch is built from four written sources is argued, and Hayes calls it a hypothesis. That God does or doesn't speak in the book is a question the method has declined. A critical scholar reading this paragraph should find their view as they hold it, including the humility.

:::video https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GK2PBAG3064 | Christine Hayes, "Doublets and Contradictions, Seams and Sources", Open Yale Courses RLST 145, lecture 4
About fifty minutes. Hayes walks through the flood story in Genesis 6 to 9, showing the doubled announcements, the two counts of animals, and the two durations, and from them the case that two accounts have been woven together. This is the historical-critical method demonstrated on a text you know, by one of its best teachers, with no theological verdict attached. Watch it before the second worked example below, and then read what the conservative side does with the same seams.

## Worked example 1: the mustard seed

Take a case where every position above agrees, because seeing the agreement teaches you where the real disagreements aren't.

> "With what can we compare the kingdom of God, or what parable shall we use for it? It is like a grain of mustard seed, which, when sown on the ground, is the smallest of all the seeds on earth, yet when it is sown it grows up and becomes larger than all the garden plants and puts out large branches, so that the birds of the air can make nests in its shade." (Mark 4:30–32, ESV)[23]

**Read badly, first way.** A sceptical reader stops at "the smallest of all the seeds on earth". Orchid seeds are smaller. So Jesus, or Mark, made a botanical error, the Bible contains errors, the conversation is over. This reading assumes the sentence was making a claim in botany.

**Read badly, second way.** A nervous believer reads the same words and starts defending them as botany: perhaps Jesus meant the smallest seed sown in Galilee; perhaps "on earth" means "in the land"; perhaps a first-century mustard variety was smaller than ours. Some of those may even be true. But notice that the defender has accepted the sceptic's framing: that the sentence is a claim about seed sizes that stands or falls on a ruler.

**Read well.** Ask what kind of sentence it is. It's a parable, spoken to a crowd, using a proverbial contrast (tiny seed, big shrub) that its hearers used every day. "The smallest of all the seeds" is the way a Galilean would say "the tiniest thing you plant", the same way you say "the whole town was there". The sentence asserts something about the kingdom of God: it starts small and ends large enough to shelter the birds. It asserts nothing about the ranking of seeds.

Now watch every position handle it. The Chicago inerrantist points to Article XIII: "observational descriptions of nature" and "the use of hyperbole" do not negate inerrancy, and the Exposition says hyperbole must be treated as hyperbole.[10] The Catholic points to *Dei Verbum* 12: find "what meaning the sacred writer intended to express" in his "contemporary literary forms".[13] The Presbyterian points to the Confession's "literary fashions of the places and times".[17] Enns says the human marks are the point. And Hayes would say, without any theological stake at all, that reading a first-century parable as a botanical claim is bad reading of an ancient text.

Six positions, one reading. That's the finding: genre and the ordinary conventions of human speech are common ground across the whole range, and the sceptic who thinks the mustard seed refutes inerrancy has refuted a position nobody holds.

Now do the next one yourself. Open Mark 1:5, which says that all the country of Judea and all the people of Jerusalem were going out to John the Baptist. Before you read on: which of the two bad readings would a hostile reader apply to that verse, what kind of sentence is it, and which clause of Article XIII covers it?

Here's mine. The hostile reading counts heads: every single inhabitant of Judea can't have gone, so the Gospel exaggerates, so it errs. The sentence is a narrator's summary using the ordinary hyperbole of crowds ("everyone was there"), which Mark's first readers would no more have taken as a census than you would. Article XIII covers it under "the use of hyperbole and round numbers". And once again, every position in the table reads it the same way. If you got that, you have the mechanism.

## Worked example 2: the death of Moses

Now a case where the positions divide, because the sort has to earn its keep on a hard one.

> "So Moses the servant of the LORD died there in the land of Moab, according to the word of the LORD, and he buried him in the valley in the land of Moab opposite Beth-peor; but no one knows the place of his burial to this day." (Deuteronomy 34:5–6, ESV)[24]

> "And there has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face" (Deuteronomy 34:10, ESV)[24]

> "These are the kings who reigned in the land of Edom, before any king reigned over the Israelites." (Genesis 36:31, ESV)[25]

The tradition, Jewish and Christian, calls the five books of the Pentateuch the books of Moses, and the Bible elsewhere speaks of Moses writing: "And Moses wrote down all the words of the LORD" (Exodus 24:4).[26] Yet the last chapter of Deuteronomy narrates Moses' death, adds that nobody knows his grave "to this day", and says no prophet like him has arisen "since", which is the voice of someone looking back across a stretch of time. And Genesis 36:31, listing the kings of Edom "before any king reigned over the Israelites", reads like the sentence of a writer who has seen Israelite kings, which on any dating means centuries after Moses.

These verses have been noticed for a very long time. Hayes's lecture on the method names Spinoza in the seventeenth century among the early critics who built on them.[22] What matters for you is how each side uses them now.

**The critical reader.** For Hayes and the tradition running from Julius Wellhausen's classic statement of 1878, these are two among many seams: doublets (two creation accounts, two flood accounts interwoven), shifting divine names, contradictions in detail, and passages that presuppose a later time.[22] The explanation is that the Pentateuch was composed from earlier written sources, classically four, called J, E, D, and P. Hayes gives the classical dates: J "about the tenth century", E "composed in the northern kingdoms about the ninth century", D "late-seventh century", P "the exilic period, the period after the fall of the Southern Kingdom in 586".[22] Since Wellhausen the field has divided. The neo-documentarians, led by Joel Baden at Yale (*The Composition of the Pentateuch: Renewing the Documentary Hypothesis*, Yale University Press, 2012), defend four continuous sources on purely literary grounds, dropping Wellhausen's theory of how Israel's religion evolved.[27] Supplementary and fragmentary models, strong in Europe, see a core text expanded in layers rather than four documents spliced. Nearly everyone agrees there's a distinct Priestly stratum; beyond that the map is contested. And, as Hayes said, no source has ever been found as a separate document.

**The conservative reader.** Moses is the substantial author of the Pentateuch, as the Pentateuch itself (Exodus 24:4), Joshua, and Jesus (Mark 12:26, "the book of Moses") say, and the books reached their final form with later editorial updating: a death notice added by a successor, "to this day" and "before any king reigned" added by an editor bringing old text up to date, place names modernised. Tremper Longman's phrase for this is "essentially Mosaic", and it's the mainstream evangelical position, held across the seminaries.[28] On the doublets and name patterns, conservative scholars (Cassuto, Kikawada and Quinn, Garrett) argue they are literary features of a unified composition, of a kind found in other ancient Near Eastern texts that nobody splits into sources.[29] And they argue that the critical reconstruction rested on a nineteenth-century theory of how religion evolves from simple to priestly that the archaeology of the ancient Near East hasn't borne out.

Now the question you've been waiting for: does an inerrantist have to reject the editorial reading? No. Look at Article XIII again: inerrancy is not negated by "a lack of modern technical precision" or "the topical arrangement of material".[10] Nothing in the Statement requires that Moses wrote his own obituary. What Article XVIII denies is "any... quest for sources lying behind it that leads to relativizing, dehistoricizing, or discounting its teaching, or rejecting its claims to authorship".[10] So an inerrantist can accept an editor's hand in Deuteronomy 34 and cannot accept a theory on which Moses is a legend and the books are a post-exilic composition claiming his name. That's the line, and it's drawn by the inerrantists themselves, not conceded on their behalf.

**The sort.** This question is *argued*. The critical position is the majority in universities; the conservative position is the majority in evangelical seminaries; each has serious scholars and real evidence. What would settle it? A manuscript of J, or an external ancient reference to Moses writing. Neither exists, and nothing available now will decide it. Hayes says the theory is "only a hypothesis"; Longman says "essentially Mosaic"; both are being exact.

**What we hold.** We hold the Pentateuch to be "essentially Mosaic" in Longman's sense: Moses the substantial source, with later editing of the kind Deuteronomy 34 shows. We hold it as a judgement on argued evidence, informed by Jesus' way of speaking of "the book of Moses", not as something history has established. And we say plainly that a Christian who accepts a source-critical account, as Enns does and as many Catholic and mainline scholars do, has not left the faith; they've taken the other side of an argued question. Chicago's Article XIX, which denies "that such confession is necessary for salvation", says as much about its own doctrine.[10]

:::checkpoint Deuteronomy 34:6 says no one knows Moses' grave "to this day". Give the critical reading and the conservative reading of those three words in one sentence each, and then say which column the disagreement between them belongs in.
Critical: the phrase is a marker of distance, written by someone for whom Moses' death is the remote past, and one more sign that the book was composed long after the events. Conservative: the phrase is an editorial update, added by a later hand to a substantially Mosaic text, of exactly the kind that Article XIII's "lack of modern technical precision" and the ordinary practice of ancient scribes allow. The disagreement is argued: both readings fit the words, neither is settled by them, and what divides the readers is how they weigh the whole pattern of seams, which is the real question.
:::

## What archaeology has and hasn't done

The other question the course deferred is the historicity of the Old Testament's story. This lesson doesn't need it, but you'll be asked, so here is the sort, done in three sentences per column with the evidence attached.

**Established.** A dynasty called the "House of David" existed: the Tel Dan stele, an Aramaic inscription found in fragments in 1993 and 1994, names it in the ninth century BC.[30] The priestly blessing of Numbers 6 was in use in the First Temple period: the Ketef Hinnom silver scrolls, found in 1979, carry a form of it from the late seventh or early sixth century BC, the oldest surviving biblical text.[31] The Babylonian exile happened, and the return. And Pontius Pilate governed Judea: a limestone inscription found at Caesarea in 1961 names him as prefect.[32]

:::figure https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8c/Limestone_Inscription_with_Name_of_Pontius_Pilate%2C_Caesarea%2C_26-36_AD_%2842500903014%29.jpg | A damaged block of pale limestone with four lines of Latin capitals partly cut away on the left, the second line reading PONTIVS PILATVS
The Pilate inscription from Caesarea Maritima, now in the Israel Museum, Jerusalem: the only inscription naming the governor who condemned Jesus, found in 1961 reused as a step in the theatre. This is what "established" looks like: a stone, a name, a date, and no argument about any of them. Photograph by Gary Todd, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons.
:::

**Argued.** The scale and date of the exodus, whether the conquest of Canaan happened as Joshua describes it or by slower settlement, the size of David's and Solomon's kingdom (the "maximalist" and "minimalist" camps, which are scholarly self-descriptions, not insults), and the historicity of the patriarchal narratives. On each, serious archaeologists and historians disagree, and the disagreement doesn't track religious commitment as neatly as you'd expect.

**Faith.** That the events happened as Scripture describes where archaeology is silent, which is most places, since most events leave no stone. A Christian holds this on the strength of the book's trustworthiness where it can be checked and of the reasons given earlier for trusting it at all. That's a faith claim, and this course makes it as one.

So "archaeology has disproved the Old Testament" is false (look at the columns above), and "archaeology has proved the Old Testament" is also false (look at the second column, and at how little a stele can carry). What archaeology has done is establish a set of specific things, leave a set of specific things open, and say nothing about the rest. *Hard Questions* and *Faith and Science* take this further; here you need only the sort.

## What people get wrong

"Inerrancy means reading everything literally." Article XIII and the Exposition refute this in the inerrantists' own words: hyperbole as hyperbole, poetry as poetry, round numbers as round numbers.[10] Whoever told you otherwise had read about the Chicago Statement, not the Chicago Statement.

"Non-inerrantist Christians don't believe the Bible." Read the Confession of 1967 again: "received and obeyed as the word of God written... the witness without parallel".[17] Read *Dei Verbum* 11.[13] Read Hopko.[15] They believe it. They differ from inerrantists about what "without error" ranges over, and that difference deserves an argument, not a verdict on their faith.

"Critical scholars are atheists." Brown was a priest; Enns is an evangelical; Hayes says nobody in her course wishes to undermine faith.[19][21] The method brackets belief. Some who use it believe, some don't, and the method can't tell you which.

"The church invented inerrancy in 1978", and its mirror, "the church always used the word." The banner is modern; the trust it names is not. Whether the older writers held the doctrine under other words is the Rogers and McKim versus Woodbridge question, and it's argued.[11][12]

"Archaeology has disproved the Old Testament", and its mirror, "archaeology has proved the Old Testament." Neither. Four things established, a list of things argued, and silence on the rest. The stele proves a dynasty; it doesn't prove the exodus, and it doesn't disprove it either.
