> "Then beginning with Moses and all the prophets, he interpreted to them the things written about himself in all the scriptures." (Luke 24:27, NET)[1]

Two people are walking home from Jerusalem, and a stranger falls in beside them. Over the next few miles he does something nobody has ever done for you: he takes the whole Old Testament and reads it as one thing. Not a shelf of separate books. One story, with a shape and a direction and, he says, a subject.

Most people who own a Bible have never seen that shape. They know episodes: the flood, David and Goliath, Daniel in the lions' den, the birth in Bethlehem. What they don't know is which episode comes before which, which promise each one is carrying, or what the whole thing is moving toward. So every passage floats. A verse from Jeremiah lands on a graduation card next to a verse from Philippians, and nobody can say whether they belong together, because nobody knows where either one lives.

This lesson gives you the shape. By the end you should be able to take any book, or any passage, and say where it sits in the story and which promise is in play there. That single skill is what turns "the Bible says" into "this part of the Bible, at this point in the story, said this to these people".

## A story with six acts

Christians have been drawing the shape of the Bible for a long time, and the drawings differ. The one this course uses comes from Craig Bartholomew and Michael Goheen's *The Drama of Scripture*, which is widely used in Christian colleges. They lay the Bible out as a drama in six acts, and their own act titles are worth having, because each one names what God is doing:[2]

1. **Creation**, "God Establishes His Kingdom". A good world, with people in it to rule it under him (Genesis 1 and 2).
2. **Fall**, "Rebellion in the Kingdom". People rebel, and the damage spreads from a garden to a whole world (Genesis 3 to 11).
3. **Israel**, "The King Chooses Israel: Redemption Initiated". God binds himself to one family and makes it a nation, and through that nation the rescue of the world begins (Genesis 12 to the end of the Old Testament).
4. **Jesus**, "The Coming of the King: Redemption Accomplished". The rescue is carried out in one man (the four Gospels).
5. **Church**, "Spreading the News of the King". The news goes out to the world (Acts and the letters).
6. **New creation**, "The Return of the King: Redemption Completed". The world is remade (Revelation, ending in chapters 21 and 22).

Between acts 3 and 4 they put an interlude, "A Kingdom Story Waiting for an Ending: The Intertestamental Period", and they say why it matters: "four hundred years of Israel's history between Malachi and Matthew" that a Protestant Bible passes over in silence.[2]

I want to say two honest things about this scheme before you lean on it.

First, it's a teaching device. Scripture doesn't number its own acts, and other good teachers cut the story in other places. Vaughan Roberts, in *God's Big Picture*, uses eight stages, all built on one definition of the kingdom of God, "God's people in God's place under God's rule and blessing": the pattern of the kingdom, the perished kingdom, the promised kingdom, the partial kingdom, the prophesied kingdom, the present kingdom, the proclaimed kingdom, and the perfected kingdom.[3] Line his eight up against the six and you can see exactly where the disagreement is. Roberts puts three joints inside the act that Bartholomew and Goheen leave whole: the promise to Abraham, the partial thing Israel actually got under Moses and the kings, and the prophets' announcement that a better one was coming. His last three stages match acts 4, 5 and 6 almost exactly. Graeme Goldsworthy's *According to Plan* organises the same material around the same kingdom theme again.[4]

And these are all Protestant evangelical schemes, which is a limit worth naming, because the same story gets told with different joints elsewhere. The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* sets it out as "the stages of Revelation", and its joints are not acts but covenants: the beginning, the fall, "the covenant with Noah", "God chooses Abraham", "God forms his people Israel", and then Christ.[5] Irenaeus, writing about the year 180, gave the story a different shape again, the one Orthodox theology still uses: Christ "commenced afresh the long line of human beings", so that "what we had lost in Adam... we might recover in Christ Jesus".[6] That is the whole Bible as one movement out and back, rather than as a sequence of scenes. Nobody in this paragraph disagrees about what happened. They disagree about where the natural breaks fall, and none of the breaks is inspired.

Second, notice how lopsided the acts are. Count the chapters. A Protestant Bible has 1,189 of them,[7] and Act 3 alone, Genesis 12 through Malachi, has 918.

Fee and Stuart put the same point in words: the Old Testament is "three-quarters of the bulk of the Bible", and "over 40 percent of the Old Testament is narrative".[8] So most of what you'll ever read sits inside one act, the story of Israel, and that act is the one that needs a map of its own. We'll build it in a minute.

:::predict Before you look at the timeline, place the four-hundred-year gap yourself. In a Protestant Old Testament, which book stands last before it, and which events come first after it? And where does Esther fall: before the gap or inside it?
The gap runs from Malachi to the first pages of the Gospels, roughly from the last of the writing prophets to John the Baptist. Every book in a Protestant Old Testament tells a story set before it, Esther included: Esther is set in Persia after the return, in the last part of Act 3. Most people put one or two of the late books on the wrong side, and the usual candidates are Esther, Daniel, and Malachi. Two cautions before you feel too settled. When a book's story is set and when the book was written are different questions, and for Daniel in particular most critical scholars date the final form to the second century BC, inside the gap; lesson 10 takes that argument up properly. And "no books" is a statement about this Bible, not about the centuries: a Catholic or Orthodox Bible carries several books from exactly that period.
:::

## The spine: five covenants

A six-act summary tells you the order. It doesn't tell you what holds the story together. For that you need the covenants.

A covenant is a binding relationship with terms, sealed by promise and usually by a sign. The Bible's story is carried forward by five of them, and every book sits under one or more. Learn these and you can tell, for almost any passage, which promise is in play.

**Noah.** After the flood God binds himself to the whole living world: "I confirm my covenant with you: Never again will all living things be wiped out by the waters of a flood; never again will a flood destroy the earth" (Genesis 9:11, NET).[9] The sign is the rainbow. What it promises is a stable stage: the story won't be wiped out before it can be told.

**Abraham.** God calls one man out of Mesopotamia and gives him four things: a land, a nation, a great name, and a blessing that reaches past him. "Go out from your country, your relatives, and your father's household to the land that I will show you. Then I will make you into a great nation, and I will bless you, and I will make your name great, so that you will exemplify divine blessing. I will bless those who bless you, but the one who treats you lightly I must curse, so that all the families of the earth may receive blessing through you" (Genesis 12:1 to 3, NET).[10] Everything after Genesis 12 is, one way or another, this promise being kept, threatened, or renewed. Paul reads that last clause as the gospel itself, "proclaimed" to Abraham "ahead of time" (Galatians 3:8, NET).[11]

**Sinai.** Abraham's family, now a nation, is brought out of Egypt and given the terms of its life with God at Mount Sinai (Exodus 19 to 24), with the law as the stipulations and the land as the setting. This is the covenant the prophets will spend centuries enforcing, and lesson 4 is about it.

Two things about Sinai that readers usually miss. The first is that grace sits inside it. When Moses asks to see God's glory, what he hears is "The Lord, the Lord, the compassionate and gracious God, slow to anger, and abounding in loyal love and faithfulness" (Exodus 34:6, NET).[12] Hold on to that verse; you'll need it when someone tells you the Old Testament is law and the New is grace.

The second is that Sinai comes with sanctions written down, and they're the most quoted and least read part of the covenant. Forty years after Sinai, on the plains of Moab, Moses restates the terms to the generation about to cross into the land, and [Deuteronomy 28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy+28&version=NET) lays out the two futures in matching pairs:

> "You will be blessed in the city and blessed in the field... Your basket and your mixing bowl will be blessed. You will be blessed when you come in and blessed when you go out." (Deuteronomy 28:3, 5 and 6, NET)

> "You will be cursed in the city and cursed in the field. Your basket and your mixing bowl will be cursed... You will be cursed when you come in and cursed when you go out." (Deuteronomy 28:16, 17 and 19, NET)

Read on, and the curses get specific about exactly the things that happened: "You will plant vineyards and cultivate them, but you will not drink wine or gather in grapes, because worms will eat them" (28:39); "You will bear sons and daughters but not keep them, because they will be taken into captivity" (28:41).[13] Deuteronomy is a renewal document, not Sinai itself, and it says so: 29:1 calls its covenant one made "in the land of Moab, in addition to the covenant he had made with them at Horeb".[13] Same terms, restated for a new generation, on a different mountain and forty years on. When you get to the prophets and they sound like prosecutors, this is the document they're prosecuting.

**David.** The nation gets a king, and God promises the king a line that will not end: "Your house and your kingdom will stand before me permanently; your dynasty will be permanent" (2 Samuel 7:16, NET).[14] From here on, "son of David" is a loaded phrase, and the collapse of the kingdom four centuries later becomes a question about whether God keeps his word.

**The new covenant.** Speaking to a nation about to lose its land, its Temple, and its king, Jeremiah promises a covenant "not like the old covenant that I made with their ancestors": "I will put my law within them and write it on their hearts and minds. I will be their God, and they will be my people" (Jeremiah 31:32 and 33, NET).[15] Jesus takes those words at the last supper (Luke 22:20), and the letter to the Hebrews quotes Jeremiah 31 at length to explain what has changed (Hebrews 8). The New Testament's name for itself comes from this promise.

Look at what the list does. Noah gives the story a stage. Abraham gives it a family and a destination. Sinai gives the family a shape. David gives it a king. And the new covenant answers what went wrong under the others, though notice what Jeremiah says went wrong: "they violated that covenant, even though I was like a faithful husband to them" (31:32).[15] The failure he names is the people's, not Sinai's. That distinction matters, and Christians have not always kept it; a covenant that people broke is a different thing from a covenant that was defective, and Jeremiah says the first.

:::callout What "covenant" is not
A covenant isn't a contract between equals, and it isn't a deal God can be talked out of. In each of the five, God binds himself first. The terms that follow (be fruitful, walk before me, keep my commandments) are the response, not the price. If you read Sinai as Israel earning the exodus, you have the order backwards: Exodus 19 comes after Exodus 14. There's a reason for that order beyond narrative tidiness. Sinai is laid out in the shape of the treaties great kings made with smaller ones in the second millennium BC, and one of that form's fixed parts is a historical prologue, in which the king recites what he has already done for the vassal before he states any terms at all.[16] Exodus 19:4 is that prologue in one sentence: "You yourselves have seen what I did to Egypt and how I lifted you on eagles' wings and brought you to myself."
:::

:::checkpoint Gabriel tells Mary that her son will be great, "and the Lord God will give him the throne of his father David. He will reign over the house of Jacob forever, and his kingdom will never end" (Luke 1:32 to 33, NET). Which of the five covenants is being invoked, and what is the sentence claiming?
David's. "The throne of his father David" and a kingdom that "will never end" are 2 Samuel 7:16 almost word for word, and the claim is that the promise of a permanent dynasty, which looked broken when Jerusalem fell in 586 BC, is being kept in this child. Notice what the sentence is doing structurally: a New Testament passage is only intelligible if you're carrying an Old Testament covenant in your head. That is what the spine is for. If you also spotted Abraham underneath ("the house of Jacob"), good; Luke stacks them, and by verse 55 he has Abraham by name.
:::

A few things to notice on the map.

**The books are not in this order on the shelf.** Lesson 1 showed you that the Old Testament is arranged by kind of book (Law, History, Poetry and Wisdom, Prophets), not by date. So Amos, a prophet from the divided kingdom, sits near the back of your Old Testament, hundreds of pages after 2 Kings, the book that tells you about the kings he preached to. The shelf and the timeline are two different maps, and the reader who confuses them will think Amos comes after Nehemiah.

**Where a story is set and when it was written are different questions.** The map answers the first one. It has to, because that's the question you need answered to read a passage. But it would be dishonest to let the map imply an answer to the second. Chronicles retells events from Saul to the exile and was put together after the return; Longman and Dillard's standard evangelical introduction calls the Pentateuch "essentially Mosaic", meaning Moses as the substantial source with later editing, while most university scholars reconstruct a longer process of composition.[17] That argument is real, it isn't settled, and lesson 10 gives both sides at full strength. What this map claims is only where each book's story stands in the sequence.

**Only a few dates are printed, and it is worth knowing which kind each one is.** Three are events: 722 BC for the fall of Samaria, 586 BC for the fall of Jerusalem, and about AD 30 for the crucifixion. The first two are fixed points of ancient Near Eastern history; Christine Hayes, [teaching the Hebrew Bible at Yale from the critical side](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-1), gives the same years.[18] The third is not seriously disputed either. Two more marks are objects rather than events, and they are there because they're the earliest hard anchors we have for anything in the pre-exilic stretch, and it's worth being exact about what each one carries. The Tel Dan stele, a broken Aramaic inscription found in 1993 and dated to the ninth century BC, names the "House of David".[19] What that establishes is that within roughly a century and a half of the time the Bible puts David, Israel's neighbours referred to the southern kingdom's royal house by the name of a founder called David. That is strong evidence for a real David, and it is not the same as confirming the Bible's account of him; the size and character of his kingdom are argued between maximalist and minimalist scholars, and the stele settles none of it. The Ketef Hinnom silver amulets, found in 1979 and dated to the late seventh or early sixth century BC, carry a form of the priestly blessing of Numbers 6:24 to 26, which shows that wording in use before the exile.[20]

For the rest, the map gives periods rather than years, and that's deliberate. The dates of Abraham, the exodus, and the conquest are argued. So, for some of them, is more than the date: the scale of the exodus, the extent of the conquest, and the historicity of the patriarchal narratives are all live scholarly disputes, and this course will say so in lesson 10 and hand the detail to *Hard Questions*. You don't need those dates to read. You need the order.

The Tel Dan stele in the Israel Museum, Jerusalem. Found in 1993, ninth century BC, it records an Aramean king's victory over "the House of David". Photo: Oren Rozen, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

**The gap is real, and it's a fact about this Bible.** Between Malachi and the Gospels, Persia gives way to Alexander's Greece, Greece to the Maccabean revolt and a short-lived Jewish kingdom, and that to Rome. The Pharisees, the synagogue as the Gospels know it, and the Greek translation of the Old Testament all take shape in those centuries. So does a large body of Jewish writing: Sirach's grandson, translating his grandfather's book in Egypt, dates his own work to about 132 BC in its prologue, which makes it a document written from inside the supposed silence.[21] Catholic and Orthodox Bibles carry several books from this period, 1 and 2 Maccabees among them, which is one reason their Old Testaments are longer, and lesson 8 gives that case in full. So the honest sentence is that the Protestant Old Testament has nothing from these four centuries, not that the centuries were empty. They were among the most productive in Jewish history. If you open Matthew straight from Malachi and wonder why everyone is suddenly speaking Greek and paying taxes to Caesar, this is where the answer went.

## Three levels at once

Now the tool that makes the map useful. Fee and Stuart, in the chapter on Old Testament narrative that lesson 3 will use in full, say that every biblical story is being told on three levels at the same time.[8]

The **top level** is the whole plan of God: creation, fall, rescue, new creation. The **middle level** is the story of Israel: the family, the nation, the land, the kings, the exile, the return. The **bottom level** is the individual story in front of you: this famine, this woman, this king.

Most readers only ever see the bottom level, and the bottom level on its own is where the graduation-card readings come from. The middle level is what the map gives you. The top level is what Jesus was showing the two on the road.

Watch the three work together on one sentence. Take Ruth 1:1: "During the time of the judges, there was a famine in the land of Judah. So a man from Bethlehem in Judah went to live as a resident foreigner in the region of Moab, along with his wife and two sons." (Ruth 1:1, NET)[22] On the bottom level, a family leaves home in a famine. On the middle level, "the time of the judges" is a period the previous book has just summed up with the line that in those days Israel had no king and "each man did what he considered to be right" (Judges 21:25, NET),[23] and "Bethlehem in Judah" is a town the reader will hear of again. On the top level, a family from Abraham's line leaving Abraham's land is a promise in danger. Three readings of one sentence, and only the first is visible without the map.

:::predict Now you do one. Daniel opens like this: "In the third year of the reign of King Jehoiakim of Judah, King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon advanced against Jerusalem and laid it under siege... He brought them to the land of Babylonia to the temple of his god and put the vessels in the treasury of his god" (Daniel 1:1 to 2, NET). Name what's happening on each of the three levels. Write it down before you open this.
Bottom: a king besieges a city, takes hostages from the nobility, and loots a temple. Middle: this is the covenant's sanctions arriving, the exile Deuteronomy 28 described in advance, and it takes the land, the Temple and the Davidic king all at once, which is every promise in the spine except Noah's. Top: the striking thing is what the top level does here. God's plan does not stop at the disaster; a book about Jewish exiles serving in a pagan court is the plan continuing through the worst thing that had happened to it. If you noticed the vessels in particular, notice where they turn up again: Ezra 1:7, coming home.
:::

## Ruth, read on one level and then on three

[Ruth](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ruth&version=NET) is four chapters long and you can read it in twenty minutes, which is why I'd like you to have it open. First the usual reading, then the reading the map makes possible.

**Read badly.** A stand-alone love story. A loyal daughter-in-law, a kind landowner, a courtship on a threshing floor, a wedding. Sermons on loyalty; sermons on finding a good spouse. All of that is in the book. None of it is the point, and if you stop there you'll never notice how strange the ending is.

**Read well.** Start at the middle level. The book opens "during the time of the judges", so we're in the period after the conquest and before the kings, and the reader who has just finished Judges knows how badly it has gone. It opens with a famine in Judah, in the land God promised Abraham, and the family goes to Moab, which isn't that land. Everyone in the first five verses dies except the widows. So the story begins with Abraham's promise, a family and a land, at its lowest point in miniature: no land, no men, no children.

Then a foreigner, a Moabite, chooses the God of Israel. A kinsman-redeemer (the Hebrew is *go'el*, a relative with the duty to buy back family land and keep a name alive) does his duty. A child is born.

:::predict You've read four chapters about Naomi, Ruth, and Boaz. How do you expect the book to end? Write one sentence.
It ends with a genealogy. Not a wedding, not a moral, not a prayer: a list of names.
:::

"The neighbor women gave him a name, saying, 'A son has been born to Naomi.' They named him Obed." Then the same verse names Obed as the father of Jesse, and Jesse as David's father (Ruth 4:17, NET).[24] Then the book closes with ten generations, ending "Obed was the father of Jesse, and Jesse was the father of David" (Ruth 4:22, NET).[24]

A romance doesn't end with a family tree. A story on the middle level does. The last word of Ruth is David, and David hasn't been born yet. In the days when there was no king, in a famine, through a foreign woman, God is keeping the promise to Abraham on its way to the promise to David. Ruth is the hinge between Judges and Samuel, and the writer tells you so by ending where he does.

Now the top level, which is our reading and which we hold as Christians. Matthew opens his Gospel with a genealogy too, and Ruth the Moabite is in it (Matthew 1:5). The line that runs through a famine in Bethlehem ends in a birth in Bethlehem. We read Ruth as one step on the road to Jesus because Jesus, on the road to Emmaus, read Moses and the Prophets that way, and we take his reading as the true one.[1] That's a faith commitment, and it's ours, and this lesson labels it rather than smuggling it.

### The objection to that, at full strength

You should be uneasy about the paragraph you just read, and if you aren't, here's why you should be. Twenty lines from now this lesson is going to say that a text can't mean what it never could have meant to its author. The writer of Ruth was not thinking about Jesus. Nobody in the story is. So the Christian top-level reading looks like exactly the move the lesson forbids: a later meaning read back into an earlier text, which is called retrojection, and once you allow it there seems to be no limit. A reader can find anything anywhere if the rule is that a later book gets to say what an earlier one meant. This is not a cheap objection made by hostile people. It is the standard critical objection, it is made in these words by scholars of every conviction, and a serious Jewish reader will press it hardest, because the reading in question annexes their scripture.

Three things to say back, and the third is a concession.

First, a distinction that all sides accept: what an author consciously intended and what an author's words come to mean inside a story that finishes after them are not the same thing. G. K. Beale's observation, which covenant theologians and dispensationalists both grant even while they disagree about nearly everything else, is that the Old Testament writers did not exhaustively grasp what they wrote.[25] That's not a religious claim. It's true of any writer whose work sits inside a larger unfinished thing. The writer of Ruth knew he was writing toward David. Whether David's line was going anywhere further was not his to know.

Second, the Christian claim isn't that any later reading is licensed. It's that one specific later reading is: the one Jesus and the apostles actually made. John Wenham's argument in *Christ and the Bible* is that a Christian's doctrine of Scripture has to start from Jesus' own use of it, because a Christian is by definition someone who has taken Jesus as authoritative on other things too.[26] So the top level is not a licence to find Christ under every stone; it's a claim that Luke 24:27 records a reading, and Christians follow it. The test of a top-level reading is whether the New Testament actually makes it, not whether it can be made.

Third, and this is the concession: none of that compels anyone who doesn't already share the faith commitment. It is an argument that runs from inside Christian belief outward, not from neutral ground inward. A Jewish reader who grants the distinction in the first point and the honesty of the second will still say that the story of Ruth is complete in the Tanakh without Jesus in it, and they'll be reading well. This course holds the Christian reading and says so on its first line. It doesn't pretend that saying so is the same as proving it.

### One more thing, and it matters

In the Jewish Bible, the Tanakh, Ruth doesn't sit between Judges and Samuel at all. It sits among the Writings, with the other four short scrolls read at the festivals, and it's read at Shavuot, the festival that falls at the wheat harvest and that Jewish tradition connects with the giving of the Torah at Sinai.[27] Read there, the book is about a harvest and about a foreigner who takes on the covenant, which fits that festival exactly. The Christian order makes Ruth a hinge in a history; the Jewish order makes it a festival scroll about faithfulness. Same words, two shelves, two readings, and a Jewish reader is reading well. Where you place a book is already a decision about what it means, and that's the second time in two lessons you've seen the point.

## Jeremiah 29:11, the verse on the graduation card

Now the hard one, and the one you've probably met on a card, a mug, or a graduation programme.

> "For I know what I have planned for you," says the Lord. "I have plans to prosper you, not to harm you. I have plans to give you a future filled with hope." (Jeremiah 29:11, NET)[28]

**Read badly.** A personal promise. God has a plan for my life, it's a good one, and this verse is his word to me about my next step. Nearly everyone reads it this way the first time, and I'm not going to sneer at that; the person who brought this verse on a card is the person this course is for. The reading feels right because the words, taken alone, say exactly that.

**Read well.** Ask the map's first question: who was this said to, and when?

:::predict Before reading on: who is the "you" in Jeremiah 29:11? A young person starting out? The nation in its prime? Someone else?
The exiles. The verse is inside a letter sent from Jerusalem to the people Nebuchadnezzar had already deported to Babylon.
:::

The chapter tells you in its first line: "The prophet Jeremiah sent a letter to the exiles Nebuchadnezzar had carried off from Jerusalem to Babylon. It was addressed to the elders who were left among the exiles, to the priests, to the prophets, and to all the other people who were exiled in Babylon" ([Jeremiah 29:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jeremiah+29&version=NET), NET).[28] So we're in Act 3, at the exile, among people who have lost the land, the Temple is about to go, and the king of David's line has been carried off. Deuteronomy 28's curses have arrived, right down to the sons and daughters taken into captivity. Now read what the letter tells them to do:

> "Build houses and settle down. Plant gardens and eat what they produce. Marry and have sons and daughters. Find wives for your sons and allow your daughters to get married so that they too can have sons and daughters. Grow in number; do not dwindle away. Work to see that the city where I sent you as exiles enjoys peace and prosperity. Pray to the Lord for it. For as it prospers you will prosper." (Jeremiah 29:5 to 7, NET)[28]

And then the timescale: "Only when the seventy years of Babylonian rule are over will I again take up consideration for you. Then I will fulfill my gracious promise to you and restore you to your homeland" (Jeremiah 29:10, NET).[28] Verse 11 follows straight after.

:::predict Your turn, before mine. You're one of those exiles. You've just been told to plant a garden, marry off your children, and wait seventy years. Then you hear "I know what I have planned for you... I have plans to give you a future filled with hope." Who, exactly, will see that future, and what do those plans include? Write your answer down first.
The "you" is plural, a people, not a person. The plans include the exile itself: God speaks in this letter as the one who sent them there, "to all those he sent into exile to Babylon" (29:4) and about "the city where I sent you as exiles" (29:7), and the seventy years are part of the plan rather than an interruption of it. The future is a return to the land, and most of the people reading the letter will be dead before it comes; the promise is to their grandchildren. So the verse isn't "your life will go well". It's "this catastrophe is not the end of my promise to Abraham and David, and your children's children will see me keep it". That's a bigger promise than the card version, and a harder one, because it's a promise to people who won't live to see it.
:::

**Now the wrinkle.** May a Christian still take comfort from this verse?

Yes. But watch how. Not by lifting the sentence off the page and addressing it to yourself; that route ignores who "you" is and makes the verse promise something it never promised anyone. The route that works goes through the story, and there's a published procedure for walking it. Duvall and Hays call it the Interpretive Journey, and it has five steps: grasp the text in their town; measure the width of the river you have to cross, which is the distance in time, culture and covenant; cross on the bridge of the principle the text is stating; check that principle against the rest of the Bible; then grasp the text in your own town.[29]

Run Jeremiah 29 through it. **Their town:** deported Judeans in Babylon, told that God has not finished with them and that the seventy years are part of his plan. **The river:** wide. Different covenant, different millennium, and they are a nation with a land to return to while you are not. **The bridge:** God's commitment to his people is not cancelled by the disaster he has brought them into, and his timescale can be longer than a lifetime. **The rest of the Bible:** Romans 8:28 and Hebrews 12 say something recognisably similar to Christians, so the principle isn't being invented here. **Your town:** you may take from it what the exiles took, that a long hard stretch is not evidence that God has dropped you. What you may not take is a guarantee that your own plans will prosper, because the letter never said that to them either.

That's the shape of the move. Lesson 7 gives you Fee and Stuart's formal tool for the same job, the "comparable particulars" test, and lesson 3 gives you the rule that governs step one.

:::checkpoint Same letter, verse 7: "Work to see that the city where I sent you as exiles enjoys peace and prosperity" (NET). On the method you've just used, what did it mean to its first readers, and what may a Christian take from it?
To the exiles it was a shock: pray for Babylon, the city that had burned yours. It meant settling in, working for the good of a pagan city, and treating seventy years as home rather than a waiting room. Cross the river and the principle is that a people living somewhere it didn't choose is told to do good there rather than withdraw from it, and the New Testament says something close enough that Peter can call his readers "foreigners and exiles" (1 Peter 2:11, NET) and then tell them to live well among their neighbours. The setting is kept, the covenant is kept, and the instruction lands. That's the difference between this reading and the card.
:::

### Where this argument stops, and why

One step in that reasoning needs labelling, because it's the step most books take silently.

To get from Jeremiah's exiles to you, I said the promise travels through the new covenant. Christians agree that it does. They don't agree on what that means for Israel. **Covenant theology** holds that there's one covenant of grace unfolding through the Bible, and that the church is now the people of the promise made to Abraham. **Dispensationalism** holds that Israel and the church remain distinct, and that promises made to Israel about land and nation keep a literal future referent that the church doesn't absorb. **Progressive covenantalism** and **progressive dispensationalism** sit between them.[30] These are not fringe positions; they're the two main families of English-speaking evangelical reading, and which one you hold changes how you read Ezekiel 40 to 48, Romans 9 to 11, and most of the prophets.

Paul himself is careful here in a way that popular teaching often isn't. In Romans 11 he pictures Gentile believers as a wild olive shoot grafted into a cultivated tree, and then says: "do not boast over the branches. But if you boast, remember that you do not support the root, but the root supports you" (Romans 11:18, NET).[31] Whatever else that image allows, it rules out the idea that the church has replaced Israel and inherited the address on the envelope.

So this course will say that a Christian may take Jeremiah 29 through the new covenant, and will not tell you which of those systems is right. Lesson 4 sets them out properly. When you meet a teacher who moves from an Old Testament promise to you without pausing at this step, they've made a decision. It may be the right one. You should be able to see that it was made.

## Why position changes meaning

You've seen this work twice now, so let me state it plainly.

Meaning depends on position in the story. A promise given to exiles carries the exile with it. A law given at Sinai carries the covenant it belongs to. A psalm sung in the rebuilt Temple carries the memory of the ruined one. When you lift a sentence out of its act, the words stay and the meaning goes, and what fills the empty space is usually your own situation. That's why the card reading of Jeremiah 29:11 feels so natural: the sentence, emptied of Babylon, fills up with you.

The reader who knows where they are can ask the one question that stops most misreadings before they start: what did this mean *then*? Lesson 3 gives that question its formal name, Fee and Stuart's basic rule, that a text cannot mean what it never could have meant to its author or readers.[8] Everything in this lesson is that rule in its simplest form. You can't know what a passage could have meant to them until you know where on the map they were standing.

## What people get wrong

**"The Old Testament is law; the New Testament is grace."** This is the oldest misreading of the shape there is, and it's usually answered too cheaply, so let me give it in its strong form first. Marcion, teaching in Rome around the year 144, was not a careless reader. He had noticed that the God who orders the destruction of Canaanite cities and the Father Jesus prays to are described very differently; he had read Paul's contrast between law and gospel and taken it at its word; and he concluded that the honest thing was to stop pretending the two were one God. He produced a Bible of his own: an edited Luke and ten of Paul's letters, with the Old Testament left out. Plenty of modern readers arrive at a softer version of the same place without ever hearing his name.[32]

The church rejected him, and in doing so had to say out loud that it already had a larger collection and meant to keep it. The reason it could reject him is that the pattern he claimed to find isn't there. Grace shows up in the act everyone calls law: Exodus 34:6 is God describing himself, at Sinai, as compassionate and slow to anger, and it's the most quoted self-description in the Hebrew Bible. Law shows up in the act everyone calls grace: "Do not think that I have come to abolish the law or the prophets. I have not come to abolish these things but to fulfill them" (Matthew 5:17, NET).[33] What changes across the gap is the covenant a reader stands under, not the character of the God on either side of it.

**"Christians don't need the Old Testament."** Count the quotations. Roger Nicole, working only with passages the New Testament writers introduce with a formula that shows they mean to quote, [counted 224 direct citations](https://www.bible-researcher.com/nicole.html); those citations draw on 278 different Old Testament verses, 94 from the Pentateuch, 99 from the Prophets, and 85 from the Writings, and his own conservative total of separate references runs to at least 295. Scholars who count looser echoes as well as formal quotations reach much higher: 613 on Toy's count, 1,640 on Dittmar's, 4,105 on Huehn's.[34] Jesus taught from the Old Testament; on the road to Emmaus he had nothing else to teach from, since none of the New Testament existed yet. Set the Old Testament aside and you've set aside the book the New Testament assumes you've read.

**"The timeline is the order of the books."** Said above, and worth repeating because it's the mistake that puts Amos after Nehemiah. It also puts Job wherever the reader last saw it, which is usually "somewhere near the end", though Job is the eighteenth book of thirty-nine and sits at the front of the poetry shelf. The Old Testament's shelf order is by kind of writing. The timeline is a separate thing you carry in your head, and this lesson has just handed it to you.

**"The Old Testament is Judaism's Bible with a Christian sequel."** Careful here. The same twenty-four books are, for Jews, the Tanakh: a complete Scripture with its own order and its own ending (Chronicles, and the call to go up to Jerusalem, as lesson 1 showed), read on its own terms by a living community today. Hayes tells her Yale students that she'll avoid the phrase "Old Testament" because it "sort of suggests the doctrine that the New Testament has somehow fulfilled or surpassed or antiquated the Bible of ancient Israel", and she's right that the phrase carries that claim.[18] This course uses "Old Testament" because we hold the claim: we read the whole as pointing to Christ because Jesus did, and we say so as our faith. But we also say plainly that a Jewish reader reads the same books as a different story with its own integrity, ending not in Bethlehem but in the return to Zion, and that when this course describes that reading it will try to describe it as a Jewish reader would.

**Part 2: four quick placements, mixed.** For each of these, write three things: the act and period, the covenant in play, and which of the three levels the sentence is working on most obviously. Don't do them in one category at a time; take them in order as they come.

1. "Solomon stood before the altar of the Lord in front of the entire assembly of Israel and spread out his hands toward the sky" (1 Kings 8:22, NET).
2. "The Lord's message came to Jonah son of Amittai, 'Go immediately to Nineveh, that large capital city, and announce judgment against its people'" (Jonah 1:1 to 2, NET).
3. "But when the appropriate time had come, God sent out his Son, born of a woman, born under the law" (Galatians 4:4, NET).
4. "Abram believed the Lord, and the Lord credited it as righteousness to him" (Genesis 15:6, NET).

**Part 3: Jeremiah 29 whole.** Read [Jeremiah 29:1 to 14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jeremiah+29%3A1-14&version=NET), all of it, in your own Bible. Then write three things:

1. The original addressee, in one line, from the text itself.
2. The original promise, in one line: what exactly is promised, to whom, and on what timescale.
3. One sentence on what a Christian may take from the passage, and one on what a Christian may not, using the five steps of the Interpretive Journey.

**Part 4: one more, and the hardest.** Open [2 Chronicles 36:22 and 23](https://www.sefaria.org/II_Chronicles.36.22-23), the last two verses of the Tanakh. In the Jewish Publication Society translation the last sentence reads: "Whosoever there is among you of all His people, the LORD his God be with him, let him go up." (2 Chronicles 36:23, JPS)[35] Say which act and which period these verses belong to, which covenant promise they're answering, and why a Jewish Bible would choose to end here.
:::

:::checkpoint Exercise Part 2: check your four placements before you go on.
**1 Kings 8:22.** Act 3, the united kingdom, at the dedication of the Temple. Covenant: David's most directly, since the Temple is Solomon's discharge of 2 Samuel 7, with Sinai underneath it because the ark carries the covenant tablets into the building. Level: middle. This is Israel's national life at its high point. **Jonah 1:1.** Act 3, the divided kingdom (Jonah son of Amittai is dated to Jeroboam II's reign at 2 Kings 14:25), with Nineveh as the Assyrian capital that will destroy the north in 722. Covenant: Abraham's, and this is the one most people miss. The whole point of the book is blessing reaching a foreign nation, which is Genesis 12:3. Level: the bottom level is a runaway prophet, and the top level is God's dealings with the nations. **Galatians 4:4.** Act 4. Covenant: the new covenant arriving, with Sinai named in the verse itself ("born under the law"). Level: top, explicitly; Paul is telling you what the whole plan was doing. **Genesis 15:6.** Act 3, the patriarchs. Covenant: Abraham's, in the chapter where it's cut. Level: bottom in form (one man believes something) and top in weight, which is why Paul builds Romans 4 on it.
:::

:::checkpoint Exercise Part 3: a model set of answers to compare against yours.
**Addressee:** the exiles Nebuchadnezzar deported from Jerusalem to Babylon, named in verse 1 as the elders, priests, prophets and the rest of the people. Not an individual, and not anyone in Jerusalem. **The promise:** that after seventy years of Babylonian rule God will bring this people back to their homeland (verses 10 and 14). The timescale is the point, and it's longer than most of the addressees will live. **What reaches a Christian, and what doesn't:** what reaches you is the principle you crossed the bridge with, that a long stretch of loss is not evidence that God has finished with his people, and that his plans can include the loss. What doesn't reach you is the specific promise: there's no land you're being restored to and no seventy-year clock running on your circumstances. If your answer to the third question could have been written without reading verses 1 to 10, you've probably skipped step one.
:::

:::checkpoint Exercise Part 4: how the Tanakh's ending works.
**Act and period:** Act 3, the return under Persia, with Cyrus's decree of 538 BC. **The covenant promise being answered:** Abraham's, the land, and behind it the seventy years of Jeremiah 29:10, which verse 22 names directly. **Why end here:** because the last word of the Jewish Bible is an instruction to go up to Jerusalem. The Tanakh closes with the people on their feet and facing home, and the story it tells is complete in that shape. Lesson 1 showed you the Christian Old Testament ending four books earlier, at Malachi, on a promise that someone is coming. Two arrangements of the same books, and each ending is an argument about what the whole thing is for. If you found yourself reaching for "but really it points to Jesus", stop and notice that you're doing the top level before you've finished the middle one. The Jewish ending is doing something on its own terms, and reading it well means seeing that first.
:::

## Connections

Lesson 1 gave you the shelves; this lesson gives you the timeline behind them, and you've now seen twice (Daniel last time, Ruth this time) that which shelf a book sits on is already a reading of it.

Every genre lesson from here on starts by asking where a passage sits on this map, because "what did it mean then?" can't be answered without it. Lesson 3 turns the three levels into a full method for reading narrative and gives you the rule this lesson kept pointing at. Lesson 4 comes back to the covenants in detail, Sinai above all, and to the systems that disagree about how they fit together. Lesson 7 gives the Jeremiah 29 move its proper name. Lesson 8 takes up the books in the four hundred years and the canons that carry them. Lesson 10 returns to the dates this lesson left unmarked, and to the question of who wrote the early books, as a real argument with two sides.

*The Story of Scripture*, later in this school, walks the six acts one at a time. This lesson is the map in a single sitting, so that the next verse you meet has an address.
