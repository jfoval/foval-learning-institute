> "They read from the book of God's law, explaining it and imparting insight. Thus the people gained understanding from what was read." (Nehemiah 8:8, NET)[1]

> "These Jews were more open-minded than those in Thessalonica, for they eagerly received the message, examining the scriptures carefully every day to see if these things were so." (Acts 17:11, NET)[1]

Two scenes, about five hundred years apart. In the first, Jerusalem's returned exiles stand in a square from early morning until midday while Ezra reads the Law aloud and thirteen named Levites teach the people the law while the people remain standing.[1] In the second, a synagogue in Berea hears Paul preach and then, rather than taking his word for it, opens the Scriptures every day to check.[1]

Put them together and you have the whole of this lesson. Reading with the sense given, so that what you read is what the writer meant. And reading daily, on a schedule, with other people, so that it keeps happening after the enthusiasm has gone. Eleven lessons have given you rules for eleven kinds of text. This one gives you one thing to carry: four questions, a handful of free tools, and a plan you'll still be following in February.

## Four questions you already know

You've been asking them since lesson 2. Here they are in one place.

1. **What kind of writing is this?** Story, law, poem, proverb, oracle, gospel, letter, vision. Lesson 3's rule: genre first, because genres are contracts, and the wrong contract misreads every sentence while feeling perfectly clear.
2. **Where in the story is it?** Which act of the six, which covenant is in force, who is speaking to whom. Lesson 2's rule: meaning depends on position. A promise to exiles is not a promise to graduates.
3. **What did it mean then?** To the person who wrote it and the people who first heard it. Fee and Stuart's basic rule, which you met in lesson 3 and used at full strength in lesson 7: a text cannot mean what it never could have meant to its author or readers.[2]
4. **What does it mean now?** Where our situation matches theirs, the word applies as written. Where it doesn't, find the principle the particular carries, and be honest about which is which. That is the comparable-particulars test from lesson 7.[3]

Why these four and not forty? Look back at every misreading this course has shown you. Jeremiah 29:11 on the card skipped question 2. Proverbs 22:6 as a guarantee skipped question 1. Philippians 4:13 on the gym wall skipped question 3. Head coverings applied without argument, or dismissed without argument, skipped question 4. I've not found a misreading in this course that didn't skip at least one of them, and I've not found one that survived all four. That's the mechanism: the questions force the decisions a careless reader makes without noticing.

If you go on to *How to Study the Bible*, you'll meet the same four under other names. Howard Hendricks, whose Bible-study course [Dallas Theological Seminary now gives away free](https://courses.dts.edu/courses/how-to-read-the-bible-like-a-seminary-professor), taught three steps: observation, interpretation, application.[4] Questions 1 and 2 are observation; question 3 is interpretation; question 4 is application. Duvall and Hays describe the same path as grasping the text "in their town", measuring the width of the river between their world and ours, crossing on a principle, and grasping the text "in our town".[5] The vocabulary differs and the method does not, which is worth knowing before you open either of them.

Take Philippians 4:13, since you've done it once and can watch the circles work. The sentence says "I am able to do all things through the one who strengthens me" (Philippians 4:13, NET).[1] The paragraph (4:10 to 13) says the "all things" are hunger and plenty, abundance and need. The book is a letter from prison to a church that sent money. The testament tells you what "in Christ" means across Paul's letters. The whole Bible tells you where contentment sits in the story of God's people. By the third circle the gym-wall reading is gone, and nothing outside the page was needed to remove it.

Or Psalm 137:9, the hardest verse in lesson 5. The sentence is a wish for the death of Babylon's children. The paragraph is a lament sung by people who had watched it done to theirs. The book is Israel's prayer book, which means this is spoken *to God*, not commanded by him. The testament supplies the covenant curses the psalm echoes. The whole Bible supplies Romans 12:19, vengeance left to God, which is what the psalmist is doing. Five circles, and the verse is still terrible, and it is no longer an endorsement.

The circles are not a formula for making hard verses easy. They are a way of making sure you've read the whole of what's there before you decide what it means. Most misreadings this course has named came from stopping at circle one.

:::checkpoint Open Habakkuk 1:1 to 4 (four verses). Run the four questions in four lines: what kind of writing, where in the story, what it meant then, and what a reader now may take from it.
Kind: verse 1 says "oracle" and "prophet", so this is prophecy; but verses 2 to 4 are not an oracle *from* God, they are a complaint *to* God ("How long, Lord, must I cry for help? But you do not listen!"), which is the shape of a lament from lesson 5.[1] Position: Judah, late in the kingdom, before the exile, with the covenant of Sinai in force and its curses looming; 1:6 names the Chaldeans (Babylon), which is how readers date it. Then: a prophet asks God why he tolerates injustice inside his own people, and (read on) God's answer is that Babylon is coming, which is worse news than the question. Now: the book puts a believer's complaint inside Scripture, and a reader who has ever asked "how long?" is on comparable ground; the particular (Babylon at the gates) is not ours, the principle (God hears the question and answers on his own terms, 2:4) is. Two genres in four verses, and the four questions caught both.
:::

## How to start a book you've never read

A whole book is different from a passage. You don't yet know its shape, and until you do, every paragraph floats. Fee and Stuart wrote a companion volume, *How to Read the Bible Book by Book*, that walks through all sixty-six with the same four headings each time: orienting data, overview, specific advice for reading, and a walk through.[6] Those four headings are also the pattern for the reading guide you'll write for this course's project, so learn them now.

**Orienting data** is the who, when, to whom, from where, and why, plus the genre and the place in the story. For an argued question (who wrote Hebrews, when Jonah was written), you write both positions, which is exactly the three-way sort from lesson 10 applied to a book's front matter.

**Overview** is the book's shape in a paragraph: how it begins, turns, and ends.

**Specific advice for reading** is what a first-time reader will get wrong and what to watch for instead: the genre's rules, the repeated word, the character the narrator never praises.

**A walk through** is the sections in order, each in a line or two.

Here is how to gather that from a cold start, and it costs one sitting. Read the book straight through once, fast, in your reading translation from lesson 11, without stopping to look anything up. Short books take minutes: [Crossway's reading-time survey](https://www.crossway.org/articles/infographic-you-can-read-more-of-the-bible-than-you-think/) puts Ruth and Philippians at about fifteen minutes each.[7] Then read it again slowly with the four questions and a pencil. The first pass gives you the overview; the second gives you everything else. People who skip the first pass end up writing a walk through with no sense of where they're walking to.

## Worked example 1: Philemon, cold

You read Philemon in lesson 7 and reconstructed the situation from the letter alone. Now do the whole method on it, as if you'd never seen it, so you can see what a finished reading of one book looks like.

:::predict Before you open it, read only verse 1: "From Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus, and Timothy our brother, to Philemon, our dear friend and colaborer".[1] From that line alone, guess: what kind of writing, and what is the writer's situation?
A letter (it has a sender and a recipient, so genre is settled before you reach verse 2), from a man in prison, to one man rather than a church, though verse 2 adds "the church that meets in your house", so it will be read aloud to a room. Everything Paul asks in the next twenty-four verses is asked in front of Philemon's congregation, which is part of how he asks it.
:::

**Question 1, genre.** A letter, and an occasional one: it exists because of one situation. So the rules of lesson 7 apply. Think paragraphs; reconstruct the situation; expect one side of a conversation.

**Question 2, position.** Act five, the mission of the church, in the decade or so after Paul's letters to the Corinthians and Romans, while he is a prisoner (verse 1, verse 9). The new covenant is in force. Slavery is a Roman institution that neither Paul nor Philemon can vote against.

**Question 3, then-meaning.** Paul appeals for "my child, whose spiritual father I have become during my imprisonment, that is, Onesimus" (verse 10), Philemon's slave, now a Christian, being sent back.[1] The request is that Philemon receive him "no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, as a dear brother" (verse 16), and "accept him as you would me" (verse 17).[1] Paul could command (verse 8) and chooses to ask, offers to pay any debt (verse 18), and closes by saying he knew "that you would do even more than what I am asking you to do" (verse 21).[1] What did it mean to Philemon, sitting in his own house church hearing this read? That the apostle who brought him to faith (verse 19) was asking him, in front of everyone, to treat his slave as his brother, and was leaving the "even more" for Philemon to work out.

:::predict **Question 4, now-meaning.** What in this letter applies to you as written, and what applies by principle? Write two lines before you read mine.
The particulars (a slave, a Roman household, a runaway) are not ours, so nothing in Philemon is a direct instruction about a situation we're in. The principle survives the test cleanly: in Christ, the person you have power over is your brother, and the gospel asks you to treat them that way before it asks anyone else to change the rules. What the letter does *not* settle is the question later Christians fought over, whether the gospel abolishes slavery or only transforms it; Paul's "even more than I say" is where abolitionists and their opponents both planted their flag, and an honest reading guide says so.
:::

**Orienting data, in the pattern:** from Paul, in prison, with Timothy; to Philemon, with Apphia, Archippus, and the house church; about Onesimus; a personal letter; act five, new covenant; one of the few letters in the New Testament occasioned from the writer's side rather than by a problem among the readers.[2] **Overview:** greeting and thanksgiving (1 to 7), the appeal (8 to 20), the confident close (21 to 25). **Advice for reading:** notice what Paul never says (he never uses the word "free"), and notice the audience in verse 2. **Walk through:** the three sections above, each in a line. Two readings and about an hour produced all of that.

## Worked example 2: Jonah, where the method has to handle an argued genre

Philemon was easy because question 1 answered itself in the first line. Jonah is the case with a wrinkle, and the wrinkle is that the first question is itself contested.

:::predict Read Jonah 1 to 3 first (it takes about ten minutes). Then, before you read chapter 4, predict: God is going to "appoint" a plant to grow over Jonah's head. What do you think the plant is for, and what will God do with it?
Most first-time readers guess the plant is a kindness, a reward for a job done. It is a kindness, for a day. Then God appoints a worm and a wind, the plant dies, and Jonah's grief for a plant becomes the argument of the book. The point of the plant is the question God asks about it in 4:10 and 11, and the book ends on that question with no answer.
:::

**Question 1, genre, and why it is argued.** Jonah sits among the twelve prophets, and lesson 4 taught you to expect collections of oracles there. Open it and you find a story about a prophet, with one line of preaching in the whole book: "At the end of 40 days, Nineveh will be overthrown!" (3:4).[1] Everything else is narrative, and narrative with an edge. The prophet runs (1:3). The pagan sailors pray while he sleeps. The Assyrians, Israel's cruellest enemy, repent "from the greatest to the least of them" (3:5) at one sentence, and the king orders the animals into sackcloth along with the people (3:8).[1] Jonah explains that he fled precisely because he knew God was "a gracious and compassionate God, slow to anger and abounding in mercy" (4:2), quoting Exodus 34:6 as a complaint.[1]

History establishes that Nineveh was a real Assyrian capital and that a prophet named Jonah son of Amittai appears in 2 Kings 14:25, in the reign of Jeroboam II in the eighth century BC.[1] Scholars argue about the rest. [Christine Hayes at Yale](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-24) calls the book "a comic tale about a reluctant prophet" and "this little satire", and says its date "really can't be ascertained and you will hear arguments in both directions".[8] The [Society for Old Testament Study's guide](https://www.sots.ac.uk/wiki/jonah/) is worth reading whole rather than in pieces, because it is choosing between non-historical genres rather than weighing history against parable. It opens the question with "Granted that the book is not a historical narrative, how should one regard its genre?", and gives its reasons for that premise: the book calls the Assyrian king the king of Nineveh, its picture of the city's size is exaggerated, and a mass instantaneous repentance of Ninevites is "unlikely and unknown to history". Within that premise it holds the book is "parabolic in intention" but "far too long to be a parable in the strict literary sense", calls it "a didactic story about a prophet", notes that "some have called it a satire", and dates it post-exilic, "probably the 4th century BCE".[9] Conservative readers reply that the narrative is set in a real reign with a real prophet, and that Jesus says "The people of Nineveh will stand up at the judgment with this generation and condemn it because they repented when Jonah preached to them" (Matthew 12:41), which reads as history rather than fable.[1] Critical readers answer that Jesus could use a known story the way any teacher uses one, and that "the sign of the prophet Jonah" (12:39, 40) is about three days and a return, not about genre.[1]

We hold by faith that the book is God's word and true. We read it as an account of something that happened, because that is how Jesus seems to us to speak of it, and we say plainly that a Christian who reads it as an inspired parable is not thereby denying Scripture; that is the honest range. The mockery is old, too. Around 409 [Augustine](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1102102.htm) answered a set of questions a correspondent had put to him, one of them about the fish and the gourd, and the question as it reached him ended "Questions such as these I have seen discussed by Pagans amidst loud laughter, and with great scorn". Augustine's reply was that either every miracle worked by divine power is incredible, or there is no reason to disbelieve this one, since the same objection would sink the resurrection.[20] What the argument does *not* change is the reading. Satire or history, the comic touches are on the page, and they point the same way.

**Question 2, position.** Set in the eighth century, in the divided kingdom, with Assyria the coming threat; if the critical date is right, written to a post-exilic community that had every reason to resent the nations. Either way, the covenant of Sinai is in force, Exodus 34:6 is the creed Jonah quotes, and the reader is Israel, being asked how it feels about God's mercy reaching Nineveh.

**Question 3, then-meaning.** Read badly, Jonah is a fish story, or a children's tale about obeying God the first time. The fish gets three verses. Read well, it is a book about God's mercy to enemies and a reader who resents it. The clue is that the prophet is the anti-hero: every pagan in the book behaves better than he does. The ending confirms it. God asks, "You were upset about this little plant, something for which you did not work... Should I not be more concerned about Nineveh, this enormous city? There are more than 120,000 people in it who do not know right from wrong, as well as many animals." (4:10 to 11)[1] The book stops there. Jonah never answers. The silence is the device. The question is left with the reader, and the first reader was Israel.

:::predict The fourth question is yours. Write one line before you read mine: what is the comparable particular here, and what is the principle?
The particular (Assyria, Nineveh, the eighth century) is not ours; the principle is that God's mercy to the people we would least choose is the thing we are most likely to resent, and the book is built to make us feel that before it tells us. Jesus, in Matthew 12:41, turns the same point on his own hearers: Nineveh repented at one sentence, and "something greater than Jonah is here!"[1]
:::

**The wrinkle, stated.** The method handled a book whose genre is argued because question 1 does not require a verdict; it requires that you know what the options are and read with the features that are actually on the page. Your reading guide's orienting data will say "genre: narrative about a prophet, with satirical features; whether it records history is argued; here are the two positions". That is a better guide than one that pretends the question away in either direction.

## The tools, and which question each one answers

You don't need Hebrew or Greek to read the Bible well. You need the four questions and a few free tools, and you need to know which tool answers which question, because the most common mistake with tools is reaching for a Greek lexicon to answer a question the paragraph would have answered.

**For question 1 and the overview: BibleProject.** A five- to ten-minute animated overview of every book, with the shape drawn on a poster, free at [bibleproject.com](https://bibleproject.com/) and on YouTube.[11] Use it after your first read, as a check.

**For questions 2 and 3: a study Bible, read knowingly, and the free option first.** The notes at the bottom of the page give the date, setting, and cross-references, and they are useful. They are also one scholar's help, dated, fallible, and written from a position. Find out who wrote the notes in yours and which tradition they come from. They are not Scripture, and a note that says "this means X" is a claim to be checked against the circles, not a verdict.

**For seeing the words: BibleGateway.** Dozens of translations side by side, free, at [biblegateway.com](https://www.biblegateway.com/).[12] This is the tool from lesson 11: when two translations differ, you've found a place where a decision was made, and the difference is your prompt to ask what the Hebrew or Greek allows.

**For the decision behind the words: the NET Bible.** A translation that shows its working, with more than 60,000 translators' notes explaining choices, free at [netbible.org](https://netbible.org).[13] When BibleGateway shows you a difference, the NET note usually tells you why.

**For the word under the English: STEP Bible and Blue Letter Bible.** [STEP Bible](https://www.stepbible.org/), built with Tyndale House in Cambridge, is free, works offline, and puts the tagged Hebrew and Greek under every English word so you can see what's there without knowing the alphabet.[14] [Blue Letter Bible](https://www.blueletterbible.org/) does the same with Strong's numbers, an inline interlinear, cross-references, and a library of commentaries.[15] Both will show you in a minute that the word behind "slave" in Philemon 16 is the word Paul uses of himself in Romans 1:1, *doulos*, which English versions variously render "slave", "servant", and "bondservant", sometimes differently in the two places.

**For reading and listening: ESV.org and YouVersion.** [ESV.org](https://www.esv.org/) gives the ESV free with cross-references and audio; the [YouVersion](https://www.bible.com/) app carries most translations and hosts most of the reading plans below, including BibleProject's.[16]

Here is where this course stops. Knowing that STEP shows you the Greek word is not the same as knowing how to do a word study: how to check every use, how to weigh a range of meaning, how not to commit the errors that a half-trained reader with a lexicon commits daily. Cross-reference technique, the use of a concordance, and how to read a commentary against its author's position are the same kind of skill. All of that is *How to Study the Bible*, the next course, and it assumes what this one has given you: the genres, the story, the basic rule, and the four questions. This lesson names the tools so that you know they exist and which question each one answers. It does not pretend that a lookup is a study.

:::callout Study Bible notes and the person who wrote them
The notes in the ESV Study Bible, the NIV Study Bible, the Jewish Study Bible, and the Catholic Study Bible are written by different scholars from different traditions, and they will disagree with each other at Isaiah 7:14, at Romans 9, and at the ending of Mark. That is not a scandal; it is lesson 10 in miniature. A reader who knows who wrote the notes can weigh them. A reader who thinks the notes are part of the Bible cannot.
:::

:::checkpoint You have three questions about three passages. Which of the tools above answers each, and which tool would answer a different question from the one asked? (a) Why does my Bible bracket the end of Mark. (b) What is the shape of Habakkuk as a whole. (c) Does "slave" in Philemon 16 translate the same word as "servant" in Romans 1:1.
(a) The NET Bible notes, because the question is about a translation decision and the NET is the translation that shows its working. BibleGateway would only show you that versions differ, which you already knew.

(b) BibleProject's overview, which is what an overview is for. A lexicon would tell you nothing about shape.

(c) STEP Bible or Blue Letter Bible, which put the tagged Greek under the English. It is *doulos* in both.

Notice that none of the three is answered by the tool a beginner reaches for first, which is usually a study Bible note.
:::

## A plan that survives February

The number matters less than the shape of it. Seventy hours once is a lot. Twelve minutes a day is not, and the difference between those two ways of spending seventy hours is the whole reason a plan exists.

The mechanism is simpler than the one people usually reach for. A little on a schedule beats a lot once mostly because it happens at all, and because a book read across two weeks accumulates context in your head that a book read in one evening does not.

Be careful with the learning research here, because it is easy to misuse and this lesson is not going to. Cepeda and colleagues' review of 317 experiments found that study spread over time reliably beats the same time spent in one block, and that the best gap grows with how long you need to keep the material.[17] But that result is about coming back to *the same* material, and a straight-through reading plan gives you new material every day, so it is not spacing in that sense. Where the research does bite is in two specific places. M'Cheyne's calendar has you read the New Testament and Psalms twice in the year, and that genuinely is spaced re-reading of the same text.[17][19] And the log's demand for one observation per sitting is retrieval practice, which is the effect Roediger and Karpicke measured: producing something from memory makes it stick in a way that rereading does not.[18] A log makes the schedule real, because the schedule you don't record is the one you'll believe you kept.

**The plans worth knowing.**

*M'Cheyne.* Robert Murray M'Cheyne, minister of St Peter's, Dundee, drew up a calendar for his congregation in December 1842 and called it "Daily Bread".[19] Four chapters a day, two for the family and two "secret", and over the year "the Old Testament once, the New Testament and Psalms twice".[19] On 1 January you read Genesis 1, Matthew 1, Ezra 1, and Acts 1, and the whole of Scripture is in play from the first morning. It is the classic, it is free at [mcheyne.info](https://www.mcheyne.info/) and on BibleGateway, and it is a lot: four chapters is about fifteen minutes on a good day.

*A Gospel-first plan.* One Gospel, a chapter a day, then the next. BibleGateway's ["Read the Gospels in 40 Days"](https://www.biblegateway.com/reading-plans/) is the ready-made version.[12] For someone new, or coming back, this is where I'd start: the person the whole book is about, in the order the church has always begun.

*The one-year plan with the map.* BibleProject's ["One Story That Leads to Jesus"](https://bibleproject.com/reading-plans/) plan on YouVersion reads the whole Bible in a year with their overview video at the start of each book, so lesson 2's map is redrawn for you as you go.[11]

*The New Testament in a year.* On [BibleGateway](https://www.biblegateway.com/reading-plans/); a chapter most days, about three minutes on Crossway's own rate of twelve minutes for the whole Bible.[12] Small enough to keep with a job and children.

**Which one?** The one you will actually keep, and M'Cheyne said so himself. Before the calendar he listed the dangers of using it, and the fourth was "a yoke too heavy to bear": "If this be the case with any, throw aside the fetter, and feed at liberty in the sweet garden of God. My desire is not to cast a snare upon you, but to be a helper of your joy."[19] The man who wrote the most demanding plan in common use told his people to drop it the moment it became a burden. The man who wrote the most demanding plan in common use told his own congregation to drop it the moment it became a burden, which is the measure: a plan you admire and don't keep is worth less than a chapter a day you do.

**Reading together.** Look again at the two scenes this lesson opened with. Ezra reads to a crowd; the Levites explain it and impart insight; the Bereans examine the scriptures as a synagogue. M'Cheyne set out his purpose for a shared calendar as wanting "all might be feeding in the same portion of the green pasture at the same time", and among the advantages he numbered, the third is that friends will "have a subject for profitable conversation in the portions read that day" and the fifth that "the sweet bond of Christian love and unity will be strengthened".[19] Private reading was rare for most of the church's history, because copies were expensive and few people could read; the normal mode was reading together, aloud, with someone explaining. That is a fact about literacy and cost rather than a rule, and Acts has a man reading Isaiah by himself in a chariot. But the practice the Bible mostly shows is the shared one. Find one other person and read the same book on the same days. The log you'll keep for the project is easier to keep when someone else will see it.

## What people get wrong

"I need Hebrew and Greek to read the Bible well." You need the four questions, a good translation, and the tools above. Nearly every misreading in this course was made by people reading in their own language and skipping a question, and the languages would not have saved them; Philippians 4:13 is just as misreadable in Greek. The languages come later, if at all, and *How to Study the Bible* will show you how far the tools take you without them.

"Reading through in a year is the mark of a serious Christian." M'Cheyne, who wrote the year-plan, listed formality, self-righteousness, and careless reading as its first three dangers before anyone had used it.[19] A year-plan is one good way to read. So is one Gospel slowly. A year-plan is one good way to read. So is one Gospel slowly. Neither is the measure, and the chapter count least of all.

"Study Bible notes are Scripture." They are one scholar's help, and a good study Bible says so on its own title page. Know who wrote yours. Weigh the notes as you'd weigh a good teacher: gratefully, and against the text.

"Reading alone is the ideal." Nehemiah 8 and Acts 17 are both crowd scenes, and M'Cheyne's calendar was written for a congregation. Solitary reading is fine and necessary; it was never the norm, and a reader with no one to ask "does that make sense?" is the reader most likely to keep a misreading for years.

:::checkpoint A friend has read the whole Bible in a year, twice, and cannot say what Habakkuk is about. Another has read Mark eleven times and nothing else. Using this lesson, what would you say to each?
To the first, that the reading happened and the sense was not given, which is M'Cheyne's third danger. Four chapters a day at speed is perfectly compatible with learning very little, and one slow book with the four questions would do more.

To the second, that a plan kept is the harder half and it is already done, so what to add is width rather than more repetition. The story of lesson 2 is the thing one Gospel cannot supply.

Neither friend has failed. They have each run out of a different half of the same practice.
:::

## Practice

:::exercise Do it now: one book, one plan, one log
**Part 1: Jonah, whole.** Read all four chapters in one sitting (about ten minutes), then read them again slowly with the four questions. Then write a half-page reading guide in the *Book by Book* pattern: orienting data (with the genre and historicity question given as argued, both positions in a line each), overview, three pieces of specific advice for a first-time reader, and a walk through of the four chapters in four lines. Make sure a reader can see which line is established, which is argued, and which is what we hold by faith.

**Part 2: your plan.** Choose one of the four plans above, or one book from the project list (Ruth, Jonah, Habakkuk, Mark, Philippians, or Philemon with Colossians). Write the first fourteen days with actual passages and actual times of day: "Day 1, Mark 1, 7:10 on the train." Make it smaller than you think you need. If you're torn between two sizes, take the smaller one; M'Cheyne's fourth danger is the one that ends most plans.

**Part 3: the log.** Set up the file, notebook, or page you'll use. Three columns: date, what you read, one observation that could only have come from the actual text (not "it was encouraging" but "the sailors pray before the prophet does"). This is the log the project asks for. Start it today with Jonah, so that day one is already filled in.

This exercise is the launch of the course project: read one short book by its genre over fourteen days, keeping the log, then read it once more straight through and write a two-page guide for someone else in the pattern you've just practised. The plan you wrote in Part 2 is day one of that project.
:::
