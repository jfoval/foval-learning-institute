> Do not answer a fool according to his folly,  
> lest you yourself also be like him.  
> Answer a fool according to his folly,  
> lest he be wise in his own opinion.  
>
> Proverbs 26:4 to 5 (NET)[1]

:::predict Which of the two is correct? Pick one before you read on, and say in a sentence why the other one is there.
Both are correct, and the placement is the point. Whoever put Proverbs together set two opposite instructions next to each other on purpose. A proverb isn't a rule that covers every case. It's a compressed observation about how things usually go, and it leaves the reader to judge which case they're in. Sometimes engaging with a fool drags you down to his level; sometimes silence lets him think he has won. Wisdom is knowing which is which. If you read either verse alone as a command, you'd be doing the thing the book is built to teach you not to do.
:::

That pair of verses is this lesson in miniature. Most of what isn't narrative in the Old Testament is poetry: Fee and Stuart put narrative at more than forty percent of the Protestant Old Testament, and the Psalms, Proverbs, Job and Ecclesiastes are almost entirely poetry, as are most of the prophets you read in lesson 4.[2] Poetry works by different rules from the narrative of lesson 3 and the law of lesson 4. The rules aren't hard. But a reader who doesn't know them will misread a psalm as a list of doctrines, a proverb as a promise, and Psalm 137 as an endorsement of killing children, and will feel perfectly clear the whole time. That's the genre trap from lesson 3 again, and this lesson is the way out of it.

## How Hebrew poetry works: the second line

Start with how a Hebrew poem is built, because everything else follows from it. Hebrew poetry doesn't rhyme sounds. It rhymes ideas. A poet says something in one line and then says it again in a second line that repeats, sharpens, contrasts or completes the first. Read the opening of [Psalm 19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+19&version=NET):

> The heavens declare the glory of God;  
> the sky displays his handiwork.  
> Day after day it speaks out;  
> night after night it reveals his greatness.  
>
> Psalm 19:1 to 2 (NET)[1]

Heavens and sky; declare and display; glory and handiwork. The second line isn't adding a new fact about a different object. Fee and Stuart, whose textbook this course follows, put it plainly: the four lines "are not trying to say four different things", and the poet "is not trying to say that the 'heavens' do one thing and the 'skies' another; together the two lines speak of one glorious reality".[3] Then the second pair adds something: the speech goes on day and night, without pause.

This pairing is called parallelism. The name comes from Robert Lowth, Professor of Poetry at Oxford and later Bishop of London, who published his lectures on Hebrew poetry in Latin in 1753.[4] He sorted the pairs into three kinds, and Fee and Stuart still teach the three with one example each from the prophets.[5]

**Synonymous**, where the second line restates the first (Isaiah 44:22, NET):

> I remove the guilt of your rebellious deeds as if they were a cloud,  
> the guilt of your sins as if they were a cloud.  

**Antithetic**, where the second line contrasts (Hosea 7:14, NET):

> They do not pray to me,  
> but howl in distress on their beds.  

**Synthetic**, where the second line simply carries the thought forward (Obadiah 21, NET):

> Those who have been delivered will go up on Mount Zion  
> in order to rule over Esau's mountain.  

The labels are useful and a little misleading, and you should know the correction. In 1981 James Kugel argued in *The Idea of Biblical Poetry* that "synonymous" lines are almost never simply synonymous: the second line seconds the first, and his shorthand for the relationship is "A, and what's more, B".[6] Standard summaries of his argument set out the ways B can second A: going further, narrowing, intensifying, completing. Test it on Psalm 19. "Declare the glory of God" is general; "displays his handiwork" makes it specific, the sky as evidence of a maker. The second line is usually where the emphasis lands.

So the habit to build is this: whenever you meet a pair of lines, ask what the second one adds. Not "what does line B mean on its own", and not "these two lines say the same thing so I can skip one", but "what's more".

:::checkpoint [Proverbs 10:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Proverbs+10&version=NET) (NET): "A wise child makes a father rejoice, but a foolish child is a grief to his mother."[1] Which of Lowth's three forms is this, and what does the second line add that the first did not say?
Antithetic. The second line contrasts with the first. And it adds more than a contrast: it brings in the mother, so the two lines together cover both parents, and it names the cost of folly (grief) rather than only the benefit of wisdom (rejoicing). One point, seen from both sides, with the weight on the warning. Notice too what the NET does with the Hebrew here: "child" where older English versions have "son". That's a translation decision, and lesson 11 is about how translators make them.
:::

## Imagery is the argument

The second thing to know is that poetry's vocabulary is, in Fee and Stuart's words, "purposefully metaphorical".[3] In the Psalms the mountains skip like rams (114:4), enemies have tongues like sharp swords (57:4), and God is a shepherd, a rock, a fortress, a shield.[1] None of that is decoration on top of a plain statement. It's how the statement is made.

The mistake runs in two directions. One is to press the picture: to read Psalm 23 as a recommendation of rural life, or, as Fee and Stuart note about Psalm 51:5 ("a sinner the moment my mother conceived me"), to extract a doctrine about the sinfulness of conception from a line of hyperbole whose point is that the psalmist's sin goes back as far as he can remember.[1][3] The other mistake is to strip the picture out and keep a proposition.

Set the two side by side and you can see what the second mistake costs. Fee and Stuart offer "God is revealed in his creation" as a prose rendering of Psalm 19:1 to 2, and call it "totally colorless".[3] It is also shorter, and the difference is the whole point. The poem makes you supply four things the sentence hands over: that the sky is *evidence*, since "declare" and "display" are what a witness does; that the evidence is *continuous*, because day follows night follows day; that the speech has *no words* and is heard anyway, which is where the poem goes next in verse 3; and that the one being testified to is a *maker*, because "handiwork" is a word about hands. The prose sentence asserts all of that and shows none of it.

That supplying is the reading work, and it's why poetry compresses. A prose writer spells out the connections; a poet leaves them for you. Read a psalm as a list of propositions and you strip out the part where the meaning lives, which is the leap between line A and line B and between the picture and what it points at.

:::checkpoint Psalm 18:2 (NET) calls God "my stronghold, my deliverer, my God, my rocky summit where I take shelter, my shield, the horn that saves me, and my refuge".[1] What do those pictures claim that the sentence "God protects me" does not?
Several things, and each is a different kind of protection. A rocky summit is high ground you climb to and an attacker has to climb after you. A shield is carried into a fight, not hidden behind. A horn is an animal's weapon, so it isn't defensive at all. A refuge is somewhere you run when you're already losing. Put together they claim that God is protection of every kind at once, including the kind that goes on the attack, and that the psalmist has been in enough different sorts of trouble to need all of them. "God protects me" is true and says none of it. If you found yourself asking which one God literally is, that's the first mistake, pressing the picture; the pile-up of seven images in one verse is the poem telling you not to.
:::

## The Psalms: words to God that are also God's word

The book of Psalms is a collection of 150 prayers and songs in the Hebrew numbering, which Protestant and most modern Catholic Bibles follow. In the Hebrew Bible it's called Tehillim, "praises", and it stands among the Writings, the third section of the Tanakh you met in lesson 1.

Two things about numbering, before you go looking anything up. The Greek and Latin tradition counts differently from about Psalm 10 to Psalm 147, so a psalm this lesson calls 51 is Psalm 50 in an Orthodox psalter or an older Catholic Bible, and Psalm 137 is 136. And the Greek Psalter has a **Psalm 151**, which Orthodox Bibles carry; the Psalms Scroll in the photograph below has it in Hebrew. Lesson 8 takes up why the canons differ.

The Psalter is prayed, and has been continuously, in more places than a Protestant reader usually notices. In the synagogue: Ashrei (Psalm 145) three times a day, Hallel (113 to 118) at the festivals, a psalm for each day of the week, Psalm 30 in the morning service, psalms in a house of mourning. In the monasteries: the Rule of St Benedict has the community pray the whole Psalter every week, and the modern Liturgy of the Hours spreads it over four. In the Orthodox churches: the Psalter is divided into twenty kathismata and read through weekly, and twice a week in Lent. That is the practice this lesson is describing from the outside, and those traditions have simply done it.

This is what praying the psalms as psalms sounds like from inside the tradition whose book it first was (Psalm 19:8, [JPS 1917](https://www.sefaria.org/Psalms.19.8)):[7]

> The law of the LORD is perfect, restoring the soul;  
> the testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple.  

Most of the Bible is words from God to people. The Psalms are words from people to God. Fee and Stuart call this "a unique problem of hermeneutics": how do "words spoken to God function as a word from God to us"?[3]

We hold by faith, with the whole church, that these prayers are Scripture. But the way they're God's word differs from the way a law or a letter is. A psalm doesn't teach doctrine by stating it. It teaches by giving you words to say to God, and shaping you as you say them. Fee and Stuart's answer to their own question is that the psalms work for us "precisely in the ways they functioned for Israel in the first place", as "opportunities to speak to God with the help of words he inspired others to use to speak to him".[3] So the first reading rule for a psalm is: this is a prayer. Ask what it does, and what it would do to you if you prayed it, before you ask what it asserts.

:::callout The line before verse 1
Most psalms open with a superscription: "For the music director; a psalm of David", or, at Psalm 88, "A well-written song by Heman the Ezrahite". This lesson leaves them out of its quotations to keep the poems short, and you should know that they're there, because in a Hebrew Bible the superscription **is verse 1**, so the whole psalm is numbered one higher than in your English Bible. Psalm 13's four "how long" questions are 13:2 to 3 in a Hebrew Bible and 13:1 to 2 in yours.

What "of David" means is argued. It may name the author, or the collection a psalm came from, or a dedication, and the Hebrew preposition will carry any of the three. This course doesn't settle it, and you should be careful of anyone who tells you a psalm's biography with confidence.
:::

### The types, and how to tell them apart

The second rule is that psalms come in types, and each type has a shape. Israelites didn't need to be told this any more than you need to be told that a lullaby and a football chant are different kinds of song. We have to learn it as a list.

The modern study of the types goes back to Hermann Gunkel (1862 to 1932), whose introduction to the Psalms was completed by Joachim Begrich and published in 1933.[8] Gunkel's argument had two halves, and this lesson uses one of them. The first half is the one everybody kept: psalms fall into recognisable forms. The second is that each form had a *Sitz im Leben*, a setting in Israel's life, usually in the temple's worship. Sigmund Mowinckel pressed that further and argued that most psalms were written for the cult rather than as private devotion, which is where the name "enthronement psalms" in the list below comes from: he read them as the liturgy of an annual festival.

That second half is argued, and it cuts against the reading rule I just gave you. If most psalms are liturgy first, then "what would this do to you if you prayed it" is not the original question; the original question is what it did in a service. Christine Hayes, who taught the Hebrew Bible at Yale, notes that the form critics have multiplied Gunkel's categories since: "the very serious form critics will give you upwards of 13, 14, 15 or more different forms".[9] What survives either way, and what this lesson needs, is that the forms are real and that knowing one changes how you read.

Fee and Stuart use seven types, which is enough for a reader.[3] Each has a test you can apply, which matters more than the psalm numbers:

- **Lament.** Is the trouble *present*? Then it's a lament: address, complaint, petition, and usually a turn toward trust. "More than sixty", individual (3, 22, 42, 88, 139 among them) and corporate (12, 44, 80, 94, 137).
- **Thanksgiving.** Is the trouble *past* and the rescue already given? Then it's thanksgiving, and Fee and Stuart give it five parts: introduction, the distress recalled, the appeal reiterated, the deliverance described, and testimony. They work it on Psalm 138. Six community (65, 67, 75, 107, 124, 136) and ten individual (18, 30, 32, 34, 40, 66, 92, 116, 118, 138).
- **Hymn of praise.** Is there no particular occasion at all, just God praised for who he is? Sixteen of them, in three groups (8, 19, 104, 148; 66, 100, 111, 114, 149; 33, 103, 113, 117, 145 to 147).
- **Salvation history.** Does it retell the exodus and the wilderness? (78, 105, 106, 135, 136.)
- **Celebration and affirmation.** Is the subject the covenant, the king, or the city? Covenant renewal (50, 81), Davidic covenant (89, 132), nine royal psalms (2, 18, 20, 21, 45, 72, 101, 110, 144), the enthronement psalms (24, 29, 47, 93, 95 to 99), and the Songs of Zion (46, 48, 76, 84, 87, 122).
- **Wisdom.** Does it read like Proverbs set to music? Eight (36, 37, 49, 73, 112, 127, 128, 133).
- **Song of trust.** Is it confidence with no crisis being worked through? Ten (11, 16, 23, 27, 62, 63, 91, 121, 125, 131).

The first test is the one you'll use most, and it's a question about tense: is the psalmist still in it?

Look at the first bar, and then at the ones under it. Lament is the largest single type, larger than any one kind of praise, and Fee and Stuart remark that this "probably says something about our common humanity".[3] Be careful about the sentence that wants to come next. Add the praise categories together, thanksgiving and hymns and celebration, and they outnumber the laments, so the Psalter is not mostly complaint. What's true, and worth knowing before you decide what counts as a proper prayer, is that no single kind of praise is as large as lament. The laments bar is drawn at exactly 60 because the source says "more than sixty" and gives no number; do not read a precision off it that Fee and Stuart didn't supply.

## Psalm 13, read badly and then well

The whole psalm is six verses.

> **1** How long, Lord, will you continue to ignore me?  
> How long will you pay no attention to me?  
> **2** How long must I worry,  
> and suffer in broad daylight?  
> How long will my enemy gloat over me?  
>
> **3** Look at me! Answer me, O Lord my God!  
> Revive me, or else I will die.  
> **4** Then my enemy will say, "I have defeated him."  
> Then my foes will rejoice because I am shaken.  
>
> **5** But I trust in your faithfulness.  
> May I rejoice because of your deliverance.  
> **6** I will sing praises to the Lord  
> when he vindicates me.  
>
> Psalm 13 (NET)[1]

**Read badly.** Treat it as a list of claims. Verse 1: God ignores people and pays them no attention. Verse 5: God is faithful and he delivers. Verse 6: God vindicates. A reader who takes the psalm as doctrine has to either pick a verse and discard the rest, or conclude that the psalmist changed his theology between lines. That isn't reading the psalm; that's arguing with a list.

**Read well.** Ask what type this is. The trouble is present, so it's an individual lament, and Fee and Stuart, comparing all the laments, find six elements that appear "in one way or another in virtually all of them", usually in this order: address, complaint, trust, deliverance (the petition), assurance, and praise.[3] Now lay the psalm on that template.

- *Address:* "Lord", in the first line, and again in verse 3, "O Lord my God".
- *Complaint:* the "how long" questions of verses 1 and 2. Notice they escalate: ignored by God, given no attention, left alone to worry and suffer in broad daylight, and then gloated over by an enemy. That's parallelism doing its "what's more" work across four questions.

:::predict Verses 3 and 4 are the psalm's next move. Which two of the six elements are they, what exactly does the psalmist ask for, and what does he say is at stake if the answer doesn't come?
*Deliverance*, the petition: "look at me", "answer me", "revive me". Then the complaint comes back, restated as stakes: death, the enemy's boast, the foes' joy. Verses 5 and 6 carry the last three elements in quick succession. *Trust:* "I trust in your faithfulness." *Assurance:* "May I rejoice because of your deliverance", asked for and not yet seen. *Praise:* "I will sing praises to the Lord".
:::

So the psalm isn't a set of claims that contradict. It's a movement, and the movement is the meaning. A person in real trouble speaks the trouble to God at full strength, asks, and then, without the trouble being resolved, chooses to stand on what he knows of God's character. The word the NET gives as "faithfulness" is the Hebrew *hesed*, the loyalty God promised in the covenant you met in lesson 2. The psalmist reaches for the covenant when he can't see the rescue.

One caution, from the same textbook. The six elements are "our discovery, not a rigid form a psalmist felt compelled to fit the musical poem into".[3] Psalm 88 is a lament that never reaches praise; it ends on the word "darkness". The shape is a strong pattern with real exceptions, and the Psalter kept the exceptions.

:::checkpoint Read [Psalm 42 and 43](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+42-43&version=NET) together, fourteen verses. Most scholars treat them as one psalm, because Psalm 43 has no superscription and because of what you're about to notice. What type is it, where is the turn, and what does the refrain do?
An individual lament. The refrain is the turn, and it comes **three** times, identically, at 42:5, 42:11 and 43:5: "Why are you depressed, O my soul? Why are you upset? Wait for God! For I will again give thanks to my God for his saving intervention" (NET).[1] That third occurrence is the main evidence that 42 and 43 are one poem split by a later editor.

Each time, the psalmist talks to himself and turns from complaint toward trust, and then the complaint comes back. The refrain shows you that the turn in a lament isn't a switch that stays flipped. It's a thing the praying person does, and does again. If you stopped at 42:11, as most English Bibles invite you to, you'd have found two turns and missed that the psalm resolves on the third.
:::

## Psalm 137, and the verse people tear out

One psalm shocks nearly everyone who finds it, and it needs everything you now have: the type, the shape, and the covenant behind it.

> By the rivers of Babylon  
> we sit down and weep  
> when we remember Zion.  
>
> Psalm 137:1 (NET)[1]

:::predict Before you read on, put this psalm on lesson 2's timeline. Which act of the story, which empire, roughly which century, and what has just happened to the temple?
Act three, late. The empire is Babylon, the century is the sixth BC, and Jerusalem has fallen: the city taken in 586, the temple burned, the survivors deported. Hayes tells her students the psalm is "clearly from the perspective of the exile".[9] What the psalm adds to the history is the detail the chronicles leave out, that the captors asked their prisoners for a song. It's a corporate lament, and Fee and Stuart list it as one.[3]
:::

Then, after seven verses, six of grief and one already asking God to remember what Edom did, the last two:

> O daughter Babylon, soon to be devastated,  
> how blessed will be the one who repays you  
> for what you dished out to us.  
> How blessed will be the one who grabs your babies  
> and smashes them on a rock.  
>
> Psalm 137:8 to 9 (NET)[1]

**Read badly.** As an endorsement: the Bible blesses killing infants. Or, the mirror reading, which is just as bad: tear the page out, because a Christian can't say this.

**Read well.** Notice, first, what the psalm is. It's a prayer, spoken to God, by people who have watched this done to their own children. Verse 8 says so: "for what you dished out to us". The image in verse 9 isn't the psalmist's invention; it's what a besieging army did, which the prophets describe elsewhere in the same words (2 Kings 8:12; Hosea 13:16; Nahum 3:10), and the psalmist asks that Babylon receive its own measure. Fee and Stuart note that the language is "hyperbolic language of the same extreme sort found in the covenant curses themselves", and that the psalmist "calls for judgment according to the covenant curses" of Deuteronomy, the sanctions you met in lesson 4.[3]

Notice, second, what the psalm doesn't do. It doesn't pick up a sword. It hands the rage to God and leaves it there. Paul asks for exactly that, and he asks for it by quoting Deuteronomy:

> Do not avenge yourselves, dear friends, but give place to God's wrath, for it is written, "Vengeance is mine, I will repay," says the Lord.  
>
> Romans 12:19 (NET)[1]

The line Paul quotes is Deuteronomy 32:35, which in the NET reads "I will get revenge and pay them back at the time their foot slips".[1] Paul's instruction is: don't take vengeance, give it to God. Our reading of the connection is that the psalm is what obeying that instruction sounds like from the bottom, when you're the one who has been wronged past bearing. That's a reading, not something the psalm says about itself. Fee and Stuart put the function this way: these psalms "guide or channel our anger to and through God verbally rather than to or at anyone else", and "it is God who is the actual hearer of these angry words".[3] They add that this doesn't contradict Jesus' command to love enemies.[3]

**And here is the objection, at its full strength, because it survives all of that.** Grant every word above. The psalm is a prayer, it's hyperbole, it invokes the covenant curses, it hands the case to God rather than taking up a sword. It still asks God to bless the man who kills babies, and this lesson is inviting you to pray it. C. S. Lewis, who loved the Psalter, called the spirit of the cursing psalms "devilish" and refused to defend it, while holding that the psalms are still Scripture and that the honest thing is to say what they are. And the Roman Catholic Church, whose clergy and religious pray the Psalter through every four weeks, reached a practical conclusion: the Liturgy of the Hours revised in 1971 omits Psalms 58, 83 and 109 altogether and cuts the imprecatory verses from others, including these two, on the ground that they're unsuitable for public prayer in the vernacular.

So Christians who pray these psalms daily have landed in different places, and you should know that before you decide. This course keeps them and prays them, for the reason given above. I won't pretend that makes verse 9 comfortable. It doesn't, and it isn't meant to. What the genre does is tell you what kind of speech this is: a prayer, from the bottom, by people who weren't allowed to sing.

## Wisdom: a proverb is not a promise

Now to the other half of the lesson. Three books in a Protestant Old Testament are usually classed as wisdom: Proverbs, Job and Ecclesiastes, with Song of Songs and the wisdom psalms sometimes added.[10] Catholic and Orthodox Bibles carry two more, Sirach and the Wisdom of Solomon, and Sirach in particular is the closest ancient relative Proverbs has; lesson 8 gives the case for each canon.

Wisdom, in the Bible's sense, is the skill of making good choices in a world God has ordered. Proverbs is its plainest form: short sayings that state how life usually goes. The Hebrew word for these sayings, *mashal*, covers "figures of speech", "parables", and "specially contrived sayings", and Fee and Stuart draw the consequence: "The briefer a statement is, the less likely it is to be totally precise and universally applicable."[10]

That's how a proverb compresses, and it's worth holding next to how a poem does. A poem compresses by leaving the connections to the reader. A proverb compresses by stating a pattern in the fewest possible words and leaving the exceptions to the reader. "Look before you leap" doesn't tell you how long to look or what to look for, and it isn't about jumping.[10] Neither is "a stitch in time saves nine" about sewing. Everyone handles English proverbs correctly, by instinct. The trouble starts when the same kind of sentence appears in the Bible and the instinct switches off, because "if it's in the Bible it must be a promise".

Fee and Stuart give four guidelines for reading Proverbs. The first is the one to memorise: "Proverbs Are Not Legal Guarantees from God."[10] The blessings in the book "are likely to follow" wise choices, "but nowhere does Proverbs teach automatic success". The other three, worth knowing you have: proverbs are worded to be memorable rather than technically precise; they use highly specific language, sometimes figurative, to make a general point; and they're not exhaustive, so no single proverb should be pressed as though it were the whole of what the Bible says about its subject.

:::checkpoint [Proverbs 15:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Proverbs+15&version=NET) (NET): "A gentle response turns away anger, but a harsh word stirs up wrath."[1] You give a gentle response and the other person gets angrier. Has the proverb failed, and what should you do with it now?
No. It has done what a proverb does: told you the usual result, in the fewest words, and left the unusual case to you. A gentle response usually defuses; it doesn't always, and the book knows that, or it wouldn't also tell you when to answer a fool and when not to. Keep the proverb, because it's still the way to bet, and keep your judgement, because the proverb never asked you to switch it off.
:::

## Proverbs 22:6, the verse that has hurt the most parents

> Train a child in the way that he should go,  
> and when he is old he will not turn from it.  
>
> Proverbs 22:6 (NET)[1]

**Read badly.** As a guarantee to parents. Raise your child rightly and they'll stay in the faith. The reading feels like taking God at his word, and it produces two kinds of damage. Parents whose grown children have left the faith conclude they failed, or that God did. And parents whose children stayed conclude it was their doing.

**Read well.** As a proverb: a compressed statement of how things usually go. A child shaped early in a good direction usually keeps it, and that's true, and it's a good reason to do the shaping. It isn't a mechanism that overrides the grown child's own choices, and the book that contains it knows that, because the same book contains Proverbs 26:4 and 5, which you read at the top of this lesson. A collection that puts two opposite instructions side by side is telling you, in its own form, that its sayings are situational.

Fee and Stuart make the same point from Proverbs 16:3, which they quote in the NIV as "Commit to the LORD whatever you do, and your plans will succeed"; the NET has "Commit your works to the Lord, and your plans will be established".[1][10] They call it "the sort of proverb that is most often misinterpreted". Some readers dedicate a selfish or foolish scheme to God and expect it to work. Others commit a good plan and watch it fail. Both, they say, "have failed to see that the proverb is not a categorical, always applicable, ironclad promise, but a more general truth".[10]

**The wrinkle.** Someone will say that this is a convenient way to explain away the verses that don't come true. It's worth asking whether anything in the Bible itself treats proverbs this way, or whether this is a modern reader's escape hatch.

There is, and it's the sharpest thing in the wisdom books. Job's three friends spend most of the book quoting wisdom at him. Suffering follows sin; the wicked don't prosper; put your house in order and God will restore you. Much of what they say could be lifted straight out of Proverbs. At the end, God speaks:

> After the Lord had spoken these things to Job, he said to Eliphaz the Temanite, "My anger is stirred up against you and your two friends, because you have not spoken about me what is right, as my servant Job has."  
>
> Job 42:7 (NET)[1]

The friends took true generalisations and applied them as guarantees to a case they didn't fit, and God says they haven't spoken rightly of him. Fee and Stuart read the verse the same way, and warn: "Do not engage in the kind of wisdom Job's friends did."[10] So the rule that a proverb is not a promise isn't a modern escape hatch. That's how we read Job 42:7, and the textbook reads it the same way. (A fair caution: 42:7 says the friends have not spoken rightly *about God*, and readers differ on whether their fault was mainly their theology or mainly their misuse of a form. The two are hard to separate, because the form they misused was a form about how God runs the world.)

:::checkpoint Read [Proverbs 3:9 to 10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Proverbs+3&version=NET) (NET): "Honor the Lord from your wealth and from the firstfruits of all your crops; then your barns will be filled completely, and your vats will overflow with new wine."[1] Now think of someone you know who has done exactly that and is not rich. Is the proverb false?
No, and saying why is the skill. The proverb states a pattern: honest generosity is, as a rule, the way a life and a community go well, and Proverbs states the rule in the most concrete terms it can find, which for its first readers were barns and vats. It's false only if you read it as a formula that must hold for every individual case, and the Bible itself refuses that reading in Job, in Ecclesiastes, in Psalm 73, where a psalmist watches the wicked prosper and nearly loses his faith over it, and at the cross. Both halves are in the same canon so that you can't keep one without the other. If you reached for a way to explain your friend's case away, notice the reflex; it's the friends' reflex in Job.
:::

## Job and Ecclesiastes, and what they do to Proverbs

Why are Job and Ecclesiastes in the Bible at all? The reading this course takes, and it's Longman's, is that they're the canon's own defence against turning wisdom into a slot machine. They do it in two different ways.

**Job** is a dialogue with a frame. A prose prologue (chapters 1 and 2) tells you, before anyone speaks, that Job's suffering isn't punishment, and a prose epilogue (42:7 to 17) vindicates him against his friends.[10] Between them sits a long argument in poetry, chapters 3 to 41, in which the friends argue that the world is fair and Job argues that it isn't. The reader is given information the speakers lack, which means you can't read any speech in Job as "what the Bible teaches" without asking who's speaking and what God says about them at the end. Job's own conclusion is double: on whether life is fair, Job prevailed, "it is not"; on why him, God prevailed, and Job never gets an answer, only God.[10]

:::checkpoint Job 15:20 (NET) reads: "All his days the wicked man suffers torment, throughout the number of the years that are stored up for the tyrant."[1] Is that the Bible's teaching about the wicked? What do you need to know before you answer, and what happens when you apply the same test to Job 32 to 37?
You need to know who's speaking. Job 15 is Eliphaz, and God's verdict at 42:7 names Eliphaz first among those who have not spoken rightly. So the sentence is in the Bible, accurately reported, and it isn't the Bible's teaching; it's a true-sounding generalisation pressed onto a case it doesn't fit, which is this lesson's whole subject.

Now the harder half. Chapters 32 to 37 are Elihu, a fourth speaker who arrives late, and God's verdict at 42:7 names "Eliphaz the Temanite and your two friends". Elihu is neither rebuked nor vindicated. Apply the rule and it returns nothing, and readers have divided ever since over whether he's a fourth friend who deserved the same rebuke or the beginning of God's own answer. That's worth meeting now: a rule that works on most of a book and goes quiet on part of it is a real rule, not a broken one, and knowing where it goes quiet is part of using it.
:::

**Ecclesiastes** is a monologue with a frame. The Teacher, *Qohelet* in Hebrew, speaks in the first person from 1:12 to 12:7. Around him sit a prologue (1:1 to 11) and an epilogue (12:8 to 14) that talk about him in the third person.[10] The book opens by calling him "the son of David, king in Jerusalem" (1:1) and has him say "I have been king over Israel in Jerusalem" (1:12),[1] which is a Solomonic voice; most scholars read that as a persona adopted by a later writer, partly because the book's Hebrew carries Persian loanwords, and date it to the Persian or Hellenistic period. The traditional attribution to Solomon himself has its defenders. Lesson 10 is where the course takes up questions of authorship properly.

His key word is *hebel*. The NET gives it as "futile", the King James as "vanity", the NIV as "meaningless". Literally it means vapour, or breath. Fee and Stuart count it thirty-seven times in this one book out of seventy-three in the whole Old Testament, and some commentaries count thirty-eight.[10]

> "Futile! Futile!" laments the Teacher.  
> "Absolutely futile! Everything is futile!"  
>
> Ecclesiastes 1:2 (NET)[1]

What the Teacher means by it is a real scholarly disagreement, and I want you to see one, because it runs through the middle of the textbook this course uses. Fee and Stuart report that their two recommended commentators, Tremper Longman and Iain Provan, "happen to be close friends" and read the book in opposite ways. Longman takes most of it as "cynical wisdom" set up as a foil, an outlook to be avoided. Provan takes it positively, as a guide to enjoying life under God in a world where everyone dies. And the two authors add that they themselves are split, one with each commentator.[10]

That's what honest reading of a hard book looks like. Notice what the disagreement is actually about: not whether the book has a frame, which nobody disputes, but what the frame *does* with the voice inside it. On Longman's reading the epilogue's closing "Fear God and keep his commandments" (12:13, NET) is the perspective that corrects the Teacher.[1] On Provan's, and on Michael Fox's, the frame narrator is presenting the Teacher approvingly, and the closing words gather up his argument rather than fencing it. **We read the frame as governing, and you should know we hold that rather than found it.** What both readings do agree on is narrower than it's often made to sound: that the book is framed, so whose voice you're reading is the first question at any verse in it.

A word for the Jewish reader, whose book this also is. Kohelet, as the tradition calls it, is one of the five Megillot, and it's read publicly in Ashkenazi synagogues on the Shabbat of Sukkot's intermediate days, and on Shemini Atzeret, the festival that follows, in years when no Shabbat falls there and in several other rites.[11] Sukkot is the festival of rejoicing. Here is its first line in the translation Jewish readers of English have used longest ([Ecclesiastes 1:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Ecclesiastes.1.2), JPS 1917):[7]

> Vanity of vanities, saith Koheleth; vanity of vanities, all is vanity.  

A community that reads that at its feast of joy has been living with the book's frame for a very long time. It's worth adding that the rabbis argued about whether Kohelet belonged in the canon at all: the Mishnah records the dispute over whether it "defiles the hands", which is their idiom for being Scripture, and the decision was that it does.[12] So "it's in the Bible on purpose" is true, and it's a conclusion someone reached, not an obvious fact.

## The one thing that doesn't survive translation

Almost everything in this lesson crosses languages, and that's a real feature of Hebrew poetry: thought-rhyme travels where sound-rhyme doesn't, which is why an English reader can see parallelism without knowing a word of Hebrew.

Not all of it crosses. Several psalms are alphabetic acrostics, where each line or stanza begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet: Psalms 25, 34, 37, 111, 112, 119 and 145, and all of Lamentations. Psalm 119 is twenty-two stanzas of eight verses, one stanza per letter, and its shape is an argument in itself, that there's a way to say everything about God's law from A to Z. In English you see none of it. Lesson 1 showed you the sharpest case: Psalms 9 and 10 are one broken acrostic, which is part of why the Greek Bible counts them as a single psalm and your Bible doesn't.

So the honest version is: parallelism survives translation, the acrostic doesn't, and knowing which is which tells you what to look for in a footnote.

## What people get wrong

**"A proverb is a promise."** The most common misreading of any Old Testament genre, and the one with the most pastoral damage. A proverb is a generalisation, by design; Proverbs 26:4 and 5 prove it, and Job 42:7 is God's verdict on the people who forgot.

**"The Psalms teach doctrine directly."** They're full of doctrine, but they teach it through prayer, which is different. Read a psalm for what it says and miss its shape, and you'll find "contradictions" that are movements.

**"Job's friends are quoting Scripture, so their speeches are what the Bible teaches."** They're in the Bible and they're accurately reported, which isn't the same thing. Ask who's speaking, every time. It's the single most useful habit this lesson can give you for the wisdom books, and it's why Job 15:20 sounds so much like a proverb.

**"Hebrew poetry rhymes in the original."** Not in the sense of matching sounds at line ends. It rhymes thoughts, which is why so much of it survives translation. The acrostics don't.

**"Ecclesiastes is a cynic who got in by mistake."** The book has a frame. What the frame does with the Teacher's voice is argued by scholars who are friends; that the book belongs is a decision the tradition reached and recorded, not an accident.

**"David wrote the Psalms and they're his diary."** Some psalms carry "of David" in their superscription, and the Hebrew phrase can mean by him, for him, or belonging to a collection associated with him. Others name Asaph, the sons of Korah, Heman the Ezrahite, Moses, or nobody. A psalm's power doesn't rest on knowing who held the pen.

**"The violent psalms should be skipped."** This one isn't simply a mistake, and the section on Psalm 137 above says why: the Catholic Liturgy of the Hours omits several of them on pastoral grounds. Our position is that the imprecations are prayers that hand vengeance to God instead of taking it, and that removing them removes the Psalter's honesty about rage and the model for what to do with it. Know that serious Christians differ.

**"Synonymous lines just repeat, so you can skim the second one."** The second line is where the emphasis lands. Kugel's "what's more" is the corrective; read pairs of lines for what B adds to A.

:::checkpoint Model answers for tasks 1 and 2. Read these after you've written yours.
**Task 1.** Address: "My God, my God" (22:1). Complaint: verses 1 to 2 and 6 to 8 and 12 to 18, which is by far the longest complaint in any psalm you've read here. Trust: verses 3 to 5 and 9 to 10, which interrupt the complaint twice, exactly as Psalm 13's trust does. Petition: verses 11 and 19 to 21. Then the turn at verse 22, "I will declare your name to my countrymen", and from there to the end the psalm is praise, widening to the whole earth and to generations not yet born. What changes at 22 is the audience: the psalmist stops speaking to God about his enemies and starts speaking to the congregation about God. What has not changed is anything in his circumstances; the psalm never says he was rescued.

**A note you should have.** Jesus quotes 22:1 from the cross, and Christians have read the psalm in the light of that ever since; a Jewish reader reads it as the psalmist's own lament and Israel's, complete without a second fulfilment, and the psalm makes full sense that way. History establishes that the psalm is an individual lament with the shape above and that Jesus quoted its first line. We hold by faith that he was praying it as his own, and that the psalm reaches further than its first singer knew. That's the same three-way sort you did on Isaiah 7:14 in lesson 4.

**Task 2.** They're both wisdom because both are about how to live well in a world God has ordered, and they differ on how tidy that world is. Proverbs 3 states the **pattern**: trust, honour God with what you have, and life generally goes well. Ecclesiastes 1 sets a **frame** around the whole question by observing that everything repeats, nobody is remembered, and death comes for the wise and the foolish alike. Neither cancels the other; Proverbs tells you the way to bet and Ecclesiastes tells you what betting can and can't secure. For a friend who has just lost a job, most people find Ecclesiastes the better companion, because it doesn't imply the loss was earned, and Proverbs 3 read at the wrong moment sounds exactly like Job's friends. There isn't one right answer here, and if you argued the other way with a reason, that's the task done.
:::

:::checkpoint Model answers for tasks 3 and 4.
**Task 3.** Verse 1 is the tricolon: doesn't follow the advice of the wicked, doesn't stand in the pathway with sinners, doesn't sit in the assembly of scoffers. Line B adds a second posture and worse company; line C adds a third posture and worse again, and the three verbs move from walking to standing to sitting, which is a progression from passing by to settling in. Verse 2: "finds pleasure in obeying the Lord's commands", and what's more, "he meditates on his commands day and night", which turns an attitude into a practice and adds time. Verse 3: "like a tree planted by flowing streams", and what's more, "it yields its fruit at the proper time, and its leaves never fall off", which adds productivity and endurance to the bare picture of being planted.

**Task 4.** Judges 9 is a genre question first: it's a fable inside a narrative, and trees don't hold elections, so the reader who asks "did this happen" has asked the wrong thing. Amos 5:24 is a second-line question, and you did it in the quiz. Proverbs 13:24 is a rule-or-generalisation question, and it's the one where getting the answer wrong does real harm. Psalm 30:5 is also a second-line question, and the pair is worth doing: "his anger lasts only a brief moment, but his favor restores one's life", so B adds not just contrast but duration and consequence. If you said "what kind of writing is this" for all four, you're not wrong, you're early: that's lesson 3's question and it always comes first. The point of this task is that once it's settled, a second question follows, and it isn't the same one every time.
:::

## Connections

Lesson 3 gave you the reflex: what kind of writing is this, and what are its rules? This lesson applied it to compressed writing, where the rules are that the second line adds, the picture argues, the prayer moves, and the saying generalises. Lesson 2's covenant came back twice, as the *hesed* Psalm 13 stands on and the curses Psalm 137 invokes, and lesson 4's exile is the setting of the hardest psalm. Lesson 4's three-way sort came back in the Psalm 22 model answer.

Lesson 6 uses the same reflex on the parables, which compress the way a proverb does and spring the way a psalm turns. Lesson 7 will need imagery again for Revelation, where readers who decode pictures into events make the mistake this lesson warned about. Lesson 8 takes up why Catholic and Orthodox Bibles have wisdom books this one doesn't. Lesson 10 handles authorship, including the Solomon question. And in lesson 11, Psalm 1:1 comes back as a worked example of how translators handle a poem's opening.

## Go deeper

- **Fee and Stuart, *How to Read the Bible for All Its Worth*, 4th ed., chapters 11 and 12.** The two chapters this lesson is built from: the seven types with their psalm numbers, the six elements of a lament worked through Psalm 3, the five elements of a thanksgiving worked through Psalm 138, and the four guidelines for Proverbs with examples.
