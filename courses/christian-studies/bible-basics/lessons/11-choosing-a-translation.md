> "And I also have given you cleanness of teeth in all your cities, and want of bread in all your places: yet have ye not returned unto me, saith the LORD." (Amos 4:6, KJV)
>
> "I gave you empty stomachs in every city and lack of bread in every town, yet you have not returned to me," declares the LORD. (Amos 4:6, NIV)[2]

Same Hebrew. Two translations, both made by scholars who could read it. One says "cleanness of teeth", the other says "empty stomachs". Most people, meeting this for the first time, ask which one is right, and that question is the whole subject of this lesson, because it is the wrong question and the right one is sitting just behind it.

Both are right. The Hebrew says, word for word, cleanness of teeth: a grim little picture of teeth with nothing on them because there is nothing to chew. The King James keeps the picture and leaves you to work out what it means. The NIV works it out for you and hands you the meaning. What you have to decide is not which translation is accurate but which kind of accuracy you want for the reading you are about to do. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say, for any English Bible you pick up, what text it was translated from, where it sits on the spectrum of translation, what it trades away to sit there, and why the King James Version differs from the rest. 

## The scale of the thing

One number first, because most people have it badly wrong.

:::predict Roughly how many of the world's languages have a complete Bible? There are about 7,400 living languages. Guess, then read on.
As of September 2026, 807 languages have a full Bible. Another 1,844 have a New Testament and 1,519 have some portion. Most people guess either far too low (a few dozen) or far too high (most of them).[3]
:::

[Wycliffe Bible Translators' statistics](https://www.wycliffe.org.uk/about/our-impact/) for 1 September 2026, drawn from the ProgressBible database, count 807 languages with a full Bible, 1,844 with a New Testament, and 1,519 with portions, out of 7,394 living languages.[3] The same page counts 1,688 languages with no Scripture at all and no translation work under way.[3]

Hold that against the fact that English has dozens of complete translations and a new major one roughly every decade. Every one of those English versions was made by people doing the same job the translators of those 807 languages do: taking a Hebrew or Greek sentence and deciding, word by word, what an English sentence has to keep and what it can let go. That job is what you're about to learn to read.

## Where the words come from

A translation starts from a printed edition of the Hebrew and Greek, and lesson 9 gave you the manuscripts those editions are built from. Three editions matter for your shelf.

That choice is a decision, not a given, and it is worth naming before the chart. The Orthodox churches hold that the church's Old Testament is the Greek Septuagint, the text the apostles quoted and the one the Greek-speaking church has read for nineteen centuries. Where the Hebrew and the Greek differ, and they differ often in the Psalms and in Jeremiah, that decision changes the text before any question of method arises.

For the Old Testament, almost every modern Protestant and Catholic translation works from the *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* (BHS), published by the German Bible Society and completed in 1977, whose text is the Leningrad Codex of 1008 or 1009, the oldest complete Hebrew Bible.[4] Its successor, the *Biblia Hebraica Quinta*, has been appearing in parts since 2004 on the same base.[4] Translators consult the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Septuagint where the Leningrad text is hard, and note it in the margin when they do.

For the New Testament, the standard edition is the Nestle-Aland *Novum Testamentum Graece*, now in its 28th edition (2012), which shares its text with the United Bible Societies' fifth edition (2014).[5] This is the "critical" or "eclectic" text: at every point where manuscripts differ, the editors weigh the witnesses by age, spread, and the kinds of error scribes make, and print what they judge the earliest recoverable reading. Nearly every English translation made since the Revised Version of 1881 follows an edition of this kind.[5][7]

The King James Version does not, and here is why. In 1516 Erasmus of Rotterdam published the first printed Greek New Testament to reach the public, in Basel, at speed. His own verdict, in a letter written within weeks of publication, was that the book "was thrown together rather than edited", and in the same breath he claimed it still beat everything that had come before.[33] The Complutensian Polyglot had been printed two years earlier and was held back, which is why the distinction matters to anyone who has heard the claim disputed. William Combs's study of the edition counts seven Greek manuscripts available to him, most of them from the twelfth century, and only one of Revelation, which was missing its final verses; Erasmus filled the gap by translating the Latin Vulgate back into Greek.[6][7] His text was revised by others through the sixteenth century, and in 1633 the Elzevir edition printed at Leiden carried a sentence, written by Daniel Heinsius rather than by the printers themselves, calling it the text "now received by all". From that Latin phrase, *textum receptum*, comes the name Textus Receptus, the Received Text.[6] The King James translators of 1611 worked from the printed Greek editions of that family, because those were the editions that existed.[7]

So the King James differs from a modern Bible wherever the Received Text differs from the critical text. The three famous passages of lesson 9 are the clearest cases. The Received Text has the longer ending of Mark, the woman caught in adultery, and the heavenly witnesses of 1 John 5:7, without brackets, because the manuscripts Erasmus had contained them (or, in the case of 1 John 5:7, because his third edition of 1522 added the sentence).[7] The New King James Version of 1982 kept the Received Text on purpose. Its [preface](http://www.bible-researcher.com/nkjv.html) says the King James New Testament "was based on the traditional text of the Greek-speaking churches, first published in 1516, and later called the Textus Receptus or Received Text". It marks every difference in a footnote, labelled NU-Text for the Nestle-Aland and UBS reading and M-Text for the Majority Text. Note that the M-Text is a third text and not the one the NKJV translates: the Majority Text editions of Hodges and Farstad and of Robinson and Pierpont are reconstructions from the weight of surviving manuscripts, and they part from the Received Text in a large number of places, because the Received Text rests on a handful of manuscripts rather than on the Byzantine tradition counted as a whole.[8] If you own an NKJV, those footnotes are a running map of every place the two textual traditions part.

Say this plainly so the next section makes sense. *History establishes* how each printed text was made: Erasmus's seven late manuscripts, the Elzevir preface, the thousands of manuscripts and papyri behind Nestle-Aland. *Scholars argue about* which form of the text stands closest to what the apostles wrote; the great majority hold the critical text does, and a minority argues for the priority of the Byzantine text on text-critical grounds, and a smaller group holds the Received Text specifically, on grounds that include a doctrine of preservation.[23] *Christians on both sides hold by faith* that God has preserved his word. Where they differ is where to look for it, and we'll come back to that, because it is the most common fight about translations you'll ever be in.

## The spectrum

Now the second thing that separates translations, and the one that matters more for daily reading. Two translations can start from the identical Greek edition and still read as differently as the two versions of Amos above. The difference is a decision about what to keep.

Fee and Stuart, whose textbook this course has followed since lesson 3, describe three approaches.[1] A **formal equivalence** translation tries to keep the form of the original: its words, its word order where English allows, its idioms and pictures. A **functional equivalence** translation (older books say dynamic equivalence) tries to keep the meaning, and will change the form to do it: "cleanness of teeth" becomes "empty stomachs". A **free** translation restates the idea in the translator's own words with little concern for the original's form at all. These are often called paraphrases, and the word repays care: strictly a paraphrase reworks an existing version in the same language, which is what Kenneth Taylor's *Living Bible* did to the American Standard Version, whereas *The Message* was made from the Hebrew and Greek by a man who taught Greek. Peterson resisted the label for that reason. Calling every free translation a paraphrase is common, and it concedes a point its makers do not concede.

Fee and Stuart put it in the middle. Most people who own one guess "literal", because it reads so smoothly that they assume nothing has been reworked. The smoothness is the reworking.
:::

Here is their placement, with the dates I've verified from the publishers, and with the CSB (which replaced the HCSB in 2017) placed where its own publisher describes it.[1][13][14]

That is one taxonomy, and it belongs to a pair of outside observers. The publishers name their own methods, and the names disagree in a way worth noticing. The ESV calls itself "essentially literal".[9] The NASB says it "adheres to the principles of a formal equivalence translation".[9] The NKJV claims "complete equivalence", and the CSB "optimal equivalence, a translation philosophy that balances linguistic precision to the original languages and readability in contemporary English".[9] The NRSVue's motto is "as literal as possible, as free as necessary".[9] Read those together and you will see that the last three are not claiming a place on the spectrum at all; they are claiming the spectrum is a false choice, and that a translator should render literally where that is clear and freely where it is not. Whether anyone can do that consistently is the question the rest of this lesson is about.

Look at the top band of the chart. The King James and the NASB are both formal, and they translate different Greek texts. The ESV and the NKJV are both formal, and one is a revision that took the 1971 RSV text as its starting point while the other is a revision of the King James.[10][8] Formal and functional describe *how* a version translates; Received Text and critical text describe *what* it translates. Those are two separate axes, and a lot of arguments about translations are two people each talking about a different one.

Every date on the chart is a first edition or a major revision, and three of them are worth a sentence each. The NIV has been revised twice, in 1984 and 2011, and the 2011 revision is the one the gender argument below is about.[11] The NLT began as a revision of Kenneth Taylor's *Living Bible*, which was a paraphrase of an English version rather than a translation from Hebrew and Greek, and the NLT's whole point was to become the second thing.[15] And the NASB, revised in 1977, 1995 and 2020, shows that a formal translation is not a fixed object either.[14]

## The mechanism: a chain of decisions

Here is why no translation can sit at both ends at once.

Translation is a chain of decisions, one per word, phrase, and sentence. At every link the translator holds the source language's *form* in one hand and the target language's *sense* in the other, and at a surprising number of links the two pull apart. "Cleanness of teeth" is a form that English readers do not automatically hear as a famine. Keep the form and you lose some sense; give the sense and you lose the form. You can protect a link, but you cannot protect both hands at every link, because English and Hebrew are not the same shape.

That has three consequences you can use.

First, every translation interprets. A formal translation makes its interpretive decisions quietly, by choosing which English word to line up with which Hebrew one; a functional translation makes them out loud. Neither is interpretation-free, so "I just want a translation that doesn't interpret" is asking for something that cannot exist.

Second, the question "which translation is most accurate?" is malformed until you finish it. Accurate to what: the words, or the meaning? For what: reading a chapter of Isaiah on the train, or tracing a Hebrew word through the Psalms? Finish the question that way and you usually have your answer.

Third, the trade-offs are honest and predictable. A formal translation preserves the form and so preserves wordplay, repetition, ambiguity, and the pictures the writer chose; it pays in idioms that don't land and sentences that don't sound like English. A functional translation preserves the sense, so a first-time reader can keep moving; it pays by hiding the form, so the reader can't see the repeated word, or the ambiguity the author may have meant, or the picture the NIV quietly resolved. A free translation gives freshness, and pays with the most translator between you and the text.

The best place to watch the chain being forged is the NET Bible, free at netbible.org, whose translators wrote a note at almost every link explaining what they did and why: 60,932 notes in the first edition, and 58,504 in the second edition according to its copyright page.[17] At Amos 4:6 the NET reads "I gave you no food to eat in all your cities" and the note says: "Heb 'But I gave to you cleanness of teeth in all your cities, and lack of food in all your places.' The phrase 'cleanness of teeth' is a vivid way of picturing the famine Israel experienced."[18] That note is a translator showing you both hands.

:::checkpoint Isaiah 7:14. The ESV reads "Behold, the virgin shall conceive"; the NRSVue reads "Look, the young woman is with child."[19] Is that a translation decision, an interpretation decision, or both, and why can't a translator avoid making it?
Both, and they can't be separated. The Hebrew word *almah* refers to a young woman of marriageable age; the NET's note observes that it does not in itself mean "virgin", and that the Greek Septuagint rendered it with *parthenos*, which does, and that Matthew quotes the Greek.[18] English has no single word that means "young woman, and possibly a virgin, leave it open". So whichever word the translator picks closes a question the Hebrew left open, in one direction or the other. Leaving it blank is not an option. That is what "every translation interprets" means at one link of the chain, and lesson 4 gave you the Jewish and Christian readings that hang on it.
:::

## Worked example 1: one verse, six ways

Take Amos 4:6 across the spectrum, keeping only the phrase that matters.[2]

- KJV: "cleanness of teeth in all your cities"
- ESV: "cleanness of teeth in all your cities"
- NIV: "empty stomachs in every city"
- CSB: "absolutely nothing to eat in all your cities"
- NET: "But surely I gave you no food to eat in all your cities"
- NLT: "I brought hunger to every city"
- The Message: "I'm the One who emptied your pantries and cleaned out your cupboards"

Read down the list and you can watch the form draining out and the sense filling in. KJV and ESV keep the picture and trust you. NIV, CSB, and the NET give you the meaning in an image of their own, and the NET puts the Hebrew idiom in a note instead.[18] NLT drops the image altogether and states the fact. The Message invents two new images, pantries and cupboards, that no Israelite farmer had, to make the sentence land on a modern reader the way Amos's landed on his.

Which would a first-time reader want? Probably the NIV or CSB: they get the famine without a footnote. Which would a careful student want? The ESV or KJV, because "cleanness of teeth" is a covenant-curse image, and a student who has read Deuteronomy 28 (lesson 4) wants to see the prophet reaching for that vocabulary. That is why lesson 4, quoting the NET, told you in a parenthesis what the Hebrew says. The functional translations have not made a mistake. They have made a choice, and the choice hides something the student wanted to see.

:::predict Now do one yourself. Romans 12:20 ends, in the NASB, "for in so doing you will heap burning coals on his head." The NIV keeps "burning coals". The NLT has "burning coals of shame on their heads". The Message has "Your generosity will surprise him with goodness."[19] For each of the three that changed something, say what it added or removed, and what a reader gains and loses. Write it down before you read mine.
The NIV kept the image and left the puzzle (what do coals on a head mean?) to you and your commentary. The NLT kept the image and added one word, "shame", which is one scholarly reading of the idiom, so the reader gains a meaning and loses the knowledge that it was one reading among several. The Message removed the coals entirely and replaced them with the interpretation it prefers; the reader gains a sentence that makes immediate sense and loses any sign that Paul was quoting Proverbs 25 with a strange image in it. Notice that the NLT is the interesting case: a functional translation that resolved an ambiguity the formal ones preserved. 
:::

## Worked example 2: the gender question, in each side's words

Now the wrinkle, and the one that makes people angry, so slow down.

Psalm 1:1 in four translations:[19]

- ESV: "Blessed is the man who walks not in the counsel of the wicked"
- NIV: "Blessed is the one who does not walk in step with the wicked"
- CSB: "How happy is the one who does not walk in the advice of the wicked"
- NRSVue: "Happy are those who do not follow the advice of the wicked"

And 1 Thessalonians 4:1, which begins in the ESV "Finally, then, brothers", with a footnote reading "Or brothers and sisters", and in the NIV "As for other matters, brothers and sisters".[19]

The Hebrew of Psalm 1 has a masculine singular noun, *ish*, "man". The Greek of 1 Thessalonians has *adelphoi*, the masculine plural of "brother", which Greek used for a group of siblings of both sexes. Nobody disputes either fact. The dispute is about what an English translation should do with them, and each publisher has said why it does what it does.

The [ESV's preface](https://www.esv.org/preface/) says that "the English word 'brothers' (translating the Greek word *adelphoi*) is retained as an important familial form of address between fellow-Jews and fellow-Christians in the first century", that "the inclusive use of the generic 'he' has also regularly been retained, because this is consistent with similar usage in the original languages "and because an essentially literal translation would be impossible without it", and that "'anyone' replaces 'any man' where there is no word corresponding to 'man' in the original languages, and 'people' rather than 'men' is regularly used where the original languages refer to both men and women".[10] So the ESV's line is: keep a singular where the original is singular and a masculine where the original is masculine, so the reader can see the referent, and put the inclusive rendering in the margin where it applies.

The [NIV's preface](https://www.blueletterbible.org/bibles/preface-to-the-new-international-version.cfm), for the 2011 revision, says the translators put questions about current English usage to computational linguists at Collins Dictionaries and its database of "over 4.4 billion words", and that on that basis the revision generally avoids the generic "he" and uses the singular "they" for a generic person, describing that usage as "standard English, spoken and written, all over the world". The Committee's decision to render *adelphoi* "brothers and sisters" where a mixed group is addressed is explained not in the preface but in its separate translators' notes, which this course has seen only in secondary summaries and so does not quote.[11] So the NIV's line is: the Greek word meant "brothers and sisters" to its first hearers, and an English reader in 2011 who reads "brothers" hears something narrower than Paul said.

The NRSVue's preface says its editors continue "the effort to eliminate masculine-oriented language when it can be done without altering passages that reflect the historical situation of ancient patriarchal culture", mostly by rephrasing or using plurals, while keeping "he" in formal legal texts where changing it would obscure the original.[12] The CSB, without a preface statement I could quote, renders 1 Thessalonians 4:1 "brothers and sisters" and Psalm 1:1 "the one".[19] The NASB's 2020 revision, a formal translation, also has "brothers and sisters" at 1 Thessalonians 4:1.[19]

Now the argument itself, because a preface is a summary and this dispute has a literature. The fullest statement of the case against inclusive renderings is Vern Poythress and Wayne Grudem's, and their claim is not the one usually attributed to them. They explicitly deny arguing that form should be matched for its own sake: "we have never argued for, nor did the Colorado Springs Guidelines advocate, a general principle that 'formal equivalents' are always a more accurate method of translating", and they grant that a masculine grammatical form does not by itself carry male meaning.[38] Their claim is about a distinction. Some generic statements in Hebrew and Greek are what they call pure generics, and some are representative generics, where a single male stands as the sample for everyone. Translate both as pure generics and something is lost: "'Blessed is the man…' becomes 'blessed are those….' 'I will come in to him' becomes 'I will come in to them.'"[38]

Their sharpest case is Revelation 3:20. On the older rendering, Christ promises to come in and eat with the individual who opens the door; on a plural, they argue, the sentence can be read as a promise to a group, and "the assurance of individual fellowship with Jesus is no longer there".[38] At Psalm 1 they add a second loss: the Hebrew sets one man against plural wicked, sinners and mockers, so "the contrast between the single man and the plurality of sinners simply drops out when we convert the singulars to plurals".[38] And where *huios* becomes "child" in Romans 8 and Galatians 4, they argue the link between our sonship and Christ's Sonship recedes.[39] Grudem counted the changes he objected to in the 2005 TNIV and published the tally: "he" made plural 271 times, changed to "you" 90 times, dropped 48 times, "father" or "fathers" removed 39 times.[40]

In 1997 twelve people met at Colorado Springs and drew up guidelines to write those constraints down: generic "he" to be retained, singulars not to be made plural, "man" to be used for the human race, "brother" not to become "brother or sister" though plural *adelphoi* could be "brothers and sisters".[41] The signatories included the presidents of the International Bible Society and of Zondervan, which is why the meeting mattered commercially as well as academically; the guidelines were revised that September, ten of the twelve signed the revision, and the International Bible Society later withdrew its endorsement.[41]

The reply from the other side is not that the losses are imaginary but that the method is wrong. Mark Strauss argues that "good translations will always translate first and foremost according to sense rather than form", that a Greek lexeme and an English one have different ranges, and that an inclusive rendering is right when it "brings out more clearly and precisely the inclusive sense intended by the author".[42] He concedes what a fair opponent should: "there is nothing inherently wrong, evil or inappropriate in the use of masculine generic terms", and the real question is whether English masculine generics still convey the inclusive sense to today's readers.[42]

Then something unusual happened, which is why this section is a good place to watch an argument work. The committee behind the 2011 NIV published its reasoning, conceded two of the three main points, and kept the third. Its notes say plainly that "using plurals instead of singulars to deal with generic forms was avoided" and that second-person forms were avoided too; what it kept was the singular "they", on the evidence of the Collins corpus, where plural forms outnumbered generic "he" by roughly ten to one even in evangelical English.[43] Poythress's answer to that data is the best single sentence in the exchange: translators "do not have the same flexibility as is available to authors who produce fresh, new texts of their own making", because their job is constrained by the meaning of the document in front of them, so "percentages have little to do with either accuracy, intelligibility, or 'naturalness' of expression".[44] The Council on Biblical Manhood and Womanhood, counting again, credited the 2011 revision with 933 improvements and objected that 2,766 of the renderings it had criticised remained.[45]

Notice what that exchange is, and is not. It is not a fight about whether God is male or whether women may read the Bible. It is an argument about whether a specific semantic component, a male sample standing for everyone, exists in the Hebrew and Greek, and if it does, whether an English translation can drop it and still be accurate. Both sides publish their reasoning with verse lists, and you can read both.

So here is what you should take from this. Every one of these translations was made by scholars who love the text and can read it. The ESV's concern is real: Psalm 1 does present one figure, and plurals blur him. The NIV's concern is real: *adelphoi* did include the women in the room, and an English "brothers" can leave them out. Both sides have a form-versus-sense trade, and they have taken it in different directions; the ESV protected the form of the noun and the NIV protected the sense of the address. The word "agenda" does not help you read either of them.

This course quotes the NET, and I owe you the reason, because it is partly a reason about law rather than about Greek. A course published under an open licence, as this one is, cannot quote most modern translations at all: their free-quotation allowances exclude openly licensed works and exclude Bible reference works, and a course on how to read the Bible is both. The NET's terms have neither exclusion, so it is one of the few current translations this course is free to use, and Editorial Standards 4.7 sets out the reasoning in full. The second reason is the better one. The NET was built with its decisions showing: tens of thousands of translators' notes that tell you what the Hebrew or Greek says and why the English went the way it did, which is the habit this whole lesson is trying to hand you.[17] It sits between the two ends of the spectrum, near the CSB, so where a passage's form mattered I have said so in the text rather than relying on the rendering to show it. If you asked me which to hand a new reader, I'd say the NIV or the CSB, and I'd say why in the terms above, not in the terms of a tribe.

:::callout "Which translation is the most accurate?"
Finish the question before you answer it. Accurate to the words, or to the meaning? For reading, or for study? For a new reader, or for someone tracing a Hebrew term? A translation is accurate *to* something *for* something. Once you say what, the spectrum answers you. Until you say what, the question can't be answered and every answer to it is a slogan.
:::

:::checkpoint A friend who reads only the ESV says the NIV "changed Paul's words" at 1 Thessalonians 4:1. State the ESV's reason for "brothers" the way the ESV's own preface states it, and the NIV's reason for "brothers and sisters" the way the NIV's committee states theirs, without using the word agenda about either.
The ESV keeps "brothers" because *adelphoi* is "an important familial form of address between fellow-Jews and fellow-Christians in the first century", so the family relation is part of what the word carries; it prints "Or brothers and sisters" in the margin so a reader can see the other reading.

The NIV renders it "brothers and sisters" because the word addressed mixed congregations, and because the committee's study of current English usage found that a reader today hears "brothers" as narrower than Paul meant.

Both are form-against-sense trades taken in opposite directions, and both publishers say so in public. Notice also that the axis does not predict the answer: the NASB, a formal translation, has "brothers and sisters" here too.
:::

## A third, brief: 1 John 5:7 from the translation side

Lesson 9 showed you the Johannine Comma as a manuscript question. See it now as a translation question, because both axes are in play.

KJV, 1 John 5:7–8: "For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one. And there are three that bear witness in earth, the Spirit, and the water, and the blood: and these three agree in one." NIV: "For there are three that testify: the Spirit, the water and the blood; and the three are in agreement," with a footnote explaining that late manuscripts of the Vulgate contain the heavenly witnesses and that early Greek manuscripts do not.[9]

The King James is not mistranslating. It is translating, accurately and formally, a Greek text that had the sentence in it. The NIV is translating, functionally, a Greek text that doesn't. If you compared the two and concluded that the NIV "took a verse out", you would have confused the *what* axis with the *how* axis. The NKJV keeps the King James reading, in modernised wording and prints a footnote saying that only four or five very late Greek manuscripts contain the words.[9] That footnote is the honest position of a Received Text translation, and it's worth reading in full in your own Bible.

## The King James question, in its own terms

You will meet Christians who read only the King James, and some who hold that everyone should. Because this course promises to describe every view so its holders recognise it, here are the positions, and then the one fact both sides have to deal with.

The mainstream position among evangelical and other scholars is that the King James is a great translation of a late Greek text, and that its English has moved. D. A. Carson's *The King James Version Debate* (1979) and James White's *The King James Only Controversy* (1995, revised 2009) are the standard statements of it.[23] Mark Ward's *Authorized* (2018) adds the point most readers miss: the problem with the King James is not the words you know you don't know, like "wist" or "wot", but the ones he calls false friends, words that still exist but have changed meaning, so that you misread them without noticing.[24] The King James of 2 Corinthians 8:1 says "we do you to wit of the grace of God"; the NIV says "we want you to know about the grace that God has given".[19] "Do you to wit" is a phrase you know you don't know. The false friends are the dangerous ones because they feel like English.

The Received Text position, in its own words, is held by the [Trinitarian Bible Society](https://tbsbibles.org), which publishes the Authorised Version in English, and by the [Dean Burgon Society](https://deanburgonsociety.blog/statement-of-faith/), formed in 1978. It rests on a doctrine: that God, having inspired his word, also providentially preserved it, and preserved it in the text the church actually used through the centuries rather than in manuscripts that lay unused for over a thousand years. The Society's own study puts it this way: "Were not the Scriptures in the Biblical languages given once ... The Trinitarian Bible Society believes they were. And have not those original Scriptures been preserved? Again, the Society believes they have", and that "the Lord God of Truth has preserved His Word, as much as He originally inspired it".[20] It points out that the great manuscripts behind the critical text came into scholars' hands late (it dates a printed text of Vaticanus to 1587, the donation of Alexandrinus to 1627, and the discovery of Sinaiticus to 1859) and asks why a text "discarded by the believing church for some 1,500 years or longer" should now be preferred.[20] The Dean Burgon Society's statement of faith holds that the closest texts to the originals are the traditional Masoretic Hebrew and "the traditional Greek Text", and that "the King James Version (or Authorized Version) of the English Bible is a true, faithful, and accurate translation of these two providentially preserved Texts".[21] Note the shape of that claim: preservation is located in the Hebrew and Greek, and the King James is praised as a faithful translation of them, not as an inspired text in its own right. Edward Hills, a trained textual critic, made the scholarly case for this in *The King James Version Defended* (1956), arguing from what he called the logic of faith that a believer should expect God's preserved text to be the one the church received.[22]

Some go further and hold that the English of the King James is itself the preserved, error-free Word of God for English speakers. That view has its own writers, and it is not the Trinitarian Bible Society's or the Dean Burgon Society's stated position; if you meet it, ask its holder where they locate the preservation, because that is the question that sorts the positions.[23]

Now the fact.

:::predict In 1611 the King James translators wrote a long preface. What do you think they said about the English translations that came before theirs, and about the idea of having more than one translation?
They called the earlier English Bibles the word of God, said their own aim was "to make a good one better", and quoted Augustine that variety of translations is profitable. Most people expect the opposite. Their words are below.
:::

The preface, ["The Translators to the Reader"](http://www.bible-researcher.com/kjvpref.html), is still printed in some editions. They wrote: "we never thought from the beginning, that we should need to make a new Translation, nor yet to make of a bad one a good one ... but to make a good one better". They wrote that "the very meanest translation of the Bible in English, set forth by men of our profession ... containeth the word of God, nay, is the word of God". They defended putting alternative readings in the margin: "doth not a margin do well to admonish the Reader to seek further, and not to conclude or dogmatize upon this or that peremptorily?" And they quoted Augustine, "that variety of Translations is profitable for the finding out of the sense of the Scriptures".[25]

Each side reads that preface its own way. The mainstream reads it as the translators disowning, in advance, the claim that theirs was the final English Bible. The Received Text reader replies that the translators were speaking of translations from the same Greek text, before the critical text existed, and that their humility about English says nothing about which Greek to translate. Both readings are honest. What the preface establishes beyond argument is that the men who made the King James expected to be revised and thought variety of translations was a help, not a threat.

Sort it, as the course has taught you to. *History establishes* how the Received Text was made and what the 1611 translators wrote. *Scholars argue about* which text form is closest to the autographs, with the critical text the large majority position, Byzantine priority defended on text-critical grounds by a minority including Maurice Robinson and Wilbur Pickering, and the Received Text defended by a smaller group on grounds that include a doctrine of preservation.[23] Those last two are not the same position, and each has argued against the other. *We hold by faith*, with both sides, that God has preserved his word; this course holds, with the majority, that the preservation is seen in the whole manuscript tradition rather than in one printed edition of it, and it says so as a position. A Received Text reader should find their view stated above as they would state it. If they don't, the feedback link at the bottom of this page is for them.

## What people get wrong

**"The most literal translation is the most accurate."** Literal is a method, not a virtue. It protects form and pays in sense, and at idioms and wordplay the payment can be the whole meaning. The KJV's "cleanness of teeth" is literal and, for most readers, a famine they will not see.[2]

**"The King James is the original."** It is a translation made in 1611 from a Greek text first printed in 1516, from a handful of medieval manuscripts.[6][7] The mirror error, "the King James is unreliable", is just as wrong; it is an excellent formal translation of the text its translators had, and where that text matches the critical text, which Wallace puts at all but about 4 to 5 percent of the time even for the late Byzantine manuscripts furthest from it, it is as reliable as anything on the shelf.[27]

**"Paraphrases are for children."** Eugene Peterson, a pastor and Greek teacher, wrote The Message for bored adults, and his introduction says its aim was to convert "the tone, the rhythm, the events, the ideas, into the way we actually think and speak", not "a word-for-word conversion of Greek into English".[16] He himself said that hearing it read aloud in a church service made him "a little uneasy".[16] The right use of a free translation is the one its own author gave it: to read fast and hear a passage fresh, beside a translation you study from.

**"One translation is the true one and the rest are agendas."** Every translation was made by people who could read the languages and had to make the same decisions. When you find a difference, your first question is which axis it lies on (text or method), and your second is what was traded. "Agenda" is a third question, and you almost never need it.

**"The translators changed the text for gender reasons."** They changed the *English*, and said why, in prefaces you can read.[10][11][12] The mirror, "the traditional renderings were neutral", is equally wrong: "brothers" for *adelphoi* is a decision too, and the ESV's preface defends it as one.[10]

**"Reading levels settle it."** Retailers publish grade levels for each translation, and a major one notes that not everyone agrees on the grades or on the formulas that produce them.[26] The formulas count syllables and sentence length and know nothing about "cleanness of teeth". Treat a grade level as a rough hint about sentence length, then read a chapter of each version yourself, which tells you more in ten minutes than any number.

## Finding this out for a Bible the chart does not list

Everything above is useless if you can only apply it to eight versions. So here is where the information lives in a physical Bible, and it takes about two minutes.

The **preface** or "to the reader" names the method, usually in the publisher's own vocabulary, so look for "essentially literal", "optimal equivalence", "dynamic", "thought for thought". The **copyright page** names the sponsoring body and the year of the edition, which is what tells you whether you have the 1984 or the 2011 NIV, or the 1971 or 2020 NASB. For the source text, the preface will name it directly ("Nestle-Aland", "United Bible Societies", "Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia", "Textus Receptus", "Masoretic Text"), and if it does not, the **footnotes give it away**: an NKJV's NU-Text and M-Text notes mean a Received Text base with the alternatives flagged, while "some manuscripts add" at Mark 16:9 and John 7:53 means a critical text base. Two minutes with those three places and you can put any Bible on this lesson's two axes yourself.

## Byzantine priority, which is not the same as the Received Text

One more position, because the lesson has now mentioned it three times and a reader deserves it whole. It is also the position most often confused with the one above, and the confusion is worth clearing up because it has a history.

Byzantine priority is a text-critical argument, not a doctrine of preservation. Its case is about how manuscripts actually multiply. Zane Hodges put it in 1968: the copies nearest the autograph will normally have the most descendants, so "in a large tradition where a pronounced unity is observed between, let us say, eighty per cent of the evidence, a very strong presumption is raised that this numerical preponderance is due to direct derivation from the very oldest sources".[28] Maurice Robinson, who edited the standard Byzantine edition with William Pierpont, calls the method "a 'reasoned transmissionalism' which evaluates internal and external evidence in the light of transmissional probabilities", and states the overriding principle as "textual criticism without a history of transmission is impossible".[29] He also rejects the caricature directly: the position is "often caricatured as only interested in the weight of numbers and simple 'nose-counting' of MSS", and he answers that such a method "would offer no solution in the many places where the Byzantine Textform is divided among its mass of witnesses".[29]

Now the part that matters for this lesson. **These editors are critics of the Received Text.** Robinson's own words: "Certainly the Textus Receptus had its problems, not the least of which was its failure to reflect the Byzantine Textform in an accurate manner. But the Byzantine Textform is not the TR, nor need it be associated with the TR or those defending such in any manner."[29] His edition's introduction puts a number on the gap: the early printed Received Text editions "differ from the Byzantine Textform in over 1800 instances, generally due to the inclusion of weakly supported non-Byzantine readings", and the edition exists to "rectify that situation".[30] Robinson's published collation against Scrivener's 1894 Received Text lists 1,883 places of difference.[30] Daniel Wallace, working from the other side of the argument, counted 1,838 differences between the Hodges and Farstad Majority Text and the Received Text, and Wilbur Pickering, an advocate, predicts "something over 1,500".[31] Three counts, three methods, one conclusion: roughly 1,800 places.

And the differences are not trivia. When Hodges announced the Majority Text edition in 1978 he named what he was deleting: "the famous Comma Johanneum... will be eliminated from our text. So will Acts 8:37 and many other readings that lack adequate MS attestation."[32] So the verse this lesson worked at length, 1 John 5:7, is absent from the Majority Text and present in the Received Text. A reader who has been told these are one position will not be able to make sense of that.

Why the confusion, then? Partly because the same man produced both impressions. Hodges in 1968 was defending the King James's text-type and told his readers to "go on using his King James Version with confidence"; Hodges in 1978 was separating his edition from the Received Text as sharply as any critical scholar would.[28][32] And partly because the position has an internal spread: Robinson explicitly disowns "questionable appeals to 'providential preservation'", while Pickering does rest part of his case on preservation.[29][31] So there are at least three positions here, not one, and the question that sorts them is the one this lesson has already given you: where does this person locate the preservation?

## If you are Catholic or Orthodox, the shelf is different

Everything above about the two axes applies unchanged. The list of versions does not, and it would be dishonest to hand you eight Protestant Bibles without saying so. Lesson 8 told you the Catholic canon has seventy-three books and Orthodox Bibles more; every version named so far prints sixty-six.

**Catholic.** The standard American Catholic translation is the New American Bible Revised Edition of 2011, which reads in the functional range. It is a composite worth understanding: the Old Testament was revised between 1994 and 2001 with a Psalter revised again in 2009 and 2010, and it is bound to a New Testament revised in 1986 from the United Bible Societies' third edition, which is the same critical text tradition as every other modern version.[34] Its Old Testament policy is stated plainly in its own preface: it follows the received Hebrew, Aramaic or Greek text, and where the translators depart from it, for example "by following the Septuagint rather than the Masoretic text" or accepting a Qumran reading, the departures are recorded in a published volume of textual notes.[34] For a formal option, the Revised Standard Version Second Catholic Edition (Ignatius, 2006) is the one to know; its publisher describes it as conforming to the Vatican's instruction *Liturgiam Authenticam* and as modernising the RSV's archaic pronouns.[35] The Jerusalem Bible of 1966 and the New Jerusalem Bible of 1985 sit in the middle with notes worth having, and there is a real translation lesson in the pair: the 1966 edition leaned heavily on the French *Bible de Jérusalem*, and the 1985 revision's chief claim was that it went back to the Hebrew, Greek and Aramaic while keeping the French edition's apparatus.

One thing to get right if you ever hear it argued. *Liturgiam Authenticam* (2001) is often described as requiring translation from the Latin. It requires the opposite: new translations "must be made directly from the original texts", with the *Nova Vulgata* consulted "as an auxiliary tool", and it is a rule about liturgical texts and lectionaries rather than about study Bibles.[36]

**Orthodox.** The Orthodox Study Bible (2008) is the usual English recommendation, and its method is unusual enough to state exactly: its New Testament is the New King James Version, and its Old Testament was produced by taking that same English base and revising it toward the Septuagint, rather than by translating the Greek from scratch.[37] Knowing that tells you what kind of book you are holding, which is the whole point of this lesson.

## How to choose

Fee and Stuart's advice, which this course adopts as its own: "the basic translation for reading and studying should be something in the NIV/NRSV range", with a formal-equivalent version "helpful as a second source".[1] I'd put it as three Bibles, and the third is optional.

One for reading: a functional translation you can read a whole book of at a sitting without stopping to decode. NIV, CSB, or NLT. This is the Bible you read a book of at a time, at reading speed.

One for study: a formal translation that shows you the form. ESV, NASB, NRSV, or NKJV if you want the Received Text with the footnotes. This is the one you open when a word repeats, an image puzzles you, or the reading Bible's smoothness makes you suspicious.

One for freshness, if you like: The Message or another free translation, for passages you know so well you no longer hear them. Never alone, and never for settling what a text says.

And the NET, free, for the days you want to see the decisions being made. It is the version this course quotes, and its notes are the reason as much as its licence is.[17]
