> "But he said to them, 'Do not be alarmed. You are looking for Jesus the Nazarene, who was crucified. He has been raised! He is not here. Look, there is the place where they laid him. But go, tell his disciples, even Peter, that he is going ahead of you into Galilee. You will see him there, just as he told you.' Then they went out and ran from the tomb, for terror and bewilderment had seized them. And they said nothing to anyone, because they were afraid." (Mark 16:6–8, NET)[1]

Now look at what your own Bible prints next. There will be a break in the page and a note, usually in square brackets, telling you that some of the earliest manuscripts do not include 16:9 to 20.[1] Nearly every modern translation prints one. Most readers skip past it. But stop and think about what it's saying. The translators are telling you that the next twelve verses, which include the risen Jesus appearing to Mary, the command to preach to all creation, the promise about handling snakes, and the ascension, are in some manuscripts and not in others, and that the ones missing them are among the oldest we have.

That note is a window into the whole question of this lesson. Lesson 8 told you which books are in the Bible. It said nothing about whether the words in those books are the words their authors wrote. Between a letter Paul dictated in the 50s and the Bible on your table lie nineteen centuries of copying by hand, and hand-copying makes mistakes. So how do we know what Paul wrote? What did the Dead Sea Scrolls actually show? What does it mean that there are, by one common count, about 400,000 variants in the New Testament manuscripts? And where does the most famous sceptic in the field, Bart Ehrman, actually stand?

By the end you'll be able to answer all of that, read the three most famous notes in your Bible and explain the evidence behind each, and tell which parts of the reliability question history has settled, which scholars still argue about, and which this course holds by faith.

## Two links, not fifty

Let's clear away the most common misconception first, because it shapes how people hear everything else. "The Bible has been translated so many times that it can't be trusted." The picture behind that sentence is a chain: Hebrew to Greek to Latin to German to English to modern English, each link copying the last, errors piling up like a game of telephone.

That is not how a modern Bible is made. The translators of the ESV, the NIV, the CSB, and the rest sat down with a printed Hebrew text and a printed Greek text and translated directly from them. The Hebrew edition is built on a single medieval manuscript we'll meet in a moment, checked against the Dead Sea Scrolls and other witnesses. The Greek edition is built from thousands of manuscripts compared against each other. So the chain has two links: manuscripts to edition, edition to your Bible. Nothing in between.

What is true, and what gives the myth its grip, is that the King James translators in 1611 worked from a Greek text that was late and thin, which is why your Bible and a King James differ at 1 John 5:7 and a few other places. We'll come to that. It's a story about the manuscripts, not about translation, and lesson 11 picks it up from the translation side.

## How a text gets copied, and how it gets fixed

Before printing, every copy of every book was made by hand. The New Testament was first written on papyrus, a paper made from reeds, and later on parchment, prepared animal skin. Early Jewish Scriptures were kept on scrolls; Christians took very early to the codex, the bound book with pages, which is why our oldest New Testament manuscripts are fragments of books rather than rolls.[2]

A scribe copying by hand makes predictable kinds of mistake, and the standard textbook on the subject, by Bruce Metzger and Bart Ehrman together, sorts them.[2] Errors of the eye: two lines end with the same word, and the scribe's eye jumps from the first to the second, dropping everything between. Errors of the ear, when a text was read aloud to a room of copyists and two Greek vowels sounded the same. Errors of memory, when the scribe carried a phrase in his head and wrote a familiar version from a parallel passage. And errors of judgement, when a scribe thought the text in front of him was wrong and "fixed" it: smoothed the grammar, harmonised one Gospel with another, or, in a small number of cases that scholars argue about, adjusted a phrase toward what he believed.

Now here's the mechanism that makes the whole field work, and I want you to hold onto it, because it's the answer to nearly every objection you'll hear.

Many independent copies are better than one perfect one. When a scribe in Alexandria makes a mistake, that mistake travels down to the copies made from his copy. It does not appear in a manuscript copied in Rome from a different exemplar, unless it was already in the text both were copying. So when copies diverge, the divergence itself tells you where an error entered, and comparing families of copies lets you work back toward what they had in common. More manuscripts help because there's more to compare. Older manuscripts help because fewer generations of copying separate them from the source. Widely spread manuscripts help because an error made in one place is exposed by copies made somewhere else.

This is why the telephone game is the wrong picture. Telephone is one chain, and you only ever hear the last whisper. This is a tree, with branches you can compare, and the comparing is the point.

:::checkpoint Two manuscripts disagree at one word. One is older; the other comes from a different region and agrees with a third. Which matters more, and why can't you say without more information?
Neither, on its own. Age tells you the manuscript is closer in time to the source, but it is still one copy and could carry an early error. Agreement across regions tells you a reading was not one scribe's slip, but two late copies could both descend from the same early mistake. What settles it is asking which reading best explains how the other one arose: which is the mistake a scribe would make, and which is the harder reading nobody would invent. That question needs the words themselves, not just the dates.
:::

## The Old Testament: a thousand years in one afternoon

Start with the oldest thing we have. In 1979, in a burial cave at Ketef Hinnom on the edge of Jerusalem, excavators found two tiny rolled-up sheets of silver.[3] When they were unrolled, each carried a form of the priestly blessing from Numbers 6:24–26:

> "The Lord bless you and protect you; the Lord make his face to shine upon you, and be gracious to you; the Lord lift up his countenance upon you and give you peace." (Numbers 6:24 to 26, NET)[4]

They date to the late seventh or early sixth century BC, before the Babylonian exile.[3] They are amulets, not Bibles, and they are the oldest surviving biblical text of any kind: a few lines, from the First Temple, that a Jew in Jerusalem could read in a synagogue this week.

:::figure https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/ff/Ketef_Hinnom_KH2_Scroll.jpg | The second Ketef Hinnom silver scroll, unrolled and flattened, a narrow strip of dark silver with faint scratched Hebrew letters, showing the priestly blessing of Numbers 6
The second Ketef Hinnom silver scroll (KH2), late seventh or early sixth century BC, the oldest surviving text from the Hebrew Bible. Israel Museum, Jerusalem. Image: public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.
:::

That's a fragment. For the text as a whole, the story before 1947 was this. The oldest complete Hebrew Bible in the world is the Leningrad Codex, copied in 1008 or 1009, and the slightly older Aleppo Codex, from about 930, lost part of its pages in 1947.[5] Both come from the Masoretes, the Jewish scholars who between roughly the sixth and tenth centuries standardised the Hebrew text, added the vowel points, and counted the letters so that copies could be checked. The text they fixed is called the Masoretic Text, and every modern Old Testament translation rests on the Leningrad Codex, through the printed editions scholars call BHS and BHQ.[5]

:::figure https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/57/Leningrad_Codex_Folio_008a.jpg | A page of the Leningrad Codex, three columns of Hebrew text with vowel points, surrounded by small marginal notes, on parchment
The Leningrad Codex, folio 8a, showing Genesis 14–15, copied in 1008 or 1009. The three columns are the biblical text with the Masoretes' vowel points; the small writing in the margins is their apparatus of notes and counts. Every modern Old Testament translation is made from this manuscript. Image: public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.
:::

So before 1947, the oldest complete Hebrew text of Isaiah was about 1,700 years younger than the prophet. Then a shepherd near the Dead Sea threw a stone into a cave.

Between 1947 and 1956, eleven caves near Qumran yielded the Dead Sea Scrolls. On the Israel Antiquities Authority's count, about 230 of the manuscripts are biblical, and every book of the Hebrew Bible is represented except Esther.[6] Most are fragments. One is not. The Great Isaiah Scroll, catalogued as 1QIsa^a, is a complete copy of Isaiah, all sixty-six chapters, on sheets of leather sewn end to end into one long roll. By the shape of its letters it is dated to about 125 BC; radiocarbon tests give a range of roughly 350 to 100 BC.[7]

:::figure https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/31/Great_Isaiah_Scroll.jpg | The Great Isaiah Scroll unrolled, a long strip of tan leather with columns of dense Hebrew writing, some sheets darker than others where they were sewn together
The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa^a) from Qumran Cave 1, about 125 BC, the only complete biblical book among the Dead Sea Scrolls. Photograph by Ardon Bar Hama, courtesy of the Israel Museum, Jerusalem; public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.
:::

Do the arithmetic. The Leningrad Codex is from 1008. The Isaiah Scroll is from about 125 BC. That's more than a thousand years of copying between them, and until 1947 nobody had seen the older end of it.

:::predict Before you read on, guess: after a thousand years of hand-copying, how different is the Isaiah of the scroll from the Isaiah of the Leningrad Codex? A different book? Different in every chapter? Or something else?
Something else. The two agree overwhelmingly. The differences are mostly spelling (the scroll uses more vowel letters, the way "colour" and "color" differ), small changes of wording, and a handful of places where a word is present in one and not the other. Nothing in the scroll changes what Isaiah is about. That is the settled finding, and it is the most important single fact about the Old Testament text.[7]
:::

That's the first half of what the scrolls show, and it's the half everyone quotes: across a thousand years, the Masoretes' text turns out to be a faithful copy of what Jews were reading before Jesus was born. If you'd asked a scholar in 1940 to bet on it, most would have bet against.

Now the second half, which is just as true and gets quoted much less. The Qumran caves held other biblical manuscripts besides the Isaiah Scroll, and some of them are not Masoretic-type texts at all. Some copies of Jeremiah and Samuel, for example, match the Greek Septuagint where it differs from the Hebrew, and some match neither.[7] In other words, around the turn of the era, more than one form of the text of some books was in circulation, and the Masoretic Text is one of them, the one that Judaism preserved. Emanuel Tov's standard textbook on the subject calls this the plurality of the text in that period, and it's why an honest lesson says "remarkably stable" and "also plural" in the same breath.[8] Anyone who tells you the scrolls prove not one word ever changed hasn't read them. Anyone who tells you they show the text was a free-for-all hasn't read them either.

Here's one place where you can watch a modern translation use the scrolls. Isaiah 53:11, in the ESV, reads: "Out of the anguish of his soul he shall see and be satisfied", and the footnote says: "Masoretic Text; Dead Sea Scroll he shall see light".[9] The NIV takes the other option and prints: "After he has suffered, he will see the light of life and be satisfied", with the note: "Dead Sea Scrolls (see also Septuagint); Masoretic Text does not have the light of life."[9] So the Hebrew of the Middle Ages has the servant "see" with no object; the Isaiah Scroll and the Greek translation made before Christ both have him "see light". Two translation committees, working from the same evidence, made different calls and told you so in the margin. That's textual criticism happening on the page in front of you.

:::checkpoint A speaker says, "The Dead Sea Scrolls prove the Bible has been copied without a single change." What's right in that sentence, what's wrong, and what would you say instead?
Right: the Isaiah Scroll shows a text overwhelmingly the same as the medieval Masoretic copies a thousand years later, which is a remarkable result. Wrong: "without a single change" is false even for Isaiah (spelling and small wording differ, and Isaiah 53:11 is a case where modern translations differ over which reading to follow), and the scrolls also show other forms of the text for some books. Say instead: the scrolls show the Masoretic Text is a faithful copy of a text already old before Jesus, and also that more than one form of some books was in circulation then.
:::

## The New Testament: fewer years, more copies

The New Testament's situation is the mirror image. The gap between writing and oldest copy is much smaller, and the number of copies is much larger. Both facts need care.

:::predict How many Greek manuscripts of the New Testament, whole or fragmentary, have scholars catalogued? Guess a number. Then guess what fraction of them are older than AD 400.
About 5,800 on the official register kept at Münster.[10] Very few of those are from before 400; the great majority are medieval, and many are fragments or lectionaries (books of readings for church services). Both guesses are usually wrong: people guess too low on the count and far too high on the early fraction.
:::

The Institut für Neutestamentliche Textforschung in Münster keeps the register, and by 2017 it stood at roughly 5,800 Greek manuscripts, in four classes: papyri, majuscules (early manuscripts in capital letters), minuscules (later ones in a running hand), and lectionaries.[10] Daniel Wallace, who has spent his career photographing them, gives the same figure: "over 5800 have been catalogued."[11] Add early translations into Latin, Syriac, and Coptic, and quotations in the church fathers, and you have far more evidence for the New Testament's wording than for any other ancient text.

Now the care. That number is quoted in every apologetics talk, and it is not the argument. Most of those 5,800 are from the ninth century onward. If they were all we had, we'd have a well-attested medieval text and no way to check it against anything earlier. The argument is the age and spread of the early witnesses, and those are worth knowing by name.

The oldest is a scrap the size of a credit card. Rylands Greek Papyrus 457, known as P52, holds a few lines of John 18:31–33 on one side and 37–38 on the other, and measures 8.9 by 6.0 centimetres.[12] Its first editor dated it to the first half of the second century, and for decades it was quoted as "about 125". Then in 2005 Brent Nongbri showed that the handwriting fits anywhere from the early second century to the early third, and the Rylands Library's own catalogue now says "Recent research points to a date nearer to 200 CE."[12][13] So say "second century" and leave it there. Even at the later end, that's a copy of John circulating in Egypt within about a century of its writing.

:::figure https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/32/P52_recto.jpg | The front of Rylands Papyrus P52, a small irregular fragment of brown papyrus with seven partial lines of Greek capital letters from John 18
Rylands Greek Papyrus 457 (P52), the recto, with John 18:31–33. Second century. John Rylands Library, Manchester. Image: public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.
:::

Then the big papyri. P46, around AD 200, contains most of Paul's letters in one codex, and is split between Dublin and Michigan.[14] P75, from about 200 to 225, holds much of Luke and John and is textually very close to the great fourth-century codex we're about to meet, which showed scholars that the text of that codex was not a fourth-century invention but was already being copied a century and a half earlier.[14]

And then the two giants. Codex Sinaiticus, found in the monastery at the foot of Mount Sinai in the nineteenth century and now mostly in the British Library, is the oldest complete New Testament in the world, from the fourth century, and you can turn every page of it at [codexsinaiticus.org](https://codexsinaiticus.org).[15] Codex Vaticanus, in the Vatican Library, is from the same century and is, for most of the New Testament, the single most important manuscript there is.[15] When those two agree against the medieval majority, editors take notice, and the three famous passages below are all cases where they do.

Look at the dashed line on the Isaiah row. That's the thousand-year gap that the Isaiah Scroll closed, and the red dot is the scroll landing near the older end of it. Now look at the John and Paul rows. The red dots are a finger's width from the writing. The New Testament's gap from author to oldest copy is a small fraction of the Old Testament's, and that's the fact that makes it the best-attested ancient text there is. It's also why lesson 6's argument about the Gospels' dates matters here: the earlier you date the Gospels, the earlier the papyri sit relative to them, but even on the critical dates the gap is short.

## What a variant is, and what 400,000 of them means

A textual variant is any place where one manuscript differs from another. That is the whole definition, and everything depends on it.

:::predict Roughly how many variants are there across all the New Testament manuscripts? And what proportion of them, would you guess, change the meaning of a passage and have a real chance of being original?
About 400,000, on the way both Daniel Wallace and Bart Ehrman count. Under 1 percent are both meaningful and viable, on Wallace's count. Both numbers are below.
:::

Both Wallace, the conservative, and Ehrman, the critic, put the number at around 400,000, and Ehrman's own phrase is "hundreds of thousands of textual variants".[11][16][17] They count the same way: a difference counts once, no matter how many manuscripts carry it. Now notice what that method implies. The more manuscripts you have, the more differences you will find, because every fresh copy brings its own slips. A text with 5,800 manuscripts will have vastly more variants than a text with ten. A large count of variants is what abundant evidence looks like, not what corruption looks like.

Then sort them. Wallace's categories, in his own words: "Spelling and nonsense readings are the vast majority, accounting for at least 75% of all variants."[11] Next come differences that don't change the meaning in translation, such as Greek word order and the presence or absence of the definite article before a name. Then differences that would change the meaning but have no chance of being original, because they sit in one late manuscript against everything else. And last: "The smallest category by far is the last category: meaningful and viable variants. These comprise less than 1% of all textual variants."[11]

Under 1 percent of 400,000 is still a few thousand places, and you have just read that Mark 16 is one of them. So don't let the small percentage do too much work either. The question is what those places are, and here the two men whose names sit on the standard textbook, and who agree about almost nothing else, say something close to the same thing.

:::predict Bart Ehrman is the most famous sceptic of the New Testament text alive. Before you read his words, guess: does he think the textual variants put any central Christian doctrine in doubt?
No. In his own words, on his own blog, to his own readers: "none of your cherished doctrines appears to be in real danger because of variations in our surviving manuscripts."[16] What he goes on to say is that he thinks that's the wrong test, and you should hear that too.
:::

Here is each man in his own terms.

Wallace, the conservative, on the meaningful and viable variants: "Even here, no cardinal belief is at stake. These variants do affect what a particular passage teaches, and thus what the Bible says in that place, but they do not jeopardize essential beliefs."[11] On his own website, listing what he and Ehrman agree about after their public debates, Wallace writes that "we both agree that the scribes changed the texts hundreds of thousands of times, that certain passages are not authentic (notably, the longer ending of Mark and the story of the woman caught in adultery), that the proto-orthodox changed the text at times, and that only a small fraction of all textual changes are both meaningful and viable."[17]

Ehrman, the critic, agrees about the doctrines, and then asks why doctrines should be the measure. "Why is it that 'threats to significant doctrines' are taken to be the only standard of what is important to the Christian religion? In my view this idea is completely wrong-headed."[18] His examples: whether Mark's Jesus is angry or compassionate when he heals the leper (Mark 1:41 has manuscripts both ways); whether Luke's voice at the baptism says "today I have begotten you"; whether Luke's Gospel includes the words about the body "given for you" at the Last Supper; whether John has the woman caught in adultery at all. None of these overturns a creed. Each changes what one book says, and Ehrman's point is that if you care what each author meant, rather than just whether the Trinity survives, then these matter. He puts it this way: "changes in the Bible can be inordinately significant without affecting any cherished doctrines".[16]

I think Ehrman is right about that, and I want you to hold his point and Wallace's together, because they don't conflict. The text is recoverable to a very high degree, no article of the creeds rests on a disputed reading, and there are a few thousand places where it matters to a careful reader which reading is right. All three are true. A Christian who says only the first two is hiding something; a sceptic who says only the third is hiding more.

## Worked example 1: the ending of Mark, and the woman caught in adultery

Back to the bracket we started with. Here's the evidence behind it, from Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary*, the book that explains the decisions behind the standard Greek edition.[19]

The twelve verses are "absent from the two oldest Greek manuscripts (א and B)", that is, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, "from the Old Latin codex Bobiensis, the Sinaitic Syriac manuscript, about one hundred Armenian manuscripts, and the two oldest Georgian manuscripts."[19] That's the external evidence: the oldest witnesses, and witnesses from different regions and languages, stop at 16:8. Remember the mechanism. An omission that shows up in Greek, Latin, Syriac, Armenian, and Georgian copies is not one scribe's slip.

Then the internal evidence, which is where you can join in. Metzger: "The vocabulary and style of verses 9-20 are non-Markan", and "the connection between ver. 8 and verses 9-20 is so awkward that it is difficult to believe that the evangelist intended the section to be a continuation of the Gospel."[19] Read 16:8 and 16:9 back to back and you'll feel the join: Mary Magdalene, who has been on stage since 15:40, is reintroduced in verse 9 as if for the first time, "from whom he had driven out seven demons".

There's a further clue. A few manuscripts have a different, shorter ending after verse 8, two sentences long, and some have both endings one after the other.[19] That's what it looks like when a book ends abruptly and later readers try to finish it: more than one attempt survives. The committee's conclusion: "on the basis of good external evidence and strong internal considerations it appears that the earliest ascertainable form of the Gospel of Mark ended with 16.8."[19]

Now the question that matters for a Christian reader. What changes if verses 9–20 are not Mark's? Nothing in the creeds. The resurrection is announced at 16:6, "He has been raised! He is not here", in the part nobody disputes, and every other Gospel narrates the appearances. What does change: the verse about believers handling snakes and drinking poison (16:18) loses its claim to be Mark's, which is worth knowing if you ever meet a church built on it, and the phrase "The one who believes and is baptized will be saved" (16:16) can't be quoted as Mark's either. Whether Mark meant to end at "because they were afraid", or whether his real ending was lost, is the part scholars argue about, and I'll flag it again below.

The second passage has the same shape with one twist. John 7:53–8:11, the woman caught in adultery, sits in your Bible inside brackets or under a note saying that the earliest manuscripts do not include it.[20] Metzger calls the evidence for its non-Johannine origin "overwhelming": it's absent from P66 and P75, from Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, and from a long list of other witnesses, and its style and vocabulary "differ noticeably from the rest of the Fourth Gospel".[21] The twist is that in the manuscripts that do have it, it floats. "Most copyists apparently thought that it would interrupt John's narrative least if it were inserted after 7.52", Metzger writes, but "others placed it after 7.36, or after 21.25, or after Luke 21.38."[21] A passage that moves around is a passage that was slotted in.

And then Metzger says something you should quote to anyone who thinks "not original" means "made up": "the account has all the earmarks of historical veracity. It is obviously a piece of oral tradition which circulated in certain parts of the Western church."[21] So the story of Jesus saying "Whoever among you is guiltless may be the first to throw a stone at her" and "I do not condemn you either. Go, and from now on do not sin any more"[20] is, in the view of the scholar who wrote the book, a real early memory of Jesus that was not part of John's Gospel as John wrote it. The current standard edition, NA28, prints it in double brackets for exactly that reason: kept, and marked.[22]

## Worked example 2: the Johannine Comma

Now a case with a wrinkle, and I'll leave a step for you.

Open 1 John 5:7–8 in the King James Version:

> "For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one. And there are three that bear witness in earth, the Spirit, and the water, and the blood: and these three agree in one." (KJV)[23]

Now the NET:

> "For there are three that testify, the Spirit and the water and the blood, and these three are in agreement." (1 John 5:7–8, NET)[23]

A King James reader has just found the clearest statement of the Trinity in the whole Bible: Father, Word, and Holy Ghost, "and these three are one". A modern reader finds it gone. The NIV's footnote explains, in one line, that the longer reading comes from "Late manuscripts of the Vulgate" and is "not found in any Greek manuscript before the fourteenth century".[24] Scholars call the extra words the Johannine Comma ("comma" here meaning a short clause).

Here's the evidence, from Metzger. The words are "absent from every known Greek manuscript except eight", and he lists them: the earliest that has the words in its actual text is a manuscript from the fourteenth or fifteenth century; the rest are sixteenth century or later, or are older manuscripts where a later hand added the words in the margin.[25] The passage "is quoted by none of the Greek Fathers", who would have loved it in the fourth-century Trinitarian controversies if they had known it.[25] Its earliest appearance is in a Latin treatise of the fourth century, attributed to the Spaniard Priscillian or a follower, and from there it made its way into copies of the Latin Vulgate.[25]

How did a Latin gloss get into a Greek printed text and from there into the King James? When Erasmus printed the first published Greek New Testament in 1516, he left the words out, because his Greek manuscripts didn't have them. Under pressure, he included them in his third edition of 1522, and that edition fed the line of printed Greek texts, later called the Textus Receptus, that the King James translators used in 1611.[26] The English Revised Version of 1881 dropped the words, and every major modern translation since has omitted or footnoted them.[26]

Now do the next step before I do. Suppose you are on a translation committee. You have the evidence above. A colleague says, "But it's the best Trinity verse we have; leaving it out weakens the doctrine." Write down what you'd reply, in two sentences, and then read on.

Here's mine. First, the job of the committee is to print what John wrote, and the evidence that John did not write this is about as strong as textual evidence gets: no Greek manuscript for over a thousand years, no Greek father, a traceable Latin origin. Second, the doctrine does not depend on it. The church defined the Trinity at Nicaea in 325 and Constantinople in 381 from texts such as Matthew 28:19, John 1:1, and 2 Corinthians 13:14, in Greek, without this verse, because the Greek-speaking church didn't have it. A doctrine that stood for a thousand years without a verse doesn't fall when the verse turns out to be a gloss.

The wrinkle, and the reason this example matters for lesson 11, is that the King James is not "wrong" here in the way a mistranslation is wrong. Its translators rendered the Greek in front of them faithfully. The Greek in front of them was a late text with a Latin gloss in it. That's the difference between a translation problem and a manuscript problem, and knowing which is which will save you from a great many bad arguments about which Bible to trust.

:::checkpoint A friend says, "Modern Bibles removed the Trinity from 1 John 5:7." What's the accurate version of that sentence?
Modern Bibles print 1 John 5:7 as the Greek manuscripts have it. The Trinitarian words were never in any Greek manuscript before the fourteenth century, entered the printed Greek text through Erasmus's 1522 edition under pressure, and reached English through the King James. Nothing was removed from John; something that had been added was set aside, and the doctrine of the Trinity, defined a thousand years before the words appeared in Greek, rests on other texts.
:::

The two men this lesson has been quoting, on one stage at Southern Methodist University on 1 October 2011, posted by Wallace's own Center for the Study of New Testament Manuscripts. It is long; set aside an evening. Watch it after this lesson and you'll hear that they agree on the facts above and disagree about what the facts mean, which is exactly the shape of the question.

## What is established, and what is argued

Now sort the whole subject, in the three-way language this course has used since lesson 1.

*History establishes* that the New Testament is by far the best-attested text from the ancient world, in the number, age, and spread of its witnesses; that its wording is recoverable to a very high degree; that the Masoretic Text of the Old Testament is a faithful copy of a text already old before Christ, and that other forms of some books were also in circulation then; and that no article of the creeds depends on a disputed reading. On that last point Metzger and Ehrman, who disagree about the existence of God, say the same thing in print, and that's why this course cites both.[2][11][16]

*Scholars argue about* several things, and you should know them.

Whether early scribes changed the text on purpose for doctrinal reasons, and how often. Ehrman's 1993 book *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* argued that scribes in the second and third centuries sometimes altered readings to defend orthodox Christology against rival views.[27] Wallace and Larry Hurtado, among others, replied that most such changes are better explained as accident or harmonisation, that the examples are few, and that the alterations, where real, never succeeded in hiding the original, since we can see them.[17] Both sides agree the changes exist; they argue about motive and scale.

How Mark ended. That the earliest recoverable Mark stops at 16:8 is the consensus; whether Mark intended to stop there, with the women afraid and silent, or whether a page was lost, is not settled and may never be.[19]

Whether "the original text" is even the right goal. Eldon Epp and David Parker have argued that for some books there may never have been a single original wording to recover, only early forms that were already being shaped as they were copied, and that the aim should be to study the living text rather than chase a lost autograph.[28] Most textual critics still work toward the earliest recoverable text, and the standard edition's editors call it the "initial text" for that reason, but the debate is real.

*We hold by faith* that these words, recovered as well as human scholarship can recover them, are God's Word. Textual criticism cannot show that and doesn't try. What it shows is the narrower and still remarkable thing: that what we have is, in nearly every line, what they wrote. Whether what they wrote is true is a question lesson 10 takes up.

## What people get wrong

**"It's been translated so many times it can't be trusted."** Two links, not fifty. Modern translations are made from Hebrew and Greek editions built from the manuscripts. The King James differs from your Bible at 1 John 5:7 because of the manuscripts its translators had, not because of translation.

**"The Dead Sea Scrolls prove not a word has changed."** Half right. The Isaiah Scroll shows a text overwhelmingly stable across a thousand years, and it also shows small differences (you read one at 53:11), and other scrolls show other forms of some books.[7][8][9] Say both halves.

**"400,000 variants means the text is hopeless."** A variant is any difference in any manuscript, counted once. More manuscripts, more variants. At least three-quarters are spelling and nonsense; under 1 percent are meaningful and viable; and the meaningful ones are known and printed in the margins, not hidden.[11]

**"There are no variants that matter."** You've just read three that do, and Ehrman's list is longer. What's true is that none touches a creed. What's also true is that a reader who wants to know what Mark, John, and the author of 1 John wrote needs to know where the text is disputed. The notes in your Bible exist so you can.

**"The Bible was written centuries after the events."** Lesson 6 handled this for the Gospels: no scholar of any stripe dates them more than about seventy years after the crucifixion, and the papyri show them circulating within a century of that. For the Old Testament, be honest that the gap is real and larger. The oldest substantial copies are from the last centuries before Christ, and most of the books describe events centuries earlier than that. How the text was transmitted before the scrolls is a question the scrolls don't answer, and this lesson doesn't pretend they do.

**"Sinaiticus and Vaticanus are the Bible."** They're the two most important witnesses, not the text. Editors weigh them, and sometimes the papyri or a reading spread across regions outweighs them. A manuscript is evidence, and evidence gets weighed.

:::callout Why more copies is good news
People hear "400,000 variants" as bad news and "5,800 manuscripts" as good news without noticing that the first is a consequence of the second. If we had one manuscript of the New Testament, we'd have zero variants and no way to know whether that manuscript was any good. The variants are the record of the comparisons that let us reconstruct the text. A text with no variants is a text with no evidence.
:::
