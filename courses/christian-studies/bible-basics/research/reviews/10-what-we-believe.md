# Reviews: The Bible, What It Is and How to Read It — 10-what-we-believe

Append-only. Newest at the bottom.

## Lesson 10, "What we believe about this book, and what others make of it" — Stage 4 round 1, 2026-09-08

Five fresh-context subagents. All five returned; everything below was applied in the same session.
This lesson is the doctrine-of-Scripture lesson and the most sensitive in the course, so the
neutrality brief asked for fairness in two directions at once: to the critical account from outside
the faith, and to the Catholic, Orthodox, mainline and inerrantist positions inside it.

### BLOCKING, applied

- **The Chicago Statement was quoted only in its permissive half, for the third time in this course.**
  SOURCES.md carries a standing instruction, written at lesson 3's Stage 4 and repeated at lesson
  6's, that any lesson citing Chicago for genre latitude must cite its limits alongside it or it
  over-reads the statement in the direction its own signatories reject. This lesson leaned harder on
  Article XIII than any other and cited neither limit. Both are now in the body: Article XII's
  second denial, and the 1982 Chicago Statement on Biblical Hermeneutics, which values genre
  criticism and then denies that "generic categories which negate historicity may rightly be
  imposed on biblical narratives which present themselves as factual".
  **Correction to the research file:** that clause is Article XIII of the 1982 statement, not
  Article XXII as SOURCES.md records it, and Article XIV is the companion. Fetched and quoted from
  the full text; SOURCES.md corrected.
- **Article XIX was quoted with its second half removed.** The lesson used "we deny that such
  confession is necessary for salvation" to support the claim that a Christian who takes the
  source-critical side has not left the faith. The article goes on, in the same breath, to deny
  "that inerrancy can be rejected without grave consequences, both to the individual and to the
  Church". Cutting that is the same failure the course keeps having to fix, and here it cut in the
  inerrantists' disfavour. Both halves now stand.
- **The course's own central argument faced no objection.** Every other position in the lesson gets
  a reply printed against it; the argument from Jesus' view of Scripture got "that's the whole
  argument, and we find it strong". Standards 3.7 does not exempt the house position. The two
  standard objections, circularity and accommodation, are now stated in their holders' terms with
  the replies, and the second is tied to the Pentateuch section, where this course explicitly does
  not treat "Jesus said the book of Moses" as settling authorship.
- **The critical view was represented by its manners rather than its conclusions.** Every quotation
  chosen from the critical side was a disclaimer or a limitation. Not one substantive critical
  result appeared. The results are now stated plainly, in the form a critical scholar would state
  them, along with the fact that some scholars hold they count decisively against any doctrine that
  calls this book God's Word, and a pointer to Ehrman at full length rather than in summary.
- **Books characterised that nobody opened.** Wenham, on whom the course's own faith argument rests,
  plus Rogers and McKim, Enns, and a claim about ancient Near Eastern parallels attributed to three
  unopened conservative works. All now say in the body that the description is second-hand, and the
  unsourced clause is deleted. The lesson no longer reports what Enns's postscript says.
- **The quiz had no application items.** All six wrapped a new speaker around a case the lesson had
  already worked, and four were the four bullets of the misconception list restated. Five items
  replaced; four of the six now work cases the lesson does not, including one that tests what a
  hypothetical new manuscript would and would not change, which is the only item that tests the
  "what would move it" half of the sort.

### Facts corrected

- The lesson said a stele moved "David existed" from argued to established. SOURCES.md records that
  exact overstatement and its correction, and the lesson's own archaeology section and quiz get it
  right; only this sentence was wrong. What the stele establishes is a dynasty named after a David.
- Miller's article was described as showing the question still open. He argues to a verdict on the
  broader side. Also: it is a single-author article in a special issue, not a symposium, which the
  research file and a quiz explanation both had wrong.
- The Pilate stone is not the only inscription naming him; a ring from Herodium carries his name.
  It is the only one that names him with his office.
- Bea "helped draft the text" is not supported by the source cited, which places him at the council.
- The four senses were missing for the third time in this course, against another standing
  instruction. Present now, with the note that they are the fathers' method and normal Catholic and
  Orthodox practice, not a beginners' error.

### Neutrality

Perspectives checked: inerrantist, infallibilist, Catholic (both readings of Dei Verbum 11 and the
pre-conciliar magisterium), Orthodox, mainline and Barthian, Enns, confessional Reformed and
Anglican, traditional Jewish, historical-critical, and sceptical.

- **The title promised "what others make of it" and delivered the academy.** Judaism and Islam both
  hold developed doctrines of these books and neither appeared, in the lesson whose subject is what
  this book is. Both now have a paragraph in their own terms, with the honest note that a paragraph
  each is not enough and that World Religions treats them properly.
- The mustard-seed example labelled one reader by an emotional state ("a nervous believer") and the
  other descriptively. Now both are named by their reading.
- Chicago's Article III was quoted against the Confession of 1967 without naming Barth, which is
  what Article III was written against, leaving a reader with a disagreement with one party unnamed.
- Two over-hardenings fixed: "false to the field" on the Tel Dan reading, where a real minority
  disputes it, and "every critical scholar" on Pauline authorship of Romans.
- Two sneers at unnamed people, symmetric but still sneers, rewritten.
- The course declined to say where it stands on the one question the section says everything turns
  on, while promising to state its view "plainly". It now names the scope it holds and why.

### Pedagogy

- Assessment aside, the teaching was strong. The failures were placement and hiding: a 1,400-word
  doctrinal core with no interaction in it, an archaeology section with none, an answer printed in
  plain prose three lines under its own question (which the build had been flagging as a draft
  warning), and the exercise's answer key sitting inside the visible exercise block.
- All four fixed: a checkpoint on the four words, a predict before the archaeology columns, the
  Mark 1:5 problem converted to a predict, and the exercise key moved into a checkpoint.
- The exercise's claims were mostly recycled from the lesson. Three swapped for claims the lesson
  never sorts, including the resurrection, which is the case where the sort is hardest and most
  worth practising.

### Voice and media

- The paragraph-closing epigram was not a tic here, it was the default cadence: roughly 23 of 45
  body paragraphs closed on a one-line moral, usually an antithesis, which is what made a doctrine
  lesson read like a sermon. Twenty of them cut or flattened; the three that carry real content kept.
- The ideological-Turing-test line ("an inerrantist reading this should find...") appeared four
  times in the same grammar, which reads as the lesson grading its own homework. Two kept, two cut.
- Zero links in 12,000 words that quote seven confessional documents, six of them free online.
  All are now linked at the point of quotation.
- **The sort diagram was invisible to both SVG validators.** Every font-size and every hardcoded
  white fill sat on `<g>` wrappers rather than on `<text>`, and both checks in `scripts/build.mjs`
  matched only on the `<text>` tag, so 24 labels between 8.5 and 11 units passed clean while other
  lessons were flagged for the same defect. White on the gold band was also about 3.2:1. The chart
  is redrawn stacked, with every size on its own element, labels outside the swatches and no
  hardcoded colour. **The validator itself is fixed in the same commit** to resolve inherited
  font-size, which is what should have caught this; it now flags lessons 1, 11 and 12, all of which
  carry the same defect. 11 and 12 are the next two reviews; lesson 1 is a small follow-up.
- The Pilate figure hotlinked a 6.7 MB original. Swapped for the 118 KB thumbnail. Every
  `:::figure` in this course does the same thing, so a sweep is on the backlog.

### Unverified, and said so

- Cassuto, Kikawada and Quinn, and Garrett: named, nothing quoted, specific works still unconfirmed.
  The lesson's characterisation is the standard description of that literature, and the unsourced
  clause about ancient Near Eastern parallels has been deleted rather than left standing.
- Ware, *The Orthodox Church*: named only, edition unverified, nothing depends on it.
- Providentissimus Deus, Divino Afflante Spiritu and the 2014 PBC document are cited for the
  Catholic argument's background on standard dates and titles; not read in full for this lesson.

### Deliberately not fixed

- The reviewer proposed replacing the six-position comparison table's third column. Left alone: it
  is organised on the one variable the lesson is about, and shortening it would cost the exactness
  that makes it worth having.
- The suggestion to drop "Now" and "So" as paragraph openers wholesale was applied only where the
  sentence was doing drumroll work. Some of them are just how a person talks.
