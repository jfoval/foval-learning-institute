The test asks whether you can answer questions about passages someone else chose. This asks the
harder thing, and the thing the course was actually for: take one short book, read it over two weeks
on a plan you wrote, and produce the guide you wish someone had handed you before you started.

Lesson 12 launched this. If you did that lesson's exercise, you already have the plan and the log
set up, and part one below is done.

Budget about five hours across two weeks. The two weeks are not padding. A book read in fourteen
sittings shows you things a book read in one sitting cannot, because you carry it around in between.

## Choose your book

Ruth, Jonah, Habakkuk, Mark, Philippians, or Philemon together with Colossians.

Two notes on choosing. Lesson 12 worked **Philemon** and **Jonah** in full, so if you pick either of
those you are checking your work against the lesson rather than doing it cold, and you will learn
less. Prefer **Ruth** or **Habakkuk** if you want the real exercise. Pick **Mark** if you want the
hardest version, since sixteen chapters over fourteen days is a real reading load and the payoff is
that you will have read a whole Gospel properly, probably for the first time.

Do not pick the book you know best.

## Part one: the plan, written on day one

Before you read a word, write the fourteen days out with actual passages, actual dates, and the time
of day you intend to read. Not "Ruth, two weeks". Something like "Tuesday 14th, 7am, Ruth 1:1 to 22".

Two rules from lesson 12. Keep each day's reading small enough that you will actually do it on a bad
day, and put the last two days aside for reading the whole book straight through in one sitting.

## Part two: the log

Three columns, kept as you go rather than reconstructed afterwards.

| Date | What I read | One observation |
|---|---|---|

The third column is the one that matters, and it has a standard: **an observation that could only
have come from reading the actual text**. "It was encouraging" does not qualify. "Naomi tells the
women to call her Bitter, and the narrator goes on calling her Naomi" does.

If you miss a day, write the miss in the log. A log with gaps in it that you can see is worth more
than a tidy one, both to you and as evidence that you did the thing.

## Part three: the guide

Two pages, in the *Book by Book* pattern lesson 12 taught, in these five parts.

**1. Orienting data.** Who wrote it, to whom, from where, when, why, what kind of writing it is, and
where it sits in the story. Where a question is argued, say so and give both positions in a line
each rather than picking one silently. For Ruth that means the date of composition; for Habakkuk the
setting and its Babylonian crisis; for Mark the dating range and the authorship question, both of
which lesson 6 gave you in both sides' terms.

**2. Overview.** The book's shape in a paragraph. How it begins, where it turns, how it ends. If you
cannot find the turn, you have not finished reading.

**3. Three specific pieces of advice for reading it well.** Specific to this book, not to the Bible
in general. "Read it slowly" is not advice. "Notice that the narrator never once tells you what God
is doing, and that every character keeps saying it" is advice.

**4. A walk through.** The sections in order, each in a line or two. Your own division, with the
seams where you think they fall.

**5. What a first-time reader will get wrong.** One paragraph. This is the hardest part and the most
useful, and it is where the genre lessons pay off: the proverb read as a promise, the narrative read
as a command, the oracle read as a prediction about next year, the parable allegorised.

## What you hand in

The plan, the log, and the guide. Keep them together in one file or one notebook.

## Rubric, out of ten. Seven to pass.

| Points | What is being judged |
|---|---|
| 3 | The genre is identified correctly, and its rules are visibly used in the guide. Not named at the top and then forgotten: the advice and the walk through should read differently for a narrative than for an oracle. |
| 2 | The book is placed on the timeline, and the then-meaning is stated before the now-meaning. If any application appears before the section on what it meant to its first readers, this scores zero. |
| 2 | Every historical claim in the orienting data is sourced, and every argued question is given with both positions. A confident date with nothing behind it loses the mark. |
| 2 | The log shows the plan was followed, or honestly adjusted, with observations that could only have come from the actual text. |
| 1 | The guide would actually help a real first-time reader. Name the person you wrote it for, even if you never give it to them. |

Mark yourself against that table honestly, one line per row. Nobody else will, and the only person a
generous self-assessment costs anything is you.

## A note on why this shape

Everything in the rubric is something the course taught and something a careless reader skips. The
first row is lesson 3's rule. The second is the whole burden of lessons 2 and 7. The third is
Editorial Standards 2 applied to your own writing, and it is why the argued questions in this course
were always given with both sides. The fourth is retrieval practice; the fifth is the reason any of
it matters, since a reading nobody else could use is a reading you have not finished thinking about.

The site has no accounts, so the log and the guide are your own files. That is not only a technical
limitation. Ezra read to a crowd and the Levites explained it; the Bereans examined the Scriptures
together. Give your guide to one person and ask them what they did not understand. That is the
oldest form of this assignment there is.
