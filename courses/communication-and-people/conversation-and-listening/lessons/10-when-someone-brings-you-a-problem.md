A friend tells you something has gone wrong, and before they have finished the second sentence part
of you is already working on the answer. The impulse is kind. It is also what most of this lesson is
about. In two studies of undergraduates and members of the public responding to imagined
complaints from friends who were not asking for advice, people offered some anyway, very early, in about 70% of cases, and more
often to the friends they felt closest to.[3] Whether that helps depends on what the friend needed,
and there's a good deal of research on how to tell.

## Nine levels of comfort

The most studied way of grading what people say to someone who is upset comes from communication
research, and it began as a coding scheme: nine levels, from work by Applegate in 1980 and Burleson in
1982, for sorting comforting messages by how **person-centred** they are.[1] This course has not
read Burleson's own chapter. It takes the levels from [a 2018 paper by Susanne Jones, Graham Bodie
and colleagues](http://www.grahambodie.com/s/58-Mapping-the-terrain-of-person-centered-supportive-conversations_Jones-Bodie-et-al-Monographs-2018.pdf),
read in full, which defines person-centred support as "the extent to which a supportive message
explicitly acknowledges and validates how a distressed person feels and thinks about a difficult
event".[1]

The nine levels fall into three bands:

- **Low** (levels 1 to 3, the paper's LPC): condemning the person's feelings, challenging whether they are
  legitimate, or ignoring them.
- **Moderate** (4 to 6, MPC): distracting the person from what happened, offering condolences that
  are not about the feeling, or explaining the event without reference to it. In the coding manual
  the paper describes, level 6 also takes in questions about what happened, paraphrases, and
  advice.
- **High** (7 to 9, HPC): explicitly recognising the feelings, elaborating and explaining them, or
  helping the person see how the feelings fit a broader context.[1]

### What the studies found

Across 23 studies, a meta-analysis by High and Dillard found "a positive linear
association between person-centeredness and actual effectiveness, and an even stronger linear
relationship between person-centeredness and perceived effectiveness", and found the association
"attenuated in confederate-based methodologies", meaning weaker when the comfort came from a
trained stranger playing a part.[2] That is a **meta-analysis**, a pooled set of studies, and this
course read only its abstract, so it can give no effect sizes. Two things in that sentence matter.
The link was stronger for how helpful messages seemed than for what they did. And, as Jones and
colleagues point out, most of the studies asked people to rate written messages about imagined
situations, rather than measuring what happened in a real conversation.[1]

Jones's team looked at live ones. They coded every utterance in 223 five-minute conversations,
recorded years earlier at Arizona State University, in which a student talked about something
upsetting to a stranger who was secretly one of four trained confederates, two men and two women,
told to give low, moderate or high comfort.[1] It is a **single study**, a new analysis of one set of
recordings.

:::predict In the conversations where the trained listener was told to give the most person-centred comfort, what share of that listener's turns do you think the coders placed in the top three levels?

About a quarter: 24% on average. The rest were almost all in the moderate band: about half of all that listener's turns were sympathy statements (level 5), and about a fifth were questions and comments about the event (level 6). Independent raters still judged those conversations as highly person-centred.[1]

The authors' reading is that a listener giving good comfort cycles between moderate and high responses, rather than staying at the top.
:::

Two more results change how you would use the scale. First, low-comfort turns went with worse
outcomes more strongly than high-comfort turns went with better ones: "In terms of magnitude, LPC turns (levels 1–3) also exerted a stronger
impact on outcomes than HPC turns", and the effects either way were small.[1] Second, one level in
the middle band behaved like the bottom. Level 4 covers distracting the person ("Let's go do
something") and remarks that "everything will work out just fine," offered without any sign of how.
In their data, "Level 4 messages thus appear to operate much more like LPC messages than MPC
messages, at least in terms of the outcomes we examined."[1]

The authors name the obvious limit themselves: "it is possible that our results are unique to
stranger dyads" (a dyad is a pair).[1] Friends were not studied. **This course's reading** of the two papers together:
the scale is a way of hearing what a reply does, not a script. The strongest thing the evidence
supports is avoiding the bottom of it, and the top levels are easier to reach honestly once you have
asked enough to know what the person feels.

### Thaddeus's news

Thaddeus tells you over a drink that he has been made redundant, after nine years at the same firm.
Below are six replies you might hear, with where each falls. The placements are this course's, made
from the paper's descriptions of each level; the coders in the study read every utterance in the
context of the whole conversation, which a list cannot do.

1. "Well, they had been cutting back. Didn't you see it coming?" **Low.** It suggests he should
   have known, which leans towards blaming him (level 1).
2. "Honestly, don't get worked up about it. It's only a job." **Low.** It challenges whether his
   feeling is legitimate (level 2).
3. "You'll walk into something better, you'll see. Come on, let's get another round in." **Moderate
   by label, low in effect.** Distraction plus reassurance with nothing behind it: level 4, the one
   that behaved like the bottom band.
4. "Oh no. I'm so sorry." **Moderate.** A condolence (level 5). The paper gives "I am so sorry" as
   its example, and in their data level-5 turns went with better outcomes, not worse.[1]
5. "When did they tell you? Is it the whole department?" **Moderate.** Questions about the event
   (level 6). Not about the feeling, and exactly how you find out what the feeling is.
6. "That must have been a real shock after nine years. It makes sense you're worried, with the rent
   to find, and it sounds as if it's not only about the money." **High.** It names the feeling,
   legitimises it, and reaches, tentatively, for what else might be in it (levels 7 and 8).

Reply 6 on its own, as an opening line, could sound rehearsed. In real talk it tends to come after
4 and 5, once you have heard enough to know that it is the rent he is lying awake about.

:::checkpoint Your turn. Your brother tells you his band has split up after six years. Put each reply in the low, moderate or high band before you open this. (a) "Six years. That's a lot to lose, and it sounds like it wasn't your choice." (b) "Bands split up all the time. You shouldn't be this down about it." (c) "What happened? Was it the tour?" (d) "Come and watch the match, it'll take your mind off it."

(a) High: it acknowledges what he has lost and guesses, tentatively, at part of why it hurts (level 7 or 8).

(b) Low: it challenges whether his feeling is legitimate (level 2).

(c) Moderate: questions about the event (level 6), and a good way to find out more.

(d) Moderate by label, as a distraction (level 4), but in the 2018 study that level behaved like the low band.

If you put (d) in the middle band, that is what the scheme says. The point of the study is that its effect did not match its label.
:::

### "It'll be fine"

Reassurance comes easily, and several traditions agree it ranks low. Lesson 8 met Rogers and Farson's warning that even "I'm sure everything
will work out O.K." can land as an attempt to steer the speaker.[10] Gordon's list of twelve
"roadblocks" to listening, which lesson 6 met, as a US government counselling manual, SAMHSA's TIP 35, reproduces it,
puts "Reassuring, sympathizing, or consoling" tenth, and explains: "sympathy is not the same as
empathy. Such reassurance can interrupt the flow of communication and interfere with careful
listening."[11] That is **practitioner advice**, written for substance-use counsellors, and no study
this course found tests the twelve as a set. Jones's level-4 result is the nearest thing to
evidence, from strangers in a lab.[1]

The 988 Lifeline, the US crisis line, adds a useful line in its advice for talking with someone who
may be suicidal: "Offer hope that alternatives are available but do not offer glib reassurance."[15]
That is advice for a crisis, and lesson 11 takes that setting up. The distinction carries, though,
on **this course's reading**: hope that is grounded in something ("you got through the last
restructuring; you know people in the trade") answers what the person said, while "it'll be fine"
closes it down.

One experiment later in this lesson complicates the picture. In it, a woman student waiting to
give a speech heard a fellow student say she had nothing to worry about and would do fine. When that
was said to the experimenter, in her hearing, her distress rose less than when the same words were
said to her face.[9] The words were the same both times; what differed was whether they came addressed
as help.

## Advice: hold it back, or give it well?

Should you give advice when someone tells you about a problem? The listening professions and the
advice researchers answer differently, at least at first sight. Each side follows at its strongest,
then where they meet.

### The case for holding back

The helping traditions lean against advice. Rogers and Farson, in their 1957 booklet for managers,
gave their reason: advice and information "are almost always seen as efforts to change a person",
and so get in the way of the speaker saying what they mean. They added that "advice is seldom taken,
and information hardly ever utilized", as an assertion rather than a finding.[10] Gordon's roadblock number 3, as TIP 35 gives it, is "Giving advice, making
suggestions, or providing solutions prematurely or when unsolicited", recognisable because such
advice often begins "What I would do is."[11] The manual adds, of all twelve: "However well
intentioned, these roadblocks to listening shift the focus of the conversation from the client to
the counselor."[11] Samaritans' listening advice, which lesson 4 quoted, includes not offering
advice and not trying to solve the person's problems,[14] and the motivational interviewing trainers
tell practitioners to "refrain from unsolicited advice, confronting, instructing, directing, or
warning".[13] All of this is **practitioner advice**, from people whose work is listening to others
in difficulty.

Part of their case is about evidence, and part, on **this course's reading**, is a value: that the
person with the problem should find their own way through it, and that a listener's job is to make
room for that. No study settles the second part.

Their case has research behind it, too. A review by Erina MacGeorge, Bo Feng and Lisa Guntzviller
sets out the account at the centre of communication research on advice, which began with
Goldsmith's work: advice
can threaten "negative face" when it feels like "butting in", and "positive face" when it seems to
imply "a lack of knowledge or competence".[4] Face, here, is the public self-image a person wants to
keep. That is the authors' account of why advice stings, and later in this lesson a set of
experiments isolates something very like it.

### The case for advice done well

The advice researchers do not say advice is bad. They say it depends on how and when. In the same
review: "Face concerns become particularly salient when advice is unsolicited", which "predicts
poorer evaluations", while polite, softened advice is received better than blunt advice, and advice
is rated higher and more likely to be used when the action it recommends seems effective, feasible
and free of big drawbacks.[4]

One study tested that in real conversations between friends. [MacGeorge and three colleagues](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0824p98f/qt0824p98f.pdf)
had students at a large Midwestern US university each bring a friend to the lab, and one of the pair
talked for up to 15 minutes about the most serious current problem they had not yet discussed with
that friend.[5] The friend was not told to give advice. Of 359 pairs, 244 of the people with the
problem said they got some, and those 244 were the ones analysed.[5] Whether they meant to act on
it went with its content: whether the advice seemed to work and was feasible. Whether it seemed
good advice, and whether it helped them cope, went with how politely it came. Who the friend was
(how expert, trusted, liked or similar) had no direct effect on any outcome, only indirect ones
through how the advice itself came across.[5] It is a **single
study** of US college students, and the authors note that "an unexpectedly large percentage" of the
people with a problem did not report getting any advice at all.[5]

Lesson 9 described the other result the advice researchers point to. In Weger and colleagues' 2014
experiment with US undergraduates talking to strangers, people given advice about the weekend or a
disappointment were no less satisfied, and liked their partner no less, than people given active
listening, though active listening made them feel more understood.[7] The authors' reading was that "people respond to listeners' overall level of
responsiveness regardless of the form it takes".[7]

Even the motivational interviewing tradition has moved. Miller and Rollnick's summary of their
2023 fourth edition lists, among its emphases, "Offering information and advice in an MI-consistent
way, recognizing that these are not the opposite of MI".[12]

### Does the order matter?

Bo Feng tested a sequence: emotional support first, then questions to understand the problem, then
advice. [A 2014 study](https://escholarship.org/content/qt5q97d0hj/qt5q97d0hj.pdf) had 572 US
college students and 540 college students at a university in northern China each read one
transcript of a conversation in which a friend helped them with a problem, either failing an
important exam or being underpaid at a part-time job.[6] The transcripts ran through eleven
different orders of support, questions and advice, with the advice itself the same in all of them.

:::predict Before reading on: of orders such as advice first, advice alone, or support then questions then advice, which do you think readers rated highest, and did the answer differ between the US and Chinese students?

Support, then questions, then advice was rated highest in quality and most likely to be followed, in both groups.[6]

The US students rated advice that came with support or questions somewhat higher than the Chinese students did. On how likely they were to follow the advice, the two groups didn't differ, and Feng's summary is that the sequence worked "very similarly" in both. Feng calls the differences "small in magnitude", and warns against reading much into them.[6]
:::

Feng is careful about the limits. The winning order's "supremacy was relatively small"; the advice
in both scenarios had been asked for, since the person in the transcript said they did not know what
to do; the problems were modest ones; and imagined responses "may differ from their responses to
those messages in real-life supportive interactions".[6] Feng also notes that groups can differ in
how they use rating scales, which is one more reason not to read the country comparison as a
difference in how supportive anyone is. It is a **single study**, in vignettes (written scenarios people imagine themselves in), of
college students.

Put the order beside the 70% figure from the start of the lesson and you have the advice
researchers' practical point: in their studies people tended to advise early, and advice was
rated better when it came later. That 70% is
itself from a pair of studies in which people responded to imagined complaints from friends, and
this course read only the abstract.[3]

### Where the two sides meet

This is a contested question, but less contested than its slogans. This course classes it as
**largely established** that timing, whether advice was asked for, and politeness matter; whether
to advise in a particular case is a judgement.[4][6] Look at the qualifiers in two of the hold-back
texts: TIP 35's "prematurely or when unsolicited" and MINT's "refrain from unsolicited advice".
Samaritans' advice for someone who is struggling carries no such qualifier, and of the three its
setting is the closest to this lesson's subject.[11][13][14] And look at where the research's best-rated
advice sits: after support and questions. **This course's reading** is that the two positions agree
more than their slogans suggest, and differ most where the person is in distress, which is where the
advice studies say least: Feng's advice had been asked for and the problems were modest ones, and
none of the studies here was of people in crisis. The listening traditions start from holding back,
the advice researchers from giving advice well.

What would settle the rest is experiments in live conversations about real problems, varying when
advice comes and whether it was asked for, and measuring how the person coped afterwards. Most of
the sequencing evidence is from vignettes, and this course found no such trial.

## Ask, offer, ask

Motivational interviewing (MI), the clinical counselling method lesson 8 introduced, has a name for
the pull you felt at the top of this lesson. Miller and Rollnick called it the righting reflex, and
in their fourth edition renamed it the **fixing reflex**.[12] TIP 35 describes it, citing their third
edition, as "the natural response to “fix” a person's problems from a desire to help".[11] The MI
trainers' network places MI itself as "a guiding style of communication, that sits between following
(good listening) and directing (giving information and advice)".[13]

For the moments when information or advice is wanted, MI uses a three-step structure the fourth
edition calls **Ask-Offer-Ask**, formerly Elicit-Provide-Elicit.[12] The trainers describe it as
offering information within open questions "that first explores what the person already knows, then
seeks permission to offer what the practitioner knows and then explores the person's response."[13]
VitalTalk, which lesson 8 met, teaches the same shape to clinicians as "ask-tell-ask".[16]

This is **practitioner advice**, built for clinicians working with a client towards a specific
change, and lesson 8 said why its steering does not belong in a friendship. What transfers, on this
course's reading, is the shape: find out what they think, offer only if they want it, then hear what
they make of it.

### Twenty minutes later

Back to Thaddeus. You've been talking for twenty minutes, mostly him. Then he says, "Honestly, what
would you do?"

Now advice has been asked for, which changes things. This is Ask-Offer-Ask in a few lines:

- **Ask.** "I've got a thought, but what have you been thinking of doing?" He says he is tempted to
  take the first job that comes up, so the gap does not scare him.
- **Offer**, with his agreement. "Can I tell you what I'd look at?" He nods. "If it were me, I'd
  check how many weeks the redundancy pay covers before grabbing the first thing. But you know your
  money better than I do."
- **Ask.** "How does that sit with you?"

The advice itself follows what the MacGeorge study found people said they meant to act on: it is feasible, it does not
carry obvious drawbacks, and it is put politely, leaving the decision where it belongs.[5] If he
says, "No, I need something coming in," the last step has done its job. You have learned something
about his situation that your advice did not account for.

Charles Duhigg's popular book *Supercommunicators*, whose three kinds of conversation lesson 3 met,
includes, in its prologue, the author's own version of Thaddeus's evening. When a colleague told him their
suggestions were being ignored, he offered fixes: "They had told me they needed empathy, but rather
than listen, I replied with solutions."[17] His frame of practical, emotional and social
conversations is a heuristic for asking which one you are in, not a finding.

:::checkpoint Rearrange this reply into Ask-Offer-Ask. A friend has asked what you think about her plan to move back in with her parents to save money, and you have answered: "You should definitely do it, you'll save loads. Just set some rules first."

One version:

Ask: "What's pulling you each way?"

Offer, if she wants it: "Would it help to hear what I'd think about? From the outside, the money sounds worth it, and the thing I'd sort out first is what the house rules would be."

Ask: "Does that match what you're worried about, or is it something else?"

Your wording will differ. The check is that her view comes first, your view comes with her agreement, and the last word is a question.
:::

## Help that doesn't announce itself

Some of the most interesting research on support is about help that goes wrong because it is
noticed. It comes from Niall Bolger's lab, and counts as **one group's programme**: two papers, one
a diary study and one a set of experiments.

In the first, 68 couples in which one partner was preparing for the New York State Bar Examination
kept daily diaries for the weeks before it.[8] On days near the exam when the partner reported
giving emotional support, the examinee's depression rose less by the next day; on days the examinee
reported receiving support, it rose more. Support the partner gave and the examinee didn't notice
was the best combination, though that last comparison fell just short of statistical
significance.[8] That is a correlation from diaries, and
it raised the question the experiments tried to answer.

[Bolger and David Amarel](http://www.columbia.edu/~nb2229/docs/bolger-amarel-support-experiments-2006.pdf)
ran three experiments, 257 participants in all, every one a woman taking introductory psychology
at New York University; they restricted the studies "to female participants and female peer
confederates".[9] Each participant was told she would have to give a speech and given a few minutes
to practise. Then a fellow participant, secretly a confederate, said something helpful. In the first
and third experiments it was a common public-speaking tip. In the second it was reassurance: that she
had "nothing to worry about, that she would do fine." In the visible condition the words were
addressed to her. In the invisible one they were addressed to the experimenter, in her hearing. The
second and third experiments also had a group who got no support at all.

Invisible support reduced distress compared with visible support, and visible support "was
either ineffective or it exacerbated reactivity" (reactivity here means the rise in distress).[9]
Against no support, invisible support came out ahead in both experiments that tested it, but in
neither did the difference reach statistical significance.[9]

:::predict Why would the same words help less when they were said to you than when they were said near you? Write your guess before you open this.

The third experiment separated the parts of the message. The authors' conclusion: "invisibility per se is not the essence of what makes invisible support effective; rather, it is (at least in part) avoiding the communication that the support recipient is inefficacious."[9]

In other words, it was not being hidden that mattered. It was not being told, by the way the help was given, that you couldn't manage.
:::

That mechanism, tested in their third experiment rather than supposed, is the authors' own, and it is hedged in their own words
("at least in part"). Notice how close it is to the face threat the advice researchers describe:
advice that implies "a lack of knowledge or competence".[4][9] **This course's reading** is that the
two literatures are describing the same risk from different sides.

The limits matter. These were women students anticipating a speech, with a stranger's tip or
reassurance, in a lab. Later research on when visible support does better exists, and this course has not read it.
"Invisible" in these studies did not mean secret; the participant heard every word. It meant help
that did not come labelled as help for someone who could not cope.

### Clementine's exam

Clementine, your flatmate, has a final exam tomorrow and is pacing the kitchen. Two ways to help:

- "You're obviously not coping. Sit down, I'll make you a timetable and test you on everything."
- Making dinner for both of you ("I'm cooking anyway"), and, when she mentions she cannot remember
  the dates, saying the thing that helped you before your own exams: "I always ended up just
  writing out the ten I kept forgetting."

The first is generous and visible, and it says, in so many words, that she cannot manage. The second
gives similar help without that message. Carrying Bolger and Amarel's finding from a speech in a lab
to an exam in a flat is **this course's reading**, and two cautions go with it. If she asks you
directly for help, give it plainly; hiding help someone has asked for is not what the studies tested.
And none of this is a licence for managing people without their knowledge. The support in the
experiments was out in the open; only the label was missing.

:::checkpoint In a team meeting, a manager says to a new starter, in front of everyone: "Don't worry, I'll redo your slides tonight. I know this is all a bit much for you." Using Bolger and Amarel's account, what might make this help backfire, and how could the same help be offered?

The help itself, a better set of slides, is not the problem. The message around it is: in front of others, it says the new starter cannot cope ("a bit much for you"), which is the communication of inefficacy the experiments isolated, and it may also threaten face in the advice researchers' sense.

A version without that message: privately, "Want to go through the slides together before Thursday? I always get someone to look over mine." It offers the same help, asks first, and puts both people in the same position. That it would work better in an office is this course's reading, not something the lab studies tested.
:::

## Your story, or theirs?

Lesson 4, "How listening goes wrong", left a question open: is telling a story of your own a failure
of listening? It is **contested**, and that lesson's reading was that it probably turns on whether
the speaker is in distress. When someone brings you a problem, they often are, and the sources
this course read lean one way.

Samaritans' advice, as lesson 4 quoted, includes not trying to identify what the person is going
through with your own experiences.[14] VitalTalk's advice to clinicians, on statements that show they
understand, is to "stop short of suggesting you understand everything (you don't)".[16] It isn't
about second stories, but it names the claim a matching story often makes. And in the 2018 conversation
study, the confederates who had been trained to give low comfort "tended to accomplish this goal by
engaging in prolonged narratives about their own experiences which have nothing to do with
disclosers' emotional experiences."[1] That last is an observation from the coding of actors told to
comfort badly, not a test of what happens when a friend tells a story.

The other side has not gone away. The fullest statement of it this course read is Huang and
colleagues' discussion: people "do not want to talk about themselves indefinitely without
reciprocity".[20] Headlee herself, as lesson 4 showed, allows a turn to yourself if the focus shifts
back. Their setting is ordinary sociable talk, and nobody in what this course read has tested a
second story against a support response with a person in distress. That is the study that would
settle it: second stories and support responses coded in real conversations, with the first
speaker's sense of being understood measured separately for distress and for ordinary talk.

### Feeling it with them

A related temptation is to feel it with them: to match their fear or grief with your own. Lesson 7,
"Ask, don't guess", set out the argument between Paul Bloom and Jamil Zaki about empathy. On this one
point, support for someone in distress, they largely agree. Bloom writes of going to someone you
love when you are anxious or sad: "Here, it is understanding and compassion we need, not
empathy".[18] He concedes the other side in the same box: "sometimes people really do want others to
feel what they feel."[18] Zaki says it's "just as important to know when to turn down one's
empathy", and gives his wife's work as a clinical psychologist as the example: "the last thing that
any of her patients need if they're depressed, is for her to be depressed as well."[19] Their
disagreement, as lesson 7 showed, is about whether empathy chosen well is a good guide to moral
decisions, and this lesson does not reopen it.

**This course's reading** of the two: caring about the person, and understanding what they feel,
are not the same as feeling it too, and both authors think the first two are what a friend in
trouble mostly needs.

## What people get wrong

**"Never give advice."** The advice studies found advice that is asked for, feasible, polite and
placed after support tends to be welcome, and in one experiment advice left US students no less satisfied
than active listening did.[5][6][7] The counselling manual's own wording is "prematurely or when
unsolicited", not never. Samaritans do say not to offer advice, but to someone who is struggling, not
as a rule for every conversation.[11][14]

**"The kindest thing is to solve it for them."** Unsolicited advice predicts poorer evaluations, and
advice can imply the person could not have worked it out themselves, which is the risk the invisible
support experiments isolated.[4][9]

**"Reassurance is supportive."** "It'll all work out" sits in a middle level of the comfort scale by
its label, and behaved like the bottom levels in the one study that coded real conversations.
Counselling and crisis-line advice agree. Bolger and Amarel's second experiment adds a twist: the same
reassurance went with less distress when it was said about the person than when it was said to
her.[1][9][11][15]

**"The best support is visible and generous."** In Bolger and Amarel's experiments, support that
announced itself was ineffective or made distress worse, and support that did not signal the person
could not cope did better.[9]

**"I know exactly how you feel."** VitalTalk tells clinicians to stop short of claiming it,
because you don't.[16]

**"More empathy is always better support."** Bloom argues against sharing the distress in
particular, and Zaki says there are times to turn empathy down; on support they largely agree.[18][19]

**"A matched story always helps," or "always hurts."** Neither has been tested head to head, and the
answer probably turns on whether the person is in distress, which is this course's reading.[1][20]

## Practice

:::exercise Six replies to rewrite
This is a **rewrite on paper** exercise. Take 15 minutes. Each line is something a person has just told you, followed by a reply that sits low on the comfort scale or blocks the conversation in another way. For each, name what the reply does (blames, dismisses, distracts, reassures, advises too early, or takes the floor), then write a better first reply.

- (a) "My cat died this morning." Reply: "Well, she was very old. You can always get a kitten."
- (b) "I got passed over for the team lead job again." Reply: "You'll get it next time. Don't stress."
- (c) "My sister isn't speaking to me after the party." Reply: "What did you do to her this time?"
- (d) "Our holiday's cancelled. The airline's gone bust." Reply: "The same thing happened to us once, and we lost the lot. It was a nightmare, we had to..."
- (e) "The house we were buying has fallen through." Reply: "Honestly, it's only a house. There'll be others."
- (f) "My boss criticised my report in front of everyone." Reply: "What you need to do is email HR today."
:::

:::checkpoint Compare yours with these. Your wording will differ; check that yours keeps the talk on them and names or asks about the feeling before anything else.

(a) Dismisses and distracts ("very old", "get a kitten"). Better: "Oh, I'm so sorry. You'd had her such a long time."

(b) Reassures with nothing behind it. Better: "Again? That must really sting. What did they say?"

(c) Blames. Better: "That sounds awful, especially with your sister. What happened?"

(d) Takes the floor with a second story. Better: "Oh no. Have you lost the money as well?" Your own story can wait, and may not be needed at all.

(e) Challenges whether the feeling is legitimate. Better: "That's gutting, after all the work of getting that far. How are you both doing?"

(f) Advises before asking, and fast. Better: "In front of everyone? That's horrible. What did she say?" If advice is wanted later, Ask-Offer-Ask.
:::

:::exercise One held impulse
This is a **try one move** exercise. Take 5 minutes to write it up, within an hour of the conversation.

In the next ordinary conversation where someone tells you about a problem (a broken boiler, a difficult week, a disagreement at work; not a crisis), notice the first impulse to fix it. Hold it until you have asked one question about how they see it. Then write in your journal what the impulse was, what you asked, and what happened, including if nothing did. Name nobody and write nothing that would identify them. If the conversation turns out to be about something serious, stop practising and use lesson 11.
:::

## For your journal

This lesson adds a column: when someone brought a problem, what was your first reply, and where
would it sit on the comfort scale? Note also whether advice came, whether it was asked for, and
whether it came before or after you had asked anything. For the project, "one question before any
advice" is a habit you can practise and measure over a few weeks.
