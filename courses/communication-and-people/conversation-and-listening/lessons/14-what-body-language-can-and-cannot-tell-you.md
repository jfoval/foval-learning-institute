You've probably been told that most of what we communicate is nonverbal, that liars can't look you
in the eye, and that crossed arms mean someone's closed off. Each of these has a real finding
somewhere behind it, and each has been stretched well past what that finding showed. This lesson
traces them back. What comes out is useful: bodies and voices do carry information, mostly about
feelings and relationships, but they tell you far less about honesty or exact emotions than
popular advice claims. And the thing that works better than reading bodies is the thing this course
keeps coming back to. Listen to what people say, and ask.

## Where "93% nonverbal" came from

The figure usually appears as a formula: 7% of meaning comes from words, 38% from tone of voice,
55% from the face. Add the last two and you get "93% nonverbal". Business magazines repeat it. One
Harvard Business Review article on listening, which otherwise reports a large set of workplace
ratings, says in passing, "It is estimated that 80% of what we communicate comes from these
signals", and gives no source.[3]

:::predict Before reading on: what kind of study do you think produced "7% words, 38% tone, 55% face"? What were people doing, and what were they judging?

Two small laboratory studies from 1967, using single spoken words and posed photographs of faces. People judged how much a speaker seemed to like or dislike someone, in cases where the word said one thing and the tone or the face said another.[1]

Nobody was having a conversation, and nobody was trying to follow an argument, a set of directions or a story.
:::

The researcher was Albert Mehrabian, and he has said on [his own web page](http://www.kaaj.com/psych/smorder.html) what the numbers mean. He
gives the formula as "Total Liking = 7% Verbal Liking + 38% Vocal Liking + 55% Facial Liking", and
explains that it and his other equations "were derived from experiments dealing with communications
of feelings and attitudes (i.e., like-dislike)". Then comes the sentence that matters: "Unless a
communicator is talking about their feelings or attitudes, these equations are not applicable."[1]

So the author limits his own finding. It's about one narrow thing, judging whether someone likes you
when their word and their tone disagree. This course hasn't read the two 1967 papers themselves,
which are behind a paywall, so it won't give their sample sizes or their arithmetic. A communication
scholar, David Lapakko, wrote a 1997 critique of how the study gets used; this course read only its
abstract, which says the popular applications "overlook important limitations which do not warrant
formulation of a precise numerical formula".[2]

Lesson 3, "Hearing is not listening", traced the "45% of our time listening" figure back through a
chain of retellings. This is the same shape: a real finding, narrow and carefully limited by its
author, turned into a claim about all human communication.

### Two sentences, one formula

First, a station announcer: "Your train leaves at 6:10 from platform 4." If 93% of the meaning were
in tone and face, you could hear this in a language you don't speak and still catch most of it.
Obviously you can't. There's no feeling or attitude in the sentence for tone to carry, which is
exactly the case Mehrabian says his equations don't cover.

Second, a friend. You ask Solveig how the new job is going, and she says "Fine" in a flat, low voice
without looking up. Here the word and the tone disagree about a feeling, which is the one situation
Mehrabian's studies were about. So is the tone the truth and the word a cover?

Maybe. But look at what the studies were. People in a lab judged single recorded words and posed
photographs of strangers, and they judged liking, not tiredness or worry or boredom. A flat "Fine"
from a friend could mean the job is going badly, or that she's exhausted, or that she doesn't want
to talk about it here. Lesson 7, "Ask, don't guess", showed that imagining someone's position didn't
make people more accurate about what they thought, and that asking did. So on this course's reading,
the move is the same even in Mehrabian's own case: "You said fine, but you sound flat. How's it
really going?" The tone told you there might be something to ask about. It didn't tell you what.

(Lesson 7 also described the illusion of transparency: people hiding a feeling from strangers in a
lab thought more of it showed than did. Whether Solveig, half-showing hers to a friend, thinks it
shows more than it does is a different situation, and applying that finding to her would be this
course's reading, not something those studies tested.)

## Can you tell when someone is lying?

Hundreds of studies have asked this, so the answer is unusually firm.

:::predict Two questions. In studies where people watched or listened to someone and judged whether they were lying or telling the truth, what share did they get right on average? And did police officers, judges and other professionals do better than everyone else?

About 54%, where guessing would give 50%.[4]

And no. Professionals did no better on average than anyone else, and averaged under 55%.[4]
:::

Those figures come from Charles Bond and Bella DePaulo's 2006 meta-analysis, which pooled 206
documents reporting the judgements of 24,483 people trying to tell lies from truths told by 4,435
others. The judges worked in real time, with no special aids and no training.[4] That makes it a
**replicated** finding in the strongest sense this course uses: a meta-analysis of hundreds of
studies. (This course read the paper from a scanned copy with some character errors, so its figures
are given here as paraphrase, not quotation.)

- **People lean towards belief.** Truths were judged correctly 61.34% of the time, lies only 47.55%.
  Judges called more statements true than false, which the authors call a truth bias.[4]
- **Results were strikingly consistent.** From study to study, the true spread in average accuracy
  (its standard deviation) was only 4.52 percentage points. Nearly all studies found a little better than a
  coin.[4]
- **Watching was the worst way to judge.** Judgements from silent video, where you could see the
  person but not hear them, were less accurate than judgements from audio, from audio and video
  together, or from a written transcript.[4]
- **Experts were no better.** In 19 studies that compared professionals (police, judges,
  psychiatrists, job interviewers, auditors) with lay people, the difference was d = -.025, close
  to zero, with a confidence interval running from -.105 to .055.[4]

A follow-up meta-analysis by the same authors asked whether some individuals are much better than
others. This course read only its abstract, which reports 247 samples and says that once measurement
error is allowed for, "the best judges are no more accurate than a stochastic mechanism would
produce", that is, no more accurate than chance variation would make some people look.[5] The
abstract adds that people differ more in how readily they believe others, and that the person being
judged matters more: some people look credible whether they're lying or not.[5]

### What liars' bodies actually do

Bella DePaulo and five colleagues pooled 1,338 estimates of 158 possible cues to lying, from 120
samples, in 2003.[6] Their summary: "many behaviors showed no discernible links, or only weak links,
to deceit". Across the 88 cues with enough data, the median difference between liars and truth
tellers was d = 0.10, and only two cues reached 0.50.[6]

Eye contact was one of the best-measured: 32 estimates from 1,491 people, with a difference of
d = 0.01. Gaze aversion, looking away, had fewer data, 6 estimates from 411 people, and a difference
of d = 0.03.[6] In their words: "none of the measures of looking behavior supported the widespread
belief that liars do not look their targets in the eye".[6]

To see how small that is, use the table from Mental Fitness lesson 2, "What a small effect is, and
what the other group got", where an effect of 0.2 puts the average person in one group ahead of
about 58% of the other. On that arithmetic, which is this course's calculation on the normal curve
and not a figure from the paper, d = 0.03 puts the average liar ahead of barely more than half of
truth tellers on looking away, where no difference at all would be 50%.

Some cues did differ a little. Liars were somewhat less forthcoming and more tense, their pupils
were somewhat more dilated (d = 0.39, from only four estimates), their voices slightly higher
(d = 0.21), and they were rated a little more nervous (d = 0.27). The cues were stronger when liars
were motivated, especially when the lie touched their identity, and low-stakes lies left almost no
trace.[6] So the honest summary isn't "the body shows nothing". It's that the differences are small,
and the famous one, the eyes, is essentially absent.

*The four signs most often named when 2,320 people in 58 countries (40 per country, mostly students)
were asked "How can you tell when people are lying?" The Global Deception Research Team, "A world of
lies", Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology, 2006, Study 1. Drawn by this course from the figures in
the paper.[7]*

That chart is the belief side. An international team of researchers, calling themselves the
[Global Deception Research Team](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2957901/), asked people in
58 countries how you can tell when someone is lying. Gaze aversion came top in 51 of the 58, and in
a second survey across 63 countries, respondents in 61 more often said liars decrease eye contact
than increase it. Across the two studies, 75 countries and 43 languages, the belief "was expressed in
every one of the 75 countries we studied".[7] The same paper notes that gaze aversion "was shown to
have little association with lying".[7]

Put the chart beside DePaulo's d = 0.03 and you have a wide gap between a belief found in
every country surveyed and what the measurements show. A 2023 survey of 50 active researchers
on deception cues found them disagreeing about most cues, but "The single issue on which more than
80% of experts agreed was that gaze aversion is not generally diagnostic of deception".[9]

### Weak signals, not bad observers

Why are people so poor at this? The natural guess is that they watch the wrong things, like the eyes.
Maria Hartwig and Charles Bond tested that guess in 2011, in four meta-analyses covering 66 cues in
153 samples. This course read only the abstract. Their conclusion is that people "rarely rely on
the wrong cues": the cues people actually use and the cues that actually differ between liars and
truth tellers line up closely. What limits accuracy, they write, is "weaknesses in behavioral cues to
deception".[8]

That's the mechanism this lesson rests on, and it's theirs: you can't read a signal that's barely
there. (One puzzle remains. People *say* they rely on gaze, as the chart shows, but in Hartwig and
Bond's abstract eye contact correlated only weakly with actual lie judgements, r = -.15.[8])

### Eyes up and to the right, microexpressions, and training

Three popular claims go further than "watch the eyes", and each has been tested.

**Eye direction.** Some neuro-linguistic programming (NLP) trainers teach that a right-handed person
looking up and to the right is constructing something, and so may be lying. Richard Wiseman and
colleagues [tested it three ways](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0040259)
in 2012. Liars and truth tellers in filmed interviews moved their eyes no differently; people taught the NLP
pattern judged clips no better than people who weren't (16.33 against 16.59 correct out of 32); and
in 52 real televised appeals for missing relatives, half of them later shown to be lies, glances
didn't differ.[11] Three small studies from one team, all null. Vrij and colleagues'
review notes that NLP's founders never claimed eye movements reveal lies.[10]

**Microexpressions.** The idea is that a hidden feeling leaks out in a flash across the face, too
fast for most people to notice. Stephen Porter and Leanne ten Brinke tested it in 2008, coding
104,550 video frames of 697 expressions in which people showed genuine or faked emotions to
pictures. This course read only the abstract. Some inconsistent emotional leakage showed up in every
participant at least once, but "Microexpressions were exhibited by 21.95% of participants in 2% of
all expressions, and in the upper or lower face only".[12] Aldert Vrij, Maria Hartwig and Pär Anders
Granhag's 2019 review describes that study as "the first and, to date, only published experiment" on
microexpressions and deception, and reports that six of the fourteen microexpressions were shown by
people telling the truth.[10] Rare, and not specific to liars.

**Training.** Paul Ekman's Micro-Expressions Training Tool (METT) is sold to teach people to spot
them. Jordan and colleagues randomly assigned 90 people to METT, to a bogus training designed
to look similar, or to no training, and then had each judge five videos. This course read only the
abstract: METT did no better than the other two groups, and "overall accuracy was slightly below
chance".[13] That's a single small study with few judgements per person. But notice the comparison,
which lesson 9, "Does active listening work?", taught you to ask about. Beating no training could
come from people simply paying more attention. Beating a bogus training is the real test, and METT
didn't.

### When professionals use the cues

Behavioural cues have been built into police interviewing and airport security, and the people who
designed those systems have their own case. Here it is in their words first.

**The Reid technique.** The Behavior Analysis Interview is the first step of the Reid technique, an
interrogation method in which, [Vincent Denault and colleagues](https://journals.copmadrid.org/apj/art/apj2019a9) report, "more than 500,000 people have
reportedly been trained".[14] Its creators, John E. Reid & Associates, describe the interview as
"designed to identify whether or not a person is telling the truth or withholding relevant information
concerning a specific crime or act of wrongdoing".[14] The technique's manual (Inbau, Reid, Buckley
and Jayne, 2013), as Denault and colleagues quote it, says that "generally speaking, a suspect who
does not make direct eye contact is probably withholding information". The same manual, they report,
cautions that a lack of eye contact may come from other things, including the suspect's culture.[14]
Denault and colleagues, writing in a legal psychology journal, answer that
research "has even shown that it is not a valid sign of lying".[14] This course read Denault and
colleagues' paper in full; it has not read the Reid manual itself.

**SPOT.** From 2007, the US Transportation Security Administration trained behaviour detection
officers to watch passengers in airport queues, about 30 seconds each, for indicators that
included "avoiding eye contact, looking down", in a programme called Screening of Passengers by
Observation Techniques.[14] In 2013 the US Government Accountability Office (GAO), Congress's
auditor, [reviewed](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-14-159) four meta-analyses covering "over 400 studies from the past 60 years" and found
"that the human ability to accurately identify deceptive behavior based on behavioral indicators is
the same as or slightly better than chance".[15] It recommended that Congress consider "the absence
of scientifically validated evidence" when funding the programme.[15]

The Department of Homeland Security disagreed, and GAO's report sets out its reasons. According to
GAO, DHS argued that the published research "lacked ecological and external validity" because none
of it was about airports, that its own research, unpublished for security reasons, supported
behaviour detection, and that SPOT aimed to spot signs of fear or stress rather than lies as such.
GAO answered each point and kept its conclusion.[15] This course hasn't read DHS's letter itself,
which the report prints only as page images.

On this course's reading, what would settle the practitioners' case is evidence of the kind DHS's
objection points to: high-stakes field studies, in real settings, where the truth is later known,
comparing trained and untrained observers. Until then, demeanour-only lie detection is about as close
to settled as this field gets: poor. GAO's report adds
a practical warning, in its summary of a 2010 review this course hasn't read: behaviour natural to
one culture "may appear suspicious" to members of another.[15] Lesson 15, "Different habits:
culture, autism, age and hearing", picks that up: where a listener is expected to look differs
between communities.

### The candidate who looked away

Tancredi is interviewing for a job on his team. One candidate, Lazlo, keeps glancing away when asked
about a project on his CV, and Tancredi comes out sure he was lying about it.

Start with the evidence: looking away barely differed between liars and truth tellers (d = 0.03),
people judging lies average about 54%, and professionals, job interviewers among them, do no
better.[4][6]

Now add a second problem, the one Logic and Argument lesson 6, "How much should the evidence move
you", taught with natural frequencies: how common is the thing you're looking for? Suppose, with
numbers invented by this course for the arithmetic, that across 1,000 interviews like this one,
1 candidate in 10 lies about something that matters, and that looking away is equally common in liars
and truth tellers, 30 in every 100 of each.

:::checkpoint Count it before opening. Of the candidates who look away, how many are lying, out of how many? And what share is that?

100 candidates lie and 900 don't. Of the 100 liars, 30 look away. Of the 900 truth tellers, 270 look away. So 300 candidates look away, and 30 of them are lying: 30 in 300, which is 10%.

That's exactly the share of liars you started with. A cue that's equally common in both groups tells you nothing, so seeing it leaves you where you began.
:::

Now be generous to Tancredi. Suppose looking away were a much better cue than any study has found:
45 in every 100 liars do it, against 30 in every 100 truth tellers. Then 45 liars and 270 truth
tellers look away, and 45 of 315 is about 14%. Even with a cue far stronger than the measured one,
six of every seven candidates who look away are telling the truth, because truth tellers are so much
more numerous. The rarer lying is, the more a "lie cue" misleads, which is the base-rate point from
Logic and Argument turned on a person.

What would help Tancredi is in what Lazlo says. On this course's reading of the findings below, the
useful move is to ask for checkable detail (which tool, who else worked on it, what went wrong) and
then check with a reference. This course teaches no interview method. The point is narrower: where
there's anything to find, it's more likely in the content of an answer than in the eyes.

### What does better

The field's positive advice has moved from watching bodies to listening to words.[10]

- In Vrij, Hartwig and Granhag's review, 35 of 50 well-studied cues were at least partly nonverbal,
  and 25 of those 35 showed no relation to lying. Verbal cues did better, averaging d = 0.30, with 8
  of 18 related to deception.[10] On Mental Fitness lesson 2's table, which again is this course's
  arithmetic, 0.3 puts the average liar ahead of about 62% of truth tellers on such a cue. Better
  than the eyes, and still far from a lie detector.
- A meta-analysis of 30 training studies by Valerie Hauch and colleagues, read here only as an
  abstract, found a small-to-medium effect of training overall (g = 0.331) and "larger training
  effects if the training was based on verbal content cues".[16]
- Timothy Levine's truth-default theory, read here only at its abstract, "emphasizes contextualized
  communication content in deception detection over nonverbal behaviors", and holds that believing
  people by default is usually sensible.[17]

How much better content and questioning can do is **contested**. Levine's programme reports high
accuracy with expert questioning, but this course hasn't read those studies. That verbal and
interview approaches beat watching the body is the consistent direction; the size is argued.

## Can you read a feeling from a face?

This is a live dispute between serious researchers, and the course won't settle it. The psychology
of emotion belongs to Understanding People, a later course in this term; here the question is only
the one a listener asks: when I see a face, how much does it tell me about what this person feels?

**The basic-emotion position.** Dacher Keltner, Disa Sauter, Jessica Tracy and Alan Cowen, in a 2019
review, argue that "upwards of 20 emotions are signaled in multimodal and dynamic patterns of
expressive behavior", and that recognising them is shaped by
context.[19] This course read the abstract and searched the full text. Their best evidence includes
a 2002 meta-analysis by Hillary Elfenbein and Nalini Ambady finding that emotions are recognised "at
better-than-chance levels" across cultures, with an in-group advantage that shrinks with exposure,[20]
and a 2021 study by Cowen and colleagues that used machine learning on 6 million videos from 144
countries and found 16 kinds of expression whose links to contexts were "70% preserved across 12
world regions".[21] Both read here as abstracts.

**The constructionist position.** Lisa Feldman Barrett and four colleagues, in a
[2019 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6640856/),
target what they call the common view: that each emotion has a standard face you can read. They
agree that people do scowl when angry, smile when happy and so on "more than what would be expected
by chance", but with "substantial variation" across cultures, situations and people.[18] They report
a meta-analysis of 47 studies in which the expected facial configuration correlated about r = .32
with measures of the emotion, and appeared in about 19% of emotional events. Hence their line: "a
scowling facial configuration is an expression of anger, but not the expression of anger".[18] Their
best evidence also includes studies in small-scale societies where the usual pattern broke down,
both read here as abstracts: Himba participants sorting photographs of posed faces without emotion words
didn't sort them into the categories the universality view predicts,[22] and Trobriand Island adolescents
read the gasping face that Western studies call fear as anger and threat.[23]

Read that correlation the way Mental Fitness lesson 2 taught: r = .32 is a real relationship
and not a percentage. It doesn't mean faces are right 32% of the time.

**What both sides say.** Faces carry social information, and context changes what they mean. Both
reviews state this.[18][19] The argument is over how much a face says on its own, and how
specifically.

**What would settle it** is large studies that record what people feel and what their faces do at
the same moment, across many small-scale societies, letting people answer freely rather than pick
from a list of emotion words.

:::checkpoint Take a colleague who frowns through a meeting. Using only what both sides agree on, what can you say about the frown, and what can't you?

You can say the frown carries some social information, and that what it means depends on the situation: the meeting, the person, what was just said.

You can't say it means anger, or disagreement, or anything in particular. Barrett's side would say a scowl turns up with anger only some of the time, and anger shows in other ways too; Keltner's side would say expressions come in patterns across face, voice and body, read in context. Neither side licenses reading one feeling off one face.

On this course's reading, the frown is a reason to wonder, and perhaps to ask afterwards, not a conclusion.
:::

## What a glimpse can tell you

Faces aren't useless, and the research on brief glimpses shows it. Nalini Ambady and Robert
Rosenthal's 1992 meta-analysis pooled 38 results in which people predicted real outcomes from
watching someone for under five minutes. The average correlation between the glimpse-based judgement
and the outcome was r = .39. Observations under half a minute predicted about as well as four or five
minutes, and it didn't much matter whether the glimpse was of the face, the body, speech or tone of
voice.[24] That's a **replicated** finding: a moderate relationship, pooled across studies.

It's also easy to overread. A correlation of .39 leaves most of the variation unexplained, and it
isn't "right 39% of the time". The outcomes were specific ones, such as teachers' ratings from their
students, not a verdict on whether someone is honest, kind or competent in general.[24] A first
impression carries some information about some things, which is a long way from "trust your gut
about people".

What about single gestures? Here the scholarly answer is general, because the specific claims mostly
haven't been tested. Denault and colleagues quote Harrigan's chapter in a handbook of nonverbal
research methods: "Unlike certain facial expressions, there are few, if any, body movements that have
invariant meaning within or across cultures".[14] This course found no study that tests whether
crossed arms mean defensiveness. So the fair statement isn't that crossed arms have been disproved.
It's that no gesture has been shown to have one fixed meaning, and crossed arms might mean no more
than a cold room or a comfortable habit. Denault and colleagues give "the body
language never lies" as an example of the dubious ideas they say circulate online, in popular books
and at seminars.[14]

## Mirroring and power poses

The last two claims are the kind you meet in workplace training: copy someone's posture and they'll
like you; stand like a superhero and you'll feel and perform better. Both started from published
experiments, and both have a replication story like the small-talk finding in lesson 12,
"Strangers, small talk and going deeper".

**Mirroring.** In 1999 Tanya Chartrand and John Bargh reported, in experiments read here only at the
abstract, that people unintentionally copy strangers' movements, and that being copied by a
confederate produced smoother interactions and more liking.[25] In 2016 Joanna Hale and Antonia
Hamilton ran a preregistered test with 40 participants talking to avatars in virtual reality, and
being copied didn't raise rapport or trust.[26] Their review the same year says the link from mimicry
to liking "may be fragile", and can break down when the copying is noticed.[27] Whether mirroring
helps is **contested**. Deliberate mirroring has no good support in what this course read, and doing
it covertly would mean using a technique on someone without their knowledge, which this course
doesn't teach.

**Power posing.** In 2010 Dana Carney, Amy Cuddy and Andy Yap reported that "two simple 1-min poses"
in an expansive posture raised testosterone, lowered cortisol, and increased both felt power and
risk-taking, in 42 participants.[28][32] Cuddy's 2012 TED talk made it famous.[32]

Then the replications came.

- In 2015 Eva Ranehill and colleagues tested 200 people, with the experimenter unaware of each
  person's condition: posing raised self-reported feelings of power, but there was "no significant
  effect of power posing on hormonal levels or in any of the three behavioral tasks".[29] The
  original authors replied that the study differed in method, including six-minute poses.[32]
- In 2016 Carney, the first author, published [a statement](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/dana_carney/pdf_My%20position%20on%20power%20poses.pdf): "I do not believe that “power pose”
  effects are real." Among her reasons she listed a tiny sample, effects that were "small and barely there",
  data collected in batches and checked along the way, and experimenters who knew the
  hypothesis.[30]
- Joseph Simmons and Uri Simonsohn analysed the pattern of results in the 33 studies the original
  authors had cited, and found it "indistinguishable from what would be expected if (a) the average
  effect size were zero", with selective reporting explaining the significant ones.[31] Their main
  analysis left out results on felt power, which they treated as a check that the manipulation worked
  rather than as an outcome.[32]
- A journal special issue of seven preregistered studies found, in its summary, "virtually zero
  effect" on behaviour and hormones, but "it is clear that an effect on felt power was observed". This
  course knows it only as quoted in a [British Psychological Society article](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/decade-power-posing-where-do-we-stand).[32]

So the hormone and behaviour claims **failed to replicate**, and that part is settled. Felt power is
where the argument continues, and both sides deserve their own words. Cuddy and two colleagues
published a reply in 2018 that added 21 studies and argued that how people feel is a legitimate
outcome in its own right, reporting strong evidence for effects on felt power. This course hasn't
read that paper; it knows it through the same BPS article, which reports it that way.[32] On the
other side, Marcus Credé pointed out, again as that article reports, that most of those studies had
no neutral posture to compare with, so a difference between expansive and slumped poses could come
from slumping making people feel worse.[32] A 2022 meta-analysis of 73 studies by Emma Elkjær and
colleagues, read here as an abstract, points the same way: slumped against neutral postures showed
an effect of g = 0.45, expansive against neutral only 0.06, and they conclude "it is the absence of
contractive displays rather than the presence of expansive displays that alters affective and
behavioral responding".[33] Cuddy greeted the same meta-analysis differently: "At this point, anyone
who claims these effects are not real cannot support that claim with science. These findings are
vindicating", as the BPS article quotes her.[32] Whether posture changes how powerful you feel is
**contested**; what would settle it is large preregistered studies that include a neutral posture.

## What people get wrong

**"93% of communication is nonverbal."** Its author says his equations apply only when someone is
talking about feelings or attitudes, and they came from single words and posed photographs.[1]

**"Liars avoid eye contact."** Believed in all 75 countries surveyed, and in the largest review eye
contact and gaze aversion barely differed between liars and truth tellers (d = 0.01 and 0.03).[6][7]

**"Looking up and to the right means lying."** Tested three ways, including real televised appeals,
and nothing was found.[11]

**"Police and body language experts can spot liars."** Professionals averaged under 55%, no better
than lay people, and the best individual judges look like chance variation.[4][5]

**"Microexpressions give liars away."** In the only published experiment a 2019 review knew of,
they appeared in 2% of expressions,
and in truth tellers too; a training tool did no better than a bogus one.[10][12][13]

**"So nobody can ever tell a lie from the truth."** That overshoots. Accuracy is a little above
chance, some cues differ slightly, and attention to content and good questions does better, by an
amount researchers still argue about.[4][10][16]

**"Crossed arms mean defensive."** No body movement has been shown to have one fixed meaning, and
this course found no study of crossed arms.[14]

**"A face shows exactly what someone feels."** Both sides of the face debate say context changes
what a face means; they disagree about how much a face says alone.[18][19]

**"Faces tell you nothing, then."** Also wrong on both sides' account: faces carry social
information, emotions are recognised above chance across cultures, and brief glimpses predict some
outcomes moderately.[18][20][24]

**"Mirror their body language and they'll like you."** A preregistered test found no effect, and a
review calls the link fragile.[26][27]

**"Power poses boost your hormones."** They didn't in a study of 200 people, and the first author
no longer believes the effects are real.[29][30]

**"Power posing was debunked, so posture does nothing to how you feel."** Also too strong: an effect
on felt power is still argued, and slumping may matter more than standing tall.[32][33]

## Practice

:::exercise True or false, by coin
This is a **practise with a willing partner** exercise. Take 10 minutes. You need a friend or family member who has agreed to play and knows it's a game about lie detection. Nobody is being tested who doesn't know it.

Before each round, your partner privately flips a coin: heads, they'll tell you something true about themselves; tails, something false. They choose small, harmless things (a food they tried once, a place they've been, a job they did as a teenager), never anything that touches a real secret or that they'd rather not discuss.

They say one statement. You may ask two questions about it, and then you say "true" or "lie". Play twelve rounds. Score how many you got right, and how many of your "lie" calls were right, and for each call note what you relied on: their face, their voice, the content, or the answers to your questions.

Compare your score with 54%, the average in the meta-analysis. Twelve rounds is far too few to show you're better or worse than average, so treat your score as a story, not a measurement. The coin keeps lies and truths about equally common, which is what makes 50% the guessing rate; the classic "two truths and a lie" game doesn't, because there guessing gets you one in three. Swap roles if your partner wants to. Don't use anything you learn to test anyone else without their knowledge.
:::

:::exercise Three claims from the internet
This is an **analyse public talk** exercise, using published material. Take 10 minutes.

Find a popular article or video about body language: "how to tell if someone is lying", "what their posture says", "confident body language for interviews". Pick three specific claims from it. For each, write:

(a) the claim, in one sentence; (b) what this lesson found about it, with the evidence label (replicated, one group's programme, single study, failed to replicate, contested, practitioner advice, or not tested as far as this course found); (c) who was studied, in what setting, and compared with what, if you can tell; and (d) what a careful version of the claim would say.

Don't name or picture any private person in your notes.
:::

## For your journal

This lesson adds one column to the observation journal: what you read from someone's face, voice or
posture, and what you concluded. When you write up a conversation, mark one moment where you read
something from the body, and note whether their words later confirmed it, contradicted it, or never
came up. If you asked, note what you learned. Don't write that anyone was lying, or describe anyone
as a liar, from their body language: the journal is about your reading, not their character.
