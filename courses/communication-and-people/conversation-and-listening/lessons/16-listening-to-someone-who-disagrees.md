Most of the listening this course has taught is easiest when you agree, or don't much care. The hard
case is the person who thinks you are wrong about something that matters to you. That is when it is
hardest not to start preparing a reply, and it is also when showing that you have understood is
worth most. This lesson covers how to show you have understood a view without signing up to it,
what one research programme found about the words that signal openness, and what the studies of
conversation across political disagreement do and do not show. It takes no side on any political
issue, and its examples are chosen so that it does not.

Lesson 2, "Why 'no' comes late", gave you the starting point. In Anita Pomerantz's recordings,
agreement came fast, plain and complete, while disagreement was often delayed, prefaced with "well",
offered in part ("yes, but...") or never said at all.[1] That is **observed in recordings**, and
lesson 2 added that flat, early refusals turn up too. This lesson is about handling disagreement as
a listener.

## Acknowledging is not agreeing

Four sources from four trades make the same point.

- The Harvard Program on Negotiation, on listening in a deal: "Acknowledgment doesn't mean
  agreement."[2]
- Mental Health First Aid's US guidance for lay helpers, under "Listen nonjudgmentally": "Try to be
  accepting, even if you don't agree with what they are saying."[3]
- Rogers and Farson, in the 1957 booklet lesson 8 quoted: "I respect your thoughts, and even if I
  don't agree with them, I know that they are valid for you."[4]
- Braver Angels, an American organisation that runs workshops between people on opposite sides of
  politics, puts "Acknowledge what you heard" second in its four steps, before anyone offers a
  view.[5]

All four are **practitioner advice**, from negotiation, mental health first aid, workplace
counselling and political dialogue, not findings. They agree on a distinction that is easy to
blur. Acknowledging a view means showing the other person that you have understood what they think
and why. Agreeing means holding it yourself. You can do the first fully and not the second at all.

:::predict Why might people find it hard to acknowledge a view they disagree with? Think of lesson 2 before you open this.

Lesson 2 showed that in recorded talk, agreement tends to come fast and plain, while disagreement comes later and softened, often opening with a partial "yes". **This course's reading** is that an acknowledgement ("I see why you think that") sounds like the start of the agreement shape, so a listener who disagrees may feel it concedes ground they will then have to take back. The sources read give no tested explanation for the reluctance.

If your answer was pride, or fear of looking weak, those may play a part too. This course has not read a study that measured them.
:::

## What receptive sounds like

The fullest attempt this course read to pin down the words is a 2020 paper by Michael Yeomans and four
colleagues, ["Conversational receptiveness"](https://receptiveness.net/assets/papers/Conversational.pdf).[6] They define receptiveness as "the use
of language to communicate one's willingness to thoughtfully engage with opposing views".[6] The
word to notice is "communicate": the paper is about what comes across in the words, not what is in
your head.

They built a computer model that scores text for receptiveness, using people's ratings of written
replies as the standard, and it picked out a small set of markers.[6] On the receptive side:
explicit acknowledgement ("I understand," "I see your point"), explicit agreement ("I agree,"
"you're right") even from someone who disagrees overall, and hedges ("somewhat," "might"). On the
other side: negations ("no," "wrong") and "a focus on explanatory reasoning (e.g., "because,"
"therefore")".[6] That last marker is the surprising one. A reply full of "because" feels like reasoning to
the person writing it, and it came across to readers as closed.

Two of the studies matter most here.

- **The government executives.** 238 senior managers on a continuing-education programme for state
  and local government executives were each paired with someone who disagreed with them on one of
  three policy questions, and discussed it for about twenty minutes in an anonymous typed chat. Executives whose words were
  rated more receptive, by their partners and by the model, were seen as better teammates and
  advisers, "but not their own self-evaluations".[6] How receptive people thought they had been
  did not predict how their partners saw them. That is a **single study**.
- **The recipe.** 771 online workers each wrote a reply to a statement opposing their own view on a
  social issue. Some were first taught a "receptiveness recipe": "positive statements, rather than
  negations; explicit acknowledgement of understanding; finding points of agreement; and hedging to
  soften claims".[6] Other online workers who held the opposite view rated the replies without
  knowing who had been taught what. They rated the recipe-writers as more receptive, more desirable
  to work with and, to the authors' surprise, more persuasive.[6] The writers found the recipe
  harder to use, and slightly more of them dropped out before finishing (17.2% against 12.6%), which
  the authors flag.[6]

The limits are real. All of it is **written text**, the chat included, and the samples are mostly
online. The main outcome is how other people rated the writer, not whether anyone's view changed.
The paper is **one group's programme**, not independently replicated as far as this course read.
And why would the markers matter? The authors' account is in their definition: receptive language
communicates a willingness to engage, and readers treated writers who used it better. Nothing in the
paper tests the step in between, and this course offers no other account.

### Why this course checked the paper first

This paper shares three of its authors with the question-asking paper that lesson 6 described as
independently audited and corrected, so this course checked its record before using it. As of 26
September 2026, neither the Retraction Watch database (its release current to 19 September) nor
Crossref lists a retraction, correction or expression of concern for it.[7] The data and code were
also independently checked in 2024. The integrity check found the raw data matched what was posted,
apart from one missing survey response. The reproducibility check judged the main-text numbers not
reproduced for the first three studies and partly reproduced for the fourth, mostly through
rounding, signs, missing random seeds and code that could not be found or run; for the results this
lesson uses, the listed differences are small (0.29 in the paper against 0.31 reproduced, for
example) and none changes whether a result held.[8]

## A reply, rewritten

Parents at your children's school are discussing, in a group chat, a proposal for a four-day school
week. Brigitta writes: "A four-day week would be a nightmare for working parents. Who's meant to look
after the kids on Fridays?" You are in favour, and your first draft is:

> "That's not really the point. The teachers are worn out and this is the only offer on the table,
> so Fridays are something families will just have to sort out."

Mark it against the recipe. "Not really the point" is a negation. "So" is the explanatory reasoning
the model scored as unreceptive. There is no acknowledgement of what Brigitta said, no point of
agreement and no hedge: "just have to" is as flat as it gets. Whatever you meant, she is likely to
hear that her worry has been waved away.

Now take it through Braver Angels' four steps, which it calls **LAPP**: "Listen to understand, not
reload. Acknowledge what you heard. Pivot by assessing whether it is OK to offer your views. Offer
your perspective (if the person has signaled a willingness to hear it)."[5] In a chat set up to
discuss the proposal, the Pivot is easy. So:

> "Fridays are the hard part, and you're right that working parents would carry it first. I still
> lean towards the four-day week, mostly because I think it might help the school keep its
> teachers, but I could be underrating the childcare problem. What would it take to make Fridays
> work for you?"

"Fridays are the hard part" is a positive statement that acknowledges. "You're right that working
parents would carry it first" is a point of agreement. "Might" and "could be underrating" are hedges.
Your view and your reason are still there. What changed is that Brigitta can see you read her.

Two cautions. The recipe was tested on written replies to strangers, so a parents' chat is close to
the studies' setting, while saying it aloud to your brother is **this course's reading**, not a
tested use. And this course teaches the recipe as a way to show you have understood, not as a script
for winning.

:::checkpoint Your turn. Another parent writes: "I don't care what the teachers want. Four days is less school, full stop." You're against the four-day week too, but you think the teachers' workload is a fair point. Write a reply with all four markers of the recipe before you open this.

One version: "I'm against it as well, and less time in class is my worry too. I do think the teachers' workload is a real problem, though, and it might be worth hearing what else could ease it."

Check yours for a positive statement rather than a negation, an acknowledgement ("less time in class is my worry too"), a point of agreement (easy here, since you share his conclusion) and a hedge ("might"). Receptiveness is not only for people you oppose: here you are questioning one of his reasons while agreeing with his conclusion.

If your reply began "That's not fair on the teachers", it opened with a negation, which is an easy slip to make.
:::

## Restating before you reply

You have met the strongest form of this move twice. Logic and Argument lesson 7, "Reconstruction and
charity: the strongest version first", gave you Rapoport's first rule as the philosopher Daniel
Dennett states it: restate the other person's position so clearly and fairly that they thank you and
wish they had put it that way.[9] Lesson 8 gave you Rogers and Farson's ground rule for a heated
discussion, where each restatement "must be accurate enough to satisfy the speaker".[4] And lesson 9
showed the Gottman Institute's exercise for couples opening the same way: "Postpone persuasion until
each person can state their partner’s position to their partner’s satisfaction."[10]

The judge in all three is the other person. That matters most in a disagreement, because the view
you are likeliest to get wrong is one you already think is mistaken. Dennett's stated reason for the
rules, as Logic and Argument lesson 7 reported it, is that they make your opponent a more receptive
audience, and that lesson added that your objection then lands on the view they actually hold.[9]
**This course's reading** adds lesson 8's point. If you restate a colleague's case for going back to
the office as "you hate the commute" and she says "No, it's that the new starters learn nothing at
home", the correction has saved you from arguing against a view she never held.

## What the dialogue organisations teach

Two organisations that design conversations between people who disagree both built their methods
from family and couples therapy.

**Braver Angels** was co-founded by William Doherty, a marriage and family therapist, who designed its
workshops "based on techniques used in couples therapy".[5] Its Red/Blue workshops bring together "a
small, balanced group of Reds and Blues", its names for the two sides of American politics.[11] It
carries rules over from marriage therapy, such as "Speak for yourself; don't interpret what's going
on in the other person's mind", and states its stance plainly: "In Braver Angels, neither side is teaching the
other or giving feedback on how to think or say things differently."[5][11]

**Essential Partners** was founded in 1989, as the Public Conversations Project, by "behavioral health
researchers and practitioners" applying family therapy practice to polarised public issues.[12] Its
groups start from agreements made before anyone talks. Three from its guide:[13]

- "We will speak for ourselves, and allow others to speak for themselves."
- "We will not criticize the views of others or attempt to persuade them."
- Replace an attribution ("You just believe that because…") with a question ("What leads you to
  that belief?").

Lesson 5 quoted its reason for building in pauses, and lesson 6 its test for a question. Its
statement of purpose: "We never ask people to change their views."[12]

All of this is **practitioner advice**; Essential Partners links to research on its method, which
this course did not read.[13] Both organisations say their conversations are not for persuasion.
Keep that in mind for the evidence below.

## When they don't want your view

After a national election, your old friend Isidore tells you over a drink why he voted as he did,
for the side you voted against. Before you can answer, he says: "I'm not after an argument. I just
want you to understand why."

Run LAPP. Listen: let him finish, and listen for his reasons rather than for a gap you could use.
Acknowledge: say back what you heard, in your own words, until he says you have got it. Then the
Pivot: is it all right to offer your view? He has told you it is not. The fourth step is conditional
("if the person has signaled a willingness to hear it"), so LAPP says hold back.[5] That leaves a
question the steps cannot answer: what is this conversation for, if you do not get a turn?

:::checkpoint Before opening: list two things the conversation with Isidore could still be for, and one reason you might decide not to have it tonight.

Some possible answers. To understand how someone you respect reached a view you do not hold. To keep the friendship, by showing him the vote has not made him a stranger. To learn whether his reasons are the ones you assumed, which, on lesson 7's evidence about guessing other people's minds, they may well not be. And it is reasonable to decide you cannot do it well tonight and say so kindly.

Which matters most is your judgement, not the course's. What LAPP rules out is pretending to listen while waiting for an opening, the "reload" in its first step. If he asks later what you think, that is the signal the fourth step waits for.
:::

## Listening while disagreeing: what the experiments found

Two results bear on whether good listening helps when two people disagree, both from the programme
lesson 9 described. In 2024 Itzchakov and colleagues reported four experiments in which
high-quality listening during a disagreement made speakers' attitudes less polarised, compared with
poor or moderate listening; this course read only the one-page abstract, so it gives no sizes.[14]
That is **one group's programme**.

The other is lesson 9's four-lab test, in which an instruction to try to learn about a disagreeing
stranger's view, against an instruction to persuade them, "did not influence perceived listening
quality or polarization outcomes in any sample".[15] The people told to learn did not report trying
any harder, so the authors say the idea was not really tested. In all four samples, though, feeling
better listened to went with feeling psychologically safe, and safety with less negative views of
the other person on most measures, a correlation rather than a cause.[15] **This course's reading**
of the two together: being listened to well in a disagreement seems to matter, and telling someone
to listen better did not, on its own, get them to do it.

## Can a conversation change a mind?

That is a separate question from whether listening helps, and it is **contested**.

### A famous study, retracted

In December 2014 *Science* published a paper by Michael LaCour and Donald Green reporting that a
single conversation with a gay canvasser produced large, lasting changes in voters' support for
same-sex marriage, and that the change spread to others. This course read only its abstract.[16]
Within six months it was retracted. The notice, as [Retraction Watch reproduced it](https://retractionwatch.com/2015/05/28/science-retracts-troubled-gay-canvassing-study-against-lacours-objections/), gave two
reasons, "(i) Survey incentives were misrepresented" and "(ii) The statement on sponsorship was
false", and added that independent researchers had noted statistical irregularities and that LaCour
had not produced the original survey data. It was retracted with Green's agreement; LaCour did not
agree.[17]

A retraction removes one study's evidence. It does not show the opposite, that conversations never
change anyone.

### Smaller, and still there months later

Two political scientists, Joshua Kalla and David Broockman, published three field experiments in
2020.[18] Canvassers from community organisations had conversations of about ten minutes with voters,
at the door or by phone: 230 canvassers, 6,869 voters, seven US locations, all preregistered. The
campaigns aimed to move voters towards one side of two contested questions, one about immigration
policy and one about transgender people. This course takes no side on either; what matters here is
the design of the first experiment, which had three arms. In the full conversation, the canvasser
asked the voter's view, the two exchanged personal stories while the canvasser listened without
judging (the training said to "make it clear [to voters] we're not there to judge them"), and then
the canvasser made the case. The shorter conversation was the same with the stories cut, leaving the
arguments. The third was a one-minute chat about something else.[18]

:::predict Which do you expect changed voters' views, in surveys up to several months later: the full conversation, the arguments-only one, both, or neither?

Only the full one. In the authors' words, conversations "deploying arguments alone had no effects", while "otherwise identical conversations also including the non-judgmental exchange of narratives durably reduced exclusionary attitudes for at least four months (d = 0.08)".[18] "Exclusionary attitudes" is the authors' term for the views the campaigns set out to change. The two later experiments found d = 0.08 and 0.04.
:::

Now the size. By the course's arithmetic on the normal curve from Mental Fitness lesson 2 (not a
figure from any study), an effect of 0.08 puts the average person who had the full conversation
ahead of about 53% of the comparison group. That is small, and the authors say so: "we do not wish
to overstate the substantive size of the effects we estimated".[18] It is averaged over everyone who
opened the door, including people who broke off early, so the authors report larger estimates for
those who stayed. The full conversations were also longer (about 11 minutes against 5), a
difference the authors discuss and argue does not explain the result. They define their strategy as an attempt to persuade that uses listening without
judgement, a different goal from this course's. They ask "what consequences would result if both
sides of an issue engaged in this strategy". And they report, citing an earlier paper of theirs this
course has not read, a door-to-door canvass on abortion in which the stories failed to persuade.[18]

### A workshop, randomised

The other trial is of a Braver Angels workshop. Baron and eight colleagues recruited undergraduates
at four universities in early 2020: 165 volunteers, 116 Blue and 49 Red, of whom 59 were randomly
assigned to a workshop and the rest to surveys only, with follow-ups at one to two weeks and about
six months. It was preregistered.[19] The workshop reduced affective polarisation, meaning dislike
of the other side, on an implicit and an explicit measure, "though the effect on the explicit
measure just misses the conventional 5% threshold for statistical significance", and made people
more willing to donate to depolarisation programmes. The implicit and explicit effects "dissipate somewhat over
time, but the effects on the behavioral measure persist after more than half a year".[19] Who was
studied matters: a small, volunteer sample that leaned Blue, and at two campuses every Red volunteer
went into a workshop, so they do not count towards the estimates.[19] It is a **single study**, and
it is not evidence about views on policy, which were not the workshop's aim.

### Both readings

**Structured, listening-heavy conversation can reduce hostility and shift views a little, and the
effects can last.** The evidence is randomised and preregistered: the canvassing experiments, in
which argument alone did nothing and the exchange of stories made the difference, and the workshop
trial, whose behavioural effect lasted six months.[18][19] Braver Angels says its workshops help
people "clarify disagreements, reduce stereotyped thinking and discover common values".[11]

**The effects are small, fragile, or about feelings rather than views.** This reading is built from
the same papers, and their authors supply much of it: effects of 0.08 and 0.04, which the canvassing
authors say many social psychologists would call small; an explicit measure that just missed significance; attitude effects that faded;
volunteers who may be unusually open to begin with; and a field whose most celebrated result was
retracted.[16][18][19] This course has not read a published critique of these particular trials.

The organisations complicate both. Essential Partners never asks anyone to change their view, and in
Braver Angels neither side is teaching the other, so on their own account "did minds change?" may be
the wrong outcome.[11][12] What would settle it: larger preregistered trials with people who didn't
volunteer, longer follow-up, an independent repeat of the workshop trial, and outcomes kept apart
(hostility towards the other side, views on policy, and behaviour).

## What it's for is partly a value question

Whether these conversations reduce hostility is an empirical question. What they are for is partly a
matter of values, and the answers differ.

- **Understanding and civility.** Braver Angels and Essential Partners, in their words above.[11][12]
- **Persuasion.** The canvassing campaigns set out to change views, and their authors call the
  strategy persuasion.[18]
- **Not talking at all, in some cases.** Some people hold that sitting down to listen to certain
  views gives them a standing they should not have. This course has not read a primary statement of
  that position, so it names it and does not argue for or against it. The 2020 canvassing paper
  touches a related point: it cites field work in which signalling that exclusionary behaviour will
  be judged negatively reduced the consequences of prejudice without changing attitudes, and says
  such norms may need balancing against conditions in which people do not feel threatened
  discussing their views.[18]

This course's standpoint, set out in lesson 3, is that its lessons aim at understanding the other
person, and that changing minds belongs to Persuasion and Influence, a later course. That is a choice
about what this course teaches, not a verdict on the other answers. Nothing here means you owe anyone
a conversation, and if someone is threatening rather than disagreeing, that is Personal Safety lesson
8, "Talking it down".

## What people get wrong

**"If I acknowledge their view, I'm agreeing with it."** Negotiators, mental health first aiders,
Rogers and Farson and Braver Angels all separate the two.[2][3][4][5]

**"One good conversation can flip someone's view."** The famous study of that was retracted, and the
later trials found small effects.[17][18]

**"Talking across a divide does nothing."** That overcorrects. The canvassing experiments and the
workshop trial found small effects, some still there months later.[18][19]

**"I can tell how open I sound."** The executives' ratings of their own receptiveness did not predict
how their partners saw them.[6]

**"If I try to listen well, they'll feel listened to."** In the four-lab test, an instruction to
learn did not change how listened-to the partners felt.[15]

**"A reply full of reasons is the most open kind."** In the receptiveness model, "because" and
"therefore" marked a reply as less receptive.[6]

## Practice

:::exercise Their view, until they say you've got it
This is a **practise with a willing partner** exercise. Take 15 minutes. Ask someone who knows it is practice and has agreed; they can say no, and they need not be kind about your attempts. They choose a disagreement that is real but not about either of your identities, faith or politics (whether dogs belong on the sofa; whether exams should be open-book; whether reclining your seat on a plane is rude). If it gets heated, stop. Do not record it, and name nobody in your journal.

Your partner gives their view and reasons for two or three minutes, uninterrupted. You say it back aloud in your own words, aiming for Rapoport's first rule. They tell you what is missing, and you try again until they agree it is their view. Only then, if they want to hear it, give yours in two sentences. Afterwards, write down how many tries it took and what you left out the first time (a reason, a feeling, their strongest point).
:::

:::exercise Four replies, rewritten
This is a **rewrite on paper** exercise. Take 10 minutes. Rewrite each reply so that it keeps the writer's view but uses the recipe: a positive statement rather than a negation, an acknowledgement, a point of agreement, and a hedge.

- To a colleague who wants to scrap the weekly meeting: "No, we need it. Without it nobody knows what anyone else is doing, so it stays."
- To a neighbour who wants the street's trees cut back: "That's ridiculous. The trees were here first and they're the only nice thing on the road."
- To a sibling who thinks your father should move in with one of you: "You're not thinking it through. He values his independence, therefore he should stay where he is."
- To a friend who thinks a book you both read was overrated: "Wrong. It won every prize going, so clearly people rate it."
:::

:::checkpoint Compare yours with these. The wording will differ; check that each keeps the writer's view and has all four markers.

The meeting: "I can see it eats an hour, and I agree it's sometimes more update than discussion. I'd still keep it, maybe shorter, because I think we'd lose track of each other without it." The "because" survives: the recipe does not ban reasons, it asks you not to lead with them in place of acknowledging.

The trees: "I get that the branches block light for some houses, and that's fair. I'd hate to lose much of them, but perhaps a trim would help."

Your father: "I can see why you'd want him close, and I agree we'd both worry less. My sense is he might hate losing his independence. What makes you think he would want to move?" The closing question is not one of the four markers; it is Essential Partners' move from attribution to curiosity.

The book: "I see what you mean; the middle dragged for me too. It deserved some of the attention, I'd say, if maybe not all the prizes." The original's only reason, the prizes, was weak, and rewriting often shows you that.
:::

:::exercise Which step did you skip?
This is a **notice** exercise. Take 5 minutes, within an hour of any disagreement this week, however small. Nobody named or identifiable, and no recording. Write down what it was about, then go through LAPP: did you listen to understand or to reload, acknowledge before replying, and check whether they wanted your view? Name the step you skipped, if any, and what happened next.
:::

## For your journal

The third exercise is this lesson's journal entry; over several weeks, the step you skip most often
is worth knowing. For the project, lesson 8 put "restating before replying" on the list of habits to
practise and measure, and this lesson adds the harder version: restating a view you disagree with
until its holder agrees you have got it.
