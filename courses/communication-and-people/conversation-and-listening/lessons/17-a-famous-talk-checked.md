If you've looked for advice on conversation, there's a good chance you've met Celeste Headlee's
talk "10 ways to have a better conversation". She gave it at a TEDx event in 2015, and when this
course looked on 26 September 2026 its page on ted.com counted 33,048,911 views.[1] It hands you ten
rules you can remember on the way home. This lesson takes each rule through the sixteen lessons
you've just done and sees what holds, what needs a condition attached, and what nobody has tested.
That is the whole course again, recalled in a new order. And since the next piece of advice you meet
will not arrive with a lesson attached, you'll finish with a checklist for checking it yourself.

The point is not to catch a popular speaker out. A talk is not a review of the research, and
Headlee does not pretend hers is one. The fair question is the one this course asks of every source:
what kind of claim is this, and what would it take to know?

## What kind of evidence a talk is

Headlee is a radio host, and she tells the audience where her rules come from: "Now, I actually use
the exact same skills as a professional interviewer that I do in regular life."[1] Run the course's
question on that sentence. Finding or advice? Advice. Who, and where? A professional interviewer,
drawing on interviews made for broadcast, who says the skills carry over to ordinary conversation.
That last step is hers. It's the same kind of step lesson 6 met when the authors of the "some" and
"any" study suggested their clinic finding "may generalize". Compared with what? Nothing, because a
talk is not a test. On the course's labels, the ten rules are **practitioner advice**, from one
practitioner.

The talk leans on other things too: a Pew survey, a teacher's article in *The Atlantic*, the
therapist M. Scott Peck, and lines credited to Buddha, Calvin Coolidge, Stephen Covey, Bill Nye
and Stephen Hawking. It cites no research on listening.[1] That does not make it wrong. It means
that where the talk is right, the evidence has to come from somewhere else, and the rest of this
course is where it comes from.

Here are the ten rules, in her order, mostly in this course's words:[1]

1. Don't multitask: be present, or leave.
2. Don't pontificate: go in assuming you have something to learn.
3. Use open-ended questions.
4. Go with the flow: let your own thoughts come and go.
5. If you don't know, say that you don't know.
6. "Don't equate your experience with theirs."
7. Try not to repeat yourself.
8. Stay out of the weeds: leave out the names and dates.
9. Listen. She calls this one the most important.
10. Be brief.

She adds that you don't need all ten: choose one and master it, and your conversations will already
improve.

### Four verdicts

Each rule gets one of four verdicts.

- **Supported**: the evidence this course read points the same way, in the people and settings it
  studied.
- **Qualified**: right in some cases and not others, or right in its aim and too broad in its
  wording, so it needs a condition attached.
- **Contradicted**: tested, in a setting close enough to matter, and found not to hold.
- **Untested**: nothing this course read bears on it directly.

The fourth is the one people misuse. **Untested is not false.** It means nobody in this course's
sources has checked, so the rule stands on its reasoning and on the experience of whoever gives it,
and you hold it as loosely as that deserves. A rule can be untested and good. "Let them finish" was
practitioner advice too, and one of the few that medicine has timed with a stopwatch (lesson 5).

:::predict Before reading on: of Headlee's ten rules, how many do you expect the research in this course to contradict?
On this course's reading, none. Three are supported, at least in part, two need a condition attached, and five have not been tested by anything this course read.

What the evidence does contradict is one of the numbers she gives in support, which comes later in the lesson.
:::

## Rule 3, checked: open questions

Take one rule all the way through, so each step is visible.

**What the talk says.** "Start your questions with who, what, when, where, why or how."[1] Her
example: ask someone "Were you terrified?" and they'll answer yes or no, because, she says, "you're
going to respond to the most powerful word in that sentence".[1] Ask "What was that like?" and they
have to stop and think. Her reasoning is that a closed question does the describing for the other
person and leaves them only to confirm it.

**Finding or advice?** Advice, and she says whose: take a cue from journalists, she tells the
audience.[1]

**What the course found.** Three kinds of evidence, which do not all say the same thing.

First, the practitioners agree with her. Samaritans suggest replacing "have you had a bad week?"
with "what's been going on for you this week?", and the NHS and the Smithsonian's oral history
guide, as lesson 6 showed, push the same way.[2] That is **practitioner advice** from people whose work is
getting others to talk.

Second, the research on asking tests something next door. Huang and colleagues' experiments, **one
group's programme** with strangers getting acquainted mostly by text chat in a US lab and online,
found that partners of people told to ask at least nine questions in fifteen minutes liked them
more than partners of people told to ask at most four (d = .35 in the first study), and that
follow-up questions carried the effect.[3] The paper was independently audited and corrected in
2025, and the audit confirmed its conclusions.[4] But look at what was compared: many
questions against few, and follow-ups against switches of topic. Nobody in those studies compared
open questions with closed ones.

Third, one experiment shows the wording of a question changing its answer even when both versions
are closed. In Heritage and colleagues' study of 20 US family doctors, a **single study**, among
patients who had come with more than one concern, 90.3% of those whose doctor asked whether there
was "something else" they wanted to address said yes, against 53.1% of those asked about "anything
else".[5] Both are yes-or-no questions. On the
authors' account, "any" quietly leans towards "no". That supports the reasoning behind Headlee's
rule, that a question's frame shapes its answer, more than it supports the rule's form.

**Where it stops.** StoryCorps's bank of questions for recorded conversations includes "Did you
always know you wanted to be a parent?", closed in form, which lesson 6 read as opening a story
between two people who care about the answer.[6] Acas, the UK's workplace conciliation
service, suggests closed questions on purpose, to check the facts once the story is out.[7] And her
list of openers includes "why", which lesson 6 found the traditions split on: crisis negotiators
avoid it as sounding like an interrogation, while workplace and oral-history guides use it.

:::predict Before you read the verdict: supported, qualified, contradicted or untested? Pick one and say which of the three kinds of evidence decided it for you.
This course's verdict is **supported, with limits**. Every practitioner source this course read favours open questions, and the nearest research backs the idea underneath: following up on what someone said went with being liked more in Huang's experiments, and a question's frame changed its answer in Heritage's. What no study here tested is open against closed as such, and "why" is where practitioners part company.

If you chose "untested", you were reading the rule's exact wording, which is fair; the difference is whether the research on follow-ups and frames counts as reaching it. A fair rewrite of the rule would be: ask questions that leave the describing to them, and follow up on what they say.
:::

## Rule 1: your turn

Rule 1 is "Don't multitask", and Headlee means more than putting the phone down. "Don't think about
your argument you had with your boss."[1] Be present, she says, and if you want to leave a
conversation, leave it instead of staying half in.

Three things from earlier lessons bear on it. Lesson 4 described an experiment from Itzchakov and
Kluger's programme, as the two researchers report it in a magazine article: 112 undergraduates
talked for ten minutes to a listener, and half the listeners, at random, were sent text messages to
answer, so that the speakers saw they were distracted. Speakers with undistracted listeners reported
feeling less anxious and clearer about their own views.[8] Lesson 9 met a second study in the same
article, in which speakers with a trained listener saw both their strengths and their weaknesses
more than speakers with an ordinary listener or an actor playing distracted.[8] And a 2026 review by
Itzchakov and Graham Bodie reports other studies in which speakers often failed to notice that a
listener was distracted and were affected anyway, studies this course has not read.[9]

Now split the rule into its three parts: the phone, the wandering mind, and "leave instead of
staying half in". For each, write which evidence reaches it, what the comparison group got, and who
rated the outcome. Then write your verdict for each part.

:::checkpoint Write your three verdicts before you open this. Which part of rule 1 does the evidence reach, and which parts does it not?
The phone part is **supported**, with the comparison named. In the texting study the comparison was a listener visibly answering messages, and the outcomes were the speakers' own reports. It is one group's programme, with undergraduates in pairs, described as the magazine article describes it.[8]

The wandering-mind part is **untested** in what this course read. Nobody in those studies was asked to think about an argument with their boss while keeping their eyes on the speaker. The review's account of unnoticed distraction points the same way, but it's a lead to follow, not a finding this course checked.[9]

"Leave instead of staying half in" is **untested** too. For now it rests on her experience.

If you marked the whole rule "supported", notice what carried you there: a result about distraction the speaker could see, stretched to cover distraction nobody could see. That is the drift this lesson finds in numbers later, on a small scale. Focus and Deep Work lesson 5, "The phone on the desk", asked a different question, about a phone's effect on your own attention, so it does not settle this one either.
:::

## Rule 6: right where it's aimed

Now a harder one. Rule 6 is "Don't equate your experience with theirs." Her examples: "If they're
talking about having lost a family member, don't start talking about the time you lost a family
member", and if they are having trouble at work, don't tell them how much you hate your job. Then:
"It is never the same."[1]

**Where the rule is aimed.** Both her examples are someone in distress: a death, trouble at work.
For that case, the sources this course read lean her way. Samaritans' advice for supporting someone
who's struggling says listening "could mean not offering advice, not trying to identify what
they’re going through with your own experiences and not trying to solve their problems."[2]
VitalTalk, whose guides are for clinicians caring for seriously ill patients, tells them to "stop short of
suggesting you understand everything (you don't)".[10] Both are **practitioner advice**. In
one coded set of recorded conversations, a **single study** of students talking to trained
strangers, the trained listeners told to give low comfort "tended to accomplish this goal by
engaging in prolonged narratives about their own experiences which have nothing to do with
disclosers' emotional experiences."[11] As lesson 10 said, that is an observation about actors told
to do badly, not a test of what happens when a friend tells a story. And Headlee's own story, from
her book, is the case itself: she answered a grieving friend with her own father's death, and the
friend heard it as a competition (lesson 4).[12]

**Where it's stretched.** "It is never the same" turns a default for distress into a rule for every
conversation. In ordinary sociable talk, the case lesson 4 made for a matching story still stands.
Huang and colleagues suggest, in their discussion, that people "do not want to talk about themselves
indefinitely without reciprocity", which is a suggestion, not something their studies tested.[3]
And Headlee herself, in her book, allows a turn to yourself: such a reply "could be fine, as long as
we allow the focus to shift back to the other person again".[12] Her book is less absolute than
her talk.

**What's settled.** Nothing, head to head. Lessons 4 and 10 classed this as **contested**, with a
value inside it (what you owe someone in distress), and found no study that compares second stories
with support responses and measures how understood the first speaker felt, separately for distress
and for sociable talk. That is the study that would settle it.

**The verdict: qualified.** Right in the cases the talk names, on the sources this course read, and
overstated as "never", by the evidence and by its own author's book. **This course's reading**,
carried from lessons 4 and 10, is that it turns on whether the person in front of you is in
distress, so the verdict is "depends, and here's on what". If a conversation like her examples turns
darker than distress, lesson 11, "When it's serious", and Mental Fitness lesson 1, "Where ordinary
ends", are where the institute deals with it.

## The other seven

**Rule 2, don't pontificate.** "You need to enter every conversation assuming that you have something
to learn."[1] Two organisations that run conversations across political difference say much the
same in their own words. Braver Angels' first step is "Listen to understand, not reload", and
Essential Partners' guide asks that a question reflect "genuine curiosity and is not a challenge in
disguise".[13][14] That's practitioner advice, and lesson 16, "Listening to someone who disagrees with
you", takes up what those organisations teach. The nearest test is lesson 9's four-lab experiment.
People given a written goal of learning about a partner's view, before a disagreement with a
stranger, were not rated better listeners than people told to persuade, and the authors' own check
showed the instruction had not changed how hard people said they tried to learn.[15] That tested an
instruction, not the attitude Headlee means. **Verdict: untested** as she states it. A test would
measure how far people actually came to a conversation to learn, and ask their partners how
listened to they felt.

**Rule 4, go with the flow.** Let your thoughts come and go, she says, and do not be the interview host
who stops listening because a clever question has occurred to him.[1] Lesson 1 gave you the
complication. Replies typically begin a fraction of a second after a turn ends, far too soon to plan
them from scratch, so on Levinson's argument "one must plan while still listening and predicting
what the rest of the incoming turn will contain".[16] Lesson 4 drew this course's reading from
that: planning is not the failure; what the planning is aimed at can be. Headlee's host is that
failure exactly, polishing a question that ignores what the guest is saying. **Verdict: qualified.**
Right about the failure, with one condition: the thoughts to let go are the ones that pull you away
from what they're saying, because on Levinson's reading of the timing, everyone prepares a reply
while listening. The Covey line she quotes later, that we listen "with the intent to reply", runs into the
same fact.

**Rule 5, if you don't know, say so.** Her reason is that careful broadcasters are wary of claiming
to know things for sure.[1] Nothing this course read tests it. The nearest finding is lesson 7's:
couples predicting each other's opinions believed they'd got about 14 of 20 right and actually got
about 6, whatever strategy they'd used, "dramatically overconfident" in the authors' words.[17]
That is about knowing another person's mind, not facts in general, so it is **this course's reading**,
not a finding, that it makes her caution well aimed. **Verdict: untested.**

**Rules 7 and 8, don't repeat yourself, and stay out of the weeds.** Both are about your own turns:
don't keep rephrasing your point, and leave out the years and names you're straining to recall.[1]
Nothing this course read measured either. Keep one thing apart: rule 7 is about repeating your own
point, not about saying back someone else's, which lesson 8 taught as a check. **Verdict: untested**,
both.

**Rule 10, be brief.** The talk gives it three words, "Be brief. Be interested in other people.", and
moves straight on to a story about her grandparents' visitors.[1] So it's unclear whether she means
your turns or the whole conversation. Lesson 13's endings study is the nearest evidence for the
second reading, and it does not point one way: in lab conversations between strangers, both people
had wanted to stop sooner in 46.83% of them, both had wanted to go on in 9.52%, and people were poor
at telling which their partner wanted.[18] That is one group's programme, with US samples. **Verdict:
untested.** A test of the first reading would vary how long people's turns ran and ask their
partners how it went.

### Rule 9, and the case against showing you're listening

Rule 9, "Listen", is the one she calls "the most important one", and it is this course's subject, so
it gets more room.[1] As a goal it's **supported**. The Itzchakov and Kluger experiments, one
group's programme, found that speakers who were listened to well came away less anxious and clearer
about their attitudes than speakers with a distracted listener, and in another study saw their own
strengths and weaknesses more than speakers with an ordinary one (lesson 9).[8] The same lesson's four-lab test adds the catch: a short instruction to
listen to learn did not change how listened to the partners felt, so good listening is easier to
value than to switch on.[15]

Her first reason we do not listen fits lesson 4: we'd rather talk, she says, because then we're the
centre of attention.[1] Tamir and Mitchell's experiments, one group's programme, found people
gave up some money to answer questions about themselves rather than judge other people's opinions
or answer trivia.[19] That compared talking about yourself with judging others, never with listening,
so connecting it to her point is this course's reading. Her figures for her second reason, speech
at 225 words a minute and listening at up to 500, lesson 3 traced: three popular sources, three
speech rates, none measured. Keep the idea that a listener's attention has room to wander; drop the
numbers.[20]

The part that needs a condition comes early in the talk. She runs through the advice people are
usually given, from looking the person in the eye to nodding and smiling and repeating back what you
heard, and says: "It is crap." Her reason: "There is no reason to learn how to show you're paying
attention if you are in fact paying attention."[1]

Some of that the evidence backs. Take eye contact first: lesson 15 concluded, as this course's
reading from three sources, that eye contact is a poor test of listening, because Tzeltal listeners
in recorded questions often showed attention without looking, and many autistic people report that
eye contact makes listening harder. On performed restating she
has company. Rogers and Farson, who coined "active listening", warned that its techniques, used
without real respect for the speaker, "will be empty and sterile".[21] And Weger's 2010 experiment,
a single study of 180 US undergraduates, found that paraphrasing, compared with plain
acknowledgements from a listener whose manner was held the same, made the listener a little more
likeable but did not make the students feel more understood.[22]

Some of it the evidence does not back. Weger's 2014 experiment, another single study, with 115
undergraduates talking to trained strangers, found that active listening (paraphrase plus
invitations to say more) made people feel more understood than advice or bare acknowledgement
did.[23] Worthington and Bodie point out that a listener's responses "are the only signals that
listening is taking (or has taken) place".[24] And lesson 8 separated two acts that look alike: a
summary offered to show you were listening, and a guess offered so that you can be corrected. Her
target is the first. Rogers and Farson's test, restating until the other person agrees you've got
it, is the second.

The recent review Itzchakov wrote with Bodie puts both halves in one sentence: paraphrasing "should
not be treated as a standalone marker of listening quality, but as a risky intervention that can
either deepen or derail understanding."[9] Lesson 9 found the question of whether active
listening helps **contested**, and this verdict does not settle it. **Verdict on "It is crap":
qualified.** Right about displays of attention, eye contact included; too broad if it throws out
the checked restatement.

## Her numbers and her quotations

The evidence a talk gives for its rules can be checked separately from the rules.

:::predict Headlee opens by saying a Pew study of 10,000 American adults found "we are more polarized, we are more divided, than we ever have been in history". What do you expect Pew's own report says?
Pew's 2014 report, from a telephone survey of 10,013 adults, says Republicans and Democrats are more divided along ideological lines "than at any point in the last two decades".[25]

Two decades, not all of history: a finding about how divided Republicans and Democrats had become, set against Pew's own polling over the previous twenty years.
:::

The next step is her own reasoning: "We're less likely to compromise, which means we're not
listening to each other."[1] The overview of Pew's report this course read says nothing about
listening. So this trace established that the talk's version of the number stretches its source. It
did not establish that she's wrong about listening, which the survey neither shows nor rules out.

Then the quotations. She credits "No man ever listened his way out of a job" to Calvin Coolidge, and
the line about listening "with the intent to reply" to Stephen Covey, and she flags her Buddha line
as a paraphrase herself.[1] This course couldn't confirm any of the three: a quotation researcher's
website, Quote Investigator, has no entry on them, and this course did not read Covey's book. That
trace established nothing, and "couldn't confirm" is not "made up".

Two lines from popular books show what a finished trace looks like. Charles Duhigg's
*Supercommunicators* opens a section with a saying it credits to George Bernard Shaw, that the
biggest problem with communication is the illusion it has taken place.[26] Quote Investigator
found no substantive evidence that Shaw said it; its earliest find is an article by the journalist
William H. Whyte in *Fortune* in 1950, and it concludes that the link to Shaw "appears to be
spurious".[27] This course has not read Whyte's article, so it knows that trace as Quote Investigator
reports it. And Kate Murphy's *You're Not Listening* credits a line about two ears and one tongue to
the philosopher Epictetus.[28] Diogenes Laertius's *Lives of Eminent Philosophers* gives a version
of it to Zeno of Citium: "The reason why we have two ears and only one mouth is that we may listen
the more and talk the less."[29] This course did not search Epictetus's own works, so what that trace
established is an older attribution to someone else, not that Epictetus never said anything like
it.

:::checkpoint Take three traces: the Pew number, the Coolidge line and the Shaw line. For each, what did the trace establish? And does any of them tell you whether the advice attached to it is good?
The Pew trace established that the talk stretched its source, from two decades of partisan division to all of history. The Coolidge trace established nothing, either way. The Shaw trace established that the attribution has no support and that the saying is older, from a business journalist.

None of them tells you whether the advice is good. A misattributed line about listening can still be wise, and a correctly attributed one can be wrong. What a failed trace does tell you is how much weight the famous name can carry for that claim: none. The claim then has to stand on evidence, which is what the rest of the checklist below is for.
:::

## A second specimen

The next piece of advice you meet may cite research of its own. Jack Zenger and Joseph Folkman's
2016 *Harvard Business Review* article compared colleagues' ratings of the top 5% of listeners among
3,492 managers in a coaching programme with everyone else's, on the 20 items where the groups
differed most, and concluded: "Good listening is much more than being silent while the other person
talks."[30] It's a magazine article, not peer-reviewed; choosing the items that differed most builds
in its own result; the ratings are impressions, not measures of anyone being understood; and it
repeats, unsourced, the "80%" nonverbal figure lesson 14 met.[30] So it is a large set of workplace
ratings suggesting people experience good listeners as active rather than silent, which fits lesson
6 on questions, and it is not evidence of what good listening is.

## Why advice drifts from its evidence

Put four of the chains this course has followed side by side.

- **The 45%.** In the 1920s, 68 Detroit adults logged their own day; a 1957 retelling gave 45%, with
  the retellers' own caveat that the survey "may mean little if harshly analyzed"; guides still
  print it as a fact (lesson 3).[20]
- **The 93%.** Two small 1967 studies of single words and posed photographs, which their author says
  do not apply "Unless a communicator is talking about their feelings or attitudes", became a rule
  about all communication (lesson 14).[31]
- **Small talk.** A correlation in 79 students, which its own authors called "causally ambiguous",
  travelled as "small talk makes you unhappy", and a pooled replication did not find the link
  (lesson 12).[32][33]
- **The Pew number.** Two decades of division between partisans became "than we ever have been in
  history".[25]

In each chain the same things fell away: who was studied, the setting, the comparison and the hedge.
The finding at the start was real, and it lost its scope as it travelled. Attributions drift the other way: a line picks up a famous name it never had, as
Whyte's picked up Shaw's, and the name lends it weight it didn't earn. That pattern is **this
course's reading** of the chains it traced. They don't show why each teller dropped what they
dropped, and this course will not guess.

Most of Headlee's rules are not drift at all. They are experience this course found no study of, which needs
the question this course asks of all practitioner advice: whose experience, built for what setting,
and is yours like it?

## A checklist for the next piece of advice

Logic and Argument lesson 9, "Bad reasoning in the news and everyday life", gave you five questions
for a news claim: three for the source (who says, how do they know, who disagrees) and two for the
number (share of what, compared with what). This course's version, for advice about conversation:

1. **Finding or advice?** If advice, whose, and from what work: a radio interviewer, a crisis line,
   a family therapist?
2. **Who was in the room, and where?** For a finding, who was studied and in what setting. For
   advice, what setting it was built for, and whether yours is like it.
3. **Compared with what?** For any effect, what the other group got.
4. **Which label does it earn?** Replicated, one group's programme, single study, failed to
   replicate or corrected, contested, observed in recordings, practitioner advice.
5. **Can you follow it back?** Trace the number or the quotation as far as it goes, and write down
   what the trace established and what it did not.
6. **What's the verdict, and what would change it?** Supported, qualified, contradicted or
   untested, and the study that would settle it.

And the rule that governs the last step: untested is not false. It tells you how much weight a claim
can bear, not whether to throw it away.

## What people get wrong

**"A talk this many people have watched must be right."** Views measure reach. Thirty-three million
of them are a fact about the talk's audience, not about conversation, and the rules still had to be
checked one at a time.

**"One rule fails in one case, so the rule is worthless."** Rule 6 is right in the cases it names and
overstated as "never". The useful verdict was a condition, not the bin.

**"Advice with no citation is false."** Almost nothing in the talk cites research, and her dismissal
of eye contact as a sign of listening lines up with what lesson 15 concluded. Untested and wrong are
different verdicts.

**"A speaker who's wrong about one number is wrong about everything."** She stretched the Pew
finding. That is a reason to check her other numbers, not a reason to doubt her advice on open
questions, which mostly holds.

**"If a study backs part of a rule, it backs all of it."** The texting experiment reaches the
visible half of rule 1, not the wandering mind. Ask which part of a rule the study actually tested.

## Practice

:::exercise One piece of advice, checked
This is paper work on published advice, and it can be your project's fourth part, "one claim checked". Take 15 minutes now; the write-up for the project can come later.

1. Find one piece of popular advice about conversation or listening (a listicle, a short video, a workplace training slide) that makes at least three separate claims, and write the three down in its own words.

2. For each claim, run this lesson's checklist, give it a verdict, and name the lesson of this course that bears on it, or write "none".

3. Trace one number or one quotation as far back as you can. Write down where the trail stopped, what the trace established, and what it did not.

4. For one untested claim, write one sentence describing the study that would test it.

Keep it private; nothing here gets posted. Choose advice about ordinary conversation. If what you find is about responding to someone at risk of suicide, check it only against lesson 11 and the services' own pages, and do not try any of it on anyone as a test.
:::
