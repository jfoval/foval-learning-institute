You have probably stood in a doorway saying goodbye for longer than the visit seemed to warrant, or
stayed on a phone call past the point where either of you had anything to add, each waiting for the
other to wind it up. And you've probably walked away from a first conversation with someone new and
spent the bus ride home replaying the thing you said that came out wrong, deciding they found you
dull. Both are about the end of a conversation, and both are about the same question: what was the
other person feeling, which they didn't say?

This lesson takes three views of the end of a conversation: a study of when people wanted theirs to
end, what recordings show about how people actually close one, and a set of studies on how people
judge, afterwards, how much the other person liked them. The last section asks whether these are one
mistake or two.

## Do conversations end when people want them to?

In 2021 four psychologists, Adam Mastroianni, Daniel Gilbert, Gus Cooney and Timothy Wilson, published [two studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7958231/)
asking a question that, as they note, behavioural science had not answered.[1] Linguists had described
the "closing rituals" people use to end a conversation, "but they have not studied how and when people
decide to use them".[1]

**Study 1** was an online survey of 806 people recruited through Amazon's Mechanical Turk, a site for
small paid tasks, and screened to be English-speaking and familiar with American culture. Each
recalled their most recent in-person conversation with one other person, lasting 1 to 45 minutes;
79% were with a partner, friend or family member, and about 2% with a stranger.[1] Each said whether
there had been a point when they felt ready for it to end, and if so when; if not, how much longer
they would have liked it to go on.

:::predict What share of people do you think said there was a point when they'd felt ready for the conversation to end? And on average, did people wish their conversations had been longer or shorter than they were?

Two thirds: 66.51% said there was a point when they had felt ready to end.[1]

And on average, people wished their conversation had been *longer*, by 24.08% of its length.[1] If you guessed "shorter", keep reading: the next paragraph shows why that average hides most of what happened.
:::

That second figure looks like a mild complaint, and it isn't. Suppose one person wished their
conversation had been half as long again and another wished theirs had been half as short. Average
the two wishes and you get zero, yet each of them missed by half. When wishes point both ways, a plain
average lets them cancel. So the authors also averaged the *size* of each person's gap, ignoring its
direction. On that measure the typical mismatch was 56.01% of the conversation.[1] In minutes, people
wished their conversations had been about 2 minutes longer on average, but 6.6 minutes different.[1]

**Study 2** checked the same thing in a laboratory, where the researchers could ask *both* people. They
recruited 366 people from two Harvard study pools, which draw on both students and the public (average
age about 23), paired each with a stranger, and told each pair to talk "for as little time or as much
time as you like", between 1 and 45 minutes.[1] Everyone had to stay for the full hour whatever they
did, so nobody had a reason to finish early, and afterwards each person, separately, answered the same
questions about themselves and about their partner. One surprise: 57 of the 183 pairs talked for the
full 45 minutes and had to be stopped. Because nobody knows when they would have stopped, the authors
left them out, and report that doing so does not meaningfully change the results.[1] That left 126
pairs, 252 people.

Here is what happened to those 126 conversations:[1]

- In 1.59%, both people wanted to end at the same moment, and did.
- In 29.37%, the conversation ended when one of the two wanted it to.
- In 46.83%, both people had wanted it to end sooner than it did.
- In 9.52%, both people had wanted it to go on longer.

### What that would look like in a hundred conversations

The percentages are hard to picture, so turn them into conversations. Imagine 100 conversations like
the ones in Study 2. Work out, before you open the checkpoint, how many ended when both people wanted;
how many ended when one person wanted; how many overran for both people; and how many stopped too soon
for both. Then notice that the four do not add up to 100, and work out what the rest must have been.

:::checkpoint Out of 100 conversations like Study 2's, how many fall in each group, and what happened in the ones left over?

Both wanted, and it ended then: about 1 or 2 (1.59). One person got their moment: about 29. Overran for both: about 47. Too soon for both: about 10.

Those add up to about 87, so about 13 are left. Those are conversations that ended at neither person's moment but somewhere between the two: one person had wanted to stop earlier, the other later. That remainder is this course's arithmetic on the paper's figures, not a number the paper prints, but it fits the authors' own summary that most conversations "did not end at any point between the conversants' incompatible desires".[1]

If your leftovers came to zero, you probably read the groups as covering every case. They don't: nobody in the leftover group got their moment, and the two people weren't on the same side of it.

### Nobody could tell

Could people at least tell when their partner wanted to stop? In Study 2, where both partners
answered, people's guesses about when the other had wanted to end were off by 63.84% of the
conversation on average.[1] And they thought they and their partner wanted much the same thing: they
believed their two wishes differed by about 29% of the conversation, when in fact they differed by
about 68%, "more than twice as large as the participants and their partners thought it was".[1]

The authors' summary, in the discussion: "it appears that when two humans talk, one almost always
wants to stop talking before the other one does."[1] Notice what that does not say: that people want
out early. In Study 1 the average person wanted *more*, and the authors single out the Study 2 pairs,
nearly one in ten, who "did not get what they wanted because both conversants wanted to talk longer
than they did".[1]

### The authors' explanation

Why can't people sort this out? The authors' abstract suggests that ending a conversation is a classic
coordination problem, one that "humans are unable to solve because doing so requires information that
they normally keep from each other."[1] A coordination problem is one
where what each person wants to do depends on what they think the other wants. Two people who each
would happily stay if the other wanted to could both leave, each thinking they were doing the other a
favour.

The information people keep back is when they'd like to stop, and the reason, on the authors'
account, is that saying so risks offending. People "may generally mask those desires when they arise
and may instead wait for the conversation to pass a suitably ambiguous offramp".[1] And they noticed
that intimates and strangers behaved alike: the conventions that lead people to hide it "may be
referred to as politeness when they govern behavior toward strangers and as kindness when they govern
behavior toward intimates".[1]

That explanation is the authors' reading of their data. They measured the mismatch and the poor
guessing; they did not test the masking directly, so keep the abstract's word, "suggest".

### What kind of evidence this is

Run the course's question over it. **Finding or advice?** A finding. **Who, and where?** Study 1 was
online survey workers, screened for familiarity with American culture, remembering a recent conversation, mostly with someone close; Study 2
was people from Harvard's study pools talking with a stranger in a lab, knowing they were being
recorded, and Study 2 itself pools three smaller studies with near-identical procedures.[1] **Compared
with what?** With each person's own reported wish, and in Study 2 with their partner's.

It is two studies in one paper from one group, so this course labels it **one group's programme**. The
authors themselves write that "Future research will determine whether these results generalize to a
wider range of human beings and social situations."[1] This course searched the papers that cite it and
found no replication by an outside team.

## How a conversation is actually closed

If people rarely say "I'd like to stop now", how do conversations end at all? Nearly half a century before the
endings study, two conversation analysts had already described how. Emanuel Schegloff and Harvey
Sacks's 1973 paper "Opening up closings", free from the [ISCA Schegloff
archive](https://www.conversationanalysis.org/schegloff-publication-archive/), is **observed in
recordings**: close study of conversations they had collected over several years, all in American
English and many of them telephone calls.[2] It counts nothing.

Their starting point is lesson 1's turn-taking system. At every place where a turn could end, the turn-taking rules make it
somebody's turn next. So one person can't just stop talking and leave it there: a silence where a turn
is due is heard as a silence, a thing someone is doing. In their words, "simply to stop talking is not
a solution to the closing problem", and an attempt to close that way "would be analyzed for actions
being accomplished in the conversation, e.g., anger, brusqueness, pique, etc."[2] The problem a closing
solves is how two people can agree, together, to stop the machinery.

The last step is familiar from lesson 2. The **terminal exchange**, a "bye" answered by a "bye", is an
adjacency pair: the first "bye" makes the second one due, and once it comes, nothing further is owed.[2]
But a "bye" out of nowhere would cut off anything the other person still wanted to say, so in the
recordings the terminal exchange comes at the end of a closing section, which has to be opened
first.

### Pre-closings

Schegloff and Sacks call it a **pre-closing**, or more exactly a *possible* pre-closing. The plainest
kind is a turn that consists of nothing but "We-ell...", "O.K..." or "So-oo", said with a falling tone,
at a point where a topic has come to an end.[2] It does not add to the topic and it does not start a new
one. The speaker, in effect, passes: I've nothing more on this.

What makes it work is what it hands the other person. A pass gives them a free turn in which they can
bring up something new, the one more thing they'd meant to mention, without seeming to change the
subject. That's why it is only a *possible* pre-closing: a new topic can follow it and run a long
way.[2] If instead the other person passes too ("O.K."
answered with "O.K."), the two of them have agreed that neither has anything more, and the closing has
begun. As the paper puts it, "The pre-closing ceases to be 'pre-' if accepted".[2]

Now put that beside lesson 2. Lesson 2 showed that a "no" tends to come late and cushioned, because
accepting and agreeing are the replies a sequence is built for. A pre-closing gets round that. It
does not propose ending and wait for a yes or a no. It offers the other person a turn, and if they have
something to say, they say it; nobody has been refused. That connection is **this course's reading**,
built from two lessons: Schegloff and Sacks describe the pass and the offered turn, lesson 2 described
the preference, and neither puts it in those words.

Other pre-closings announce a reason to close, and several refer to the other person's interests. From
a call about a lunch:[2]

> "Well I'll letchu go. I don't wanna tie up your phone."

Some use something the other person said earlier. Near the start of one call, the person who answered
asks whether the caller is watching *Daktari*, a TV programme, and describes what is happening in it;
minutes later the caller closes with "Okay, I
letcha go back tuh watch yer Daktari."[2] The authors point out that the routine questions at the start
of a conversation ("What are you doing?") can turn up exactly the material that later warrants the
close. And "I gotta go" can even cut into a topic, and does not invite a new one the way "O.K."
does.[2]

Whether the same forms work the same way in other languages, the paper does not say, and this course
has not read a study that checked, or one that counted how often each form is used.

### The end of a phone call, marked up

Taavi and Rozenn are friends. They've been talking for twenty minutes about a party they're hosting
together on Saturday. Near the start of the call, Taavi mentioned he had a pile of marking to get
through tonight. This is how it ends.

```
1  Rozenn:  So I'll bring the folding chairs,
            and you're doing the cake.
2  Taavi:   Cake's sorted.
3  Rozenn:  Okay.
4  Taavi:   Oh, did you ever hear back
            about the flat?
5  Rozenn:  I did! They said yes. I move
            in on the fifth.
6  Taavi:   That's brilliant. You'll have
            to show me round.
7  Rozenn:  I will. Right, well. I'll let
            you get back to your marking.
8  Taavi:   It won't mark itself. See you
            Saturday.
9  Rozenn:  See you Saturday. Bye.
10 Taavi:   Bye.
```

:::checkpoint Before reading on, find three things: the first possible pre-closing and what happened to it, the second one and what kind it is, and the terminal exchange.

**Line 3 is the first possible pre-closing.** Lines 1 and 2 finish a topic, making arrangements, which Schegloff and Sacks describe as a kind of topic that makes a close relevant. Rozenn's lone "Okay." passes. Taavi uses the free turn it gives him to raise something new at line 4: the flat. That topic runs for three lines, and nobody has been refused anything.

**Line 7 is the second pre-closing**, and it's the other kind: it announces a reason to close, and the reason is Taavi's, taken from the start of the call. Taavi accepts at line 8 by agreeing and bringing back the arrangement ("See you Saturday"), which leaves nothing open.

**Lines 9 and 10 are the terminal exchange**, a "bye" and the "bye" it makes due.

If you marked line 2 as a pre-closing: "Cake's sorted" adds to the topic, it doesn't pass. If you thought line 4 was rude, look at where it comes: straight after a pass, which is the place the recordings show as open for a new topic.
:::

## Putting the two together

The recordings show a system that lets two people end a conversation smoothly, with nobody refused and
a chance, at each pass, to raise one more thing. The endings study shows that, smooth or not,
conversations mostly don't end when the people in them want. On **this course's reading**, the two fit:
the pre-closing system manages the ending without carrying the information that would time it well.
An "Okay." at the end of a topic does not say whether its speaker is desperate to leave or would happily
go on for an hour.

Nothing here tests a technique. No study asked people to offer a pre-closing earlier, or to say
honestly that they were ready, and measured what happened. So the course's reading stays modest: a
pre-closing offered at the end of a topic is the ordinary way to begin to leave, and it gives the other
person a turn to keep going if they want to. Whether offering one sooner, or more honestly, would close
the gap the endings study found is untested.

## How it went: the liking gap

Now the second question from the start: afterwards, how much did they like you? In 2018 Erica Boothby,
Gus Cooney, Gillian Sandstrom and Margaret Clark published [five
studies](https://clarkrelationshiplab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/BoothbyCooneySandstromClark2018.pdf)
on it, run at Yale, Harvard and in the UK. Their abstract names the finding: after conversations,
"people systematically underestimated how much their conversation partners liked them and enjoyed
their company, an illusion we call the liking gap."[3]

In the first study, 36 people recruited by fliers near Yale's campus came to the lab in same-sex pairs
of strangers and talked for about five minutes, with a sheet of ice-breaker questions. Then, separately, each rated on a 1-to-7 scale how much they liked the other person, and
how much they thought the other person liked them.[3]

On average, people rated their liking of their partner at 5.82 and their partner's liking of them at
5.17.[3] That has to be a mistake, because each person was both a liker and a liked, so the two
averages cover the same pairings. In the authors' words, "it cannot logically be true that participants,
on average, liked their conversation partners more than their conversation partners liked them".[3]
So the gap between the two ratings is an error in the guesses.

The study also measured four personality traits. Only one changed the size of the gap: shyness. Split
into thirds, the shyest people's gap was 1.14 points on the seven-point scale, the middle third's 0.55,
and the least shy third's 0.25, which was not statistically significant.[3] Self-esteem, narcissism
and sensitivity to rejection made no difference. Keep the size in view: each third was ten people.

:::predict The conversations in that first study were filmed. Two research assistants who didn't know the hypothesis watched the videos and rated how much each person seemed to like the other. Could they tell? And did their ratings match what people actually felt, or what people thought their partner felt?

They could tell. The coders' ratings predicted how much people actually liked their partners, but not how much people *thought* their partners liked them.[3]

So the signals of liking were there on the video. The authors' conclusion: people "neglected this information when estimating how much their conversation partners liked them".[3] Fifteen videos were coded, so this too is a small study.
:::

### The authors' explanation, and what they tested

Why would people miss the signs? The second study, with 84 Yale students and recent graduates talking
freely for five minutes, tested one answer. Afterwards, each wrote down the top three moments that had
shaped their impression of the partner, and the top three they thought had shaped the partner's
impression of them, and rated how positive or negative each moment was.[3] The moments people thought
had shaped the partner's view of them were more negative than the moments that had shaped their own
view of the partner. And statistically, that difference accounted for part of the liking gap, though
not all of it.[3]

That is the mechanism this lesson gives: **harsher thoughts about your own performance**, tested by
Boothby and colleagues and accounting for part of the gap. The authors offer further possible reasons
in their discussion, drawn from other research and not tested here, and the lesson leaves them there.
Their introduction adds why the question is hard at all: "conversations are conspiracies of politeness
in which people do not reveal their true feelings".[3]

### Longer conversations, workshops and a year of suitemates

Three more studies took it outside five-minute lab chats.[3]

- **Longer conversations.** At the Harvard Decision Science Lab, 102 people, students and members of
  the public, paired with a stranger and talked as long as they liked, from 2 to 45 minutes. People
  underestimated both how much the partner liked them and how much the partner enjoyed it. People who
  talked longer liked each other more, but the gap did not shrink with length. (Split into three length
  groups, the enjoyment gap was clear in all of them, and the liking gap only in the medium one; the
  short and long groups had 18 and 24 people.)
- **Workshops.** At "How to Talk to Strangers" workshops in the UK, 100 people rated how interesting
  they found a new partner after five minutes (4.30 on a five-point scale) and how interesting they
  thought the partner found them (3.61).
- **Suitemates.** 102 first-year Yale students, living in suites assigned by the college rather than
  at random, rated their suitemates five times between September and May. People underestimated how
  much their suitemates liked them "at all time points except for the final one".[3] By May the gap
  was gone.

:::predict Why do you think the gap had disappeared by May?

Nobody knows. The authors offer two possibilities: by then the students knew each other well, or they were deciding whether to live together the next year, which "may have forced discussions that revealed liking".[3] They tested neither.

So the accurate summary is that the gap lasted **for several months** in these suitemates and was gone by the last survey, not that it lasts indefinitely. Offering people a $100 prize for accuracy at that last survey made no difference to their estimates, which the authors take as a sign that people meant what they reported.[3]
:::

### What kind of evidence the liking gap is

**One group's programme.** Five studies in one paper, with samples from Yale, Harvard's lab pool and UK workshops. The paper says its design and
analysis plans were not preregistered, and it made its data public.[3] Other labs have published
related studies; this course read only their abstracts and doesn't rely on them, and its search of the
papers citing the 2018 study found no direct replication by an outside team.

### Joaquina and the new neighbour

Joaquina has just moved into a terraced street. On Sunday the woman next door came out while Joaquina
was carrying boxes, and they talked for about forty minutes over the garden wall: the street, the
bins, where each had lived before. Walking back inside, Joaquina starts replaying it. She talked too
much about her old job. She laughed at something that wasn't meant as a joke. She forgot the woman's
name halfway through. By the evening she has decided the neighbour found her hard work.

Before you open the checkpoint, write two lists: what the liking-gap studies give Joaquina reason to
think, and what they don't tell her.

:::checkpoint What does the evidence say to Joaquina, and what doesn't it say?

What it gives her: after first conversations, the people in these studies, strangers in labs, workshop attendees and new suitemates, typically underestimated how much the other person liked them, and the gap went with more negative thoughts about their own performance. Replaying her worst moments is exactly the pattern in the second study. And a forty-minute conversation is not too long for the gap: in the lab study where people talked as long as they liked, it did not shrink with length. So she has good reason to hold her gloomy verdict loosely.

What it doesn't tell her: how *this* neighbour felt. The gap is an average over many pairs, and an average leaves room for partners who liked someone less than that person guessed. Nor does it mean everyone likes her; it means people's guesses about being liked ran low. And the samples were students, lab pools and UK workshop-goers, not neighbours over a garden wall.

What would tell her more: the neighbour, over time. Lesson 7, "Ask, don't guess", found that asking beat imagining when people tried to predict another person's opinions. She can't sensibly ask "Did you like me?", but the next conversation, and whether the neighbour starts one, is information her replay can't supply.
:::

## The same kind of mistake?

Set the two findings side by side, with lesson 12's.

- **At the start** (lesson 12), people underestimated how willing a stranger would be to talk, and how
  much the other would care about what they disclosed.
- **At the end**, people could barely tell when their partner wanted to stop, and thought their two
  wishes were closer than they were.
- **Afterwards**, people underestimated how much their partner liked them.

What they share is that each is a guess about something the other person felt and didn't say. Both
papers point to conventions of politeness for why it goes unsaid: Mastroianni's group to the
politeness and kindness that keep people from announcing they're ready to go, Boothby's group to
"conspiracies of politeness".[1][3] The research groups overlap, too: Gus Cooney is an author of both
papers. So on **this course's reading**, they are the same kind of misjudgement: a person judging what's in someone else's head from what's in their own, about
something the other has good social reasons not to say.

They differ in direction, and that matters. The liking gap runs one way: guesses about being liked ran
low. The endings mismatch runs both ways: some people wanted to stop sooner, some later, and the error
was in how far apart the two people were. So the liking gap is a reason to discount your own gloomy
verdict a little, on average. The endings finding is not a reason to stay longer or to leave sooner;
it is a reason not to trust your guess either way.

Neither tells you what one particular person felt, and neither was tested outside the people and
settings described above. What lesson 7 said still holds: asking beats guessing, and where asking
would be odd, what the other person does next is better evidence than your replay.

## What people get wrong

**"They'll tell you when they want to go."** In the lab study, people's guesses about when their
partner wanted to stop were off by about two thirds of the conversation on average, and only 1.59% of
conversations ended when both wanted.[1]

**"Everyone's secretly desperate to leave."** In Study 1 the average person wanted their conversation
to be *longer*, and in about one lab pair in ten both people wanted to go on.[1] The finding is a
mismatch, not a universal wish to escape.

**"They didn't like me much."** After first conversations, people in the 2018 studies typically
underestimated how much the partner liked them, and the gap was largest in the shyest.[3]

**"So the liking gap means everyone liked me."** It's an average error in guesses, not a guarantee
about any person, and an average leaves room for partners who liked someone less than that person
guessed.[3]

**"Ending a conversation first is rude."** In the recordings Schegloff and Sacks describe, somebody has
to make the first move, and the ordinary ways are a pass like "Okay" or "Well" at the end of a topic,
or a reason that refers to the other person's time. It leaves the other a turn to raise anything more.[2] What reads as rude is stopping
without that: a silence where a turn is due.

**"The average person got about the length they wanted."** That's the signed average, where wishes
for longer and shorter cancel. The average size of the gap was about half the conversation.[1]

## Practice

:::exercise Five endings, noticed
This is a **notice** exercise. Take 20 minutes in all: a few minutes after each of the next five conversations you're part of that end in the ordinary way, and ten minutes at the end to read them over.

For each ending, write from memory, within an hour:

1. Who made the first move towards ending, you or the other person, and how: a lone "Okay" or "Right" or "Well", a reason ("I'll let you get on"), "I've got to go", or something else.
2. Whether it came at the end of a topic or cut into one.
3. Whether a new topic came in after the first move, and who raised it.
4. The terminal exchange, if there was one: what the last two turns were.

Name nobody, and write nothing that would let someone identify the other person. Phone calls, doorstep goodbyes and the end of a chat at work all count; messages don't. If one of the conversations was about suicide, self-harm, abuse or assault, leave it out and choose another; lesson 11, "When it's serious", and Mental Fitness lesson 1, "Where ordinary ends", are where the institute deals with that conversation.
:::

## For your journal

This lesson adds endings to what the journal notices: who pre-closed, how, and whether a new topic came
in. It also adds one line after any first conversation with someone new: how much you think they liked
you, written straight away, so that as you get to know them you can look back at your first guess. For the project's
conversation analysis, "how it ended" is now something you can describe move by move. And if you want a
move to try, offer an honest pre-closing a little earlier than usual in one ordinary conversation you'd
like to end, at the end of a topic and never with someone who is upset, and note what the other person
does with the turn it gives them.
