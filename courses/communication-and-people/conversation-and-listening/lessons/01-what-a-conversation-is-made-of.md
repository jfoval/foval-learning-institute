Most of what goes wrong between people goes wrong in ordinary talk. A question that wasn't really a
question, an answer to something the other person didn't say, a turn that went on too long, a
silence that meant more than anyone intended. It's tempting to think of conversation as the loose,
unplanned part of life, the part without rules. It isn't. When researchers timed the answers to
questions in recorded conversations in ten languages, the same things slowed an answer down in every
one of them. Ordinary talk runs on a system precise enough to measure in milliseconds, and you already
run it without knowing its parts.

This lesson names the parts: the turn, the places where it can change hands, the three rules for who
speaks next, and what the timing says about what a listener is doing while the other person talks.
Then it sets out how the course labels its evidence and the question it asks of every claim. The
exercise has you listen to a published conversation and start the journal the course project is
built from.

## Turns, and the places they change hands

In 1974 three researchers, Harvey Sacks, Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson, published a model of
how people take turns at talk. It was built from recordings of American English conversation: group
therapy sessions, telephone calls, everyday talk, transcribed and analysed by hand. It measured
nothing. It listed the facts any account of conversation has to explain, and proposed the simplest
system that would produce them.[1] You can read [the paper free from the International
Society for Conversation Analysis](https://www.conversationanalysis.org/schegloff-publication-archive/).

Two of their facts are the ones most often quoted. "Overwhelmingly, one party talks at a time." And
"Occurrences of more than one speaker at a time are common, but brief."[1] Most changes of speaker
happen with no gap and no overlap, or only a slight one. So something is coordinating people very
closely, with no one in charge.

Their answer starts with the unit a turn is built from. A turn is made of one or more
**turn-constructional units**: a sentence, a clause, a phrase, even a single word ("Tuesday?"). As a
unit unfolds, a listener can hear roughly where it will be complete. The point where a unit could be
complete is a **transition-relevance place**, the place where the turn could change hands. It does not
have to change hands there. It just could.

At each of those places, the model says, three rules apply, in order:[1]

- **Rule 1a.** If the current speaker has picked out who goes next, for example by addressing a
  question to them, that person has the right, and the obligation, to speak next.
- **Rule 1b.** If nobody has been picked out, anyone may start, and the first to start gets the turn.
- **Rule 1c.** If nobody else starts, the current speaker may carry on, though they need not.

If the current speaker carries on, the same three rules apply again at the next possible completion,
and so on. The order matters: being selected outranks starting first, which outranks carrying on.
The authors called the system "locally managed", run turn by turn by the people in it rather than
set in advance, and nothing about who talks, for how long or about what is fixed beforehand.[1]

### A lift to the station

Bram and Linnea are arranging a lift. This is the whole exchange.

```
1  Bram:    Are you still okay to drive me
            to the station tomorrow?
2  Linnea:  Yes. What time's your train?
3  Bram:    Ten past eight.
4  Linnea:  Then I'll pick you up at half seven.
```

Line 1 is one unit, a question, and the transition-relevance place comes at its end. The question is
addressed to Linnea, so **rule 1a** makes her the next speaker. She is not only allowed to answer;
she's expected to, and if she didn't, the silence would be noticed (lesson 2 is about what that
silence says).

Line 2 is two units. "Yes." is complete on its own, so there's a transition-relevance place right
after it. Bram could have come in there ("Great, thanks"). He didn't, and Linnea carried on: that's
**rule 1c**, the current speaker continuing. Her second unit, "What time's your train?", is a question
to Bram, so **rule 1a** selects him.

Line 3 is an answer, and it doesn't select anyone. At its end, nobody has been picked out, so
**rule 1b** applies: anyone may start. Linnea does, and her line 4 closes the arrangement.

Four lines of ordinary talk, and all three rules turned up, with nobody planning any of it in
advance.

:::predict Before the numbers. When one person asks a question and the other answers, how long is the typical silence between the end of the question and the start of the answer? Write down a figure, in seconds or milliseconds.

In the ten-language study in the next section, the most common gap was 0 milliseconds, the middle value was 100 milliseconds, and the average was 208 milliseconds: about a fifth of a second.

If you guessed half a second or more, the real figure is about the length of one syllable, far shorter than the time it takes to get even one word ready to say. That mismatch is the puzzle two sections from now.
:::

## The numbers that came later

The 1974 paper claimed a general system but left one question open on purpose. Whether it held in
other languages, the authors wrote, "can, of course, be settled only empirically".[1] That took
thirty-five years.

In 2009 Tanya Stivers and ten colleagues compared video recordings of informal conversation in ten
languages from five continents, from Danish, English and Japanese to Tzeltal (spoken in Mexico),
Yélî Dnye (Papua New Guinea) and ‡Ākhoe Hai‖om (Namibia).[2] The recordings were of two to six
consenting people talking however they liked, often while eating or doing something else. For each
language a contributor took 350 consecutive questions, and to compare like with like the analysis
kept to **yes/no questions** and their answers, with the gap measured to the nearest 100 ms. The
paper is [free to read](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2705608/).

In every language the gaps clustered in one peak, with the most common value between 0 and 200 ms.
Across all ten, the most common gap (the **mode**) was 0 ms, the middle value (the **median**) 100
ms, and the average (the **mean**) 208 ms. Every language's mean fell within about 250 ms of that
either side, which the authors put at about the time it takes to say one English syllable. And the
same things slowed an answer everywhere: non-answers such as "I don't know" came later than answers,
and "no" later than "yes", which is lesson 2. The authors' summary was that every language showed "a
general avoidance of overlapping talk and a minimization of silence between conversational
turns".[2]

Three things about those numbers matter for the rest of the course. First, "the gap is 200 ms" is
shorthand for the centre of a wide spread, which included long gaps, so it matters which statistic
someone quotes. Second, this is **one study across ten languages**, of one kind of turn in one kind of
setting; the authors noted that courts, church services and news interviews run on different
rules.[2] Third, the headline agrees with other collections of recorded talk: Stephen Levinson's
review of the field, citing several studies, puts the modal gap between turns "around 200 ms".[3]
When this course calls the timing well established, that agreement, with its scope, is what it
means.

:::predict The study was partly a test of reports from anthropologists, which described Japanese conversation as leaving long silences and Italian conversation as tolerant of overlap. Of the Japanese, Italian and Danish recordings, which do you expect had the fastest average answer, and which the slowest?

In the authors' words, "Danish has the slowest response time on average (+469 ms) and Japanese has the fastest (+7 ms)." The Italian conversations averaged +310 ms, slightly slower than the overall mean, with only 17% of responses in overlap, "not at all an unusual proportion". The Danish recordings fitted the reports of Nordic slowness best, but even there the most common gap was about 100 ms.[2]

If you expected the Italian conversations to be fastest, you were predicting from the reputation the study was testing.
:::

So the reported differences were real in direction for Danish and wrong for Japanese and Italian, and
in every case the differences were fractions of a second. The authors' reading was that local
variations are "quantitative only", and they offered an explanation for why travellers report
dramatic differences anyway: within a community the tempo is tight, and "Speakers become
hypersensitive to perturbations in timing of responses, measured in 100 ms or less."[2] On their account, an outsider
used to a faster tempo hears a quarter of a second as a meaningful silence; an insider, calibrated
to the local norm, hears it as on time. That explanation is the authors' argument, not something the
study tested directly.

One caution before lesson 15 takes this further. Each language here was one corpus, often from one
community, and the Japanese, Danish and Italian conversations were recorded among particular groups
of speakers. The study measured those recordings. It says nothing about what any individual Japanese,
Danish or Italian speaker does.

## Why you have to plan while they're still talking

The timing creates a puzzle. Getting a word ready to say is slow. Lab studies in which
people name or describe pictures as fast as they can put the preparation for a single word at about 600 ms if
the word has been primed, about 1,000 ms if not, and about 1,500 ms for a short clause. Levinson's
summary: "preparation before output begins takes 600 ms for a single word if primed".[3] Yet the
answers in the recordings typically begin about 200 ms after the question ends.

Those two figures come from different places, which is worth saying plainly. The planning times are
from laboratory picture tasks, not from conversation; the gaps are from recorded talk.[4]
Levinson's argument joins them, and the arithmetic is simple enough to do yourself.

### The arithmetic, done once

Say an answer begins 200 ms after the question ends, and the answer is a single word that needs at
least 600 ms of preparation. Count back from the moment the answer starts:

1. The answer starts at +200 ms (200 ms after the question ended).
2. Preparation took at least 600 ms before that, so it began at 200 − 600 = −400 ms.
3. A negative figure means before the question ended.

So the listener started preparing at least 400 ms before the question was finished, while the other
person was still speaking. For a short clause, on the same arithmetic, the start moves back to about
1,300 ms before the end. Levinson puts the average turn at around two seconds, so for a longer answer
the planning begins somewhere near the middle of what the listener is hearing.[3] This is this
course's arithmetic on Levinson's figures, not a measurement of any one conversation.

Levinson's conclusion, in a 2016 review: "one must plan while still listening and predicting what the
rest of the incoming turn will contain".[3] On his account a listener has to work out early what kind
of act the turn is (a question, an offer, a request), start building a reply to it, and estimate
from its grammar and meaning when it will end; he cites brain-recording (EEG) studies suggesting that
the kind of act can be recognised within a turn's first 400 ms.[3] That account is the standard one
in the field, and the course uses it as Levinson's, with [the longer review he wrote with Francisco
Torreira](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4464110/) as the place to read it in full.[4]

:::checkpoint Try the arithmetic on a new case. A reply begins 300 ms after a statement ends, and it is a short clause that takes about 1,500 ms to prepare. When did the listener start preparing it, relative to the end of the statement?

Count back from +300 ms: 300 − 1,500 = −1,200. So preparation began about 1,200 ms before the statement ended, well inside it.

If you got 1,800 ms, you added the 300 instead of subtracting it: the reply starts after the end, so the gap shortens the distance back, it does not lengthen it.
:::

### What that means for "waiting for your turn"

A common complaint about bad listeners is that they are just waiting for their turn to talk,
planning a reply instead of listening. On Levinson's argument, the timing means that everyone plans a
reply while listening: in the recordings, there is not time to do it any other way. So, on this course's reading, planning
during the other person's turn is not the failure, and nobody can stop doing it. What can go wrong is
what the planning is aimed at: a reply to what the person is actually saying, or the point you were
waiting to make whatever they said. Lesson 4, "How listening goes wrong", takes that distinction
apart.

## Overlap: common, and brief

The 1974 paper said overlap is "common, but brief". Both halves turned out to be true, and people
often keep only one of them.

In 2015 Levinson and Torreira analysed 348 English telephone calls from the Switchboard collection,
about 38 hours of two people talking.[4] Measured as a share of time, the recordings were 77% one
person speaking, 19.2% silence (pauses within a turn and gaps between turns), and only 3.8% two
people speaking at once. Leave the silence out and 95.3% of the speech was one voice at a time.

Then they counted a different thing: changes of speaker. Of all the moments when the floor passed
from one person to the other, 30.1% happened in overlap, with the next speaker starting before the
last had quite finished. Those overlaps were short: the most common length was 96 ms and the median
205 ms.[4]

So "overlap is rare" is true of time and false of changes of speaker. Nearly a third of handovers

:::checkpoint A friend reads the first bar and says, "So people almost never talk over each other." Another reads the second and says, "People talk over each other all the time." Who's right?

Both are right about different measures, and both are wrong as stated. Overlap is rare as a share of time (3.8%) and common as a share of changes of speaker (30.1%). The two fit together because each overlap is so short.

Any claim about "how much overlap" needs to say which of the two it means.
:::

What were the overlaps? In a sample of 200, 73% involved a **backchannel** or a brief token of
agreement: "mm hm", "uh huh", "yeah", "right", the small sounds a listener makes while someone else
talks, which do not claim a turn.[4] Schegloff, in a paper devoted to overlap, lists other kinds that
nobody treats as a problem: a next speaker starting just as the last one finishes (a **terminal
overlap**), one person helping
another find a word, two people laughing or greeting together. For the rare competitive ones he
describes a resolution that works almost syllable by syllable, and reports that "the vast majority of
overlaps are resolved to a single speaker by the third beat".[5] Overlap is not the same thing as
interruption, and lesson 5, "Let them finish", is about the difference.

## Two starts, a "mm", and an early finish

A harder case, with more people. Three housemates, Oona, Imre and Soraya, are in the kitchen.
Square brackets mark where two people's talk overlaps: the bracket on one line starts at the same
moment as the bracket on the line below.

```
1  Oona:    Has anyone seen the big pan?
2  Imre:    [It's in the-
3  Soraya:  [I think it's-
4  Imre:    It's in the oven. I put it there
            after [the roast so it'd be
5  Oona:          [mm
6  Imre:    out of the way.
7  Soraya:  Oh, that's where it went.
            Oona, are you making soup again?
8  Oona:    I am, if the pan's clea[n
9  Imre:                           [It's clean.
```

There are three overlaps here. Imre and Soraya start together at lines 2 and 3; Oona's "mm" at line 5
comes in the middle of Imre's turn; Imre's "It's clean" at line 9 starts as Oona is finishing. Not one
of them is an interruption in the sense anyone would complain about. The first is two people doing
what rule 1b invites, and one of them dropping out within a couple of syllables. The second is a
backchannel, a listener showing they're following without taking the turn. The third starts at a
place where Oona's turn was, to everyone's ear, about to end.

Now the part for you. There are four changes of speaker, or places where one could have happened,
that are worth labelling:

- **A.** From Oona's question (line 1) to Imre (line 4).
- **B.** After "It's in the oven." (line 4), where Imre carries on.
- **C.** From Soraya (line 7) to Oona (line 8).
- **D.** From Oona (line 8) to Imre (line 9).

:::checkpoint Label each of A to D with the rule that decided it (1a, 1b or 1c), and say why, before you open this.

**A: rule 1b.** "Has anyone seen..." is addressed to the room, not to one person, so nobody was selected. Two people self-selected at once; Soraya dropped out within a couple of syllables and Imre held the turn.

**B: rule 1c.** "It's in the oven." is a complete unit, a transition-relevance place. Nobody else started, and Imre carried on.

**C: rule 1a.** Soraya names Oona and asks her a question, which selects her as next speaker.

**D: rule 1b.** Oona's turn selects nobody. Imre self-selects, starting a fraction early, in the terminal overlap described above.

If you labelled A as 1a because Oona asked a question: a question selects someone only when it is addressed to them. "Has anyone" is a question to whoever wants it, which is exactly the case rule 1b covers. If you labelled B as nothing at all, look again at "It's in the oven.": it could have been the end of Imre's turn, and a place where a turn could end is where the rules apply, whether or not it changes hands.
:::

### What it covers, and what it leaves to other courses

This course is about one-to-one and small-group conversation: how it works (lessons 1 and 2), what
listening is and how it fails (3 to 5), the listener's tools, from questions to saying back what you
heard (6 to 9), the two conversations where listening matters most, someone with a problem and a
friend who may be at risk of suicide (10 and 11), and beginnings, endings, bodies and people who
differ from you (12 to 16). Lesson 17 checks a famous talk about conversation against everything
before it.

Neighbouring subjects belong to other courses, most of them later in this term and not yet
written: speaking to an audience (Public Speaking), manners and occasions such as introductions and
apologies (Social Skills and Etiquette), settling disagreements (Conflict Resolution), couples' and
parenting methods (Relationships and Family), emotion theory (Understanding People) and changing
someone's mind (Persuasion and Influence). This course does not teach counselling, risk assessment or
lie detection. If a conversation you're part of turns to suicide or a crisis, lesson 11 covers what
a friend does, and Mental Fitness lesson 1, "Where ordinary ends", has the crisis lines.

This course is education, not therapy or professional advice about your own relationships or anyone's mental health.

### Seven labels for evidence, and one for the course

Advice about conversation is everywhere and most of it is confident. Some of it comes from a study
of forty students, some from a profession's decades of experience, and some from nowhere at all, and
it all sounds the same. So every finding in this course carries a label, in the sentence, saying what
kind of evidence it is:

- **Replicated**: found again by a different group, or across a pooled set of studies, and the course
  says which.
- **One group's programme**: several experiments, mostly from one lab or network, not independently
  repeated as far as this course found.
- **Single study**: one study, with its sample in the same sentence.
- **Failed to replicate, corrected or retracted**: with the replication, correction or notice named.
- **Contested**: serious researchers disagree; the course gives both positions in their own words,
  says what would settle it, and gives no verdict.
- **Observed in recordings**: the patterns conversation analysts found by studying recorded talk
  closely, like the turn-taking rules above. These are careful descriptions, not measured rates,
  and the course says where they were later counted.
- **Practitioner advice**: what a profession teaches from experience (crisis lines, medicine,
  negotiators, interviewers), always with the profession and its goal, because advice built for a
  doctor with ten minutes is not automatically advice for a friend.

And an eighth, for the course's own voice: **this course's reading**, wherever the course draws a
conclusion no single source states. You've met two already: the arithmetic on Levinson's figures, and
the point that planning during a turn is not the failure.

### The course's question

Mental Fitness lesson 2, "What a small effect is, and what the other group got", taught you to ask of
any number: what did the comparison group get? This course keeps that question and puts two in front
of it. For every claim about conversation:

1. **Finding or advice?** Is this something a study found, or something someone recommends?
2. **Who was in the room, and where?** Who was studied, or who gives the advice, and in what setting:
   strangers in a lab, a text chat, a clinic, a phone line, friends at home?
3. **Compared with what?** For any effect, what did the comparison group get?

Run it on this lesson. The turn-taking rules are observed in recordings of American English, later
tested across ten languages, but only on yes/no questions in informal talk. The overlap figures are
from one English phone corpus. The planning times come from lab picture tasks, not conversation.
None of that makes them wrong. It tells you how far each one reaches.

### The journal

A conversation course can't be practised alone, so the exercises in this course come in five kinds,
and each one says which it is. **Notice**: an entry in your observation journal. **Analyse public
talk**: a conversation already published for listening, never anyone's private recording. **Rewrite
on paper**: drafting questions or replies and comparing them with a model. **Try one move**: one small
change in a conversation you were going to have anyway. **Practise with a willing partner**: someone
who has agreed to it and knows it's practice.

The observation journal is the backbone. The design comes from a first-year seminar on conversation
at Ohio State University, where weekly journals of conversations the students noticed in their own
lives make up 40% of the grade.[6] Each entry is one ordinary conversation, written from memory
within an hour, with nobody named or identifiable. That rule is about the people you talk to: they
did not agree to be in your notes. Writing it up from memory is also useful in itself. How to Learn
Anything lesson 3, "Retrieval practice: one of the two techniques that hold up", showed that writing
down what you can remember, with the passage out of sight, beat reading it again when students were
tested a week later. Each entry is that, done on the course's ideas.

## What people get wrong

**"A good listener doesn't think about their reply until the other person has finished."** The gaps
are shorter than the time it takes to prepare a word, so replies are planned during the other
person's turn.[3] On this course's reading, the useful question is what the planning is aimed at.

**"Overlap is rare."** As a share of time, yes: under 4% in the phone calls. As a share of changes of
speaker, no: about 30%.[4] Say which you mean.

**"People answer within 200 milliseconds."** That is the centre of a spread, and which centre matters:
in the ten-language study the mode was 0 ms, the median 100 ms and the mean 208 ms, and every language
had slower answers too.[2] It's a description of what usually happens, not a rule anyone follows.

**"Conversation is just people talking, with no real structure."** Nobody plans it in advance, and
it still runs on rules precise enough that a fifth of a second can carry meaning. Being unplanned and
being unstructured aren't the same thing.

**"Some cultures leave long silences and others talk over each other."** In the recordings, the
Japanese-language conversations had the fastest average and the Italian ones an ordinary one, with
17% of answers in overlap.[2] The differences between languages were fractions of a second, and
lesson 15 takes up what they do and don't mean.

## Practice

:::exercise Five transitions, two overlaps, one entry

This exercise has two kinds of work: **analyse public talk**, then **notice**.

Take 25 minutes: about fifteen for the listening and ten for the journal entry, which you write later in the day.

1. Choose a conversation that was published for people to listen to: a podcast interview, a radio
   phone-in or a televised panel. Not a recording of anyone's private conversation, and don't record
   anyone yourself. Pick a stretch of about three minutes with at least two speakers and listen
   through once without stopping.
2. Listen again, pausing as you go, and find **five transition-relevance places**. For each, write
   the words just before it and which rule decided what happened next: 1a (someone was selected),
   1b (someone self-selected) or 1c (the speaker carried on).
3. Find **two overlaps**. For each, write what kind it was (a backchannel, two people starting
   together, a start just as the other finished, or something else) and whether it caused any
   trouble for the people talking: did anyone stop, repeat themselves or complain?
4. Start your journal. Today, have one ordinary conversation you were going to have anyway, and
   within an hour write it up from memory: roughly how it went, and one place where you noticed a
   transition, a selection (rule 1a) or an overlap. Name nobody and include nothing that would let
   someone else identify the other person. If the conversation you'd pick was about suicide,
   self-harm, abuse or assault, choose a different one for the journal; lesson 11 and Mental Fitness
   lesson 1 are there for that conversation.
:::

:::checkpoint When you've done steps 2 and 3, check your labels against these questions.

For every 1a: can you point to the words that picked the next speaker out, a name, a "you" said while looking at one person, a question only one person could answer? If not, it was probably 1b.

For every 1c: was there really a place where the turn could have ended? If the speaker was mid-sentence, it was not a transition place and no rule applied yet.

For each overlap: most overlaps in recorded talk involve a backchannel, and most are over in a syllable or two. If yours caused trouble, what made it different: did someone start mid-unit, far from any possible end? Lesson 5 is about exactly that difference.
:::

- **Tanya Stivers and colleagues, ["Universals and cultural variation in turn-taking in
  conversation"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2705608/), *PNAS*, 2009.** Free and short.
  Read the results and the conclusion for the ten-language comparison and the authors' explanation
  of why small differences feel large. This course read the full text.
- **Stephen Levinson and Francisco Torreira, ["Timing in turn-taking and its implications for
  processing models of language"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4464110/), *Frontiers in
  Psychology*, 2015.** Free. The section on the Switchboard corpus is the clearest account of overlap
  as time against overlap as transitions. This course read the full text.
- **Harvey Sacks, Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson, "A simplest systematics for the organization
  of turn-taking for conversation", *Language*, 1974**, free from the [ISCA Schegloff
  archive](https://www.conversationanalysis.org/schegloff-publication-archive/). Long and dense. The
  list of fourteen facts near the start and the rules in section 3 are the parts to read first. This
  course read the full text.
