Sooner or later you'll talk with someone whose habits aren't yours. They answer a beat later than
you expect, or look away while you're talking, or need you to face them, or want more time to get a
word out. Each of these can be misread as rudeness or inattention, and each has been studied. This
lesson takes four kinds of difference, culture, autism, hearing and age, and asks the course's
question of each: what was measured, in whom, and how large was the difference? What comes out is a
rule you've already met in lesson 7, "Ask, don't guess", now applied to the person in front of you:
the habits that show attention vary, so watch what someone says more than where they look, and when
it matters, ask how they want to be spoken with.

## A small difference, felt as large

You've met the timing data already. In Tanya Stivers and colleagues' recordings of ten languages,
from lesson 1, "What a conversation is made of", every language's average gap before answering a
yes/no question fell within about 250 ms of the overall mean, and the authors called the local
variations "quantitative only". Lesson 1 also gave their account of why
travellers still come home with stories about slow Nordic replies: each community is tuned to its
own tempo, so an outsider hears a gap that is normal locally as a meaningful pause. That is the
authors' argument, not something their study tested.[1] Lesson 2 added that a longer silence may
read as a bad sign in more than one language, even if where the line falls in each isn't known.[1]

So if someone replies a fraction slower than you're used to, that is not yet evidence that they're
reluctant. It may just be their normal pace, and if you're the faster one, you may be heard as
jumping in. On the authors' account, neither of you is doing anything wrong.

## Where a listener looks

Timing is one channel. Gaze is another, and here the differences between communities turn out to be
larger.

:::predict When one person asks another a question, who do you expect is more likely to be looking at the other's face during the question: the person asking, or the person being asked?

In recordings from three unrelated communities, the person asking. People asking a question looked at the other person more often than the person being asked looked back, in all three.[2]

If you expected the listener to look more, you expected what most earlier accounts of gaze had said. The authors call their result a surprise, because it runs against those accounts.
:::

Federico Rossano, Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson took 300 question-and-answer sequences
from roughly ten pairs of speakers in each of three communities with no historical connection:
northern Italians, Tzeltal speakers in Mexico, and Yélî Dnye speakers on Rossel Island in Papua New
Guinea. The talk was informal and natural. For each question they coded whether the person asking,
and the person being asked, looked at the other at least once while the question was being asked.[2]
That makes it a finding **observed in recordings** and then counted: one study, three communities,
questions only.

*Share of questions in which the person asking, and the person being asked, looked at the other at
least once during the question, in 300 questions from roughly ten pairs of speakers in each
community, informal conversation. Rossano, Brown and Levinson, "Gaze, questioning and culture",
2009, Table 7.1. Drawn by this course from the figures in the chapter.[2]*

Two things stand out. First, in every community the questioner looked more. Second, the people being
asked differed far more than the people asking. In the authors' words, "Q-speaker gaze behavior seems
to be much more similar across languages than Q-recipient gaze behavior." Tzeltal recipients looked
in 42.3% of questions, against 67.3% for Yélî Dnye recipients.[2]

The Tzeltal listeners weren't inattentive. They showed attention another way. Tzeltal recipients of a
story are expected to respond at regular intervals by repeating part of what was just said, and the
normal way to answer "yes" to a yes/no question is to repeat its words rather than say "yes". In one
recorded exchange someone is asked whether the other's mother is still alive, and answers "She's
alive."[2] The authors conclude that the difference between the three communities "strongly
challenges the view that looking at the speaker's face would always be the default gaze behavior for
a recipient". And they add something that rules out a simple story about the setting or the people:
"Tzeltal participants are similar to the Italian when they speak but not when they listen to
questions."[2] The same people, in the same surroundings, looked differently depending on whether they were asking or being asked.

Keep the scope attached. This is questions only, 300 in each community, among particular
groups of speakers; the authors name the restriction to question sequences as a limitation.[2] It
describes those recordings. It does not tell you where any individual Tzeltal or Italian speaker
looks.

### The same look, read differently

A second study asked not where people look, but how a look feels. Hironori Akechi and colleagues
tested 20 Finnish and 20 Japanese university students, 10 women in each group, in 2013. Each sat
facing a woman from their own country behind an electronic shutter that turned clear to show her
looking straight at them, looking away, or with her eyes closed. Nobody talked. Both groups' hearts
slowed in the same way to direct gaze, and both rated it as more arousing, so the body's response was
much the same. The ratings differed: "Japanese participants rated the model as angrier when the model
displayed a direct rather than an averted gaze, whereas gaze direction had no effect on Finnish
participants' anger ratings." Approachability went the other way round: "Finnish participants rated
direct eye contact as more approachable than displays of averted gaze; however, gaze direction had
no effect on Japanese participants' approachability ratings." Compared with the Finns, the Japanese
students also found her direct gaze less pleasant, though that difference was only marginal.[3] The
difference in anger ratings was only just statistically significant (p = .048).[3]

That is a **single study** of 40 students, with a silent face rather than a conversation, and its
authors are careful about it: "we collected data from individuals representing only one Western and
one East Asian country. Thus, we do not know to which extent the present results generalize to
Western and East Asian cultures, in general."[3] They put the difference down to "display rules and
cultural norms", learned expectations about looking, rather than to physiology.[3]

A 2020 review by Roy Hessels adds that where people look on a face depends on the task and the
situation, so cultural differences in gaze don't reduce to a simple rule.[4]

Lesson 14 quoted a US Government Accountability Office warning that behaviour natural to one culture
can look suspicious to another. You can now see one reason: where a listener looks, and how a steady
look feels, both differ between the groups measured.

## What a country's label can and cannot tell you

If groups differ, why not learn the differences country by country? It is tempting to use two
well-known frameworks that way: learn the country, predict the person. The anthropologist Edward
Hall divided cultures by how they communicate, in Geert Hofstede's summary of him "into high-context
(much of the information is implicit) and low-context cultures (nearly everything is explicit)".
Hofstede's own model gives each country a score on dimensions of national culture, such as
individualism against collectivism.[22]

**This course hasn't read Hall's or Hofstede's books.** It has read a short 2011 account by Hofstede
of his own model, and that account says something the predict-the-person use leaves out. A
dimension, he writes, "is an aspect of a culture that can be measured relative to other cultures",
and he cites more than 400 significant correlations between his country scores and other studies'
results. But culture is "always a collective phenomenon", and "Within each collective there is a
variety of individuals." His
conclusion: "national culture scores should not be used for stereotyping individuals."[22] So on
Hofstede's own account, predicting one person from a country's score misuses the model. The course
has no comparable statement from Hall himself.

The critics, read here at the level of abstracts, question something else: the country
classifications themselves.

- A systematic review by Markus Kittler, David Rygl and Alex Mackinnon in 2011 describes Hall's
  concept as the idea that people "combine preprogrammed culture specific context and information to
  create meaning", with country rankings attached to it that have changed over time. It found that
  most research using those classifications rests on "seemingly less-than-adequate evidence", and that the conventional
  classifications "are flawed or, at best, very limited".[5]
- Peter Cardon's 2008 review of 224 articles in business and technical communication journals found
  the contexting model the most cited framework, few of its propositions tested, and that the tests
  "have failed to support many contexting propositions, particularly those related to directness".[6]
- Ronald Fischer and Shalom Schwartz analysed three large value surveys, with between 41,968 and
  84,887 respondents each, in 2011. They measured values, not conversation, and found "more consensus
  than disagreement on value priorities across countries, refuting strong claims that culture
  determines values". Conformity values were the exception, looking like "good candidates for
  measuring culture as shared meaning systems".[7]

No source this course read tests Hofstede's dimensions against how people talk. Together, these
sources make a narrower claim than "culture doesn't matter". The Rossano and Akechi studies show that
groups do differ in measurable ways. The reviews question the country labels, and Hofstede himself
warns against reading a person off a national score. Either way, a label is a weak guide to the
person in front of you.

So this is **contested**. How far cultural variation goes, and whether national typologies help,
are open questions. What would settle them is recordings of natural conversation across many
communities, with the variation within each country reported beside the differences between
countries. There is also a value question that evidence cannot settle: how far you should adjust to
someone else's habits, and how far they should adjust to yours.

On this course's reading, the useful stance follows from both sides at once. Differences exist, so
don't assume your habits are everyone's. A label predicts poorly, so do not assume you know someone's
habits from where they come from. Watch and ask.

## The colleague who looks away

Ottilie is briefing a new colleague, Cosimo, on how their team handles customer complaints. Through
most of it he looks at the desk or out of the window, and at one point she wonders whether any of it
is going in.

List what his looking away could mean, using only this lesson so far and lesson 14. It could be a
habit of where a listener looks, of the kind Rossano's recordings found differs between communities.
It could be that looking at someone's face makes it harder for him to listen, which, as the next
section shows, many autistic people report. It could be that he's bored or thinking about something
else. Or it could be none of these: lesson 14 found no body movement with one fixed meaning.

:::checkpoint Before opening: can the gaze itself tell Ottilie which of these it is? And what could she attend to instead, and ask, without guessing and without asking him to explain himself?

No. Each reading produces the same behaviour, so on this course's reading the gaze can't distinguish them, and trying to work out which one applies is guessing, the kind of imagining that lesson 7 found doesn't make people more accurate.

What she can attend to is his words, the channel the Tzeltal listeners used: does he ask questions, repeat back a step, pick up a detail? If it matters that he's got it, she can check the content rather than the eyes: "Shall we run through the first complaint together, so you can see how it goes?" If she wants to know what works for him, she can ask about the briefing rather than about his gaze: "Is this a good way to go through it, or would a written version help?"

What she shouldn't do is ask him to look at her, or decide privately that he's autistic, or shy, or from somewhere with different customs. Each of those is a guess about a person from one behaviour.
:::

## Autistic people and eye contact

The advice "good listeners make eye contact" is repeated so often that it can sound like a finding.
No source this course read names it as a myth, but three findings in this lesson cut against it: the
Tzeltal recipients, the Japanese students' ratings, and what autistic people say about eye contact.
So calling it a poor test of listening is **this course's reading**, an inference from three sources
rather than a tested claim.

The third source is the most direct. Dominic Trevisan and colleagues analysed 698 passages of
first-hand description from 364 people, drawn from ten YouTube videos and a forum for autistic
people, in 2017. The diagnoses were self-declared: the authors write, "We could not verify diagnosis
of our participants."[8] The themes included discomfort, feeling invaded and sensory overload, and in
conversation, in the authors' summary, "an inability to listen to another person while making eye
contact at the same time". One forum post put it this way: "I can't concentrate while making eye
contact, particularly if I need to listen to what the other person is saying to me."[8] Not every
account was of looking away; some people said they were accused of "staring relentlessly".[8]

(The authors' own term is "people with self-declared ASD". This course writes "autistic people" and
"non-autistic people", the words used by the other researchers in this section, and says so here so
you know it is a choice.)

That makes it a qualitative study of self-selected accounts on the internet, not a measurement of all
autistic people. But on the narrow point, it is the right source: people describing what eye contact
does to their own listening. For some of them, looking away is how they manage to listen. A rule that
reads looking away as not listening gets these people exactly backwards.

If a conversation about any of this turns to real distress, that is a different situation, and lesson
11, "When it's serious", and Mental Fitness lesson 1, "Where ordinary ends", are where to go.

## Whose difficulty is it?

Behind the eye-contact point sits a larger question, and it is **contested**, with an empirical side
and a value side. When an autistic and a non-autistic person misunderstand each other, where does the
difficulty lie?

**The deficit reading.** The clinical definition of autism describes difficulties in social
communication as part of the condition. A 2025 paper opens by stating it this way: "Autism is
clinically defined by social communication deficits, suggesting that autistic people may be less
effective at sharing information, particularly with one another."[10] A 2026 study whose authors include Simon Baron-Cohen describes it in its background:
"Social communication difficulties in autism were traditionally attributed to deficits in
empathy".[13] This course has not read a primary statement of that tradition, such as the diagnostic
manual or the theory-of-mind papers, so these are descriptions of it rather than its own case.

**The double empathy reading.** In a short 2012 conceptual paper, Damian Milton argued against
defining autism as a deficit in "theory of mind". A breakdown in understanding between an autistic
and a non-autistic person, he wrote, is "a 'double problem' as both people experience it, and so it
is not a singular problem located in any one person".[9] On his account, non-autistic people misread
autistic people too, and the difficulty belongs to the pairing.

That is an idea, and it predicts something testable: if the problem lies in the mismatch, autistic
people should communicate well with each other, and pairs of an autistic and a non-autistic person
should do worse.

:::predict Catherine Crompton and colleagues tested that with chains of people passing a story along, one to the next, like a careful game of telephone. In 2020, with 72 adults, chains mixing autistic and non-autistic people passed on fewer details than all-autistic or all-non-autistic chains. A larger, preregistered replication followed in 2025. What do you expect it found?

It found no difference in how well information passed along in mixed chains compared with single-neurotype chains. The all-autistic chains again did as well as the non-autistic ones. Rapport, how the interactions felt, still differed.[10]

If you expected the 2020 result to hold, that was a reasonable expectation. The authors predicted it too, in the plan they registered before collecting the data.
:::

Here are the two studies side by side, because neither should be read alone.

- **Crompton and colleagues, 2020** (a **single study**, with preregistered analyses): 72 adults in
  nine chains of eight, three all-autistic, three all-non-autistic, three mixed. Each person retold a story with 30 details to
  the next. Autistic chains passed on about as many details as non-autistic chains (12.96 against
  12.40), and mixed chains fewer (8.92). "Information transfer selectively degrades more quickly in
  mixed pairs, in parallel with a reduction in rapport." The authors listed their limits: "the sample
  size was relatively modest, and replications are warranted", and participants knew which kind of
  chain they were in.[11]
- **Crompton and colleagues, 2025** (a **registered report**, its methods accepted by the journal
  before the data were collected): 311 adults in 54 chains at three sites, two of them independent of
  the original study, in the UK and the US. "We found no difference in information transfer between
  single-neurotype and mixed-neurotype chains. Non-autistic chains indicated higher rapport, and
  disclosing diagnosis improved rapport. This result challenges assumptions about autistic
  communication deficits but contrasts with prior findings."[10]

Read that last sentence carefully, because both sides can take something from it. Autistic people
passed information to each other as well as non-autistic people did, in both studies, which is hard
to square with a strong deficit reading in this task. But the mixed-chain drop, the result that most
directly supported double empathy, did not replicate. The authors suggest why: their larger sample was more
varied, and "The experimental context tested here may have failed to capture difficulties experienced
in real-world cross-neurotype interactions."[10] That is their explanation, offered after the fact,
not something they tested. All the participants were from the US and UK.[10]

Since 2025 the idea itself has drawn published argument. This course read each of these as an
abstract only.

- Lucy Livingston, Lili Hargitai and Punit Shah, in a 2025 critique, grant that the research could
  improve understanding of social processes, but argue that "The DEP is poorly conceptualized, and we
  find that it is being conflated with many other constructs". They call for a stronger chain from
  theory to prediction, and add: "Until then, we caution against the translation of DEP research into
  applied settings."[12]
- Caroline Bollen and Janna van Grunsven replied in 2026 that the critique's "warning note is
  problematic both from an ethical and philosophy of science point of view".[12]
- The 2026 study with Baron-Cohen among its authors, testing how accurately autistic and non-autistic adults
  read autistic and non-autistic storytellers' feelings, concluded: "Findings suggest partial support
  for the double empathy problem theory" (its key interaction was a trend, not a significant
  effect).[13] A 2026 study of observers judging filmed suspects
  reported that "The double empathy hypothesis was not supported".[14]

So the empirical question is open. On this course's reading, what would settle it is larger studies of real, back-and-forth
conversation between autistic and non-autistic people, measuring both how well information passes
and how each person experiences it, across more than one culture.

The value side is separate, and evidence won't settle it: whether autism is best described as a
disorder of one person or as a difference that shows in how two people fit. The words used carry a
position, which is why this course uses the terms the researchers in this section use and describes
each reading in its own proponents' language. Guy Itzchakov and Graham Bodie's 2026 review of
listening research adds a point that applies to this whole course, not only to autism: much of the
guidance on "good listening" reflects "neurotypical and often Western relational ideals".[15]

For a listener, you don't need to settle either question to act well. Both readings agree that
misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people happen, and the practical advice is the
same under both: do not read eye contact as a test of listening, attend to what's said, and ask what
works.

## Hearing loss

The advice here comes from RNID, the UK charity for people who are deaf or have hearing loss,
speaking from its community's experience. In this course's terms it is **practitioner advice**.

:::predict Most people, talking to someone who has not caught what they said, do two things without thinking. What are they, and what would you expect a charity for deaf people to say about them?

They talk louder and they slow down. RNID asks people to avoid both shouting and speaking unnecessarily slowly, and to face the person and speak clearly instead.[16] RNID's page doesn't say why, and this course doesn't supply a reason of its own.
:::

RNID's [communication page](https://rnid.org.uk/information-and-support/how-to-communicate-with-deaf-people-hearing-loss/)
starts with "Please face me when you talk to me. Lots of people rely on lipreading to some degree to
understand." It asks people to speak clearly and "avoid shouting, speaking too fast or unnecessarily
slow", and says: "Don't be afraid to repeat or rephrase and try something different."[16]

Two more lines carry the principle. "Not all deaf people and people with hearing loss are the same."
And: "Ask someone how they want to be communicated with". On interpreters or a companion, it says,
"You should always follow the advice of the person with communication needs." Its practical tips
include "In a noisy place, move to a quieter area if possible", and writing things down, on paper or
a screen.[16]

### Dinner with a grandmother

Ferdinand's grandmother has hearing loss. At the family's birthday dinner she sits at the far end of a
long table, the room is loud, and she's gone quiet. Her relatives, when they talk to her at all, lean
across and shout, slowly, in short sentences. She nods, and then does not answer.

Before opening the checkpoint, plan what Ferdinand could do, using RNID's advice and nothing else:
where, how, and what he'd ask her. Write three or four steps.

:::checkpoint Compare your plan with this one.

First, the question: he sits down beside her and asks, "What works best for you when it's noisy like this?" She knows what helps her, and RNID's point is that people differ. She may want the quiet room, or something he hasn't thought of.

Second, the place, if her answer allows it. RNID's advice for a noisy room is to move somewhere quieter if possible: a quieter end of the table, or ten minutes in the next room.

Third, the manner. Face her and speak clearly at a normal pace. No shouting, no exaggerated slowness. If she does not catch something, repeat it or say it a different way rather than louder, or write a word down.

Fourth, what not to do: talk about her to someone else in front of her, or decide on her behalf that she'd rather be left out.

If your plan put the place or the manner first, move the question up. Everything else depends on her answer.
:::

## Through an interpreter

The interpreting company LanguageLine Solutions, in telephone tips the Minnesota Department of Health
publishes (a company's advice, labelled so), says "SPEAK DIRECTLY TO THE CUSTOMER", not to the
interpreter, and "SPEAK NATURALLY, NOT LOUDER - Speak at your normal pace, not slower." It asks you
to "Speak in one sentence or two short ones at a time", keeping each thought whole.[17] That is
RNID's pattern again: talk to the person, at a normal pace.

## Older people

There is a well-studied habit here, and its setting matters. **Elderspeak** is the name researchers
give to talk that treats an older adult like a small child. The research team below coded it by
features such as pet names and other diminutives, "we" in place of "you", exaggerated intonation and
a high pitch. In their words,
it is "a speech style similar to baby talk".[18]

Kristine Williams and colleagues studied it in US nursing homes, with residents who have dementia. In
a 2017 **randomised trial** across 13 nursing homes, 42 pairs of staff and residents were filmed
during care. After three one-hour training sessions, staff cut their elderspeak, from 34.6% of their
talk by 13.6 percentage points on average, and residents' resistance to care fell, from 35.7% of the
time by 15.3 points. How much resistance fell tracked how much elderspeak fell. The trial was
designed to compare trained homes with untrained ones, but too few pairs qualified to analyse it that
way, so these figures are before-and-after changes once staff had been trained (the comparison homes
were trained later too).[18] The authors also name a small sample and the chance that staff behaved
differently while being filmed. An earlier observational study by the same group, of 80 filmed care
episodes with 20 residents, found resistance to care more likely after elderspeak than after normal
talk. Its authors say an experiment is needed to show cause, and they record the other side:
some dementia-care researchers and clinicians recommend parts of elderspeak, such as simpler grammar
and a slower rate, and one study found that some older people value its warmth.[19] Both are **one
group's programme**, in dementia care.

The trial also reports, citing two studies this course has not read, that older adults without
cognitive impairment "readily recognize and react negatively to elderspeak", and that it does not help
them understand.[18] That is a claim at one remove. So the step from nursing homes to your healthy
grandfather is **this course's reading**, not a finding: if care staff slip into
this, family members may too, and there is no evidence here that it helps. The rest is the rule you've
seen twice already. If an older person has hearing loss, RNID's advice applies, and it is about hearing,
not age. If they don't, talk to them as you'd talk to anyone, and if you're unsure what helps, ask.

## Stammering, again

Lesson 5, "Let them finish", gave STAMMA's advice for talking with someone who stammers: give them
time, do not finish their sentences, don't tell them to slow down or relax.[20] Two more lines from the
same page belong here. It asks listeners to "Stay neutral and keep natural eye contact." And it says,
"Knowing that someone stammers doesn't mean you know anything else about them."[20]

Put that first line next to the autistic accounts earlier and you have the lesson in miniature. One
advocacy body asks for natural eye contact; many autistic people describe eye contact as making it
harder to listen. Neither is wrong. They're describing different people. There is no single rule about
where to look that serves everyone, which is why each organisation this course read comes back to the same
move.

## The rule they share

RNID says "Ask someone how they want to be communicated with". STAMMA says what people who stammer
want is time, and that knowing one thing about someone does not tell you the rest. The interpreting
tips say to speak to the person, not about them. Each is advice from one organisation about one kind
of difference. What they share is this course's reading of them together: **adjust to the person,
and ask them how.**[16][17][20]

It is lesson 7's finding in a new setting. Imagining yourself in someone's position didn't make people
more accurate about them; asking did. A category, whether a country, a diagnosis, an age or a hearing
aid, is another way of imagining instead of asking.

Asking well has its own shape. Ask about the conversation, not about the difference: "Is this a good
place to talk, or shall we find somewhere quieter?" rather than "What's wrong with your hearing?"
Ask once, early, and then act on the answer. And accept "I'm fine" as an answer too.

## What people get wrong

**"Good listeners make eye contact."** Tzeltal recipients in recorded questions looked in about four
questions in ten and showed attention by repeating words; many autistic people report that eye
contact makes listening harder. That eye contact is a poor test of listening is this course's
reading, from three sources.[2][3][8]

**"Some nations leave long silences."** In the ten-language recordings, the Japanese conversations
were the fastest and the Danish the slowest, but every language's average was within about 250 ms of
the overall mean.[1]

**"A country's label tells you how a person will talk."** Reviews of the research call the
high- and low-context country classifications flawed or very limited, large value surveys find more
agreement than disagreement across countries, and Hofstede himself warns against using national
scores to stereotype individuals.[5][7][22]

**"Culture makes no difference, then."** Also wrong. Where listeners look, and how a direct look
feels, differed measurably between the groups studied.[2][3]

**"When autistic and non-autistic people misunderstand each other, the difficulty is all on the
autistic side."** That is one reading, not a settled fact. Autistic people passed information to each
other as well as non-autistic people did in both chain studies, and the double empathy reading holds
that misunderstanding runs both ways.[9][10][11]

**"The double empathy problem is proven."** The mixed-chain drop that most directly supported it
did not replicate in the larger registered study, and the idea has published critics as well as
defenders.[10][12]

**"Speak louder and slower to someone with hearing loss, or to an older person."** RNID asks people
to avoid shouting and unnecessarily slow speech, the interpreting tips say "not louder", and
elderspeak was linked to more resistance in dementia care.[16][17][18]

## Practice

:::exercise How you show you're listening
This is a **notice** and **try one move** exercise. Take 10 minutes over the first part; the second happens in conversations you were having anyway.

First, from memory, list what you do to show someone you're listening: where you look, what you do with your face and hands, what sounds you make, what you say. Be specific ("I nod every few seconds", "I say 'right' a lot", "I hold eye contact until they stop"). Then mark each one: is it something the other person can see, hear, or both?

Second, pick one signal that does not depend on the eyes, such as saying back a detail, asking a follow-up about something they just said, or the Tzeltal listeners' move of repeating a key word. In one or two ordinary, low-stakes conversations you were going to have anyway, use it a little more than usual. Afterwards write a line on what happened, including nothing.

Do not test anything on anyone without their knowledge, and don't use this to judge whether anyone else is autistic, or anything else about them. The exercise is about your habits.
:::

:::exercise Three questions for an older relative
This is a **practise with a willing partner** exercise. Take 15 minutes, most of it the conversation, with a few for notes afterwards.

Ask an older relative or friend whether they'd be willing to talk for a quarter of an hour about their life, and say it is for a course you're doing. They may say no, and that is fine. If they agree, ask first how they'd like to do it: where, at what time of day, whether somewhere quiet would help.

Choose three questions from [StoryCorps' question list](https://storycorps.org/participate/great-questions/), for example "Where did you grow up?", "What is your earliest memory?" and "How has your life been different than what you'd imagined?"[21] Ask them one at a time and follow up on what interests you. Let silences run. Do not record it unless they explicitly agree beforehand; notes afterwards are enough.

Afterwards, write a few lines: what you did to show you were listening, whether you adjusted anything to them (volume, pace, place, where you sat), and whether you asked or guessed. Don't write down anything they told you that they wouldn't want written, and do not name them in your journal.
:::

## For your journal

This lesson adds one note to each observation journal entry: how the other person showed they were
listening, and whether you noticed it or only noticed where they were looking. Where you adjusted
anything for someone, note whether you asked first or guessed. Never write that anyone is autistic,
or describe anyone by a diagnosis or a nationality's supposed traits; describe what they did and
said, and what you did.
