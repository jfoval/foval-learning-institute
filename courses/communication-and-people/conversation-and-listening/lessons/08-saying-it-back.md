You have probably finished a conversation sure you understood someone, and found out later that you
hadn't. There's one person who can tell you whether you understood, and it isn't you. Carl Rogers
and Richard Farson put that at the centre of a booklet for managers in 1957: say back what you
think the other person meant, and let them tell you whether you got it.[1] They proposed a harder
version too, for arguments: nobody gets to make their own point until they have restated the other
person's well enough to satisfy them. Try that in a real disagreement and you may find out how often you
can't.

This lesson is about saying it back: what Rogers and Farson meant by it, the forms it takes
(repeating, rephrasing, reflecting a meaning, naming a feeling), the check that runs the other way
when you are the one explaining, and the case against the whole thing, made by people who think a
restatement is usually a performance. Whether any of it works is lesson 9's question. This one is
about doing it well enough to find out.

## Total meaning

Rogers and Farson's booklet [*Active Listening*](https://wholebeinginstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/Rogers_Farson_Active-Listening.pdf), which coined the phrase, was written for people
who ran workplaces. This course read the excerpt reprinted
in 1987, not the full 1957 booklet.[1] It is an essay, not a study: it says "clinical and research
evidence" backs it without naming any.[1]

Their central idea is **total meaning**. "Any message a person tries to get across usually has two
components: the content of the message and the feeling or attitude underlying this content."[1]
Their example is a machinist who comes to his foreman and says he has finished a lathe setup. The
content asks for the next job. Now suppose he says, "Well, I'm finally finished with that damned
lathe setup."[1] The content is the same, and the message is not.

:::predict You are the foreman. The machinist has just said, "Well, I'm finally finished with that damned lathe setup." What would you say back? Write a reply before you open this.

Rogers and Farson's point is that handing him the next job answers the content and misses the rest. The replies they suggest are short: "Glad to have it over with, huh?" or "Had a pretty rough time of it?", or anything else that tells him he was heard.[1]

They add that this need not change the next job or turn into an hour of complaints. If your reply was about the next job, that is the ordinary response, and it is the one they were writing against.
:::

The booklet puts three instructions under "What to Do": listen for total meaning, **respond to
feelings**, and note all cues (hesitations, the voice, the face, posture, breathing).[1] Sometimes
the feeling is the whole message. If the machinist says, "I'd like to melt this lathe down and make
paper clips out of it", answering the content would be absurd.[1]

And there is a list of what to avoid. Passing judgment is one, "whether critical or favorable".
Advice and information are another, since, they say, people take them as attempts to change them. Praise can
block too: they say it is "almost as destructive" to a relationship to tell someone they are good or
right as to tell them otherwise, because it makes it harder to own up to what is wrong. Even
encouragement ("I'm sure everything will work out O.K.") can land as an attempt to steer the speaker
"or hold him off, rather than as support".[1]

### Technique and attitude

Lesson 3 quoted Rogers and Farson calling active listening a set of "definite techniques" aimed at
change in the person listened to, and that is accurate. But the excerpt opens with a condition: if
the listener's basic attitudes conflict with the method, the behaviour "will be empty and
sterile", and, they say, the people around them will notice.[1] The respect has to be real for the
technique to carry it.

Rogers made the same point in [his 1957 paper on therapy](https://counselling-tutor.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/CSR+Documents/The+Necessary+and+Sufficient+Conditions+of+Therapeutic+Personality+Change.pdf), a theoretical paper setting out six
conditions he thought necessary for change in therapy, offered as hypotheses to be tested. The
technique of reflecting feelings, he wrote, "is by no means an essential condition of therapy"; it
helps only as a channel for empathy and regard, and it can carry the opposite: "Feeling may be
“reflected” in a way which communicates the therapist's lack of empathy."[2] That is therapy, not
conversation between friends, and it is his theory, not a finding. But he was writing about
client-centred therapy, where reflecting feelings was a described technique, and it is the root of
the complaint you'll meet at the end of this lesson.

## The test: until they say you have got it

The booklet then turns listening from an attitude into something you can check. "Because
understanding another person is actually far more difficult than it at first seems", they say, you
should test your understanding constantly, by "reflecting in your own words what the speaker seems
to mean". Then: "His response to this will tell you whether
or not he feels understood."[1]

And for a lively or heated discussion they suggest a ground rule: "Before either participant in the
discussion can make a point or express an opinion of his own, he must first restate aloud the
previous point or position of the other person." The restatement has to be in your own words,
because "merely parroting the words of another does not prove that one has understood but only that
he has heard the words". And it has to be good enough for the other person: "The restatement must be
accurate enough to satisfy the speaker before the listener can be allowed to speak for himself."[1]

Two things in that are easy to read past. The judge of the restatement is the speaker, not you. And
the speaker is allowed to say no.

### Why a restatement is a check

Lesson 2 showed a small version of this in recorded talk. In one of the repair paper's recordings,
Lori says single beds are "awfully thin" to sleep on, and Ellen offers "Y'mean narrow?", which Lori
takes up.[3] Other people's corrections, the authors found, are "frequently modulated in form",
often put as a question that the speaker gets to accept or refuse.[3] That is **observed in
recordings**, and the "Y'mean X?" form is the everyday ancestor of what Rogers and Farson turned into
a rule.

What makes a restatement useful is that it puts your understanding where the speaker can see it and
correct it. Lesson 3 met the other half of this: self-ratings of empathy predicted real accuracy
badly in one of William Ickes's studies. Ickes also reports a
study by Marangoni, Garcia and himself in which 80 undergraduates watched three simulated therapy
sessions, each with a client discussing her real problems, and wrote down what they thought she was
thinking at each pause. Half got no feedback. For the other half, after each guess, the client's
actual thought appeared on the screen, and that feedback "accelerated the rate at which the
perceivers' empathic accuracy improved".[4] It is a **single study, as Ickes reports it**, of students
watching tapes, not of people talking.

**This course's reading** puts the two together: a restatement the speaker corrects is the
conversational version of that feedback. You made a guess, and you have been told the answer. On that
reading a correction is the check succeeding, which is why Rogers and Farson's rule keeps going
"until" rather than stopping at the first try. Lesson 7, "Ask, don't guess", looks at why asking
beat imagining in the experiments on reading another person's view; saying it back is one way of
asking.

## Repeat, rephrase, reflect

The most detailed modern instructions for saying it back come from **motivational interviewing**
(MI), a clinical counselling method for helping someone resolve mixed feelings about a specific
change, such as drinking less. This course read MI's methods through two practitioner sources: a US
government treatment manual for substance use counselling, SAMHSA's [TIP 35](https://library.samhsa.gov/sites/default/files/tip-35-pep19-02-01-003.pdf), which draws on Miller
and Rollnick's MI textbook, and the MI trainers' network's own summary.[5][6] Both are professional
tools with a clinical goal, and that matters later in this section.

The manual's name for saying it back is **reflective listening**, and it says what it is not: "It is
not simply a matter of being quiet while the client is speaking."[5] You form a hypothesis about what
the person means and give it back "with your best guess".[5] The trainers' summary describes
reflections as "repeating, rephrasing or offering a deeper guess about what the person is trying to
communicate".[6]

The manual sorts reflections into **simple** ones, which repeat or rephrase what the person said,
and **complex** ones, which use different words to reflect the meaning or feeling underneath.[5] Its
table uses one client statement, "My wife is nagging me about my drinking", for both simple kinds.
Repeat: "Your wife is nagging you about your drinking", with the note "Avoid mimicking." Rephrase:
"Your wife is pressuring you about your drinking."[5] Both count as simple in the table, even though
the second changes a word. The complex examples go further. To a client who wants to quit smoking
cannabis so that the smoke will not worsen his daughter's asthma, the complex reflection of feeling is
"You're afraid that your daughter's asthma will get worse if you continue smoking marijuana."[5] The
fear is the counsellor's guess. The client never said it.

And the manual's steps for a complex reflection end the way Rogers and Farson's test does: offer
it, then "Wait for the client's response", and "Be open to being wrong."[5]

### One statement, five replies

Radomir, a friend, says to you over coffee: "I've rewritten this report three times and she still
sends it back."

- "You've rewritten it three times and she still sends it back." A **repeat**. Accurate, and close
  to parroting. It shows you heard the words. Radomir can hardly say more than "Yes."
- "So your manager keeps returning it." A **rephrase**, still simple. It picks out one thing, that
  it keeps coming back, and invites more.
- "It sounds like you're starting to wonder whether anything you write will satisfy her." A
  **complex reflection** of meaning: a guess about what three rewrites add up to, in words he did not
  use.
- "You must be furious." A **label**, and a strong one. It names a feeling, at full volume, as a
  statement rather than a guess.
- "Have you asked her exactly what she wants?" Not a reflection at all. It is a question with advice
  folded into it, and it moves to fixing before you know what the problem is. Lesson 10, "When
  someone brings you a problem", takes that move up.

Suppose you went with "You must be furious." Radomir says: "Not furious. Just tired. I don't even
know what she wants any more."

That reply is the most useful thing he has said. You had the direction roughly right and the
intensity wrong, and now you know both, plus something new: he does not know what she wants. A good
next restatement uses it: "So it's less anger than being worn down, and not knowing what would count
as finished." On Rogers and Farson's test, you are closer. On this course's reading, the "wrong"
label did its job, because he corrected it.

:::checkpoint Your turn. Hesketh says, "My brother's moving back in with us for a few months, which is fine, obviously." Write a simple reflection and a complex reflection before you open this.

A simple reflection repeats or rephrases: "Your brother's coming to stay with you for a few months."

A complex reflection guesses at what is underneath, and here the clue is "which is fine, obviously", an addition that may be doing more work than it looks. For example: "It sounds like you're not completely sure it will be fine." Or, turned down a notch: "It sounds like there might be a bit more to it than fine."

If your complex reflection was "You're dreading it", that may be right, but it is a big claim on two words, and he may feel he has to defend his brother. The softer guess leaves him room to say more, or to say it really is fine. Either answer tells you something.
:::

### A clinical tool, and a friend

MI's complex reflections are used on purpose. In the manual's table, the complex examples carry a
note to reinforce what counsellors call change talk, the client's own statements in favour of
changing, so the counsellor chooses which of the client's words to reflect.[5] The MI trainers are
blunt about the limit: MI "is not a way to “get people to change” or a set of techniques to impose
on the conversation."[6] A friend who has not asked for help to change anything is not a client, and
choosing which of their words to reflect so as to move them somewhere is a different act from trying
to understand them. So this lesson borrows the distinction between simple and complex, and the
habit of offering a guess and waiting. It does not borrow the steering, and MI's evidence from clinics
says nothing about conversations between friends.

## Naming a feeling, lightly

Radomir's "furious" shows one way a label goes wrong: it's too strong. [VitalTalk](https://www.vitaltalk.org/guides/responding-to-emotion-respecting/), an
organisation founded by clinicians to teach communication in cancer care, teaches a set of responses
to emotion called NURSE. Its first is Naming, with the example "It sounds like
you are frustrated" and the note "In general, turn down the intensity a notch when you name the
emotion".[7] The second is Understanding, with a note that doubles as advice for anyone tempted to
say "I know exactly how you feel": "stop short of suggesting you understand everything (you
don't)".[7] That is **practitioner advice** for oncology.

FBI crisis negotiators, as three researchers describe their model, label emotions too, and claim
from practice that even a wrong label shows the person you are trying to understand; their model's
goal goes beyond understanding, to what it calls "behavioral change", which is a different aim from
this course's.[8] Lesson 6 met the same model's advice on "why".

Why turn it down? The sources read give no tested reason. **This course's reading**: a label that is
too weak is easy to correct upwards ("It's more than frustrating, actually"), while a label that is
too strong asks the person to push back against your version of them, and some will not bother.

## What the practitioners share

Put the helping traditions side by side and they say nearly the same thing, in their own settings.

- **Samaritans**, the UK and Ireland listening service, in [its SHUSH advice](https://www.samaritans.org/how-we-can-help/if-youre-worried-about-someone-else/how-support-someone-youre-worried-about/what-do-if-you-think-someone-struggling/) for supporting someone:
  "Say it back", with the instruction "Check you've understood, but don't interrupt or offer a
  solution."[9]
- **988 Lifeline**, the US crisis line, on [its page for helping someone else](https://988lifeline.org/help-someone-else/), under "Summarize What You Hear", offers phrases like "what
  I'm hearing is...", after first saying to let the speaker finish before asking anything.[10]
- **R U OK?**, an Australian suicide-prevention organisation, on [how to ask](https://www.ruok.org.au/how-to-ask): "Show that you've listened by repeating back
  what you've heard (in your own words) and ask if you have understood them properly."[11]
- **The Calgary-Cambridge Guide**, a framework used in teaching doctors to consult: "Periodically
  summarizes to verify own understanding of what the patient has said; invites patient to correct
  interpretation or provide further information".[12]
- **The Harvard Program on Negotiation**, in [a blog post](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/listening-skills-for-maximum-success/) on negotiating a deal, ends a model paraphrase with "Have I
  captured your main points?", and says paraphrasing "allows the other side to clarify or correct
  details before tensions rise".[13]

Every one of these is **practitioner advice**, built from experience in its own setting (a phone
line, a surgery, a negotiation), not a tested finding. What they share is the shape Rogers and
Farson gave it: your own words, and then a door left open for the other person to correct you. The
open door is the part that gets dropped. "So what you're saying is..." followed by your next point
is not a check; it is a run-up.

The negotiation blog adds a line that heads off a common worry: "Acknowledgment doesn't mean
agreement."[13] Rogers and Farson said the same in their own words, describing what a good listener
conveys: "I respect your thoughts, and even if I don't agree with them, I know that they are valid
for you."[1] Lesson 16, "Listening to someone who disagrees with you", builds on that.

:::checkpoint A colleague finishes explaining why she thinks the team should move its weekly meeting to Mondays. You reply, "So you're saying Mondays are better. But Fridays suit most of us." Using this section, what is missing, and what would fix it?

Two things. The restatement is thin ("Mondays are better" leaves out her reasons, so she cannot tell if you understood them), and there is no door left open: you move straight to your own point, so the restatement works as a run-up, not a check.

A fix: "So your case is that Monday gives us the whole week to act on what we decide, and Friday afternoons are when people are least focused. Have I got that right?" Then wait. Once she says yes, or corrects you, your point about Fridays comes next.
:::

## The other direction: teach-back

Everything so far checks whether you understood someone. The same logic runs the other way when
you are the one explaining. In medicine it is called **teach-back**, and the US Agency for Healthcare
Research and Quality (AHRQ) publishes [a tool on it](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool5.html) for clinicians. It defines teach-back as "a way of
checking understanding by asking patients to state in their own words what they need to know or do
about their health".[14]

The part that transfers is the framing: "Remember, you are checking how well you explained
something, not testing the patient."[14] The example questions are built on it: "When you get home,
what will you tell your partner about this visit?"[14] And the tool is direct about two familiar
questions: "Do you understand?" and "Does that make sense?" "are NOT teach-back
questions", because, it says, patients tend to answer yes whether they understand or not.[14] It adds two
more rules that map onto Rogers and Farson's: explain in small pieces and check each one ("Chunk and
Check"), and do not settle for your own words played back: "If they parrot your words back to you,
they may not have understood."[14]

Why does "Does that make sense?" get a yes? AHRQ gives the observation, not a mechanism. **This
course's reading** connects it to two earlier lessons: lesson 2 showed that a question can be built
so that one answer is the easy one to give, and lesson 6 showed a doctor's "anything else?" drawing far
fewer yeses than "something else?". On that reading, a question that can be closed with a yes
often will be.

Clinical traditions do not all agree: VitalTalk's own "ask-tell-ask" example ends with "Does that
make sense…?", the very question AHRQ rules out, a reminder that a technique list is someone's
list, not a law.[7]

How good is the evidence? AHRQ's page says the methods can "Improve patient understanding and
adherence", without citing a study for it.[14] Using teach-back outside
a clinic, to check your own explaining, is **this course's reading** of the method, not something
anyone has tested.

### A video call, two ways

You are showing your great-aunt Wendeline how to video-call her granddaughter on the new phone
you have just set up for her. You go through it: open the app with the green camera on it, find her
granddaughter's name, press the green camera symbol, not the green handset, and hold the phone out
in front of her. You finish with, "Does
that make sense?"

"Yes, lovely," she says. Next Sunday, she rings you instead, because she pressed the handset.

Her yes was not a lie. It was the answer the question was built for, and it may also have meant
"that's enough for now". The explanation had four steps, delivered in one go.

The second time, you chunk it. You show her the first two steps and then say: "I've thrown a lot at
you, and I want to make sure I've explained it properly. If you wanted to ring her now, what would you
press first?"

:::predict Wendeline answers, "The green one, like you said." Has the check worked?

Not yet. "The green one, like you said" could be your words coming back, which is AHRQ's warning about parroting: it may not mean she understood. And there are two green symbols once she has found the name, the camera and the handset.

A better next move is to ask her to show you, or to ask a question she can only answer if she has it: "And once you've found her name, which symbol do you press?" If she hesitates, that is your explanation failing, not her. Say it a different way and check again.
:::

## The case against saying it back

Not everyone thinks this is good advice. Celeste Headlee, a radio host and writer, opens [her
TED talk on conversation](https://www.ted.com/talks/celeste_headlee_10_ways_to_have_a_better_conversation/transcript) by listing what people are usually told: "look, nod and smile to show that
you're paying attention, repeat back what you just heard or summarize it". Then: "So I want you to
forget all of that. It is crap."[15] Lesson 4 quoted her reason, that there is no point learning to
show attention if you are paying it.

Her case, put at full strength in this course's words rather than hers: advice to display
attention produces displays, and a restatement done because a course said to do it is about the
listener looking good. On that she has more company than you might expect. The negotiation blog says active listening "does
not mean sitting patiently while your counterpart talks. Nor does it simply involve nodding,
maintaining eye contact, or saying, “I understand.”"[13] Even the textbook tradition hedges its own
advice: the chapter outline for the 13th edition of Adler and Proctor's *Looking Out, Looking In*,
which is all this course read of it, tells readers to keep paraphrases tentative and to use them
sparingly.[16] And the strongest version of her point is
Rogers's own: reflecting feelings can communicate the lack of empathy it is supposed to show.[2]

Where Headlee and Rogers and Farson part company is on what to do about it. She throws out
repeating back. They keep it, as a test the speaker grades. **This course's reading** is that their
disagreement is partly about two different acts that look alike: a summary offered to show you were
listening, and a guess offered so that you can be corrected. The first is what Headlee is attacking.
The second is what the rule describes.

Whether saying it back actually makes people feel understood is a question for evidence, and the
evidence gives Headlee some support. In one experiment with 180 undergraduates, who gave their views
on comprehensive exams in peer interviews and got back either a paraphrase or a simple
acknowledgement, the paraphrases made the listener seem more socially attractive but were not
associated with the students feeling more understood. That is a **single study**, and this course
has read only its abstract.[17] Lesson 9, "Does active listening work?", takes up that study and the
others, which point in more than one direction.

## What people get wrong

**"Active listening means nodding, eye contact and repeating back."** Rogers and Farson rejected the
techniques without the attitude, the negotiation blog says it is not nodding, and Headlee rejects the
display outright.[1][13][15]

**"If I repeat their words, they'll know I understood."** Rogers and Farson: parroting proves only
that you heard the words. AHRQ says the same of patients who parrot a clinician.[1][14]

**"A restatement they correct is a failure."** On Rogers and Farson's test, the correction is the
information you were checking for, and the MI manual tells counsellors to be open to being
wrong.[1][5]

**"'Does that make sense?' checks whether they understood."** AHRQ says it is not a teach-back
question, because, it says, patients tend to say yes either way.[14]

**"Saying their view back means I agree with it."** The negotiators and Rogers and Farson both say
it does not. Lesson 16 comes back to it.[1][13]

**"If you're really paying attention, there's no need to say anything back."** Paying attention is
what Rogers and Farson asked for too, but they add that understanding is harder than it seems, and
only the speaker can tell you whether you have it. Attention the speaker cannot see cannot be
corrected.[1]

**"Saying it back always makes people feel understood."** In one experiment, paraphrasing did not
make students feel more understood than a plain acknowledgement did.[17] Lesson 9 has the rest.

## Practice

:::exercise Restate before you reply
This is a **practise with a willing partner** exercise. Take 15 minutes: 10 for the conversation and 5 for the ratings and your notes. Ask a friend who knows this is practice and has agreed to it; they can say no, and they can say nothing kind about your restatements. Pick a topic neither of you cares about much, and if it gets heated, stop. Do not record the conversation, and name nobody in your journal.

1. Choose a topic from this list on which the two of you mildly disagree: whether phones belong at the dinner table; whether a holiday is better planned or left loose; whether the book is always better than the film; whether a shared kitchen needs a cleaning rota; whether it is better to get up early or stay up late.
2. For 10 minutes, follow Rogers and Farson's rule: before either of you makes a point, you restate the other's last point in your own words, and they say whether you have got it. If not, try again. Afterwards, your partner rates each of your restatements as "got it", "partly" or "missed", and says what was missing from the partial ones.
3. Write in your journal how many tries your restatements took, what you tended to leave out (their reasons, their feeling, their strongest point), and whether the rule changed the conversation.
:::

:::exercise Three explanations, checked
This is a **rewrite on paper** exercise. Take 5 minutes. Each explanation below ends with "Does that make sense?". Rewrite the last line as a teach-back request, framed as a check on the explaining.

1. You have given a friend who's driving to your house the directions from the motorway.
2. You have helped a neighbour through the first page of a long form, explaining which boxes to fill.
3. A pharmacist, at the counter, has told a customer to take one tablet twice a day with food, for seven days, even if they feel better sooner.
:::

:::checkpoint Compare yours with these. The exact words matter less than the shape: your explaining is what is being tested, and they answer in their own words.

1. "Tell me back how you'll get off the motorway, so I know I haven't missed a turn." Chunk it: check the junction before you describe the last mile.

2. "Before we go on, which of these boxes will you leave blank?" Asking about a specific choice gets further than a general summary.

3. Here the setting matters: this is a pharmacist's job, with AHRQ's clinical framing. "I want to be sure I've explained it clearly. How will you take these, and when will you stop?" If the answer is "until I feel better", the explanation needs another go on that point.
:::

## For your journal

From now on, note the restatements in your conversations, yours and other people's: whether each was
a check (your own words, and room to correct) or a run-up to the next point, and what happened when
one was corrected. For the project, "restating before replying" is one of the habits you can practise
and measure, and this lesson's first exercise is a start on it.

## Connections

Logic and Argument lesson 7, "Reconstruction and charity: the strongest version first", gave you
Rapoport's first rule: restate an opponent's position so well that they wish they'd put it that way.
That is Rogers and Farson's ground rule applied to arguments, with the same judge. Reading Well lesson
7, "Judging what you have understood", made the same point about books: no verdict until you can say
you understand. Lesson 2's "Y'mean X?" is the everyday form of the check, and lesson 3's evidence
that self-ratings are weak is the reason the speaker has to be the judge.

Lesson 7, "Ask, don't guess", looks at why asking beat imagining in the perspective-taking
experiments, and what raises accuracy in reading another person. Next, lesson 9, "Does active listening work?", asks
what the experiments found when they tested paraphrasing and good listening against other
responses. Lesson 11, "When it's serious", uses saying it back in the hardest conversation in the
course, and lesson 16 uses it when you disagree. Teach-back comes back in Teaching and Mentoring, a
later course, as a check on your own explaining.
