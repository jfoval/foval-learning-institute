S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Conversation and Listening, on the lesson called Does active listening work? The honest answer starts with two more questions: work at what, and compared with what?

S2: Why isn't it just yes or no?

S1: Because the studies mean different things by it. On this course's reading there are at least four: paraphrase alone, a bundle of behaviours, any warm response to a partner's upset, and the speaker's own sense of being listened to. A result for one doesn't carry to another.

S2: Start with the narrowest, then.

S1: Paraphrase alone. In a two thousand and ten experiment, Harry Weger and colleagues had a hundred and eighty undergraduates interviewed about a proposed graduation exam, by an interviewer who either paraphrased each opinion or said things like "O.K." and "That's great". The manner was held the same in both, so only the words differed. The paraphrasers were liked a little more, a small effect. On feeling understood, the difference wasn't statistically significant. It's a single study.

S2: But feeling understood is the whole point. Did anyone try again?

S1: Weger and colleagues, four years later: a hundred and fifteen undergraduates talking with a stranger who was secretly a trained confederate. The confederate did active listening, paraphrasing and inviting more, or gave advice, or just nodded and said "I see". This time active listening did make people feel more understood than either. But on satisfaction and liking, advice did as well as active listening, both beat bare acknowledgement, and the authors call those effects "small". Another single study, of strangers in a lab.

S2: So the technique is a bit of a damp squib.

S1: In these students, small effects on some outcomes and not others. Now a different box. Guy Itzchakov and Avraham Kluger lead a programme on what they call high-quality listening: full attention, trying to understand, not judging. It's one group's programme, with many experiments, several preregistered. Two main papers, read by the course only as abstracts, report that this listening reduced speakers' social anxiety, made their attitudes less extreme, and made them clearer about them. The abstracts give no effect sizes, so neither do we.

S2: Those sound bigger.

S1: So ask what the comparison group got. In one design, described in an article two of the researchers wrote, the other listener was visibly answering text messages. That's a low bar. In another, as the article reports it, trained listeners also beat an undergraduate listening as usual, a higher bar. But the good listeners brought a package, attention and technique together, so you can't tell which part did the work. So there are two claims: a trained, attentive listener beats a distracted or ordinary one, and restating beats saying "OK" with the same attention.

S2: And at work?

S1: Kluger led a registered meta-analysis of six hundred and sixty four effect sizes. Perceived listening correlated with good work outcomes at an average of point three nine.

S2: So listening improves work by thirty nine per cent?

S1: No. A correlation isn't a percentage. It says two measures tend to move together. Three quarters of the effects came from one person rating both the listening and the outcome. Same person, the average was point four three; different sources, point two six. Keeping only effects measured by different sources or methods, listening and job performance still correlated at point two one. Experiments were rare, thirty of the six hundred and sixty four effects. They averaged point three nine, about the same as the correlational designs' point four, which is the broad side's answer to "it's all correlational". But only one paper tested listening's effect on actual performance experimentally, and the authors are careful: "this study only exposed the possibility that listening predicts performance."

S2: So where does that leave it?

S1: Contested. The broad case: many experiments pointing the same way, and a workplace association that survives, smaller, when the same-source problem is removed. On that view the null results come from testing techniques in isolation. The narrow case: isolate the technique and the effects are small and patchy, and advice did as well on satisfaction and liking. A twenty twenty six review Itzchakov wrote with Graham Bodie concludes that paraphrasing "should not be treated as a standalone marker of listening quality, but as a risky intervention that can either deepen or derail understanding." This course's reading is that both can be true at once, because they mostly answer different questions. It found no study that crosses attention with technique among people who know each other.

S2: And couples?

S1: In nineteen ninety eight, John Gottman's team filmed a hundred and thirty newlywed couples discussing a disagreement for fifteen minutes, and followed them for three to six years. Their definition was the widest of the four: any time one spouse's negative feeling met the other's interest, affection, humour or validation. On average it took about four seconds out of nine hundred. In their words, "These active listening exchanges hardly ever occurred, and they were not predictive of differential marital outcomes."

S2: That sounds damning.

S1: The authors were shocked, because they had recommended the approach themselves. Going back through the tapes, even their stable, happy couples rarely paraphrased or summarised each other's feelings. So they put it as a hypothesis: "we are led to the hypothesis that the active listening model may be expecting a form of emotional gymnastics from people who, at that moment in that relationship, are somewhat emotionally disabled by conflict." And their advice to therapists was conditional: if they were right, marital therapy should abandon the active listening model, in favour of gentler start-ups, de-escalation, the husband accepting more influence from his wife, and more physiological soothing. It's one study, observed in recordings.

S2: Who pushed back?

S1: Scott Stanley, Thomas Bradbury and Howard Markman, in two thousand. Markman and Stanley are among the authors of PREP, a couples programme with a structured speaker-listener exercise. The course read only their abstract. They called the recommendations "premature at best", and objected to non-random selection of couples and to "the use of correlational data to make strong causal inferences". The strongest form of their objection, as this course reads it, is that watching what happy couples do naturally can't test a technique built for when things go wrong. In the course's terms, it's like finding that good drivers rarely use the emergency brake. As Weger reports it, they also pointed to a review suggesting communication skills training produces measurable improvements, though Weger and colleagues add, fairly to both sides, that "improvements in marital functioning tied specifically to active listening do not dominate the literature."

S2: Has either side come round?

S1: The Gottman Institute's later materials include a conflict exercise whose first instruction is: "Postpone persuasion until each person can state their partner’s position to their partner’s satisfaction." It sits inside a framework of gentleness and soothing. It isn't a statement that the nineteen ninety eight conclusion was wrong, and either side could claim it. What would settle it, on the course's reading, is randomised trials of couples' programmes taken apart: listening training alone, soothing skills alone, both, and neither, followed for years. The course found none.

S2: The full lesson also has a study of trained listeners and real problems, a four-lab test of whether you can get people to listen well by asking them, and two exercises. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. When someone says it works, ask: at what, and compared with what?
