S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Conversation and Listening, on the lesson called Saying it back. You've probably finished a conversation sure you understood someone, and found out later that you hadn't. There's one person who can tell you whether you understood, and it isn't you.

S2: Who says so?

S1: Carl Rogers and Richard Farson, in a booklet for managers from nineteen fifty seven called Active Listening. The course read the excerpt reprinted in nineteen eighty seven, not the full booklet. And it's an essay, not a study. Their central idea is total meaning. In their words, "Any message a person tries to get across usually has two components: the content of the message and the feeling or attitude underlying this content." Their example is a machinist who could tell his foreman he's finished a lathe setup, or say, "Well, I'm finally finished with that damned lathe setup." Same content. Not the same message.

S2: So how do I know whether I've understood?

S1: They say you should test your understanding by "reflecting in your own words what the speaker seems to mean". Then: "His response to this will tell you whether or not he feels understood." And for a lively or controversial discussion they suggest a ground rule. Nobody makes a point of their own until they've restated the other person's, in their own words, because "merely parroting the words of another does not prove that one has understood but only that he has heard the words". And "The restatement must be accurate enough to satisfy the speaker before the listener can be allowed to speak for himself."

S2: So the judge is the speaker, not me. And they're allowed to say no, which sounds like I've failed.

S1: On this course's reading, a correction is the check succeeding. You made a guess, and you've been told the answer. That's why the rule keeps going until they're satisfied, rather than stopping at the first try.

S2: What does a good restatement sound like?

S1: The most detailed instructions come from motivational interviewing, a clinical counselling method, read here mainly through a US government treatment manual. It sorts reflections into simple ones, which repeat or rephrase, and complex ones, which use different words for the meaning or feeling underneath. You give it back "with your best guess", and the manual says, "Be open to being wrong." Take Radomir, a friend, who says over coffee, "I've rewritten this report three times and she still sends it back." A repeat: "You've rewritten it three times and she still sends it back." Close to parroting. A rephrase: "So it keeps coming back to you." A complex reflection: "It sounds like you're starting to wonder whether anything you write will satisfy her." A guess, in words he didn't use.

S2: And if I just said, "You must be furious"?

S1: That's a label, at full volume, as a statement rather than a guess. Suppose he says, "Not furious. Just tired. I don't even know what she wants any more." That's the most useful thing he's said. You had the direction roughly right and the intensity wrong, and now you know both. VitalTalk, an organisation founded by clinicians to teach communication in serious illness, advises: "In general, turn down the intensity a notch when you name the emotion". That's practitioner advice for clinicians with seriously ill patients.

S2: Do the other helping traditions agree?

S1: Largely, though it's practitioner advice, not a tested finding. Most of them share Rogers and Farson's shape: your own words, then a door left open for the other person to correct you. Not all: Samaritans and nine eight eight, the US crisis line, count plain repeating too, and nine eight eight doesn't mention inviting a correction. The open door is the part that gets dropped. "So what you're saying is", followed by your own next point, isn't a check. It's a run-up.

S2: What about when I'm the one explaining?

S1: Then it runs the other way. In medicine it's called teach-back, and the US Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality publishes a tool on it for clinicians. The part that transfers is the framing: "Remember, you are checking how well you explained something, not testing the patient." And it says "Do you understand?" and "Does that make sense?" are not teach-back questions, because, it says, patients tend to answer yes whether they understand or not.

S2: I say that all the time.

S1: Picture showing your great-aunt Wendeline how to video-call her granddaughter on a new phone. Open the app, find the name, press the green camera symbol, not the green handset. "Does that make sense?" "Yes, lovely," she says. Next Sunday she rings you instead, because she pressed the handset. Her yes was the answer the question was built for. So the second time you chunk it, two steps, and then: "I've thrown a lot at you, and I want to make sure I've explained it properly. If you wanted to ring her now, what would you press first?" Using teach-back outside a clinic like that is this course's reading of the method, not something anyone has tested.

S2: Some people call all this repeating back fake.

S1: Celeste Headlee, a radio host and writer, says it in her TED talk. She lists the usual advice, including "repeat back what you just heard or summarize it", and then: "So I want you to forget all of that. It is crap." Her case, in this course's words rather than hers, is that advice to display attention produces displays. And the strongest version of her point is Rogers's own. In a theoretical paper on therapy, also from nineteen fifty seven, he wrote that a feeling can be reflected in a way that communicates the therapist's lack of empathy. That's his theory, about therapy, not a finding.

S2: So who's right?

S1: They part company on what to do about it. She throws repeating back out. Rogers and Farson keep it, as a test the speaker grades. This course's reading is that they're partly talking about two different acts that look alike: a summary offered to show you were listening, and a guess offered so that you can be corrected. Headlee attacks the first. The rule describes the second.

S2: But does saying it back make people feel understood?

S1: That's lesson nine's question. One experiment bears on Headlee's point. A hundred and eighty undergraduates gave their opinions in peer interviews and got back either a paraphrase or a simple acknowledgement. The paraphrases were associated with the listener seeming more likeable, but not with the students feeling more understood. It's a single study, the lesson draws only on its abstract, and lesson nine sets it beside studies that point in other directions.

S2: The full lesson also has what Rogers and Farson told listeners to avoid, their warning that technique without the attitude is empty, the feedback study behind reading a correction as a check, why a friend isn't a counselling client, why acknowledging isn't agreeing, and two exercises, one with a partner and one on paper. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Say it back, and let them grade it.
