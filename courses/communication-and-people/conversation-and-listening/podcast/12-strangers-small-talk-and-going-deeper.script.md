S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Conversation and Listening, on the lesson called Strangers, small talk and going deeper. It starts on a commuter train, where nobody talks to the stranger in the next seat.

S2: Which hardly looks like a choice.

S1: In twenty fourteen Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder treated it as one. At a station outside Chicago, research assistants approached a hundred and eighteen people walking alone to a morning train and gave each one of three instructions: talk to a new person on the train, keep to yourself and enjoy your solitude, or commute as you normally would. Sixty six other commuters imagined the same instructions and predicted how each commute would go.

S2: And the predictors thought talking would be worse.

S1: Less pleasant and less productive, yes. The talkers actually reported a more positive commute than the people told to sit in solitude, and no less productive a one. Against commuters who travelled as usual, though, the difference wasn't statistically significant.

S2: So why were the guesses so far off?

S1: A later experiment found they underestimated how willing others would be. Among the talkers who returned their surveys, everyone who tried had managed a conversation, and not a single person reported being rebuffed. The authors' verdict on the risk: "As far as we can tell, it posed no risk at all." That rests on surveys posted back from morning trains near Chicago.

S2: Did it hold up anywhere else?

S1: The same group ran a version on trains in and out of London, with three hundred and eighty three commuters completing the survey. The talkers again had a more positive commute. But these commuters expected talking to be more pleasant, though an accidental change of wording had them imagine succeeding at it, not trying. A second experiment found they thought trying to have a conversation would be less pleasant than having one, and that only about a quarter of passengers would be willing. The paper's summary: "People may avoid pleasant conversations with strangers because of miscalibrated concerns about starting them."

S2: That sounds pretty settled.

S1: It isn't yet. This is one group's programme. London is a conceptual replication by the same group, so it isn't independent, and the course searched the papers citing both and found no direct replication by an outside team.

S2: Then going deeper, which is where I'd expect people to balk.

S1: Michael Kardas, Amit Kumar and Epley ran twelve experiments asking strangers to discuss deep questions. In one, eighty nine pairs of master's students got either five shallow questions or five deep ones. Both groups overestimated the awkwardness, the deep group far more. The deep talks did feel somewhat more awkward than the shallow ones, but in the authors' words, "the difference in participants' expectations was roughly four times larger than the difference that participants actually experienced." By the paper's own figures it was nearer three times than four.

S2: So deep talk makes people happier.

S1: That's the overreading. Whether a deep conversation felt better than a shallow one, as opposed to better than expected, varied: people felt more connected in two experiments and not in two others, and were at most marginally happier in any. The authors call these "somewhat inconsistent differences". And in that student experiment the deep group felt somewhat lonelier afterwards. The authors didn't predict it, pointed to the questions about embarrassing moments and crying, and measured loneliness in no other experiment. They name their own limits too: everyone knew they were in an experiment, each conversation was a single short one, and all of it ran in the United States. They argue it both ways for daily life, and which way it goes outside the lab is untested.

S2: Now the one I've actually heard of. Happy people don't do small talk.

S1: In twenty ten Matthias Mehl, Simine Vazire and two colleagues had seventy nine undergraduates wear a small recorder for four days. The happiest students had roughly a third as much small talk as the least happy. The authors were careful even then: "Naturally, our correlational findings are causally ambiguous."

S2: And then someone checked.

S1: In twenty eighteen Anne Milek and seven colleagues, Mehl and Vazire among them, pooled the original data with three new samples of adults. A fair comparison lines up the same measures. Small talk as a share of conversations, against life satisfaction, was minus point two five in twenty ten and minus point zero eight pooled, with an interval that took in zero. Substantive conversation, as a share of all recordings, held: point two six then, point two three pooled.

S2: With two of the original authors on that team. How did they put it?

S1: Without softening it: "The negative correlation suggested by the underpowered original study seems to have been a false-positive finding." And it's all correlation. Point two three isn't a twenty three per cent improvement in anything, and whether satisfied people draw more substantive talk, or substantive talk makes people more satisfied, "is still to be clarified".

S2: So which is it, small talk or depth?

S1: The course sets out both and gives no verdict. The depth position, from Kardas and colleagues: people stop short because they wrongly expect depth to be awkward and unwelcome, and in their words, "Misunderstanding others can encourage overly shallow interactions." Their best case is a consistent gap between expectation and experience across twelve experiments, and people who had both kinds of conversation preferring the deep one.

S2: And the case for small talk?

S1: That it's worth having in itself. The commuters who later said how they'd started mostly opened with something like the weather or "Where are you from?" Across Kardas and colleagues' own experiments, people "tended to report positive experiences after relatively shallow conversations". Surveys in Turkey and Japan, which the course read only as abstracts, found brief contact with strangers and people they weren't close to went with greater life satisfaction in one and greater happiness in the other. The pooled replication couldn't tell whether the small talk was with a stranger or a friend. And small talk is how depth is often reached: Kardas and colleagues suggest shallow questions may be "a gateway to deeper conversation". How much of yourself to share with someone at the bus stop is not something an experiment can decide. The course's reading is that small talk and depth aren't rivals, and in these studies people tended to stop short of the depth they'd have enjoyed.

S2: What about people who'd simply rather read?

S1: The evidence is about expectations, not proof that everyone wants to be talked to. The London paper opens with "Tube chat?" badges, answered by badges reading "Do not even think about talking to me". Kardas and colleagues note that "Cultures may vary in their openness to strangers", and where asking a near-stranger about their life is rude, that's a norm, not an error, on this course's reading. The London authors call their own setting "a culture well-known for being reserved", and the effect held there, in one British commuter setting. And none of the studies measured how people who value privacy feel after a stranger breaks it.

S2: So what do I actually do at the bus stop?

S1: The course's reading: try once, lightly, where the other person can easily decline, and take the answer you get. A one-word reply and a turn back to the book is, on the course's reading, a no without the word, and nothing in these studies licenses carrying on after that.
