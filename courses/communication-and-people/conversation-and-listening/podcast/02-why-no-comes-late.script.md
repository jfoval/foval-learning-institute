S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Conversation and Listening, lesson two. Why no comes late.

S2: Start me off. What's late about a no?

S1: Picture it. You ask a friend whether she can help you move on Saturday. There's a pause. Then, oh, Saturday, well. You know the answer before she gives it, and she knows you know. It's one of the most regular patterns conversation researchers have found in recorded English talk, and its timing half has been measured in ten languages. Accepting and agreeing tend to be quick and plain. Refusing and disagreeing tend to be late and wrapped in other words.

S2: Nobody ever taught me that. So where does it start?

S1: With turns that come in pairs. A question makes an answer due. A greeting, a greeting back. An invitation, an acceptance or a refusal. Emanuel Schegloff and Harvey Sacks named this in nineteen seventy three, the adjacency pair: two turns, next to each other, from different speakers. And the first part limits what can count as a fitting second. You answer an invitation by accepting or declining, not with a greeting.

S2: That sounds obvious.

S1: The consequence isn't. Once someone has produced the first part, the next speaker should produce the second. That's a pattern observed in recordings of English talk, not a measured rate. And it turns a silence after a question into an event. Nothing is being said, but something is missing, and people treat the missing answer as meaning something.

S2: Give me an example.

S1: There's a short recorded exchange that Sacks and later Anita Pomerantz discussed. Someone asks, "D'they have a good cook there?" Then a silence of one point seven seconds. Nobody has said no. But the person who asked comes back with "Nothing special?"

S2: Oh, they've rewritten their own question.

S1: So that no becomes the easy, agreeing answer. Kobin Kendrick and Francisco Torreira, who reproduce it in a twenty fifteen study, read it this way: the speaker treats the silence as a sign that an unwelcome answer is on its way, and acts on it before it arrives.

S2: And when an unwelcome answer does arrive, what does it look like?

S1: Pomerantz studied what happens after someone gives an assessment, like "That was a good film", and the other person agrees or disagrees, in recorded American English conversation. What follows is her findings in the course's words, not hers. Agreements filled the turn, were stated outright, and came with little or no gap. Disagreements were often delayed, often prefaced with a well or an uh, often partial, a yes but, and sometimes never stated at all. And silence, or a what, was heard as disagreement that hadn't yet been said.

S2: Do those two shapes have names?

S1: Preferred and dispreferred, and the words are a little misleading. Preferred doesn't mean what either person wants. It names the reply the sequence is built for, the one that can come plainly and at once. Dispreferred is the one that arrives late and cushioned. The same pattern shows up with invitations, offers and requests, where accepting is preferred and declining dispreferred.

S2: Make it concrete for me.

S1: Birgit asks Emrys, do you want to see a film on Friday? Reply one: Yes! What time? Reply two: Oh, well, Friday's a bit, I'd love to, but it's my sister's birthday.

S2: Poor Birgit.

S1: Look at the parts of reply two. A delay: the oh and the pauses. A preface: well. A qualification: Friday's a bit, which starts a reason without finishing it. An appreciation: I'd love to. An account: his sister's birthday, a reason that isn't about Birgit. And no no. He never says it. She has to hear it, and she will. The first five are what Kendrick and Torreira, summarising earlier conversation analysis, list as what a refusal of an invitation uses and an acceptance doesn't. The sixth is one this course adds from Pomerantz.

S2: So how long does a pause have to be before it means no?

S1: That's where the counting comes in. Kendrick and Torreira took a hundred and ninety five responses to invitations, offers, requests and similar, from English telephone recordings, and timed them. Sixty three percent were preferred and thirty seven percent dispreferred. The prefaces mattered. When a response began with well, the chance it was dispreferred was point seven eight. With none of the markers they coded, no well, no um, no in-breath, it was point two one.

S2: And the timing?

S1: Not what you'd expect. Most responses of both kinds came within about zero to seven hundred milliseconds, and many dispreferred ones came early. But after about seven hundred milliseconds, the dispreferred ones outnumbered the preferred. A quarter of the dispreferred responses came after that point, against eight point nine percent of the preferred. The authors sum it up in one sentence, which I'll give close to their words. They do not find that long delays are characteristic of dispreferred actions, since short gaps are the most frequent before dispreferreds. But they do find that dispreferred actions are characteristic of long delays.

S2: Say that again, slowly.

S1: Most unwelcome replies don't come late. But among the replies that do come late, the unwelcome ones outnumber the rest. So a long delay shifts the odds towards an unwelcome answer. It doesn't decide the answer. And a pause under about three hundred milliseconds tells you almost nothing. Past about three hundred, a plain, unqualified acceptance got less likely, and a hedged reply of either kind, like a yes but, got more likely.

S2: What about the person waiting? When does a pause start to sound like reluctance?

S1: In twenty thirteen, Roberts and Francis played recorded phone calls between friends, acted for the study, to three hundred and eighty undergraduates at one US university, Purdue. Each call ended with a request answered by the same recorded sure, after a silence set somewhere between two hundred and twelve hundred milliseconds, and the students rated how willing the person answering was. Ratings dropped off noticeably at six hundred milliseconds, with a statistically significant difference between seven hundred and eight hundred. Beyond about nine hundred, they stopped falling further.

S2: So in under a second, a yes starts to sound like a no.

S1: Like a less willing yes, anyway. And keep the scope attached. Those six hundred to eight hundred figures come from American English speakers, US undergraduates, rating a sure to a friend's request. The authors say so themselves: they were "only testing the effect in one language group", and six hundred milliseconds shouldn't be treated as an absolute value. Lesson one showed that average gaps differ between languages, and nobody in what this course read tested where the line falls in other languages.

S2: Does it ever run the other way, where the no is the one that comes fast?

S1: It does. Say Hollis fumbles a card trick at a party and says, I'm hopeless at this, aren't I? Pomerantz found that after someone runs themselves down, the usual preference reverses. Agreeing would be criticising them, so that's the reply that comes late and wrapped. Disagreeing supports them, so it comes quickly and outright. No, you're not, you nearly had it.

S2: And if I pause before my no?

S1: Then your disagreement has the dispreferred shape. Pomerantz notes that after a self-deprecation a silence can itself be heard as agreement not yet said. On this course's reading, a pause before your no can carry some of that, so Hollis may hear the pause as the part that agrees. That flip is why conversation analysts treat preference as a feature of the sequence, not of anyone's wishes.
