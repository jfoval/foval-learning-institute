S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Conversation and Listening, on the lesson called What body language can and cannot tell you.

S2: Start with a famous figure. Seven per cent words, thirty eight per cent tone, fifty five per cent face. Where's that from?

S1: Two small laboratory studies from nineteen sixty seven, by Albert Mehrabian. People judged single spoken words and posed photographs of faces for how much a speaker seemed to like or dislike someone, where the word said one thing and the tone or face another. Mehrabian limits it himself, on his web page: "Unless a communicator is talking about their feelings or attitudes, these equations are not applicable."

S2: So it's about liking, when the signals disagree. What about lying?

S1: Hundreds of studies have asked that, so the answer is unusually firm. Charles Bond and Bella DePaulo's twenty oh six meta-analysis pooled the judgements of over twenty four thousand people. On average they got about fifty four per cent right, where guessing gives fifty. They leaned towards belief, calling more statements true than false. And police, judges and other professionals did no better, averaging under fifty five per cent. The course read that paper from a flawed scan, so its figures are paraphrased.

S2: But surely liars look away.

S1: A twenty oh three pooling by DePaulo and five colleagues found that eye contact and looking away barely differed between liars and truth tellers. Yet when an international team asked people in fifty eight countries how to tell someone is lying, looking away came top in fifty one. Across two surveys, the belief, in the team's words, "was expressed in every one of the 75 countries we studied".

S2: So are people just watching the wrong things?

S1: Maria Hartwig and Charles Bond tested that in twenty eleven; the course read only their abstract. They say people "rarely rely on the wrong cues". What limits accuracy is "weaknesses in behavioral cues to deception".

S2: Yet police and airports use these cues. What's their case?

S1: Take the Reid technique, an interrogation method in which, Vincent Denault and colleagues report, more than half a million people have reportedly been trained. Its creators, John E. Reid and Associates, describe its first step, the Behavior Analysis Interview, as "designed to identify whether or not a person is telling the truth or withholding relevant information concerning a specific crime or act of wrongdoing". The technique's manual, as Denault and colleagues quote it, says that "generally speaking, a suspect who does not make direct eye contact is probably withholding information". And the manual cautions, they report, that a lack of eye contact may come from other things, including culture.

S2: And the answer?

S1: Denault and colleagues reply that, for a lack of eye contact, research "has even shown that it is not a valid sign of lying". The nineteen ninety four study they say is often presented as support, which the course hasn't read, "suffers from fatal methodological shortcomings", in their words, among them a small sample and no comparison with untrained evaluators. But they allow that "some other aspects of BAI may offer avenues for research". The course read Denault's paper, not the manual.

S2: And the airports?

S1: From two thousand and seven, in a programme called SPOT, US airport security officers watched passengers in queues for signs including avoiding eye contact. In twenty thirteen the Government Accountability Office reviewed four meta-analyses and found "that the human ability to accurately identify deceptive behavior based on behavioral indicators is the same as or slightly better than chance". Homeland Security disagreed, and GAO's report gives its reasons: the published research "lacked ecological and external validity", because none of it was about airports; its own unpublished research supported behaviour detection; and SPOT looked for fear or stress, not lies as such.

S2: Those aren't bad points.

S1: GAO answered each and kept its conclusion, while granting that "behavior detection holds promise for use in certain circumstances and in conjunction with certain other technologies". The course hasn't read the department's own letter. On the course's reading, what would settle it is field studies where the truth is later known. Until then, judging lies from demeanour alone does poorly.

S2: So is there anything that does better?

S1: Listening to words. In a twenty nineteen review by Aldert Vrij and two colleagues, ten of thirty five cues that were at least partly nonverbal were related to lying. Of eighteen verbal cues, eight were, and the authors call the verbal results "more promising". Among the cues that were related, the verbal ones averaged an effect of point three, the nonverbal ones point two six.

S2: So the working verbal cues aren't much stronger. It's that a bigger share of them works.

S1: Exactly. And how much better words and good questions can do is contested: the direction is consistent, the size argued.

S2: Now faces. Can I read a feeling from one?

S1: That's a live dispute, and the course won't settle it. On the basic-emotion side, Dacher Keltner and three colleagues argue, in a twenty nineteen review, that "upwards of 20 emotions are signaled in multimodal and dynamic patterns of expressive behavior", with recognition shaped by context. The course read their abstract and searched the full text. Their evidence includes a meta-analysis finding emotions recognised above chance across cultures, and a machine-learning study of six million videos from a hundred and forty four countries.

S2: And the other side?

S1: The constructionists. Lisa Feldman Barrett and four colleagues agree that people scowl when angry and smile when happy "more than what would be expected by chance", but with substantial variation. They report a meta-analysis where the expected face correlated about point three two with measures of the emotion, and showed up in about nineteen per cent of emotional events. Hence their line: "a scowling facial configuration is an expression of anger, but not the expression of anger".

S2: Point three two isn't thirty two per cent right, I take it.

S1: No, it's a real relationship, not a percentage. And both reviews say faces carry social information and that context changes what they mean. The argument is over how much a face says on its own.

S2: Last, a workplace-training favourite. Power posing?

S1: In twenty ten Dana Carney, Amy Cuddy and Andy Yap reported that two one-minute expansive poses changed hormones and increased felt power and risk-taking, in forty two people. Then a study of two hundred found posing raised felt power, but no effect on hormones or behaviour. Carney herself wrote, "I do not believe that “power pose” effects are real." The hormone and behaviour claims failed to replicate, and that part is settled.

S2: But felt power isn't?

S1: No, and Cuddy's side first. She and two colleagues argued in twenty eighteen that how people feel is a legitimate outcome in its own right, reporting strong evidence for it, as a British Psychological Society article reports; the course hasn't read that reply. And when a twenty twenty review of seventy three studies came out, Cuddy said, as that article quotes her: "At this point, anyone who claims these effects are not real cannot support that claim with science. These findings are vindicating".

S2: And the other side?

S1: Marcus Credé pointed out, again as that article reports, that most of the studies in Cuddy's reply had no neutral posture to compare with. And that same review, which the course read at the abstract, found slumped against neutral postures had an effect of point four five, expansive against neutral only point oh six. So slumping may matter more than standing tall. It's contested, and large preregistered studies with a neutral posture would settle it.
