S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Conversation and Listening, on the lesson called Ask, don't guess. It's about two mistakes that feel like knowledge: being sure you know what someone thinks, and being sure your own feeling was obvious.

S2: Start with the first. Almost everyone's been told to put themselves in the other person's shoes.

S1: And Tal Eyal, Mary Steffel and Nicholas Epley tested whether it makes you more accurate about what someone else thinks or feels. About two thirds of three hundred and thirty six people asked beforehand predicted it would. Across twenty five experiments, on faces, lies and a partner's opinions, the people told to take a perspective did try, but it lowered accuracy slightly: d equals minus point two three. In the first fifteen the drop was significant in only four of seventeen comparisons, and even those didn't hold up when repeated.

S2: So imagining someone's view makes you worse?

S1: Slightly less accurate. The authors are careful: they say their experiments can't confirm that perspective taking is ineffective, because a study can't prove there's no effect. And they measured accuracy, not kindness. It's also one team's programme, not independently repeated as far as this course read.

S2: What did they try instead?

S1: What they call perspective getting: asking. The last experiment recruited a hundred and four couples from the community, together ten years on average. One partner predicted how the other would rate twenty opinion statements. Some had no instruction. Some spent five minutes writing about "a typical day in the life of your partner". Some spent five minutes asking their partner about all twenty statements, and some asked about only half.

S2: I'm guessing asking won.

S1: By a wide margin. Asking about all twenty got eight point six of twenty exactly right. No instruction got four point nine. Writing about the partner's day got three point nine five.

S2: Couples ten years in got fewer than five out of twenty?

S1: Yes. Ten years together isn't knowing what they think about a particular thing. And asking worked only for what was asked: those who asked about half got better on that half and no better on the rest. Nobody felt the difference. Confidence didn't vary across the conditions: on average people believed they'd got thirteen point seven one right, and actually got six point two. The authors call every group "dramatically overconfident". With about twenty couples per condition, it's a large effect in a small experiment. Their advice: "If you want to know what another person is thinking, it may be best to put them in a situation where they can answer honestly and then ask them directly."

S2: Now the second mistake. My feeling was obvious.

S1: Thomas Gilovich, Kenneth Savitsky and Victoria Husted Medvec called it the illusion of transparency: overestimating how far other people can tell what you're feeling or thinking. In one study, Cornell undergraduates sipped cups of red drink on camera, some cherry Kool-Aid, some foul, and kept a neutral face. They expected four point nine one of ten observers to spot each foul drink. Three point five six did, when guessing blind would give three point three three. In the authors' words, "Observers performed no better than chance and fell far short of tasters' estimates."

S2: So nobody can read a face.

S1: That's further than the studies go. Every study in the set asked people to hide a feeling, from strangers, in a lab. And the authors don't claim it's everywhere: "In many cases, people may be well calibrated regarding how much their thoughts, feelings, and emotions are apparent to others." They found it for the foul drink, not for the pleasant one.

S2: So what follows?

S1: On this course's reading, which goes beyond strangers in a lab and so is weaker than the finding: if you want a feeling known, say it. And if someone else shows no sign, that's weak evidence either way. Not "she's fine", and not "she's secretly furious". When it matters, ask.

S2: Does knowing someone well help?

S1: Some. In a single study reported in William Ickes's review, pairs of male friends scored about fifty percent higher on empathic accuracy than pairs of male strangers: twenty nine point eight against nineteen point nine. The review doesn't say what scale those are on, and they're similarity ratings, not the share of thoughts read. The advantage came, it seemed, from knowing more about each other's lives.

S2: The lesson ends on an argument about empathy.

S1: Between Paul Bloom, then at Yale, and Jamil Zaki at Stanford. Bloom's target is emotional empathy, feeling what you believe someone else feels, not understanding them and not caring about them. Most of his argument is about moral and policy decisions, where he says "empathy is narrow in its focus, rendering it innumerate and subject to bias". He grants that close relationships are a harder case, but argues that when you go to someone you love feeling anxious or sad, you don't want them anxious or sad too: "Here, it is understanding and compassion we need, not empathy". And in his response to Zaki, emotional empathy "is corrosive in personal relationships, exhausting the spirit and making us less effective at helping those we love." He argues for understanding and caring, over sharing the distress.

S2: So Zaki's the "more empathy" side?

S1: No, and that's the cartoon to avoid. For Zaki, empathy is something you can steer: "If you instead view empathy as something that people can control, then people can choose to align their empathy more with their values." He wants more of it where people fail to empathize, and he's clear it runs both ways: "It's just as important to know when to turn down one's empathy, especially if you need to engage in self-care." His example is his wife, a clinical psychologist: "the last thing that any of her patients need if they're depressed, is for her to be depressed as well."

S2: That sounds a lot like Bloom.

S1: On support, largely, on this course's reading: both say there are times a helper shouldn't simply share someone's distress. Bloom's written response even agrees empathy can be chosen, and says that flexibility is part of the problem, because it "can easily be manipulated by others." No reply from Zaki is on the page, so the exchange stops without a winner. Where they differ is whether empathy, chosen well, is a good guide to moral decisions. Bloom says it's too narrow and biased to be one; Zaki says people can choose to align it with their values. The empirical part would be settled by long-term trials of whether support from someone who shares a friend's distress helps more than calm, caring support, rated by the friend. Nobody has run them, as far as this course found. What ought to guide how you care for people, no trial settles.
