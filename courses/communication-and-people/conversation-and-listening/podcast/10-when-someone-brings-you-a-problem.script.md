S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Conversation and Listening, on the lesson called When someone brings you a problem. This course is education, not care. If you're thinking about suicide or self-harm, or don't feel able to keep yourself safe, contact emergency services, like nine one one in the US and Canada or nine nine nine in the UK, or a crisis line. In the US and Canada you can call or text nine eight eight. In the UK and Ireland, Samaritans are on one one six, one two three. The box at the top of the lesson has lines for other countries. If a friend's problem turns out to be serious, lesson eleven, When it's serious, is where the institute deals with it. This one is about ordinary problems: a lost job, a failed exam, a row with a sister.

S2: So what's the usual first reaction?

S1: Part of you starts working on the answer before they've finished the second sentence. In two studies where people responded to imagined complaints from friends who weren't asking for advice, they offered some anyway, very early, in about seventy per cent of cases. The course read only the abstract.

S2: How do you tell a good reply from a bad one?

S1: Communication researchers grade comforting messages on nine levels of how person-centred they are, meaning how far a reply acknowledges and validates what the person feels. Low is condemning the feeling, challenging whether it's legitimate, or ignoring it. Moderate is distracting them, condolences, or questions about what happened. High is recognising the feeling, explaining it, or helping them see how it fits a broader context. A two thousand and eighteen paper by Susanne Jones, Graham Bodie and colleagues coded every turn in two hundred and twenty three five-minute conversations, where a student talked about something upsetting to a stranger who was secretly a trained confederate.

S2: And the confederates told to give the best comfort lived at the top?

S1: No. On average only twenty four per cent of their turns were in the top three levels. About half were sympathy statements, and raters still judged those conversations highly person-centred. The authors' reading is that good comfort cycles between moderate and high. Low-comfort turns went with worse outcomes more strongly than high-comfort turns went with better ones, though the effects either way were small. And level four, distraction, and reassurance that it will all work out with nothing behind it, behaved like the bottom band. It's a single study, of strangers, and the authors say their results may be unique to strangers. The course's reading is that the scale is a way of hearing what a reply does, not a script, and the strongest thing the evidence supports is avoiding the bottom of it.

S2: Then should I give a friend advice at all?

S1: The listening professions and the advice researchers answer differently at first sight. The helping traditions lean against it. A US counselling manual gives Gordon's roadblock of "Giving advice, making suggestions, or providing solutions prematurely or when unsolicited". Samaritans' advice for someone who's struggling includes not offering advice. That's practitioner advice. And part of their case, on this course's reading, is a value: the person with the problem should find their own way through it. No study settles that part.

S2: And the other side?

S1: The advice researchers say it depends on how and when. In a study by Erina MacGeorge and colleagues, students each brought a friend to the lab, and one of the pair talked about a real problem. Of three hundred and fifty nine pairs, two hundred and forty four of the people with the problem said they got some advice. Whether they meant to act on it went with whether it seemed to work and was feasible. Whether it seemed good advice, and helped them cope, went with how politely it came. One study, of US college students. And in lesson nine's experiment with strangers, people given advice were no less satisfied than people given active listening, though active listening made them feel more understood.

S2: Does the order matter?

S1: Bo Feng had five hundred and seventy two US and five hundred and forty Chinese college students each read a transcript of a friend helping with a problem, in eleven different orders. Support, then questions, then advice was rated highest in quality and most likely to be followed, in both groups. The US students rated advice with support or questions somewhat higher than the Chinese students did, but Feng says the sequence worked "very similarly" in both, and calls the differences "small in magnitude". The winning order's edge was relatively small, the advice had been asked for, the problems were modest, and the conversations were imagined.

S2: So who's right?

S1: The course classes it as largely established that timing, whether advice was asked for, and politeness matter; whether to advise in a particular case is a judgement. Its reading is that the two sides agree more than their slogans suggest, and differ most where the person is in distress, which is where the advice studies say least.

S2: And if a friend who's lost his job does ask?

S1: Motivational interviewing uses a shape called Ask-Offer-Ask. It's practitioner advice for clinicians; what transfers, on the course's reading, is the shape. Ask what he's been thinking of doing. Offer, with his agreement: "If it were me, I'd check how many weeks the redundancy pay covers before grabbing the first thing." Then ask how that sits with him.

S2: The lesson also says help can backfire just by being noticed.

S1: That's from Niall Bolger's lab, one group's programme. In a diary study of sixty eight couples before the New York bar exam, on days near the exam when the partner reported giving emotional support, the examinee's depression rose less the next day; on days the examinee reported receiving it, it rose more. Support given but not noticed was the best combination: depression tended to fall, though not by a statistically significant margin. A correlation from diaries. So Bolger and David Amarel ran three experiments, two hundred and fifty seven women students facing a speech. A confederate gave a tip or reassurance, either to her face or to the experimenter in her hearing.

S2: And hearing it sideways worked better?

S1: Invisible support reduced distress compared with visible support. Against no support at all, invisible support came out ahead in both experiments that tested it, but in neither did the difference reach statistical significance. The third experiment pointed at why, in the authors' words: "invisibility per se is not the essence of what makes invisible support effective; rather, it is (at least in part) avoiding the communication that the support recipient is inefficacious." Invisible didn't mean secret. She heard every word. It just didn't come labelled as help for someone who couldn't cope. These were women students, a speech, and a lab.
