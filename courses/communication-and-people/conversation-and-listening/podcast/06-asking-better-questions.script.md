S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Conversation and Listening, on the lesson called Asking better questions. Most advice about being good company tells you what to say. Some of the clearest experimental evidence is about what to ask.

S2: Starting where?

S1: With Karen Huang and four colleagues at Harvard. In their first experiment, three hundred and ninety eight people sat in separate cubicles and chatted with a stranger by instant message for fifteen minutes, to get to know each other. In each pair, one person was secretly told to ask at least nine questions, or at most four.

S2: And the people told to ask more were liked more?

S1: They were. Their partners rated liking at five point seven nine on a scale of one to seven, against five point three one for the low askers. And notice the comparison: not people who asked nothing, but people told to ask at most four questions in fifteen minutes. A second study, run online, found high askers liked more again.

S2: Did the askers see that coming?

S1: No, and that's the part I like. When they predicted how much their partner liked them, the prediction didn't differ between the two groups. Across everyone, how many questions a person asked was unrelated to how much they expected to be liked.

S2: So you'd think outsiders reading the chats would like the askers too.

S1: They didn't. A third group read the transcripts of the online chats, and they did not like the high askers more. They liked the people who'd been asked a lot of questions, perhaps, the authors suggest, because people answering many questions came across as fuller, more interesting people. The warmth towards the asker was felt by the person being asked, not by people watching.

S2: Why would asking make you liked, though?

S1: The authors' answer is responsiveness, which they describe as "an interpersonal construct that captures listening, understanding, validation, and care". Coders rated high askers as more responsive, and in the authors' words, "responsiveness explains the effect of question-asking on liking". That's their mechanism, supported by a statistical test, not seen directly. And it's one group's programme: strangers chatting by text, in a US university lab and online. It has been checked, though. In twenty twenty five the journal published a correction after an independent audit, and the notice says "The audit confirmed that all the conclusions in the paper are valid".

S2: Did it matter what kind of questions people asked?

S1: It did. Coders labelled every question with one of six types. The two big ones are the follow-up, which asks about something the partner just said, and the full switch, which moves to a new topic unrelated to what they said. People who asked more follow-ups were rated more responsive and were liked more. People who asked more full switches were rated less responsive. A follow-up is only possible if you took in what the person said. On the authors' account, that's why it works.

S2: Give me one of each.

S1: Saoirse is back from a week in Madeira. Lorcan, a colleague, asks, "Did you have a good holiday?" She says, "Yes, lovely, thanks." He asks what the best part was, and she says the walk along the levadas, the old water channels cut into the hillsides. Then, "What were the levadas like?" That's a follow-up. It could only come from someone who heard her.

S2: And the switch, straight after "Yes, lovely", would be, "Did you see the news about the office move?"

S1: Right. Nothing's wrong with the question. It just tells Saoirse the holiday was a formality.

S2: So the answer is ask as many follow-ups as you can?

S1: The authors themselves raised a limit, as a possibility they didn't test. The relation may be curvilinear, and they write, "When one asks too many questions without reciprocating self-disclosure, liking may decrease." A twenty twenty six review of the field puts it this way: "Question asking is not inherently good or bad for listening; it depends on purpose, timing, and relational stance."

S2: Now, the lesson says one word can matter.

S1: In a clinic, yes. In two thousand and seven John Heritage and colleagues worked with twenty family doctors in Los Angeles County and a town in Pennsylvania. After four normal visits, each was randomly assigned to one question, asked once the patient had given their first concern: "Is there anything else you want to address in the visit today?" or "Is there something else you want to address in the visit today?"

S2: And?

S1: Among patients who had come with more than one concern, ninety point three percent said yes to "something". To "anything", fifty three point one. In visits with no special question, thirty seven percent of those patients left with a concern unmentioned. With "something", the adjusted figure fell to nine percent. With "anything", the difference from asking nothing wasn't statistically significant. And neither question made visits longer.

S2: Why would "any" do that?

S1: The authors' explanation is grammatical. "Any" belongs in negative sentences: you say "I haven't got any samples", not "I've got any samples". So on their account, "anything else?" quietly signals that the expected answer is no. That's their account, not something the study tested separately. This course's reading is that it fits lesson two: a question built for one answer makes that answer the easy one to give.

S2: One study, though.

S1: Twenty volunteer doctors, two areas, and the authors call it relatively small, too small to detect modest differences, so they ask for caution about the non-significant results, including "anything" against asking nothing. The course found one attempt to repeat it. In the UK, Geraldine Leydon and colleagues, with Heritage among the authors, ran a feasibility trial, a smaller trial run to see whether a full-scale one could be done, with twenty one GPs and three hundred and twenty patients: "any", "some", or usual care. In their words, they were drawing on the US study, "but not replicating the design". So it was not a copy, and as a feasibility study they reported descriptive figures only.

S2: What did they see?

S1: The wording "seemed to have no bearing on the number of patient concerns elicited, on consultation length or on patient satisfaction". But there was little for any question to fix. Only nine of two hundred and fifty two patients reported leaving with a planned concern unmentioned, across all three arms. The authors concluded that the wording "may not be as important as the placement of the GP solicitation", that is, asking early.

S2: So which is it?

S1: This course's reading: in a US clinic where more than a third of patients with several concerns left with one unsaid, one word made a large difference. In a UK trial where almost nobody did, there was nothing for the word to change. The one thing both found is that asking didn't make visits longer.
