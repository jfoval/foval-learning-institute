S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Conversation and Listening, on lesson three, Hearing is not listening. And it starts with a number. A guide for UK managers from Acas, the government-funded advisory service on employment, lists how we spend the working day communicating. The last line is listening, forty five percent. There's no footnote and no study named.

S2: Forty five percent sounds like somebody measured it.

S1: It does. We'll follow it back, because that teaches a lot. But first, what the word even means.

S2: Hearing and listening. Aren't they the same thing?

S1: Every interpersonal communication textbook this course looked at separates them. Hearing is the physical part: sound reaches you. Listening is what you do with it: attending, making sense of it, responding, remembering. You know it from ordinary speech. I heard you, I just wasn't listening. And the scholars' word isn't yours. When "the average individual" talks about listening, the listening scholars Worthington and Bodie write, they mean "a state of interpersonal connection with and presence of others". So a study of recall from a recorded talk and a friend who says you never listen to me are using the same word for different things.

S2: All right. The forty five percent. Where did it come from?

S1: Paul Rankin, who worked for the Detroit public schools. Ralph Nichols and Leonard Stevens tell the story in their nineteen fifty seven book called Are You Listening. As they tell it, Rankin selected sixty eight adults of different occupations and asked them to keep tabs, every fifteen minutes, of how long they spent talking, reading, writing and listening, for about two months. That's a single study, self-reported, with sixty eight people. And this course couldn't open Rankin's own article, so it knows the survey only through the people who retold it.

S2: Do the retellings agree?

S1: No. Of the time those adults spent communicating, Nichols and Stevens give nine percent writing, sixteen reading, thirty talking and forty five listening. An open textbook by Usera says Rankin found adults listen forty two percent and speak thirty two percent of their daily communication time. Two retellings, two numbers, citing different Rankin publications, and this course can't say which matches Rankin's text.

S2: And the Acas guide?

S1: Writing nine, reading sixteen, speaking thirty, listening forty five. The same four numbers in the same order as Nichols and Stevens. That's this course's reading, not something Acas says: four identical figures make it very likely the guide carries the version Nichols and Stevens printed, whether from their book or one of the many places that repeated it, restated as a fact about our working day.

S2: Did Nichols and Stevens think the survey was solid?

S1: This is the part worth hearing. They wrote that it "may mean little if harshly analyzed in terms of scientific research", and that an expert might doubt its accuracy. Then, in the next sentence, that "regardless of technical merit", it "may be one of the most important pieces of educational research in several generations". Their reason was that it showed schools taught reading and writing and neglected speaking and listening. The numbers were rough. The point was what mattered to them.

S2: So what can I honestly say?

S1: Something like this. Surveys going back to the nineteen twenties, all self-reported, have found listening to be the communication activity people report doing most, or near it, with estimates from about a quarter to over half of communication time, depending on who was asked and what counted. The direction is reasonably settled. No single percentage is a fact.

S2: Here's my confession. If you asked me to rate my own listening, I'd score myself pretty high.

S1: Listening gets measured three ways. You rate yourself, the person you talked to rates how listened-to they felt, or someone records the conversation and codes what you did. A self-rating measures what people say they do. The nearest evidence this course read on whether that matches what they do comes from Ickes's work on empathic accuracy: how well one person infers what another is actually thinking and feeling. Two people are videotaped talking, each later lists what they were thinking at each moment, and each then guesses the other's. In one of his group's studies, of thirty eight pairs of opposite-sex strangers, self-report measures of empathic skill and accuracy "proved to be disappointingly poor predictors of actual empathic accuracy". Most of the correlations were in fact negative.

S2: So my high score means nothing.

S1: It's weak evidence, which isn't the same. That's a single study inside one group's programme, with student samples, and it's about reading thoughts and feelings, which is part of listening, not all of it. From that study and others in his programme, Ickes concluded that "people lack metaknowledge regarding their own empathic accuracy".

S2: Last question, then. What is listening for? Getting people to like you?

S1: That one evidence can't settle. It's a value question. Is listening mainly something that gets you what you want, or attention you owe a person whatever it gets you? Dale Carnegie, in How to Win Friends and Influence People, in nineteen thirty six, puts the principle as "Be a good listener. Encourage others to talk about themselves." His anchor story is a dinner where he listened to a botanist for hours, said very little, and was later called "a most interesting conversationalist".

S2: That sounds like a technique for being liked.

S1: He's clear it worked because it was real. He had listened, he says, "because I was genuinely interested". For Carnegie listening is generous and it also works: people like you for it, and in his examples angry customers calm down. Those are anecdotes, many from his courses, not research. But one group's experiments give his core observation some support: strangers getting acquainted, mostly in online chats, liked partners who asked them more questions. Lesson six takes that up.

S2: And the other side?

S1: Rogers and Farson, who coined active listening in a nineteen fifty seven booklet for managers, also expected results. Active listening, they wrote, "aims to bring about changes in people", and "relies upon definite techniques". But the change they meant was in the speaker, who grows less defensive and clearer about what they think. And they warned that the techniques done without real respect for the speaker "will be empty and sterile". It's an essay, not a study, and it cites clinical and research evidence without naming any.

S2: So they both want listening to do something. They just disagree about what.

S1: And there's a third view, which treats listening as a question of who gets attention at all. It's associated with the sociologist Charles Derber. This course hasn't read his book, and the one account of him it cites is Celeste Headlee's, so it can't give his view here in its own terms. Lesson four takes up the part Headlee describes.

S2: Where does the course come down?

S1: It takes a side and says so: its lessons aim at understanding the other person. That's a standpoint, not a finding. Techniques for changing someone's mind belong to a later course, Persuasion and Influence.

S2: So nobody's been proved wrong here.

S1: Not by evidence. It's a question of what you think listening is for.
