S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Conversation and Listening, on lesson eleven, When it's serious. It's about talking with someone you're worried may be thinking about suicide. First, what the lesson puts at the top. This course is education, not care. If you're thinking about suicide or self-harm, or don't feel able to keep yourself safe, contact emergency services, nine one one in the US and Canada, nine nine nine in the UK, one one two across the EU, zero zero zero in Australia, or a crisis line. Call or text nine eight eight in the US and Canada. Call Samaritans on one one six, one two three in the UK and Ireland, or Lifeline on thirteen eleven fourteen in Australia. Elsewhere, find a helpline dot com lists free, confidential lines by country.

S2: And if the person listening is the one who's struggling?

S1: Then those numbers are for you, and they matter more than anything else we say. You don't need to keep listening, though you're welcome to. The rest of this is for the friend, the colleague, the relative.

S2: So what stops people starting this conversation?

S1: Often a fear that asking will make things worse. In one survey of a hundred and three family doctors in England, reported in a two thousand and fourteen review, a third believed that questions about suicidal behaviour could put thoughts of self-harm into a patient's head. The services that deal with suicide every day disagree. Their advice is practitioner advice, from long experience, and much the same from one service to the next.

S2: What do they tell you to do first?

S1: Notice, and say what you've noticed. R U OK?, an Australian charity, gives an example: "You seem less chatty than usual. How are you going?" Then ask directly. Be The One To, a campaign the nine eight eight Lifeline created, gives the question in words: "Are you thinking about suicide?" And R U OK? says plainly, "Asking the question does not lead to suicide." Then listen, without shock and without a lecture. Nine eight eight: "Don't act shocked. This will put distance between you." And "Don't lecture on the value of life."

S2: So what does the evidence say about the fear, that asking plants the idea?

S1: Two reviews. In two thousand and fourteen, Dazzi and three colleagues found thirteen papers. Their conclusion, where suicidal ideation means suicidal thinking: "None found a statistically significant increase in suicidal ideation among participants asked about suicidal thoughts." That's no increase on average, not for everyone. In one study they describe, drawing on interviews with sixty three people who had self-harmed or had suicidal thoughts, fifty seven per cent improved after being asked, and twenty two per cent came out worse. Then in two thousand and eighteen, Caroline Blades and three colleagues at the University of Western Australia pooled eighteen studies, thirteen thousand one hundred and ninety two participants aged thirteen to ninety two. Being asked went with small reductions in suicidal thinking.

S2: I can hear a but coming.

S1: Several, most of them the authors' own. That reduction came from studies that measured the same people before and after, with nobody unasked to compare them with, so part of it could be regression to the mean. Where exposed and unexposed groups were compared directly on suicidal thinking, there was no difference either way. A result with fewer reported suicide attempts counted a school programme and follow-up calls as "exposure", which is wider than being asked a question. Every study had ethics approval and a plan for adverse events, so the sample may be selective. And the three studies that leaned, not significantly, towards more suicidal thinking all involved people with significant mental health issues, though where the authors could test it, clinical and non-clinical samples didn't differ. In most studies the suicide questions came among questions about depression and support, so the effect of the suicide questions alone is unclear. Their own summary: "evidence to date suggests that asking research participants about suicide does not increase risk, and may be associated with small benefits."

S2: So is it settled or not?

S1: Both, depending where. It's established that asking in research, screening and clinical settings hasn't been found to increase suicidal thinking. But no study in either review tested a conversation between friends or family. "Ask directly", for a friend, is the services' unanimous advice, extended from that evidence to a setting nobody has measured. The strongest caution today is about reach: the evidence comes from structured questionnaires and trained clinicians with support in place, not a frightened friend in a kitchen.

S2: Say they answer yes. Is it my job to work out how serious it is?

S1: Mental Health First Aid says "your role is not to diagnose someone or solve the problem, but instead to provide support and information." Two of the services, Be The One To and Mental Health First Aid, do ask helpers some questions about how much danger the person is in. This course doesn't teach them, and that's the course's own decision, not the services' advice. It leaves them to the crisis lines and trained responders, and if you're unsure, the lines will advise you.

S2: So what do I actually say?

S1: Take Leopold, the lesson's colleague who's gone quiet. If he says yes, you might say, "I'm really glad you told me. I don't know how to help with this on my own, but people do. Would you ring a line with me now?" And stay with him while you do. R U OK? says that if the person says yes when you ask, the most important things are to keep them safe, "do not leave them alone", and to get them some immediate professional help. If he won't ring, you can call a line yourself for advice, without leaving him on his own. If a life is in danger now, the emergency number comes first.

S2: What if someone makes you promise not to tell?

S1: That's Fenella's case. She makes you promise first, then tells you she's been thinking about ending her life. Three of the services speak to it. Nine eight eight: "Never keep it a secret if a friend tells you about a plan to hurt themselves." Be The One To: "Do not ever promise to keep their thoughts of suicide a secret." R U OK? says not to agree to keep a plan or suicidal thoughts a secret. That's three services, not all five. And on the course's reading, nine eight eight's never covers a promise you've already made. You don't have to keep it.

S2: But how do you say that without her feeling betrayed?

S1: The services don't give a script, so this is the course's wording, built from their advice. First, stay with what she's told you. "Thank you for telling me. That must have taken a lot." Then take the promise back honestly. "I said I'd keep it to myself, and I can't, because I care about you too much to hold this on my own. I'm not going to go behind your back. Can we call someone together, now?" And offer only what you will do.
