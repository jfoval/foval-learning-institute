S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Conversation and Listening, on the lesson called How it ends, and how it went. It's about the goodbye that goes on too long, and the ride home replaying what you said wrong.

S2: Start with the goodbye. Do conversations end when people want them to?

S1: In twenty twenty one, Adam Mastroianni and three colleagues asked exactly that. Their first study was an online survey of eight hundred and six people, each recalling their most recent in-person conversation with one other person, mostly with a partner, friend or family member. About two thirds said there'd been a point when they felt ready for it to end.

S2: And did people wish their conversations had been longer or shorter?

S1: On average, longer, by about twenty four per cent of its length. But that's the mean. The median was zero: the person in the middle wished for no change at all. If one person wanted their conversation half as long again, and another wanted theirs half as short, the average wish is zero, yet each missed by half. So the authors also averaged the size of each gap, ignoring direction. The typical mismatch was about fifty six per cent of the conversation.

S2: That's a survey of memories, though.

S1: So the second study went to a lab at Harvard, where they could ask both people. Three hundred and sixty six people, paired with a stranger, told to talk for as little or as much time as they liked, between one and forty five minutes. Everyone had to stay the full hour anyway, so nobody had a reason to finish early. Fifty seven of the hundred and eighty three pairs talked the full forty five minutes and had to be stopped, and the authors left them out, reporting that it doesn't meaningfully change the results. That left a hundred and twenty six pairs.

S2: And how did those end?

S1: In under two per cent, both people wanted to stop at the same moment, and did. In about twenty nine per cent, it ended when one of the two wanted. In about forty seven per cent, both had wanted it to end sooner. And in about nine and a half per cent, both had wanted it to go on longer.

S2: So nearly half overran for both of them. Could people at least tell when their partner wanted out?

S1: No. Their guesses about when the other had wanted to end were off by nearly two thirds of the conversation on average. They believed their two wishes differed by about twenty nine per cent, when in fact it was about sixty eight. The authors sum it up: "it appears that when two humans talk, one almost always wants to stop talking before the other one does."

S2: Which sounds like everyone's desperate to leave.

S1: It doesn't say that. The wishes ran both ways, and in nearly one lab pair in ten both people wanted to go on. The authors suggest why nobody sorts it out: ending is a coordination problem that, in their abstract's words, "humans are unable to solve because doing so requires information that they normally keep from each other." People keep back when they'd like to stop, because saying so risks offending. But that's their reading. They measured the mismatch and the poor guessing; they didn't test the hiding directly. And it's one group's programme. The course found no replication by an outside team.

S2: So if nobody says "I'd like to stop now", how does anything end?

S1: Emanuel Schegloff and Harvey Sacks described that in nineteen seventy three, from recordings of American English conversations, many of them phone calls. You can't just go quiet, because a silence where a turn is due is heard as someone doing something. In their words, "simply to stop talking is not a solution to the closing problem". So there's a pre-closing, or more exactly a possible pre-closing: at the end of a topic, a turn that's nothing but "Okay" or "Well", with a falling tone. The speaker passes.

S2: Why only possible?

S1: Because it hands the other person a free turn, where they can raise the one more thing without seeming to change the subject. If instead they pass too, "Okay" answered with "Okay", the closing has begun. As the paper puts it, "The pre-closing ceases to be 'pre-' if accepted".

S2: And nobody's been refused.

S1: That's the link to lesson two, where a no comes late and cushioned, and the link is this course's reading. Beside the endings study, again on the course's reading, the system ends things smoothly without carrying the information that would time them well. And no study the course found tested offering one sooner.

S2: Now the ride home. How much did they like me?

S1: In twenty eighteen, Erica Boothby and three colleagues published five studies. Their abstract: "people systematically underestimated how much their conversation partners liked them and enjoyed their company, an illusion we call the liking gap." In the first, thirty six people near Yale talked with a stranger for about five minutes. On a seven-point scale, they rated their liking of the partner at five point eight two, and the partner's liking of them at five point one seven.

S2: Maybe they just liked the other person more.

S1: Not on average, because each person was both a liker and a liked. So the gap is an error in the guesses. Shyness was the one trait of four that changed its size: the shyest third's gap was one point one four points, the least shy third's point two five, which wasn't significant. But each third was ten people.

S2: Were people hiding their liking?

S1: The conversations were filmed, and two assistants who didn't know the hypothesis rated the videos. Their ratings predicted how much people actually liked their partners, but not how much people thought they were liked. So the signals were visible, and people "neglected this information when estimating how much their conversation partners liked them". Fifteen videos, so it's small too. The second study tested one reason: the moments people thought had shaped the partner's view of them were more negative than the ones that had shaped their own view of the partner, and that accounted for part of the gap, not all of it.

S2: So is it the same mistake at both ends?

S1: Each is a guess about what the other person felt, made from inside your own head. For endings, the authors suggest, without testing it, that people keep the wish back. For liking, the videos said people weren't hiding it; they missed it. On this course's reading, only in the endings study is the other person thought to be keeping it back. And direction differs. The liking gap runs one way, low, so discount your gloomy verdict a little, on average. The endings mismatch runs both ways, so it's no reason to stay longer or leave sooner, only not to trust your guess. And neither finding tells you what one person felt.
