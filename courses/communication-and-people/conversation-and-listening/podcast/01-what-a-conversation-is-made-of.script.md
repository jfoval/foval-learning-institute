S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're starting Conversation and Listening, with lesson one. What a conversation is made of.

S2: I didn't know a conversation was made of anything. You just talk.

S1: It feels that way. But ordinary talk runs on a system precise enough to measure in milliseconds, and you already run it without knowing its parts.

S2: Where does that idea come from?

S1: In nineteen seventy four, Harvey Sacks, Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson published a model of how people take turns at talk, built from recordings of American English conversation, transcribed and analysed by hand. It measured nothing. It listed the facts any account of conversation has to explain. Two are quoted most. "Overwhelmingly, one party talks at a time." And "Occurrences of more than one speaker at a time are common, but brief."

S2: So something coordinates people very closely, with nobody in charge.

S1: Their answer starts with the unit a turn is built from: a sentence, a clause, a phrase, even one word. As it unfolds, a listener can hear roughly where it will be complete. That point is called a transition-relevance place, where the turn could change hands. It doesn't have to.

S2: So what decides whether it does?

S1: Three rules, in order. Rule one A: if the current speaker has picked out who goes next, say by addressing a question to them, that person has the right, and the obligation, to speak next. Rule one B: if nobody has been picked out, anyone may start, and the first to start gets the turn. Rule one C: if nobody else starts, the current speaker may carry on, though they need not.

S2: You promised milliseconds. How fast does an answer actually come?

S1: The first large measured test across unrelated languages came in two thousand and nine. Tanya Stivers and ten colleagues compared video recordings of informal conversation in ten languages from five continents, three hundred and fifty questions per language, and to compare like with like they kept to yes or no questions and their answers.

S2: I'd guess half a second.

S1: Much shorter. Across all ten, the most common gap was zero, the middle value a hundred milliseconds, and the average two hundred and eight milliseconds. About a fifth of a second. Every language's average fell within about two hundred and fifty milliseconds of that, either side, which the authors put at about the time it takes to say one English syllable.

S2: So the gap is two hundred milliseconds.

S1: That's shorthand for the centre of a wide spread, which included long gaps, so it matters which figure someone quotes. And it's one study, of one kind of turn in one kind of setting. Courts, church services and news interviews, the authors noted, run on different rules.

S2: Has anyone found it again?

S1: The scale has turned up in other collections of recorded talk. Stephen Levinson, one of the ten-language study's own authors, reviews several, including that study and an analysis of English phone calls he did with Francisco Torreira, and puts the most common gap "around two hundred milliseconds". The ten-language study's own most common gap was zero, so what agrees is the scale, a fifth of a second or less, not one number. When the course calls the timing replicated, that's all it means: the same scale in different collections of informal talk, brought together by a reviewer who worked on two of them.

S2: But a fifth of a second isn't long enough to think of anything.

S1: That's the puzzle. Lab studies where people name or describe pictures as fast as they can put the preparation for a single word at about six hundred milliseconds under the most favourable lab conditions, about a thousand otherwise, and about fifteen hundred for a short clause. Those come from lab picture tasks, not conversation, and the gaps come from recorded talk. Levinson's argument joins the two.

S2: Walk me through it.

S1: Say an answer starts two hundred milliseconds after the question ends, and it's one word that needs at least six hundred milliseconds of preparation. Count back: two hundred after the end, minus six hundred, puts the start of preparation at least four hundred milliseconds before the question ended, while the other person was still talking. For a short clause, the same sum moves it back to about thirteen hundred milliseconds before the end. That's the course's arithmetic on Levinson's figures, not a measurement of any one conversation.

S2: So you're building your reply while I'm still mid-sentence.

S1: On Levinson's account, yes. In his words, "one must plan while still listening and predicting what the rest of the incoming turn will contain". It isn't the only account. Other researchers argue the timing alone can't show whether listeners predict the end of a turn or simply react to it as it ends. The course hasn't read their paper, so it reports that disagreement only as Levinson and Torreira describe it.

S2: Then what about the old complaint, that bad listeners are just waiting for their turn to talk?

S1: On Levinson's argument, everyone plans a reply while listening. In the recordings, there isn't time to do it any other way. So, on this course's reading, planning during the other person's turn is not the failure. What can go wrong is what the planning is aimed at: what they're actually saying, or the point you were waiting to make whatever they said. Lesson four takes that apart.

S2: And overlap, common but brief. Is it?

S1: Both halves turned out to be true. In twenty fifteen Levinson and Torreira analysed three hundred and forty eight calls from Switchboard, recorded in the United States in nineteen ninety and nineteen ninety one, where an automated operator paired each caller with another person, suggested a topic, and never put the same two people together twice. About thirty eight hours. As a share of time, seventy seven percent was one person speaking, nineteen point two percent silence, and only three point eight percent two people speaking at once.

S2: So overlap is rare.

S1: As a share of time. But of all the changes of speaker, thirty point one percent happened in overlap, with the next speaker starting before the last had quite finished.

S2: About three in ten handovers, yet all the overlapping talk together came to under four percent of the time?

S1: Because the overlaps were short. The most common length was ninety six milliseconds, and the middle value two hundred and five milliseconds. So any claim about how much overlap has to say which of the two measures it means. And in a sample of two hundred overlaps from the same calls, seventy three percent involved a backchannel or a brief token of agreement. Mm hm, uh huh, yeah, right. The small sounds a listener makes that don't claim a turn.
