S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Conversation and Listening, on the lesson called Let them finish: interruption, overlap and silence. It opens in a clinic. In one set of recorded American consultations, when doctors asked for the patient's concerns at all, they cut in two times out of three, after a median of eleven seconds.

S2: Eleven seconds. So the lesson is, don't interrupt.

S1: It's harder to pin down than that, because much of the overlapping talk in any conversation isn't interruption at all. In the phone calls from lesson one, seventy three percent of the overlaps involved a backchannel such as "mm hm".

S2: So what counts as the harmless kind?

S1: Emanuel Schegloff sorted out the kinds speakers in his recordings don't usually treat as a problem. Continuers, like "mm hm", which don't claim the turn. Terminal overlaps, a syllable or two early. Help with a word someone is searching for, where the turn stays theirs. And choral talk, like laughing together. What's left, this course calls an intrusive interruption: starting well inside someone's sentence, far from any place the turn could end, and taking the floor.

S2: And even then, it depends who's talking?

S1: That's Deborah Tannen. She studied about two and a half hours of taped dinner-table conversation in which she herself took part. Some speakers often started while someone else was talking. She called that a high-involvement style, where the priority is showing interest. Others, high-considerateness speakers, put the priority on not imposing. Between people who shared the first style, overlapping was, in her words, "supportive rather than obstructive, evidence not of domination but of participation". When it met the other style, those speakers "hesitated, faltered, or stopped, feeling interrupted and, more to the point, dominated".

S2: That sounds a bit convenient for the people doing the talking over.

S1: Tannen is just as clear about that. The impression of dominance wasn't what the high-involvement speakers intended, she writes: "Neither, however, was it the creation of the imaginations of those who felt interrupted." And this is a close analysis of a handful of speakers, not a measured rate, with the analyst at the table. It's best used as a lens on misunderstanding, not a map of which groups talk how.

S2: Give me the case.

S1: Tobiah and Mireille share an office. When Mireille explains something, Tobiah comes in with "Yes, and that means..." before she's finished. Mireille stops each time. He reads her stopping as having finished; she reads his overlaps as not letting her finish. On Tannen's account each is reading the other through their own habit, and Mireille's side isn't imagined. She really doesn't get to finish.

S2: Do men interrupt women more?

S1: It's contested, and serious researchers disagree about the answer and about whether the question is well posed. It started in nineteen seventy five, with Don Zimmerman and Candace West. In twenty same-sex conversations they found seven interruptions. In eleven between a man and a woman they found forty eight, and their table gives forty six of them to the men.

S2: Couldn't one odd pair explain that?

S1: One conversation held thirteen of the forty eight. The authors raised that themselves: interruptions turned up in ten of the eleven cross-sex conversations, against three of the twenty same-sex ones, so they read the pattern as systematic. Their preliminary findings, they wrote, indicate "that there are definite and patterned ways in which the power and dominance enjoyed by men in other contexts are exercised in their conversational interaction with women." That's the dominance view. And they marked its limit: they were not claiming conversations between men and women always show it.

S2: What happened when people pooled the studies?

S1: In nineteen ninety eight Kristin Anderson and Campbell Leaper combined forty three published studies of adults, mostly middle-class European American samples. Across all of them men were significantly more likely to interrupt, but the size of the difference was, in the authors' word, "negligible": d equals point one five. For the seventeen studies of intrusive interruptions, the ones aimed at taking the floor, it was point three three, which they called "small but substantial". On this course's arithmetic, not a figure from the study, point three three puts the average man's rate above about sixty three percent of women's, where no difference would be fifty. It says nothing about any particular man or woman.

S2: And did it change with the situation?

S1: For intrusive interruptions, a lot. In unstructured talk the difference was point seven three; in task-focused activities, point zero five, virtually none. Whether the talk was mixed or same-gender made no reliable difference. And in studies with a woman as first author the difference was point five four; with a man, minus point two one. Activity and author gender were both significant moderators. The authors say that gap "potentially reflects some form of researcher bias", and they read it both ways: some women researchers may be inclined to find men more dominant, some men researchers inclined not to. They don't say which group was closer to the truth, and nor does this course.

S2: So where does that leave it?

S1: With three positions. The dominance view: interruption is one way power shows up in conversation, the asymmetry in the first recordings was large, and men did interrupt more on average, most of all in intrusive interruptions, in open talk, in groups and in the few natural settings studied. The contextual view, Anderson and Leaper's: the difference is small and depends on definition, setting, even who ran the study, so situations shape it more than fixed differences do. And the style view, Tannen's: counting overlap can't measure dominance, because whether it's supportive depends on styles and intentions, so the question is badly posed.

S2: The dominance side must have an answer to that.

S1: It does, and Tannen reports it herself, from Henley and Kramarae: that the style account is "covering up real domination with a cloth of cultural difference". Her reply: "Though I am sympathetic to this view, my conscience tells me we cannot have it both ways." Counting overlap as dominance would brand high-involvement speakers, many women among them, as pushy. The dominance side can answer in turn, on this course's reading, that the intrusive category already sets friendly overlap aside, and that's where the difference was concentrated.

S2: And Tannen herself?

S1: She opens her conclusion "As a woman who has personally experienced the difficulty many women report in getting heard in some interactions with men", says that's why those studies tempt her, and then, as a high-involvement speaker, objects to treating her own style as a fault. That experience of not getting heard is real whichever position is right, and an average over forty three mostly short studies can't say what happened in any one woman's meetings.

S2: So both slogans are wrong. "Men interrupt women far more", and "The gender difference is a myth".

S1: The first overstates a small average that wasn't reliably bigger in mixed talk. The second misses that men did interrupt more on average, and in some settings not by a little. Both skip the part the evidence is about, which is when and where. It's not a verdict on the dispute, which is as much about what the difference means as about its size.

S2: Back to the clinic, then. What if doctors just waited?

S1: In a Basel clinic, doctors were trained to wait. Of three hundred and thirty five patients, seventy eight percent had finished within two minutes, and seven went past five, all of them, the doctors judged, giving important information. It's one clinic, with no interrupted group to compare, and the numbers don't transfer to a friend. What might, on this course's reading, is that the fear of someone going on forever didn't come true there.
