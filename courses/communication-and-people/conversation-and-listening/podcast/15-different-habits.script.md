S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Conversation and Listening, on the lesson called Different habits: culture, autism, age and hearing.

S2: Start with the obvious one. A good listener looks at you. True?

S1: It depends who's listening. Federico Rossano, Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson took three hundred question-and-answer sequences in each of three communities with no historical connection: northern Italians, Tzeltal speakers in Mexico, and Yélî Dnye speakers on Rossel Island in Papua New Guinea. For each question they coded whether the person asking, and the person being asked, looked at the other at least once while it was asked.

S2: And I'd guess the listener looks more.

S1: So did most earlier accounts. In all three communities the person asking looked more, and the authors call that a surprise. The people being asked also differed far more than the people asking. Tzeltal recipients looked in forty two point three per cent of questions, against sixty seven point three per cent for Yélî Dnye recipients.

S2: So the Tzeltal listeners weren't paying attention?

S1: They showed it another way. A Tzeltal listener to a story is expected to respond at regular intervals by repeating part of what was just said, and the normal way to answer yes to a yes-or-no question is to repeat its words. And the same people looked differently depending on their role. In the authors' words, "Tzeltal participants are similar to the Italian when they speak but not when they listen to questions."

S2: That's one study, though.

S1: One study, three communities, questions only. It describes those recordings, not where any individual looks. A second study, by Hironori Akechi and colleagues, published in twenty thirteen, asked how a look feels. Twenty Finnish and twenty Japanese students each sat facing a woman from their own country behind an electronic shutter that turned clear to show her looking straight at them, looking away, or with her eyes closed. Nobody talked.

S2: What changed?

S1: Not their hearts. Both groups' hearts slowed in the same way to a direct gaze. The ratings differed. A direct gaze made the Japanese students rate her angrier, and made no difference to the Finns' anger ratings. Approachability went the other way round: the Finns rated direct eye contact as more approachable, and gaze made no difference to the Japanese students' approachability ratings. The anger difference was only just statistically significant. The authors put it down to "display rules and cultural norms", and they say themselves that they studied only one Western and one East Asian country.

S2: So should I learn the rules country by country?

S1: That's the tempting move. In Geert Hofstede's summary of him, the anthropologist Edward Hall divided cultures "into high-context (much of the information is implicit) and low-context cultures (nearly everything is explicit)". Hofstede's own model gives each country a score on dimensions such as individualism against collectivism. The course hasn't read either man's books, but it read a short twenty eleven account by Hofstede. He stands by the scores, citing more than four hundred significant correlations between them and other studies' results. But culture, he writes, is "always a collective phenomenon", and "Within each collective there is a variety of individuals." His conclusion: "national culture scores should not be used for stereotyping individuals."

S2: So culture doesn't matter after all?

S1: That's wrong too. The gaze studies show groups do differ. The claim is narrower: a label is a weak guide to the person in front of you. How far cultural variation goes is contested.

S2: What about autistic people and eye contact?

S1: Dominic Trevisan and colleagues analysed first-hand descriptions from three hundred and sixty four people, drawn from YouTube videos and a forum for autistic people, with the diagnoses self-declared. One forum post put it this way: "I can't concentrate while making eye contact, particularly if I need to listen to what the other person is saying to me." For some of them, looking away is how they manage to listen. So calling eye contact a poor test of listening is the course's reading, drawn from three sources: the Tzeltal listeners, the Japanese students' ratings, and these accounts. It isn't a tested claim.

S2: There's a bigger argument behind that, isn't there?

S1: Yes, and it's contested. The clinical definition of autism describes difficulties in social communication as part of the condition. A twenty twenty five paper states it this way: "Autism is clinically defined by social communication deficits, suggesting that autistic people may be less effective at sharing information, particularly with one another." That's the deficit reading. The course hasn't read a primary statement of it, only descriptions like that one. In twenty twelve Damian Milton argued instead that a breakdown in understanding between an autistic and a non-autistic person is "a 'double problem' as both people experience it, and so it is not a singular problem located in any one person". That's the double empathy reading.

S2: Can you test that?

S1: Catherine Crompton and colleagues did, with chains of people passing a story along. In twenty twenty, with seventy two adults, mixed chains passed on fewer details than all-autistic or all-non-autistic chains, and the authors said replications were warranted. A registered replication in twenty twenty five, with three hundred and eleven adults at three sites, found "no difference in information transfer between single-neurotype and mixed-neurotype chains". Rapport still differed.

S2: So which side wins?

S1: Both take something. Autistic people passed information to each other as well as non-autistic people did, in both studies, which is hard to square with a strong deficit reading in this task. But the mixed-chain drop, the result that most directly supported double empathy, didn't replicate. The authors suggest the experiment may have missed difficulties in real-world interactions, though that's their explanation after the fact, not something they tested.

S2: And since then?

S1: Published argument, which the course read only as abstracts. Lucy Livingston and two colleagues argue that the double empathy problem, which they shorten to DEP, is poorly conceptualised and call for a stronger chain from theory to prediction. They add: "Until then, we caution against the translation of DEP research into applied settings." Caroline Bollen and Janna van Grunsven replied that the critique's "warning note is problematic both from an ethical and philosophy of science point of view". So the question is open. But the practical advice is the same under both readings: don't read eye contact as a test of listening, attend to what's said, and ask what works.

S2: Hearing loss, then. When someone doesn't catch what I said, I talk louder and slower.

S1: RNID, the UK charity for people who are deaf or have hearing loss, asks people to "avoid shouting, speaking too fast or unnecessarily slow". Its page begins, "Please face me when you talk to me. Lots of people rely on lipreading to some degree to understand." And it says, "Don't be afraid to repeat or rephrase and try something different." Two more lines carry the principle: "Not all deaf people and people with hearing loss are the same." And, "Ask someone how they want to be communicated with". That's practitioner advice, from its community's experience.

S2: There's a thread running through all of this.

S1: RNID, and each of the other organisations the course read, comes back to the same move, and putting them together is the course's reading: adjust to the person, and ask them how. Ask about the conversation, not the difference. Ask once, early, act on the answer, and accept "I'm fine" as an answer too.
