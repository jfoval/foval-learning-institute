S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Conversation and Listening, on the lesson called How listening goes wrong. And it opens with a story the radio host Celeste Headlee tells against herself. A good friend's father had died, and Headlee found her sitting alone outside their workplace, distraught. Not knowing what to say, Headlee talked about her own father, who drowned when she was nine months old, and how she'd always mourned him.

S2: She meant it as comfort.

S1: You're not alone, I understand. And her friend snapped: "Okay, Celeste, you win." She'd had her father for thirty years, the friend said. Headlee had it worse. She supposed she shouldn't be so upset. Then she walked away. Nothing in that reply was unkind, and nothing was untrue. It still failed.

S2: So what's the name for what went wrong there?

S1: Textbooks have names for the ways listening goes wrong. The lesson uses six bad listening practices from the University of Minnesota's open textbook. Pseudo-listening is behaving as if you're paying attention when you're not. Aggressive listening is paying attention in order to attack something the speaker says. Narcissistic listening is trying to make the interaction about you, with the pivot back to yourself, or one-upping. But they're a teaching list, not findings. Nobody counted how often each happens. And the textbook itself says occasionally matching stories doesn't make you a narcissistic listener. Each name describes something done in a moment, not a kind of person.

S2: The same textbook lists rehearsing your reply while someone's still talking. But I do that all the time.

S1: Everybody does. Lesson one gave Levinson's argument that replies typically begin within a fraction of a second of a turn ending, far less time than it takes to plan even a word. So everybody plans while the other person talks. And the textbook's own words agree more than you'd think: rehearsal is an important part of listening, and it becomes a problem when it starts before you've had time to understand. This course's reading, putting the two together, is that the question is what the planning is aimed at. Planning a question about what they just said is listening. Planning your counter-argument from their first sentence, or your own better story, is the failure.

S2: Which brings us back to Headlee's better story.

S1: She uses the sociologist Charles Derber's name for it: conversational narcissism. This course hasn't read Derber's book; it knows him through secondary accounts, chiefly Headlee's. She describes two kinds of reply. A shift response turns attention back to yourself. A support response supports the other person's comment. Here's the lesson's case. Ifeoma tells a colleague her landlord's putting the rent up by a hundred and forty a month from March. Reply one: "Tell me about it. Mine went up in the summer and I'm still furious."

S2: Shift. It's the colleague's rent now.

S1: Reply two: "That's a big jump. Did he give a reason?"

S2: Support. It stays on her.

S1: And reply four: "Mine went up last year too, and I ended up moving. Are you thinking of staying?" That starts as a shift and hands the floor back with a question. Whether it works depends on things the words don't show: how upset she is, and whether she wanted company in the problem or room to talk.

S2: So why is the shift so tempting?

S1: One set of studies gives part of an answer. Tamir and Mitchell let people answer questions about themselves, about someone else, or about facts, each paying between one and four cents. In their second study, thirty seven people made a hundred and ninety five such choices, and on average gave up seventeen percent of what they could have earned to answer about themselves. In brain-imaging studies, disclosing your own opinions and traits activated the brain's reward system more than judging someone else.

S2: So people are selfish.

S1: That's exactly what it doesn't show. Nothing in the studies measured selfishness. It's one lab's programme, and the course found no independent replication. Rewarding here means brain activation plus willingness to give up a little money. And nobody in those studies was listening to a conversation partner. You may see a figure attached to this paper, that people spend thirty to forty percent of their speech on themselves. That isn't Tamir and Mitchell's measurement. And Headlee's excerpt calls humans hardwired to talk about themselves, which is more than these studies show. This course's reading is that the same pull helps explain why a shift comes easily, and why support takes some effort against it.

S2: What about the listener who's just not there? On their phone?

S1: Itzchakov and Kluger describe a study in the Harvard Business Review. They paired one hundred and twelve undergraduates as speaker and listener, the speaker talking for ten minutes about a proposal. Half the listeners, at random, were sent text messages and told to answer them briefly, so the speakers saw the distraction. Speakers with undistracted listeners felt less anxious, more self-aware, and clearer about their own attitudes.

S2: So the cost lands on the person talking.

S1: With limits. The distraction was visible, and the outcomes are what speakers reported about themselves. It's one group's programme, described as the magazine describes it; the course hasn't read the journal paper. A twenty twenty six review adds that in other studies speakers often didn't notice the distraction and were affected anyway. Treat that as a lead, not a finding.

S2: Back to the matching story, then. Is it ever right?

S1: That's contested, with a value in it: what you owe someone in distress. Headlee's rule in her TED talk is don't equate your experience with theirs, though in her book she allows it could be fine if the focus shifts back. Samaritans, the UK and Ireland listening charity, advise, for supporting someone who's struggling, not trying to identify what they're going through with your own experiences. That's practitioner advice from people whose work is listening to someone in distress. On the other side is the view that swapping stories is part of how people get close. Huang and colleagues report earlier research, which this course didn't read, in which more reciprocal self-disclosure went with more liking. They say people don't want to talk about themselves indefinitely without reciprocity, and suggest you may ask too many questions without revealing enough about yourself.

S2: And neither side's been tested head to head?

S1: Not in what this course read. This course's reading is that it probably turns on distress. Friends swapping worst-holiday stories, each one topping the last, are trading, and nothing's gone wrong. Someone telling you his mother's just been diagnosed with a serious illness is a different case, and the same move takes the floor from someone who needed it. What would settle it is studies coding these replies in real conversations and measuring how understood the first speaker felt, separately for distress and for sociable talk.
