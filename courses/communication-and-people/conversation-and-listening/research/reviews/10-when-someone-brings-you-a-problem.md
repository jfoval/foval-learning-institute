### Findings

**R1 [major] Bolger and Amarel's three experiments are described as one tip experiment, which
attaches the no-support comparison to the wrong support and hides a reassurance result this lesson
needs.** Lines 425 to 428: "Each participant was told she would have to give a speech, given a few
minutes to practise, and then heard a fellow participant, secretly a confederate, pass on a common
public-speaking tip. ... Invisible support reduced distress compared with visible support and with
no support." Evidence (full text): Study 1 was the tip, visible against invisible, with no control
group. **Study 2 was emotional support, and it was reassurance**: "Look, you've got nothing to worry
about, you'll do fine" said to the participant (visible), against "[Participant] is going to do
fine, she's got nothing to worry about" said to the experimenter (invisible), against no support
(Table 2). Study 2 is the only one where the no-support comparison in the lesson's sentence comes
from. Study 3 went back to the tip and separated the parts of the message. So (a) the sentence
misdescribes the design, and (b) the lesson omits the fact a well-informed reader would raise
against its "It'll be fine" section, its misconception "Reassurance is supportive" and its quiz item
3 explanation ("reassurance without substance"): in the one experiment in this lesson that tested
reassurance, reassurance that did not come addressed as help reduced distress compared with none,
and the same words said straight to her did not help. Also line 424: "the first drew on New York
University undergraduates"; all three studies recruited NYU Introduction to Psychology students.
SOURCES Part C §6.4 carries the same conflation ("a confederate peer gave a public-speaking tip ...
Invisible support reduced distress relative to visible and no support") and needs the same fix.
Fix, lines 423 to 429: "ran three experiments, 257 participants in all, all women students at New
York University, and restricted them 'to female participants and female peer confederates'. Each
participant was told she would have to give a speech and given a few minutes to practise; then a
fellow participant, secretly a confederate, said something helpful. In the first and third
experiments it was a common public-speaking tip; in the second it was reassurance: that she had
nothing to worry about and would do fine. In the visible condition the words were addressed to her.
In the invisible one they were addressed to the experimenter, in her hearing. Invisible support
reduced distress compared with visible support, and in the second experiment, the only one with a
no-support group, compared with no support as well. Visible support 'was either ineffective or it
exacerbated reactivity.'[9]" Then add one sentence to "It'll be fine" (after line 250): "One
experiment later in this lesson complicates this: reassurance helped women students waiting to give
a speech when it was said about them rather than to them, and did not help when it was said to
their face. What went wrong there was the message around it, not the reassurance itself.[9]" Adjust
the misconception at 522 to match ("... and in Bolger and Amarel's second experiment the same
reassurance helped only when it didn't come addressed as help") and quiz 3's explanation for the
"certain to pass" distractor ("reassurance said straight to her is the visible kind, which in the
reassurance experiment didn't help").

**R2 [major, neutrality] "Where the two sides meet" and the misconception "Never give advice" give
two sources' qualifiers as the whole listening tradition's wording.** Lines 342 to 346: "Look at the
qualifiers in the hold-back tradition's own texts: 'prematurely or when unsolicited', 'refrain from

lesson have none: Samaritans' page says "This could mean not offering advice, not trying to identify
what they're going through with your own experiences and not trying to solve their problems" and

always seen as efforts to change a person". Samaritans' setting (someone who is struggling) is also
the closest of the four to this lesson's subject. So the misconception rebuts a position with a
quotation from one manual and puts a "never" in the listening side's mouth that its most relevant

**R3 [major, neutrality: Turing test, defect 9] The hold-back side is given its weakest line, and
the reason in the same sentence is dropped.** Line 260: Rogers and Farson "wrote that 'advice is
seldom taken, and information hardly ever utilized', as an assertion rather than a finding." The
excerpt's sentence before it, which carries their case: "advice and information are almost always
seen as efforts to change a person and thus serve as barriers to his self-expression and the
development of a creative relationship. Moreover, advice is seldom taken" (the 1987 excerpt, p. 1 to
2; the PDF's text layer prints "selfexpression"). The lesson quotes the "Moreover" clause, labels it
an unsupported assertion, and so presents the tradition as making an empirical claim that the
advice research then answers. A Rogerian, a Samaritans volunteer or an MI trainer would not
recognise that as their case: theirs is that the person's own view and own solution come first,
which is partly a value (MINT: MI "honors client autonomy"). OUTLINE's neutrality line asks for the
hold-back tradition at full strength and the neutrality notes ask for "the value element named as
value". Fix, line 260: "Rogers and Farson, in their 1957 booklet for managers, gave the reason:
advice is 'almost always seen as efforts to change a person and thus serve[s] as barriers to his
self-expression and the development of a creative relationship.' They added that 'advice is seldom
taken', as an assertion rather than a finding.[10]" (check the bracket against the source's
grammar, or quote only "almost always seen as efforts to change a person"), and at the end of the
section (line 270): "Part of their case is about evidence, and part is a value: that the person
with the problem should find their own way through it, and that a listener's job is to make room for
that. No study settles the second part." Record the new string in SOURCES as a gate closure before
use.

**R4 [minor] Bolger 2000: a trend is printed as a result.** Lines 417 to 419: "When the partner
