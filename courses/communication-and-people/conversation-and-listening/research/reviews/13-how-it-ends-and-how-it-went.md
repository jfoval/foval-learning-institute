**Findings:**

**R1 [major] The last section's organising claim runs against Boothby's own Study 1b, and objective
4 presupposes it.** Lines 481 to 486: "What they share is that each is a guess about something the
other person felt and didn't say. Both papers point to conventions of politeness for why it goes
unsaid ... So on **this course's reading**, they are the same kind of misjudgement: a person judging
what's in someone else's head from what's in their own, about something the other has good social
reasons not to say." Line 14 (objective 4): "Explain why the endings finding and the liking gap are
the same kind of misjudgement".
Evidence: Boothby et al. set up Study 1b to decide between a "no-signal account" (partners don't
show their liking) and a "neglected-signal account", and report that "Study 1b supported the
neglected-signal account, which suggests that although people do signal that they like one another
during conversations, people neglect these signals". The lesson teaches that result at line 400 ("So
the signals of liking were there on the video"). The "conspiracies of politeness" sentence is the
first of three general reasons in the paper's introduction for why partners' views are hard to know,
cited to other research; it is not the authors' account of the liking gap, which is the tested
self-critical-thoughts mediator. So for the liking gap the evidence points to feelings that were
shown and missed, while for endings the authors suggest (untested) that the wish was hidden. The
reading is labelled, but it is built on a premise one of its two papers argues against, and the
objective asks the reader to explain it as fact.
Fix: objective 4 to "Explain in what way the endings finding and the liking gap are alike, and how
they differ, and what they do and do not license". Lines 481 to 486 to something like: "What they
share is that each is a guess about what the other person felt, made from inside your own head.
They differ in why the guess goes wrong. For endings, the authors suggest, without testing it, that
people hide the wish to leave, out of politeness or kindness. For liking, Boothby's group tested and
rejected the idea that people hide it: the coders could see the liking on the video, and people
missed it, busy with their own harsher thoughts about how they'd done. Their introduction does name
the 'conspiracies of politeness' that make any partner hard to read, and Gus Cooney is an author of
both papers. So on **this course's reading**, both are a person judging what's in someone else's
head from what's in their own; only in the endings study is the other person thought to be keeping
it back." Quiz and podcast script unaffected, but check the script when it is written.

**R3 [major] Study 1's mean "wished it longer" is taught as what the average person wanted, and the
median, printed beside it, is left out.** Line 147: "on average, people wished their conversation
had been *longer*, by 24.08% of its length ... If you guessed 'shorter', keep reading"; line 226: "In
Study 1 the average person wanted *more*"; lines 504 to 505, the misconception answer: "In Study 1 the
average person wanted their conversation to be *longer*"; quiz 5 explanation (line 94 to 95): "a
small average signed difference".
Evidence: the paper, same sentence: "participants wished their conversations had been 24.08% longer
than they were (median = 0.00%)", and Table 1 row 4 median 0.00 min. Two thirds (66.51%) said there
was a point at which they felt ready to end. The mean is also pulled upward by the measure itself,
which is the reviewer's arithmetic and not the paper's: a wish for a shorter conversation can be at
most 100% of its length, while a wish for a longer one has no ceiling, so a few large "longer" wishes
outweigh many "shorter" ones. The typical (median) person wanted exactly what they got on the signed
measure, and a reader who guessed "shorter", on the two thirds who felt ready to end, was not wrong
in the way the predict implies. The authors call the 24.08% "substantial", not small.
Fix: line 147: "And the *mean* wish was for a longer conversation, by 24.08% of its length, though
the median was zero: half wanted it at least as long as it was, half at most.[1] Two thirds had felt
ready to end at some point, so if you guessed 'shorter' you were half right. The next paragraph shows
why neither average tells you much." Line 226: "In Study 1 the mean wish was for more". Misconception
answer: "The wishes ran both ways: some people wanted less, some more, and in about one lab pair in
ten both people wanted to go on.[1] The finding is a mismatch, not a universal wish to escape." Quiz
5 explanation: "a signed average of 24% sat beside an average absolute gap of 56%". SOURCES Part B §6
"May not say" carries the same "average person" wording; add a Stage 4 note there.
