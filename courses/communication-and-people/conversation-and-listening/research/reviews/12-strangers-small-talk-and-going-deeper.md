**R2 [major] Kardas et al.'s supermarket caveat is quoted without the counter-argument in the same
paragraph (defect 9; fails the Turing test for the depth researchers).** Lines 290 to 294: "a question
about the last time someone cried 'might be received much differently when you raise the question to
the person standing next to you in the supermarket line than it was in our experiments.' In daily life,
they add, a listener might read a deep question as romantic interest, or as an attempt to manipulate."
Evidence: the next sentences of the same paragraph (Kardas et al. 2022, General Discussion, p. 391):
"The deeper question one might consider asking in the supermarket line might still yield a better
conversation than expected. In fact, we think people might be even more likely to underestimate the
positive outcomes of deep conversations in naturalistic settings outside the lab", with the reasoning
that their procedure "may provide a more conservative test", and "In daily life, listeners may be even
more concerned about intimate details that another person freely shares with them. Nevertheless, it
could also be that listeners form more negative inferences ...". The authors argue both directions and
say the gap "might vary across contexts"; the lesson prints only the direction that weakens their case.
Fix (after "or as an attempt to manipulate."): "But they argue the other way too: outside the lab the
gap between expectation and experience could be even larger, because their participants already knew
the other person would talk, which they call a more conservative test. They leave it open which way
daily life would go. So would we." Quote only after recording the sentence in SOURCES (R1's section).

**R3 [major] "Roughly four times larger" is the answer to a predict block, and the paper's own figures
put it nearer three.** Line 252 (and the predict question at 248). Evidence: Kardas et al. 2022,
Experiment 2 results: expected awkwardness differed between conditions by a mean difference with 95% CI
[3.17, 4.48] (d = 2.49); experienced awkwardness by CI [.84, 1.87] (d = 1.12). Midpoints 3.83 against
1.36, a ratio of 2.8; in d units 2.2. The quotation is verbatim and attributed, so this is not a
misquotation, but the lesson teaches the ratio as the finding. Fix: keep the quotation and add, in the

awkwardness scale and the felt one about 1.4, so nearer three times than four; either way, the expected
difference was much the larger." (Record the two CIs in SOURCES with R1.)

**R4 [major] The small-talk position is argued weakly, mostly from the depth side's papers, and its
strongest read material is missing (defect 14; fails the Turing test for a small-talk defender).**
Lines 479 to 485 give small talk two points: "not harmful" (a null) and "how depth is reached"
(instrumental), with three of four citations from depth-side papers. A defender would say small talk
is worth having in itself, and the course has read evidence for that:
- Kardas et al.'s own General Discussion: "participants across our experiments also tended to report
  positive experiences after relatively shallow conversations, and tended to report more positive (and
  slightly less negative) experiences in these conversations than they anticipated as well", and the
  shallow condition's overestimate of awkwardness (d = .55, already in the lesson at line 250).
- The commuter experiments themselves are evidence that ordinary conversation with a stranger, opened
  with the weather, beat expectations; the lesson files them under undersociality and never lets the
  small-talk side claim them.
- Milek et al.'s limitations: they "did not account for ... with whom participants were having small
  talk or substantive conversations (e.g., a stranger vs. a friend)" and cite weak-tie research (Sandstrom
  and Dunn 2014, as Milek describe it) as a reason small talk may matter in some contexts. This is also a
  scope limit the lesson needs: the replication does not speak to small talk with strangers in
  particular.
Fix: rewrite the small-talk paragraph to open with its own best case: "Small talk is worth having.
Across Kardas and colleagues' experiments, shallow conversations with strangers also went better than
people expected, and the commuters' conversations, most of which opened with the weather or 'Where are
you from?', were the pleasant ones.[1][3] In ordinary life, the pooled replication found no link between
small talk and life satisfaction over 429 people, though its authors note they could not tell whether
the small talk was with a stranger or a friend, and point to research on brief exchanges with
acquaintances as a reason it may matter more in some settings.[8]" Then the gateway point and the
labelled course's reading as now. Name, in one clause, that the course has read no primary statement of
the small-talk position beyond these (Sandstrom and Dunn is unread), per decision 12.

**R5 [major] "Privacy is a preference, not a miscalibration" closes, in a heading, the empirical
question the depth authors raise (fails the Turing test for both the privacy reader and the depth
researchers).** Line 525 and 527 to 531. Evidence: Kardas et al. predict that "Cultures could vary in
the relative frequency of deep versus shallow conversations because they vary in their social
expectations rather than in their social experiences", which, if true, makes some cultural reticence a
miscalibration in their sense. The lesson reports the prediction (and says it is untested), but its
heading asserts the opposite in the course's voice. Whether a person should be left alone is a value
question the course can take a view on; whether their expectations are calibrated is empirical and
open. Also line 527 to 528 ("Some come from families or places where you don't ask a near-stranger
about their life, and asking would be rude") is an unsourced claim in the course's voice, and line 529
calls the counter-campaign "a joke with a point", a characterisation the paper doesn't make.
Fix: heading "Privacy: a preference to respect, and an open question". Body: keep the first sentence;
then "For some people, and in some families and places, asking a near-stranger about their life is
rude, and that is a norm, not an error. The London paper's authors report a rival campaign whose
badges read 'Do not even think about talking to me'.[2] Kardas and colleagues predict that cultures
differ more in what people expect from deep talk than in how it goes, which would make some reticence a
misjudgement in their sense; that is their prediction, and nobody here tested it.[3] Whether it's
right or not, a stated preference for quiet is the other person's to hold, which is this course's
reading and a value, not a finding." Keep "None of the studies here measured ...".

**R8 [major] Replication and outside evidence as it stands: three items the lesson's framing leaves
out.** Found today through OpenAlex (abstracts only; none is in SOURCES, so none may be cited until
recorded):
- **Sun, Harris and Vazire 2020**, "Is well-being associated with the quantity and quality of social
  interactions?", JPSP (doi 10.1037/pspp0000272): N = 256, EAR plus experience sampling for a week;
  conversational depth and self-disclosure "generally associated with greater well-being", with "larger
  and more consistent" effects "within-person (vs. between-person)". That is close to the within-person
  design Milek named as what would settle it (lesson lines 487 to 489; quiz item 5's explanation, "Both
  sides' own authors name what's missing"). Vazire is an author, so it is not independent of Mehl and
  Milek.
- **Sprecher 2025**, "Barriers to interaction with new weak ties", *Personal Relationships* (doi

  expectation gap (not of the commuter or deep-talk designs). Note this is not "Sprecher 2025 liking

- **Ascigil, Günaydın and Selçuk 2023** (SPPS; Turkey, nationally representative, N = 3,266, with a
  60,141 English-speaking convenience replication) and **Ishiguro 2023** (Japan, nationally
  representative, 957 analysed): brief interactions with strangers and weak ties, including greeting and

  directly relevant to the small-talk defender (R4) and the reserved-culture reader.

experiments so far are from the US and UK"), but the picture a reader takes away (one network, no
outside confirmation, nothing within-person, nothing beyond the US and UK) is less complete than the
evidence. Fix: the orchestrator decides whether to read and record these (abstract level is enough for
one sentence each). At minimum, reword quiz item 5's explanation to "what both sides' authors called for"
rather than "what's missing", and, if Sun et al. is recorded, add after line 489: "A later study from
Vazire's group did compare moments within the same people, and found deeper conversation went with
feeling better, more strongly within people than between them; it is the same group, and it measured
depth rather than small talk."
