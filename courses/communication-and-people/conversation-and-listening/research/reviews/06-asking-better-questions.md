### Findings

**R1 [major, pedagogy] The checkpoint's answer for Osric's (1) has open and closed reversed, and its
diagnostic sentence then teaches the reversal.**
Passage: "(1) Introductory, and closed in form, though nobody answers "how's it going?" with a yes."
and "If you called (1) open because it can't really be answered yes or no, that's fair, and it's the
StoryCorps point from the other side".
Evidence: "How's it going?" is a *how* question: open in form by the lesson's own definition ("can be
answered with one word" is the closed test; the NHS list it cites is "how", "what", "where" or
"when"). What it does in practice is the closed thing: it draws a routine "fine". So the form/function
split is real, but the lesson has it backwards, and a reader who correctly typed it "open" is told
that is a charitable reading of an error. Tracing the diagnostic backwards (defect 13): the reader it
addresses made no slip. Separately, the transcript puts Anouk's "I went to the climbing wall after
work" *before* Osric's "How's it going?", so the "introductory" question comes second, after she has
already told him something, which fits Huang's "routine questions at the beginning of the
conversation" badly and reads more like a switch.
Fix: move "(1) How's it going?" above Anouk's first line (he walks in, asks, she answers "I went to the
climbing wall after work"). Rewrite the answer: "(1) Introductory. Open in form, since it starts with
'how', but in practice it gets a one-word 'fine', so it does a closed question's job." Rewrite the
diagnostic: "If you typed (1) closed because nobody gives it more than a word, that's fair: it's the
StoryCorps point from the other side, where the form of a question and what it does come apart."

**R2 [major, facts and neutrality] Gordon's roadblock 11 is misquoted, and the manual the lesson cites
squares the tension itself, in the paragraph the lesson quotes from and two pages earlier.**
Passage: "include "questioning, probing" as number 11 ... That is **practitioner lore** ... on questions
it sits against the experiments above" and the opener "A list that practitioners have used for
decades points the other way."
Evidence: TIP 35 Exhibit 3.5, item 11, reads "Questioning or probing", not "questioning, probing" (the
comma form is SOURCES Part C §6.5's paraphrase list, inherited: defect 26). The exhibit's own gloss on
item 11: "Do not mistake questioning for good listening. Although you may ask questions to learn more
about the client, the underlying message is that you might find the right answer to all the client's
problems if enough questions are asked. In fact, intensive questioning can disrupt communication, and
sometimes the client feels as if he or she is being interrogated." And the same manual, p. 41, lists
"Asking Open questions" first among the core skills of motivational interviewing (OARS), with Exhibit
3.4 of closed and open questions. So the source does not "point the other way" on asking; it objects
to intensive, interrogating questioning while teaching open questions as a core skill. The lesson's
"whose agenda a question serves" reconciliation is labelled as this course's, but the source's own
reconciliation is omitted, which makes the practitioner position look cruder than it is (Turing test,
defect 10 in miniature).
Fix: correct the quotation to "questioning or probing". Replace the first paragraph's framing with the
manual's own words, e.g.: "Gordon's twelve roadblocks to listening, as a US government treatment
manual reproduces them, include "questioning or probing" as number 11, with the note that "intensive
questioning can disrupt communication, and sometimes the client feels as if he or she is being
interrogated". The same manual lists asking open questions first among its own core skills. So the
lore isn't against questions; it's against a certain kind." Keep "no study this course found tests the
twelve as a set". Then the course's agenda reading can follow as an extension, labelled as now.
Reword quiz 6's distractor explanation ("the roadblock list is practitioner lore that conflicts with
the question-asking evidence") to "the roadblock list objects to intensive, interrogating questioning,
not to asking". Add a line to SOURCES Part C §6.5 correcting the item wording.

**R3 [major, neutrality and facts] The "why" disagreement omits Acas's own caveat, which is the
negotiators' point, and then gives that point to "this course's reading".**
Passage: "Acas gives "Why did you leave?" as its example of a good open question" ... "On this course's
reading, it helps to notice what "why" asks for: a reason, which can sound like a demand to justify
yourself." Also quiz 6 option B: "since Acas gives "Why did you leave?" as a good open question".
Evidence: Acas's table lists "Why did you leave?" as its example of an *open question* (it does not
call it good; its drawbacks column says the person "may talk too much, drift away from the subject").
Two rows down, under "Probing/reflective questions", its example is "Why did you say that?" and the
drawback is "The employee may feel threatened. Attention must be given to anticipating and monitoring
the effect on the person." That is close to Vecchi et al.'s "perceived as interrogatory". The traditions
disagree less than the section says, and the "demand to justify yourself" point is in a cited source,
not the course's own. (Also relevant: Headlee's transcript, already cited as [7], says "Start your
questions with who, what, when, where, why or how".)
Fix: change "as its example of a good open question" to "as its first example of an open question".
Add after it: "though the same guide warns that a probing "why" ("Why did you say that?") can leave the
person feeling threatened.[9]" Replace the course's-reading sentence with: "So the difference may be
narrower than it looks: both warn that a "why" can land as a demand to justify yourself, and they differ
on how often that risk is worth taking." In quiz 6, option B: "since Acas gives "Why did you leave?" as
an example of an open question" (re-measure lengths after). The misconception entry on "why" can stay
but should say "the negotiators avoid it, workplace and oral-history guides use it with a warning".

**R4 [major, facts] "The UK trial adds" a mechanism the UK trial doesn't state.**
Passage: "The UK trial adds that when asking comes early and people are used to speaking up, the word
may matter less."
Evidence: Leydon et al. conclude that wording "may not be as important as the placement of the GP
solicitation", and list as differences the patients' problems (new and long-standing), recruitment,
demographics (older, mostly white) and a service "free at the point of access". Nothing in the paper
says UK patients are "used to speaking up". The "asking comes early" half is theirs; the other half is
this course's inference, attributed to the trial (defect 22, third form).
Fix: "The UK trial adds that when the question comes early, as it did in both studies, the word may
matter less than the timing." If the course wants the "used to speaking up" idea, label it: "and, on
this course's reading, in a room where people already speak up, there is less for any wording to
change."

**R5 [minor, facts] Leydon's "some" wording differs from Heritage's, and Philippa's proposed wording is
Leydon's form, the one that showed no difference.**
Passage: "Should she say "Are there some questions?" instead?" and the Leydon paragraph, which never
gives the UK wording.
Evidence: Leydon's arms were "Are there ANY other concerns that you'd like to discuss today?" and "Are
there SOME other concerns that you'd like to discuss today?"; Heritage's were "anything else" and
"something else". The lesson carries Heritage's result to "some" in general.
Fix: in the Leydon paragraph give the two UK questions in a clause ("Are there any/some other concerns
you'd like to discuss today?"). In Philippa, either change her candidate to Heritage's form ("Is there
something you'd like to ask?") or note that the plural "Are there some...?" is the form the UK trial
used.

**R6 [minor, facts] The 37% and the 9 of 252 are different denominators and sit close enough to be
compared.**
Passage: "37% of patients with more than one concern left with one unmentioned" and "only 9 of 252
patients reported leaving with a planned concern unmentioned" and the course's reading "in a US clinic
where many patients left with something unsaid ... in a UK trial where almost nobody did".
Evidence: Heritage's 37% is the no-intervention arm, patients with two or more concerns only. Leydon's
9 of 252 is all patients across all three arms (3 control, 4 "some", 2 "any"). Heritage's own
whole-sample figure is 20 of 204 (10%) across all arms, and the discussion gives 18% overall in the
no-intervention visits.
Fix: add "across all patients and arms (about 4%)" after "9 of 252", and in the course's reading say
"where more than a third of patients with several concerns left with one unsaid", so the reader is not
invited to set 37% against 4%.

**R7 [minor, facts] Heritage's own caution about non-significant results is dropped where the lesson
leans on one.**
Passage: "could not be statistically distinguished from asking nothing at all" (predict answer), "the
difference from asking nothing wasn't statistically significant", and "and the authors call it small".
Evidence: "The study was relatively small and lacked statistical power to distinguish small
differences, so that findings that were not statistically significant should be viewed with some
caution." The "any vs nothing" null is exactly such a finding, and "anything" still drew a yes from
53.1%.
Fix: in "Has it held up?", replace "and the authors call it small" with "and the authors call it
relatively small, too small to detect modest differences, so they ask for caution about its
non-significant results, including 'anything' against asking nothing."

**R8 [minor, facts] Essential Partners' line is a facilitator's invitation, not an "agreement".**

said that you'd like to understand better? If you ask a question, be sure..."), not one of the
communication agreements in §2.4. SOURCES' gate-closure line calls it the "Question agreement", which
is where the label came from (defect 26).
Fix: "and Essential Partners, ... script their facilitators to invite questions this way: "If you ask
a question...". Correct the SOURCES label. (Optional, for Connections: the same invitation opens with
"Is there something someone said...", a "some" question.)
