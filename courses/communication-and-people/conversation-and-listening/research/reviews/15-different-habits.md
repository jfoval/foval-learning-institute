### F1. Akechi's approachability and pleasantness results are misstated (High: a factual error)

> The Japanese students also rated her as less approachable and less pleasant when she looked at them.[3]

The paper says the opposite on approachability: "Finnish participants rated direct eye contact as
more approachable than displays of averted gaze; however, gaze direction had no effect on Japanese
participants' approachability ratings." On pleasantness the culture-by-gaze interaction was
significant (p = .043), but the simple effect of culture on the direct-gaze difference was only
marginal (p = .094). The abstract's summary is comparative, "perceive another's face as being angrier,
unapproachable, and unpleasant when making eye contact as compared to individuals from a Western
European culture", and does not say the Japanese students' ratings fell when she looked at them.
The paper also found a sadness effect in the Japanese group (p = .024), which the lesson leaves out;
that omission is acceptable.

**Fix:** "Compared with the Finnish students, the Japanese students also found her direct gaze less
approachable and, marginally, less pleasant. Looking at them made the Finnish students rate her as
more approachable, and it made no difference to the Japanese students' approachability ratings."

### F2. The elderspeak trial is described as a randomised comparison, and its headline figures are not one (Medium)

> In a 2017 **randomised trial** across 13 nursing homes, 42 pairs of staff and residents were filmed
> during care. Staff in the homes assigned to three one-hour training sessions cut their elderspeak,
> from 34.6% of their talk by 13.6 percentage points on average, and residents' resistance to care
> fell, from 35.7% of the time by 15.3 points.

The homes were randomised, but "The NHs assigned to control later crossed over and received the
intervention". Too few dyads met the inclusion criteria (42 against 80 planned), and the authors say
"the sample sizes for a traditional randomized controlled trial analyses that compared intervention
and control groups were inadequate". So they used "a linear mixed modeling (LMM) approach that
utilized all available data pre and post intervention". The 34.6 to 13.6 and 35.7 to 15.3 figures are
before-and-after changes, not differences from a control group. "Homes assigned to" training
therefore describes every home in the end. The 3-month fall in resistance was not significant
(13.4 points, p = .077). The lesson does already give "small sample" and filming as limits.

**Fix:** keep "randomised" for the design and add one clause: "The trial was designed to compare
trained homes with untrained ones, but too few pairs qualified to analyse it that way, so the figures
are before-and-after changes once staff had been trained (the comparison homes were trained later
too)." Change "Staff in the homes assigned to" to "After".

### F3. The quiz 5 explanation contradicts RNID on repeating (Medium)

> And when someone doesn't catch a sentence, RNID suggests rephrasing it, not saying the same words again.

RNID's page says: "If someone doesn't understand you, repeat what you said or phrase it differently,
use plain language", and near the top, "Don't be afraid to repeat or rephrase and try something
different." Repeating is part of RNID's advice. The distractor is still wrong: its rationale, that
two identical repetitions let a person lipread "the rest", is invented, and it drops the "try
something different" step.

**Fix:** "And RNID's advice when someone doesn't catch a sentence is to repeat it or say it a
different way, not to repeat everything twice by rule."

### F4. The Livingston et al. quotation drops the conditional that softens it (Medium: neutrality, defects 9 and 19)

> "we caution against the translation of DEP research into applied settings".[12]

The abstract reads: "Until then, we caution against the translation of DEP research into applied
settings." "Then" refers to "a strengthening of the derivation chain", so the caution is
conditional. The same abstract also grants that DEP research "has the potential to improve
understanding of both typical and atypical social processes". Printed without either, the critique
reads as a flat rejection, which is harsher than the authors' own framing. Bollen and van Grunsven's
abstract acknowledges the same thing ("they acknowledge that the DEPH offers promising insights").

**Fix:** open the quotation at "Until then" or paraphrase the condition ("until the idea is better
defined, they caution..."), and add one clause: "while granting that the research could improve
understanding".

### F5. The national-typology block states the typologies' claim in the critics' framing and misstates what the critics dispute (Medium: neutrality, defect 10)

> If groups differ, why not learn the differences country by country? That is the promise of two well-known frameworks

> What the reviews dispute is that a country's label predicts what a particular person will do, because people within a country vary so much.

Quiz 3 explanation: "Applying a country label to each person is the step the critics object to most."

(a) "That is the promise" credits Hall and Hofstede with offering individual prediction. The lesson
says it hasn't read either book, so this is exactly the claim it can't make in their words. A
Hofstede-tradition reader would say the scores were never meant to describe individuals. The course
can't assert that either without reading him, but it shouldn't assert the reverse.
(b) Kittler et al. and Cardon, in the abstracts read, dispute the country classifications themselves
("flawed or, at best, very limited"; propositions "unsubstantiated and underdeveloped"). They do not
say the problem is applying them to individuals, and neither abstract mentions within-country
variation. That point comes from Fischer and Schwartz alone, and they measured **value priorities**,
not communication habits. Their abstract also reports that conformity values do look like "good
candidates for measuring culture as shared meaning systems", which the lesson leaves out.
(c) Nothing cited addresses Hofstede's dimensions at all, since McSweeney was cut. Hofstede is
named and then implicitly dismissed.
(d) The quiz 3 claim "the step the critics object to most" is supported by nothing read.

**Fix:** "That is how they are often used in training: learn the country, predict the person. This
course has not read Hall's or Hofstede's books, so it can't say how far their authors meant them to
be used that way." Then: "The reviews of Hall's scheme question the country classifications
themselves; Fischer and Schwartz, working on values rather than conversation, found countries agree
more than they disagree. Either way, a label is a weak guide to a person." Either drop Hofstede or
say that nothing this course read tests his dimensions. In quiz 3, replace the last sentence with
something supported: "The reviews question the country classifications themselves, so applying one
to a single person rests on weak ground twice over."

### F6. Small tilts in the double-empathy evidence list (Low to Medium: neutrality)

(a) Rum et al. is given as "Findings suggest partial support for the double empathy problem theory"
without what "partial" rests on. The abstract reports "No significant main effects" for rater or
target neurotype and "A trend-level interaction (p=.059, d = 0.13)". It also says "Individual
variability patterns caution against treating neurotypes as homogeneous categories". Brewer et al.'s
null gets its headline sentence; Rum et al.'s qualified positive gets only its headline.
**Fix:** add "(a trend, not a significant effect)" after the Rum quotation.

(b) The lesson applies the authors' after-the-fact caveat, "may have failed to capture difficulties
experienced in real-world cross-neurotype interactions", only to the non-replication. It argues
from the same lab task that autistic-to-autistic transfer "is hard to square with a strong deficit
reading". If the task may miss real-world difficulty, that cuts both ways. A social-deficit
researcher would say that a story-retelling task measures structured verbal recall, not the
reciprocal and nonverbal features in the diagnostic description. The course has no source for that
reply and shouldn't supply it, but it can apply the authors' caveat symmetrically.
**Fix:** after "hard to square with a strong deficit reading", add "in this task".

(c) Low: the 2020 analyses were also "pre-registered (Crompton & Fletcher-Watson, 2019)". The
predict's "A larger, preregistered replication" and the "(a single study)" and "(a registered
report)" labels read as if 2020 was not. Optional: "(a single study, with preregistered analyses)".

The rest of the count passes. The deficit reading gets three sources (Crompton 2025's opening, Rum's
background, Livingston). The double-empathy reading gets three (Milton, Crompton 2020, Bollen). Each
side has one misconception against it. The lesson says no primary statement of the deficit tradition
was read, which is the honest handling under defect 10. Double empathy is explained (its mechanism
and its prediction) while the deficit reading is only quoted, but the lesson declares why. Last word:
the Itzchakov and Bodie value sentence, then advice shared by both readings. Acceptable, though the
quoted value sentence leans toward the neurodiversity framing. Itzchakov and Bodie actually write
"most of the guidance", so "much" softens it slightly, in the safe direction.
