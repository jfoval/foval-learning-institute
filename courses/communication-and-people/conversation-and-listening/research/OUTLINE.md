# Outline: Conversation and Listening

*Stage 2, 2026-09-26. Built from `research/SOURCES.md`, read in full (the synthesis and Parts A to E),
after `npm run taught`, which shows twenty-one earlier courses and 238 lessons, all written. This is
the first course of Term 4, so it draws the lines the later people courses inherit. **A standards 3.4
domain** (psychology, and gender, culture, disability and political dialogue inside it):
`sensitive_domain: true` stays, and every lesson gets a neutrality pass before it is published (rule
6 of `courses/CLAUDE.md`), with the full audit on the lessons named under "Neutrality notes". Most of
the course is uncontroversial skill teaching built on unusually good evidence. What can go wrong here
is different from Car Basics: not a car off a jack, but a finding from forty American students taught
as a fact about humanity, a practitioner's slogan taught as a finding, a contested question closed in
the direction the course would like, and one conversation (about suicide) where a wrong sentence
matters.*

## How many lessons, and why

**Seventeen, a course-end test and a project.** SOURCES suggested fourteen and an optional fifteenth.
This outline keeps the fourteen, splits two of them, adds the capstone, and moves one topic:

- **Active listening is two lessons (8 and 9): the practice, then the evidence.** SOURCES open concern
  7 named this as the natural split. Saying it back (Rogers' "total meaning", restating to the
  speaker's satisfaction, simple and complex reflections, naming a feeling lightly, teach-back) is a
  full sitting of skill. Whether any of it works is the most contested empirical question in the
  course (SOURCES contested 1 and 2), with eight or more studies, a four-lab null, a meta-analysis
  and the Gottman controversy, and it is the lesson behind the paywall gate (G1). Keeping them apart
  means the skill lesson can be drafted while the gate is worked, and neither lesson passes the
  ceiling.
- **Talking across difference is two lessons (15 and 16).** SOURCES lesson 14 bundled culture, gaze,
  autism, age, hearing loss, interpreters and political disagreement. On paper that runs past two
  hours, and the two halves ask different things of a listener: the first is adjusting to a person
  whose habits differ from yours, the second is listening to a person whose view you reject. Open
  concern 1 said this area is the one most likely to sprawl; two bounded lessons hold it better than
  one crowded one.
- **The liking gap moves from the strangers lesson to the endings lesson (13).** Both endings
  (Mastroianni et al. 2021) and the liking gap (Boothby et al. 2018) are misjudgements of what the
  other person felt, found at or after the end of a conversation, partly by the same authors. Paired,
  each is a full lesson; the strangers-and-depth lesson (12) was over-full with it.
- **A capstone, lesson 17: a famous talk, checked.** SOURCES offered "Weighing a claim about
  conversation" as an option. This outline takes it, in a form that is not the institute's usual
  "Reading a claim about your X" (Mental Fitness, Memory, Focus, Habits and Time Management all end
  that way, and a sixth copy would read as a template). The lesson takes Celeste Headlee's TED talk,
  which most learners will have met, and checks each of its ten rules against what the course has
  taught. That makes it spaced retrieval of the whole course, and it teaches the evidence habit on a
  real specimen rather than in the abstract.

The sequence, in five groups:

**How conversation works**
1. **What a conversation is made of.** Turns, the transition point, who speaks next, the 200 ms gap,
   planning while listening, overlap as a share of transitions and of time. The course's frame.
2. **Why "no" comes late.** Adjacency pairs, preference, repair; what a pause means and does not; the
   fast-reply finding and its limits.

**What listening is, and how it fails**
3. **Hearing is not listening.** Definitions, purposes, the stock numbers and where they came from,
   listening styles, why you can't rate your own listening, and whether listening is a technique or
   attention owed.
4. **How listening goes wrong.** The named failures; the shift and support responses; why talking
   about yourself is rewarding; distraction; the second-story question.
5. **Let them finish: interruption, overlap and silence.** Cooperative and intrusive overlap;
   conversational style; the gender question; medicine's stopwatch; silence as a tool; stammering.

**The listener's tools**
6. **Asking better questions.** Follow-up questions and liking; open and closed; one at a time;
   "some" and "any"; counterfeit questions; when not to ask.
7. **Ask, don't guess.** Perspective taking against perspective getting; the illusion of
   transparency; empathic accuracy; whether empathy can be taught, and whether more is better.
8. **Saying it back.** Rogers' idea and its warning; paraphrase as a check, not a performance;
   reflections; naming a feeling; teach-back.
9. **Does active listening work?** What each study meant by it, what each found, the four-lab test,
   the meta-analysis, and Gottman.

**When it matters most**
10. **When someone brings you a problem.** Person-centred support; advice and its timing; help that
    backfires; the fixing reflex.
11. **When it's serious.** A friend at risk of suicide: noticing, asking directly, listening, not
    promising secrecy, connecting to help. Not counselling.

**Beginnings, endings and other people**
12. **Strangers, small talk and going deeper.** Undersociality; depth; the small-talk finding that
    did not replicate; what people talk about.
13. **How it ends, and how it went.** Endings as a coordination problem; pre-closings; the liking gap.
14. **What body language can and cannot tell you.** Mehrabian; lie detection; faces; thin slices;
    mimicry; power posing.
15. **Different habits: culture, autism, age and hearing.** Timing and gaze across cultures; national
    labels; autism, eye contact and double empathy; hearing loss, interpreters, older people.
16. **Listening to someone who disagrees with you.** Acknowledgement is not agreement; receptiveness;
    structured dialogue; what the trials show about changing minds.
17. **A famous talk, checked.** Ten rules against the course.

**Why this order.** The machinery first, because every later lesson leans on it: "waiting for your
turn instead of listening" means nothing until lesson 1 has shown that everyone plans their reply
during the other's turn, and "hear the no in a slow well" needs lesson 2. Listening's definition and
its failures come next, so the tools in lessons 6 to 9 have a named problem to solve. Questions come
before "ask, don't guess", which gives the reason questions and checks are needed (you cannot read a
mind from the outside), and that comes before saying it back, which is the check. The evidence on
active listening follows the practice, so the reader has done the thing before being asked whether it
works. The two hard conversations come after the tools they use. Openings, endings, bodies and
difference come later because each applies earlier ideas to a new case: the Stivers timing data in
lesson 1 return in lesson 15; the preference for fast agreement in lesson 2 returns in the
pre-closings of lesson 13; asking rather than guessing (7) is the positive advice of lesson 14 and
the rule of lesson 15. The capstone retrieves everything.

**Why not fewer.** The fourteen-lesson sequence put active listening's practice and evidence in one
sitting (past two hours on paper) and difference in one (the same). Merging 12 and 13, or 3 and 4,
each came out around two hours.

**Why not more.** Interruption and silence stay one lesson, because the medical stopwatch and the
gender literature are about the same act. Questions stay one lesson; "some" versus "any" is a
section, not a lesson. Empathy is a section of lesson 7, not its own lesson, because Understanding
People owns empathy's psychology and this course needs only what bears on accuracy and support.
Nonverbal communication is one lesson, because most of what the sources say about it is what it
cannot do.

**Lesson length, planned for the ceiling from the start.** The floor is completeness (each objective
with a concrete case, a fully worked example, its misconception and practice the reader does). The
ceiling is one sitting, about 90 measured minutes (`npm run minutes`, which counts exercises and the
Sources list). Every lesson below is planned at 70 to 85 minutes, because the institute's drafts have
run over their estimates in every course measured. Each lesson carries **"Cut first if long"**: the
sections to shorten, in order, if a draft passes 90. A drafter who has cut the whole list and is still
past 90 writes one line in their reply saying why the lesson earns the length, and the orchestrator
decides whether to accept it or split. There are no named seams; a split renumbers the course, so
cross-references always carry the title as well as the number.

## What is deliberately left out, and said so in lesson 1

- **Public speaking** (one-to-many talk, delivery, speech anxiety, slides, critical listening to a
  speech). Public Speaking, Term 4.
- **Occasions and manners**: introductions and names, small-talk scripts for events, networking,
  compliments, thanks, apologies, invitations, phone and message etiquette. Social Skills and
  Etiquette, Term 4. Compliments and gratitude (Zhao and Epley 2021; Kumar and Epley 2018) get one
  line in lesson 12 as part of the undersociality pattern and nothing more.
- **Settling a disagreement**: interests and positions, the *Difficult Conversations* framework,
  mediation, negotiation. Conflict Resolution, Term 4. This course keeps listening while you disagree
  (lesson 16).
- **Couples' and parenting methods**: Gottman's research in full, *Parent Effectiveness Training*,
  *The Lost Art of Listening*. Relationships and Family, Term 4. The Gottman active-listening
  controversy gets one section of lesson 9, as the limit of paraphrase.
- **Emotion theory, personality, empathy's neuroscience, the replication crisis as a subject.**
  Understanding People, Term 4. Lesson 14 uses the face debate only as far as "can you read what
  someone feels from their face?"; lesson 7 uses the empathy evidence only as it bears on accuracy.
  **Neural synchrony** (Duhigg's "neural entrainment", Stephens, Silbert and Hasson 2010) is not
  taught here: unread, gated, and Understanding People's.
- **Biases in general.** Cognitive Biases and Clear Thinking, Term 4, which links back to the
  conversation-specific miscalibrations taught here (the liking gap, endings, undersociality, the
  illusion of transparency).
- **Persuasion and negotiation technique**, including Chris Voss's "tactical empathy" and the end of
  the FBI stairway (influence and behavioural change). Persuasion and Influence (Term 14) and
  Negotiation (Term 17). Where a source frames listening as a way to get something (Carnegie; the FBI
  model; deep canvassing), the lesson says so and says it is a different goal (decision 8).
- **Counselling, risk assessment and safety planning.** The course teaches what a friend does in a
  conversation (lesson 11) and points to Mental Fitness lesson 1 and the crisis lines for the rest
  (decision 5).
- **Lie detection as a professional skill.** Lesson 14 says what demeanour can and cannot show and
  that content and questioning do better; it teaches no interview method.
- **Written and online conversation.** Writing Clearly owns written communication. The receptiveness
  studies (lesson 16) were mostly written text, and the lesson says so; texting etiquette is Social
  Skills'.
- **Group facilitation and meetings.** One line at most (lesson 5 or 16) on the disputed "equal
  turn-taking" claim around Project Aristotle and Woolley et al. 2010, which are gated (G19); no
  lesson teaches meeting technique.
- **Sign languages, speech and language therapy, and hearing technology.** Lesson 15 gives RNID's
  and STAMMA's advice to a conversation partner and nothing clinical.
- **Dating and romance** as a setting. The speed-dating data in lesson 6 are used for what they show
  about follow-up questions, not as dating advice.

## Twenty decisions, settled here so no lesson re-opens them

**1. Honest objectives, and exercises that involve other people only with their knowledge.**
Objectives use verbs a reader can be tested on from a page: **explain, identify, classify, predict,
rewrite, compare, apply, trace, plan, state**. None says "master", "connect deeply" or "become a
great listener". `course.yaml`'s outcomes are reworded to match (done at this stage).

A conversation course cannot be practised alone, so the exercises are of five kinds, and each lesson
says which:

- **Notice**: an entry in the **observation journal** (the project's first deliverable, started in
  lesson 1), written from memory within an hour of an ordinary conversation, with nobody named or
  identifiable. The design is borrowed from the Ohio State first-year seminar on conversation, where
  weekly observation journals are 40% of the grade (SOURCES Part A §5.3).
- **Analyse public talk**: a recorded conversation already published for listening (a podcast
  interview, a radio phone-in, a televised panel), for timing, overlap, questions and endings. Never
  anyone's private recording.
- **Rewrite on paper**: questions, responses, restatements, receptive replies, drafted and compared
  with a model hidden behind a checkpoint.
- **Try one move**: in an ordinary, low-stakes conversation the reader was going to have anyway (wait
  a breath before replying; ask two follow-ups; restate before disagreeing), then write what happened,
  including nothing.
- **Practise with a willing partner**: a friend who has agreed to it and knows it is practice, who
  rates the reader on a few named items. Any rating exercise says the partner may decline and may say
  nothing kind.

**No exercise asks a reader to:**

- record anyone without their explicit, prior consent (the law on recording conversations differs
  between countries and US states, which this course did not read, so the course's rule is simply
  consent first); the observation journal is written from memory, and a recording made for the
  project is made with everyone's agreement and deleted after;
- raise suicide, self-harm or any crisis with someone as practice, or role-play a crisis with a person
  who may be struggling; lesson 11's practice is on paper and in finding the lines;
- practise "active listening" or any technique on someone in distress as an experiment;
- use a technique on someone covertly, test a finding on people without their knowledge (no secret
  mirroring, no covert lie tests), or deceive anyone;
- diagnose anyone (autistic, depressed, lying, narcissistic), or describe a real, identifiable person
  in those terms in the journal or the project;
- talk to a stranger if they would rather not. Lesson 12's stranger exercise is optional, in an
  everyday public setting the reader already uses (a queue, a shop counter, a waiting room), leaving
  the moment either person wants to, and points to Personal Safety lesson 6, "Out and away: streets,
  travel and meeting people", for anything beyond that;
- disclose anything personal they would not otherwise share, or post a journal entry or a partner's
  rating anywhere.

The drafter writes the relevant limits into each exercise, not into a footnote.

**2. Every finding carries an evidence label, in the prose.** Labels are words in the sentence, not
badges, and the course teaches them in lesson 1:

- **Replicated**: found again by a group other than the original one, or in a pooled or meta-analytic
  set of studies. Say which ("the pooled replication of four samples"; "a meta-analysis of 206
  documents"). The turn-timing results count here only with their scope (Stivers et al. 2009 is one
  study across ten languages; Levinson's figures pool corpora).
- **One group's programme**: several experiments, mostly from one lab or network, not independently
  replicated as far as this course read (the Epley network's undersociality work; the Itzchakov and
  Kluger listening experiments; Huang et al. 2017). Where one independent replication exists (Liu et
  al. 2023 Study 4a, through the Transparent Replications report), say that.
- **Single study**: one study; give its sample in the same sentence (Heritage et al. 2007: 20 family
  physicians, 224 visits; Akechi et al. 2013: 20 Finnish and 20 Japanese students).
- **Failed to replicate**, **corrected** or **retracted**: name the replication, correction or notice
  (Milek et al. 2018; the 2025 correction of Huang et al.; LaCour and Green 2014).
- **Contested**: serious researchers disagree; both positions in their own words, what would settle
  it, and no verdict (decision 12).
- **Observed in recordings**: conversation analysis's qualitative findings (Sacks, Schegloff and
  Jefferson 1974; Pomerantz 1984; Schegloff 2000; Tannen), described as patterns found in recorded
  talk, not measured rates, and later quantified where they were.
- **Practitioner advice**: what services and professions teach from experience (SHUSH, the 988 steps,
  NURSE, OARS, LAPP, the FBI stairway, Gordon's roadblocks, Calgary-Cambridge), untested as such.
  Always with the profession and its goal (decision 9).

And **this course's reading**, said in those words, wherever the lesson concludes something no single
source states. The label carries into the quiz explanations, the test and the podcast scripts.

**3. The course's question: finding or advice, who was in the room, and compared with what?** Car
Basics asked "law, handbook or advice: whose, and for which car?". The failure this course guards
against is different: a finding from one kind of person in one kind of setting, or one profession's
rule of thumb, repeated as if it described every conversation. So every claim a lesson teaches
carries, in the sentence: **whether it is a finding or advice** (decision 2); **who was studied or who
advises** (US undergraduates, London commuters, family physicians with a clock, FBI negotiators,
Samaritans volunteers); **the setting** (strangers in a lab, a text chat, a clinic, a phone line,
friends at home); and, for an effect, **what it was compared with** (Mental Fitness lesson 2's
question, reused by name). Introduced in lesson 1 and asked in every lesson. SOURCES open concern 2:
"lessons should say who was studied, and not present them as human universals".

**4. Effect sizes are read the way Mental Fitness lesson 2 taught.** That lesson's table (an effect of
0.2 puts the average person in one group ahead of about 58% of the other; 0.3, 62%; 0.5, 69%; 0.8,
79%) is reused by name and labelled, as there, as "the course's arithmetic on the normal curve, not a
figure from any study". A correlation (r = .39; r = .23) is said as a correlation, never as a
percentage improvement and never as cause (Kluger et al. 2024 "may not say": "that listening 'causes'
a 39% improvement"). Every effect names what the comparison group got: a distracted listener (the
Itzchakov contrast), a bare acknowledgement (Weger 2010), advice (Weger 2014), sitting in silence
(Epley and Schroeder 2014), "at most four questions" (Huang 2017).

**5. Crisis handling: the Mental Fitness callout, word for word, in lessons 10 and 11, and no
counselling anywhere.** SOURCES "Boundaries" and Part E §1.9 settle the scope: the course teaches
only what a friend does in a conversation (notice, ask directly, listen without fixing, do not promise
secrecy, help the person connect to a crisis line or a professional, follow up, look after yourself),
and teaches **no counselling, risk assessment or safety planning**, and says so in lesson 11.

- **Lessons 10 and 11 carry Mental Fitness's crisis callout**, byte for byte, directly after the
  opening paragraph. It is the text in `scripts/build.mjs` (`CRISIS`), whose body is one line in the
  file:

  ```
  :::callout If you're struggling right now
  This course is education, not care. If you're thinking about suicide or self-harm, or don't feel able to keep yourself safe, contact emergency services (911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 112 across the EU, 000 in Australia) or a crisis line: call or text 988 in the US and Canada, call Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK and Ireland, or Lifeline on 13 11 14 in Australia. Elsewhere, findahelpline.com lists free, confidential lines by country.
  :::
  ```

  Lesson 10 carries it because a reader supporting someone through a problem may be struggling too,
  and because distress can turn serious mid-conversation; lesson 11 because it is the lesson's
  subject. **For the orchestrator:** add a build check keyed on `cdir.name ===
  "conversation-and-listening"` and lesson files beginning `10-` and `11-`, the same shape as Personal
  Safety's `CRISIS_PS` check for its lessons 15 and 16, before lesson 10 is drafted; add the
  callout's sentences to `REPETITION_EXEMPT` if the within-course repetition warning fires on the pair.
  The numbers in the callout are re-read on the services' pages on the day each of those lessons is
  drafted and again on publication day (gate G7). If a number has changed, the fix is made in the
  build constant and in every course that carries it, by the orchestrator, never in one lesson.
- **No course-wide callout on every lesson.** This course is not safety-critical in the way First Aid,
  Personal Safety, Home Repair and Car Basics are: no instruction in lessons 1 to 9 or 12 to 17 can
  hurt anyone if it is wrong, and a box repeated on seventeen lessons would stop being read (Car
  Basics decision 4's own reasoning for keeping its callout short). Where a lesson outside 10 and 11
  meets distress (lesson 3's scope section, lesson 12's depth questions, lesson 15's autism section,
  the project), it gives one sentence pointing to lesson 11 and Mental Fitness lesson 1, "Where
  ordinary ends".
- **Other disclosures belong to Personal Safety.** When a friend discloses domestic abuse or stalking,
  lesson 11 points to Personal Safety lesson 7, "Domestic abuse and stalking" (its section "Helping
  someone"); sexual assault, to Personal Safety lesson 11, "Sexual assault: the freeze, resistance and
  the law" (its section "When a friend tells you"). It does not reteach either.
- **Evidence wording** (Part E gates 4 and 5): asking about suicide "has not been found to increase
  suicidal thinking" in research, screening and clinical settings (Dazzi et al. 2014, a short
  narrative review of 13 papers, not a meta-analysis); **no study tested a conversation between
  friends**, and the lesson says so; "ask directly" is taught as the unanimous advice of the services.
  Samaritans' "can protect" and #BeThe1To's "may reduce" are attributed, never restated. The 988
  steps in the service's exact wording: Ask; Be there; Help keep them safe; Help them connect; Follow
  up.

**6. The education note, once.** Standards 2.5: lesson 1's scope section carries one sentence,
"This course is education, not therapy or professional advice about your own relationships or
anyone's mental health." It is not repeated elsewhere (the crisis callout covers lessons 10 and 11).

**7. Boundaries, by course and lesson, each by name and never retaught.** SOURCES "Boundaries" is
binding. The pointers a lesson gives, at the place the reader needs them:

- **Mental Fitness** lesson 1, "Where ordinary ends" (where distress stops being a self-help
  question; the crisis lines), from lessons 10 and 11; lesson 2, "What a small effect is, and what the
  other group got" (effect sizes, comparison groups), from lessons 6, 7, 9 and 12.
- **Logic and Argument** lesson 7, "Reconstruction and charity: the strongest version first"
  (Rapoport's rules, the steelman), from lessons 8 and 16; lesson 6 (base rates in natural
  frequencies), from lesson 14; lesson 9 (checking a claim in the news), from lesson 17.
- **Reading Well** lesson 7, "Judging what you have understood" (criticism follows interpretation),
  from lesson 8.
- **Personal Safety** lesson 6 (meeting people; public settings), from lesson 12; lesson 7 and lesson
  11 (disclosures), from lesson 11; lesson 8, "Talking it down" (de-escalation when someone is
  threatening), from lessons 5 and 16, which do not teach de-escalation.
- **Focus and Deep Work** lesson 5, "The phone on the desk", from lesson 4 (distraction): that lesson
  is about the listener's own attention and a phone's presence; lesson 4's evidence is about what a
  distracted listener does to the speaker.
- **Memory** lesson 4, "What a question does to a memory" (how wording shapes an answer), from lesson
  6's "some" and "any".
- **How to Learn Anything** (retrieval practice; the journal as spaced retrieval), from lesson 1.
- Later Term 4 courses are named as where a topic goes next, never pointed to as if written (see
  "What downstream courses will need from this one").

**Open the earlier lesson and find the sentence before you write "as lesson N showed".**

**8. The course's standpoint: listening to understand, not to persuade.** SOURCES contested 18 is a
value question: Carnegie frames listening as "implied flattery" that wins friends and customers,
Rogers as understanding, Derber (as quoted by others) as a question of whose talk gets attention.
Lesson 3 describes the three positions in their own terms and then says plainly that this course's
lessons aim at understanding the other person, and that techniques for changing someone's mind
belong to Persuasion and Influence. Where a source's own goal is influence (the FBI stairway's
"influence, and behavioral change"; deep canvassing; Voss), the lesson names the goal once and does
not import it. **The course does not teach listening as a way to be liked**, even where the evidence
shows it has that effect (Huang et al.); it reports the effect as a finding.

**9. Practitioner scripts are labelled as professional tools, with their setting and goal.** SOURCES
contested 16 and Part E C3. NURSE (VitalTalk, oncology), OARS and reflections (motivational
interviewing, a clinical method for resolving ambivalence about a specific change), the FBI stairway
(crisis negotiation), Calgary-Cambridge (medicine), SHUSH and the 988 steps (crisis lines), LAPP
(Braver Angels workshops) and Gordon's roadblocks (a training list) are each introduced with who
built it and for what. The lessons teach the **moves the traditions share** (let them finish, allow
silence, open questions, say it back and check, name a feeling lightly, hold back advice until asked),
and say that using a clinical or negotiating script on a friend who did not ask for help is a
different act (MINT: MI "is not a way to 'get people to change'"). MI's clinical effect sizes are
never applied to conversation between friends.

**10. Every professional setting is named where its finding is used.** Medicine is the one field that
timed its own listening (Part E §2), and its findings are the course's best numbers, but "a doctor
with a clock and an agenda" is not two friends. The interruption and "some/any" findings are taught
with their setting in the sentence, and the transfer to ordinary talk is labelled as this course's
reading or as the authors' own suggestion (Heritage: "may generalize").

**11. Where a source gives no mechanism, the lesson gives none.** Where SOURCES records a mechanism,
it is taught as its authors' (Heritage's negative polarity of "any"; Templeton's "honest signal", "the
authors' interpretation, not something they tested"; Levinson's planning-time argument; Bolger and
Amarel's inefficacy mechanism; Huang's responsiveness mediation). Where none was recorded (why the
liking gap closes by May; why shy people's gap is larger beyond Boothby's own-performance mediator),
the lesson gives the finding and says the sources read give no settled explanation. No mechanism from
general knowledge.

**12. Contested questions are taught with both positions, what would settle each, and no verdict.**
The list is SOURCES "Contested questions", 1 to 18, classified there under standards 3.1, and the
lessons carry them as follows: 1 and 2 (lesson 9); 3 (lessons 4 and 10); 4 (lesson 10); 5 (lesson
12); 6 and 7 (lesson 5); 8 (lessons 1 and 15); 9, 10 and 11 (lesson 14); 12 (lesson 7); 13 (lesson
15); 14 (lesson 11); 15 (lesson 16); 16 (lessons 8 and 10); 17 (lesson 3); 18 (lesson 3). **Every
contested block runs Logic and Argument lesson 7's rule: the strongest version of each side, in its
own words, before any comparison.** Where a side has no primary statement in SOURCES (Derber's own
text; the case for national cultural typologies in Hall's or Hofstede's words; the critics of double
empathy), the lesson says the course has not read one and does not supply it from its opponents.
Count citations, last words and whose errors appear in the misconception list (defect 14) before
calling a block balanced. Settled things are stated as settled (turn-taking's shared system with small
variation; demeanour-only lie detection near chance; no hormonal effect of power posing; no increase
in suicidal ideation from being asked in the settings studied); false balance is a bias too.

**13. Language for people and groups.**

- **Gender**: "women" and "men" as the studies code them, averages as averages, and the setting and
  definition beside any difference (Anderson and Leaper 1998). No claim about what any individual man
  or woman does. The popular "20,000 versus 7,000 words" claim is not addressed with Mehl et al. 2007
  or Leaper and Ayres 2007, which are on the unread line; if a lesson names the myth, it says the
  course did not read the study that tested it.
- **Culture**: never "the Japanese do X" or "Danes are slow". Say what was measured, in whom, and how
  large the difference was, and that within-country variation is large (Fischer and Schwartz 2011).
  Communities are named as the studies name them (Tzeltal speakers in Mexico; northern Italians;
  Finnish and Japanese university students).
- **Autism**: "autistic people" and "non-autistic people", the words Milton, Crompton et al. and
  Trevisan et al. use; "self-declared" where Trevisan's sample was; no "suffers from", no "high- or
  low-functioning". The deficit reading and the double-empathy reading are each described in their
  proponents' terms; where the course has not read a critic of double empathy (Part D (f)), it says so.
- **Disability and age**: people who stammer, people who are deaf or have hearing loss, older people;
  the advice is the advocacy bodies' own (STAMMA, RNID), quoted as theirs.
- **Politics**: no position on any political issue. Examples of disagreement use topics on which
  reasonable people split without party lines (a town's cycle lanes, school homework, a household's
  car, a four-day school week) or, where a political case is needed, describe both sides in
  symmetrical terms with neither side's issue named as the example of error. Braver Angels and
  Essential Partners are described in their own words ("Reds and Blues"; "We never ask people to
  change their views").
- **Religion**: nothing in SOURCES bears on any tradition; the course makes no claim either way.

**14. Tiers and neutrality passes.** Every lesson gets a neutrality pass (rule 6; standards 3.4 and
3.5). The shape:

- **Tier B split** (two fresh-context reviewers in parallel, F for facts, neutrality and safety, P

### 1. What a conversation is made of (~75 min; Tier A, light neutrality pass)

- **Objectives:** explain how turns change hands at transition points, and apply the three
  next-speaker rules to a short transcript; explain why a listener must plan a reply while the other
  person is still talking, using the gap and planning-time figures; distinguish overlap as a share of
  transitions from overlap as a share of speaking time; state what this course covers, how it labels
  its evidence, and the question it asks of every claim.
- **Core ideas:** the reason to care: most of what goes wrong between people goes wrong in ordinary
  talk, and ordinary talk runs on a system precise enough to measure in milliseconds. **The
  turn-taking system** (Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson 1974, observed in recordings): turns built from
  units; the end of each possible unit a transition-relevance place; at it, (1a) a speaker the current
  one selected goes next, (1b) otherwise anyone may self-select and the first starter wins, (1c)
  otherwise the current speaker may go on; "Overwhelmingly, one party talks at a time"; "Occurrences of
  more than one speaker at a time are common, but brief"; locally managed. The 1974 paper measured no
  gaps and left cross-cultural validity to be settled "only empirically". **The numbers that came
  later**: Stivers et al. 2009 (ten languages, five continents, 350 questions each, polar questions
  only): overall mode 0 ms, median +100 ms, mean +208 ms; every language within about 250 ms of the
  mean; Japanese fastest (+7 ms), Danish slowest (+469 ms); "a general avoidance of overlapping talk
  and a minimization of silence". Levinson 2016: under 5% of speech has two speakers; modal gap about
  200 ms; planning a single word takes 600 ms if primed, about 1,000 ms if not, about 1,500 ms for a
  short clause, so "one must plan while still listening". Levinson and Torreira 2015 (Switchboard, 348
  English phone calls): 77% one speaker, 19.2% silence, 3.8% overlap, yet 30.1% of floor transfers
  overlapped (modal 96 ms), and 73% of overlaps involved a backchannel. **The course's frame**: what
  it covers and leaves out (this file's list, in a paragraph); the evidence labels (decision 2); the
  course's question (decision 3); the education note (decision 6); the journal (decision 1).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why the gap is shorter than the time it takes to plan a word: listeners
  predict where a turn will end and start planning their reply during it (Levinson's argument, with
  the EEG evidence of speech-act recognition within about 400 ms as he reports it). The practical
  consequence, stated as the course's reading: "waiting for my turn" is not the failure, since
  everyone plans during the other's turn; the question for lesson 4 is what the planning is aimed at.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a four-line exchange between two people arranging a lift, marked
  up: the units, the transition places, which rule chose each next speaker; then the course's
  arithmetic: a reply that begins 200 ms after the question ends, when a one-word reply takes at least
  600 ms to prepare, was being planned at least 400 ms before the question finished. (b) wrinkle: a
  three-person kitchen exchange in which nobody is selected, two people start at once, one drops out
  within a couple of syllables, and a third says "mm" in the middle of someone's turn: three overlaps,
  none of them an interruption. Gap: the reader labels the rule at each of four transitions before the
  checkpoint opens.
- **Misconceptions:** good listeners don't think about their reply until the other has finished (the
  timing makes that impossible); overlap is rare (true of time, false of transitions; Part B D8); the
  200 ms is a rule (it is the centre of a wide distribution; say which statistic, D7); conversation is
  unstructured; some cultures leave long silences and others talk over each other (a preview of lesson
  15: Stivers' data, Japanese fastest, Italians ordinary at +310 ms with 17% of responses in overlap).
- **Practice:** a public recorded conversation (a podcast interview or a radio phone-in the reader
  chooses): find five transition places and say which rule chose the next speaker; find two overlaps
  and say whether either was a problem for the people talking. Then the first **journal** entry: one
  ordinary conversation today, written up from memory, with nobody identifiable (decision 1).
- **Images:** SVG bar of the Switchboard split (77%, 19.2%, 3.8%) beside the 30.1% of transfers that
  overlapped, captioned with source, corpus and year; optional dot plot of three language means and the all-ten mean
  SOURCES records (Japanese +7, overall +208, Italian +310, Danish +469), captioned "four of the ten
  languages; polar questions only".
- **Sources:** synthesis findings 4, essentials 1 and 2; Part B §1.1, §2.1, §2.2, (a)1 to 3, (d) D7
  and D8; Part A §5.3 (the observation-journal model); Part D §5.4 (Stivers' "local variations are
  quantitative only").
- **Connects:** How to Learn Anything (the journal as spaced retrieval of the course's ideas); leads to
  lesson 2 (what a slow reply means) and lesson 5 (overlap and interruption).
- **Spaced review of:** Mental Fitness lesson 2's question, "compared with what?", introduced here as
  part of the course's question.
- **Neutrality:** Stivers' language comparisons stated as measured, in whom, and how large; no
  national character.

### 2. Why "no" comes late (~70 min; Tier A, light neutrality pass)

- **Objectives:** identify adjacency pairs and explain why an unanswered first part is noticed;
  predict the shape of a preferred and a dispreferred reply, including the reversal after a
  self-deprecation; explain what a pause before a reply does and does not tell you, with the English
  thresholds and their limits; apply the preference for self-repair to correcting someone in
  conversation.
- **Core ideas:** **adjacency pairs** (Schegloff and Sacks 1973: two utterances, adjacent, different
  speakers, the first constraining the second; question and answer, greeting and greeting, offer and
  acceptance or refusal; a missing second part is heard as missing; *corrected 2026-09-26: the phrase "noticeably absent" is not in Schegloff and Sacks 1973, and the lesson does not attribute it to them*). **Preference** (Pomerantz
  1984, observed in recordings, OCR checked): agreements fill the turn, come fast and plain;
  disagreements are delayed, prefaced ("well", "uh"), partial ("yes, but"); silence and "what?" are
  heard as disagreement not yet stated; after a self-deprecation the preference flips, so "no, you're
  not" comes fast. **The numbers** (Stivers et al. 2009: slower replies went with "no" and non-answers
  in every language, confirmations 100 to 500 ms faster than disconfirmations, significant in 7 of 10;
  Kendrick and Torreira 2015: 195 responses in English phone corpora, "after approximately 700 ms, the
  proportion of dispreferreds was greater than that of preferreds"; Roberts and Francis 2013: n = 380,
  gaps before an identical "yes" from 200 to 1,200 ms, "a notable drop-off in ratings at 600 ms"). The
  speaker acts on silence: Pomerantz's cook example as Kendrick and Torreira reproduce it (a 1.7 s
  silence, then the question revised to "Nothing special?"). **Repair** (Schegloff, Jefferson and Sacks
  1977): self-repair predominates; others initiate ("what?", "who?") and leave the fix; when others
  correct, "they are frequently modulated in form" ("Y'mean X?"), which is also an understanding check
  (a seed for lesson 8); the adult-child exception. **Fast replies** (Templeton et al. 2022: Dartmouth
  undergraduates, same-gender groups, correlational in Studies 1 and 2; outside listeners rated
  shortened gaps as more connected; the "honest signal" as the authors' argument, D9), and the tension
  with advice to pause and think, presented both ways.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why a dispreferred reply is late and wrapped: preference as a structural
  feature of the sequence, not a personal wish (Pomerantz; the self-deprecation reversal is the proof);
  why a listener hears the delay (Kendrick and Torreira: it shifts the odds, it does not decide them).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: an invitation to a Friday film and two replies, "Yes! What time?"
  and "Oh... well... Friday's a bit... I'd love to, but...", each part of the second named. (b)
  wrinkle: a friend says "I'm hopeless at this, aren't I?", and the reader predicts which reply is
  preferred now and what a one-second pause before "no" would convey. Gap: the reader rewrites a blunt
  refusal of a favour into the shape the recordings show, then compares with the checkpoint.
- **Misconceptions:** any pause means no (a probability; most short delays mean nothing); a hedged
  refusal is dishonest (it is the normal shape of a refusal in these recordings); correcting someone
  mid-sentence helps them (the system leaves self-repair first); "reply faster and people will feel
  closer" (Templeton's authors argue the fastest replies are not under conscious control; "fast" is
  locally calibrated, lesson 15); the 600 to 800 ms thresholds apply everywhere (English, lab-rated,
  request acceptances only; Part B §2.3 "keep to").
- **Practice:** three journal entries of dispreferred replies noticed this week (the reader's own
  included), each with its parts marked; a rewrite set of five replies (a refusal, a disagreement, a
  correction of a friend's fact, a reply to a self-deprecation, a reply to "does this look all
  right?"); one "try one move": when you hear a delayed, prefaced reply to a request, offer an easy way
  out, and record what happened. The offer is labelled this course's reading, not a finding.
- **Sources:** synthesis essentials 3; Part B §1.2, §1.3, §1.4, §2.1, §2.3, §2.4, (d) D9, D13
  (Roberts, Francis and Morgan 2006 only as Kendrick and Torreira and Roberts and Francis 2013 describe
  it); Part A §5.3.
- **Connects:** lesson 1 (the 200 ms baseline against which a delay is heard); leads to lesson 8 ("you
  mean X?" as a natural check), lesson 13 (pre-closings are built from the same preference), lesson
  15 (local calibration).
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's statistics (mode, median, mean) and the rule that a figure carries
  its scope.
- **Neutrality:** the thresholds are English-language; the lesson says so where it states them.
- **Cut first if long:** Templeton (to one paragraph); the adult-child repair exception; one of the
  rewrite items.

### 3. Hearing is not listening (~75 min; Tier A, full neutrality pass)

- **Objectives:** explain the difference between hearing and listening, and why listening scholars
  keep several definitions rather than one; identify which purpose a conversation calls for
  (understanding information, judging a claim, supporting a person) and what changes with it; trace a
  stock listening statistic to its source and state what the source can support; explain why your own
  rating of your listening is weak evidence, and what better evidence would be.
- **Core ideas:** **hearing and listening** as every textbook separates them (Part A §1.6, with its
  read level: tables of contents and a 13th-edition publisher outline); Worthington and Bodie's
  history (listening as affective, behavioural and cognitive; "definitions... instead of a single
  definition... are the goal"; lay people mean "a state of interpersonal connection with and presence
  of others"; the ILA's consensus definition, dated 1995 or 1996 by different sources); the staged
  models (Adler's five, Brownell's HURIER six) as teaching schemes, not findings. **Purposes**: the
  textbooks' understanding, judging and supporting (Part A (a)3, G15); Duhigg's practical, emotional
  and social conversations, taught as a heuristic, not a finding; Yeomans, Schweitzer and Brooks's

### 4. How listening goes wrong (~75 min; Tier A, full neutrality pass)

- **Objectives:** identify the named listening failures in described exchanges; classify responses as

### 5. Let them finish: interruption, overlap and silence (~85 min; Tier B)

- **Objectives:** classify an overlap as a continuer, a terminal overlap, help with a word, choral
  talk or an intrusive interruption; explain how conversational style can make the same overlap feel
  engaged to one person and domineering to another; state what the studies of gender and interruption
  found, with their sizes, settings and definitions, and why the question is contested; apply the
  medical findings on interruption and the practitioners' advice on silence to an ordinary
  conversation, saying what transfers and what does not.
- **Core ideas:** **overlap is not one thing** (Schegloff 2000: terminal overlaps, continuers,
  conditional access, choral talk; "the vast majority of overlaps are resolved to a single speaker by
  the third beat"; Levinson and Torreira's 73% backchannel, from lesson 1). **Style** (Tannen 1989,
  her own summary of the 1984 study: two and a half hours of a 1978 Thanksgiving dinner among six
  friends including Tannen; "cooperative overlapping"; high-considerateness speakers who "hesitated,
  faltered, or stopped, feeling interrupted and, more to the point, dominated"; qualitative, not a
  cultural map; the ethnic detail given as Tannen gives it, with her own qualifications, or left out).
  **Gender** (contested 7): Zimmerman and West 1975 (31 segments, 11 cross-sex, 48 interruptions, 46
  by men in the table and "98%" in the text, one segment holding 13, some recorded without prior
  consent; G4); West and Zimmerman 1983's 75% as Tannen reports it; Anderson and Leaper 1998 (43
  studies; d = .15 overall, negligible; .33 for intrusive interruptions; larger among strangers, in
  unstructured talk and the three naturalistic studies; mixed-gender d = .11 against same-gender .24;
  women first authors .54 against men first authors -.21, non-significant; the group-size figure
  omitted, D2); Tannen's view that counting overlap measures the wrong thing. The accurate summary: a
  small average difference for floor-taking interruptions that varies strongly with setting. **The
  stopwatch** (setting stated each time, decision 10): Singh Ospina et al. 2019 (112 recorded visits;
  agenda elicited in 36%; interrupted in 27 of 40 after a median of 11 seconds; uninterrupted patients
  took a median of 6 s; the authors' counter-view quoted); Langewitz et al. 2002 (335 patients,
  doctors trained to wait; mean 92 s, median 59 s, 78% finished within two minutes; seven over five
  minutes, all judged important; "Doctors do not risk being swamped"); the older 18 to 23 s figures
  only as those papers report them (G5). **Silence as a tool** (practitioner advice, decision 9):
  Samaritans ("you don't have to fill every silence"), R U OK? ("sit patiently with the silence"),
  Calgary-Cambridge item 10 ("leaving space for patient to think"), the Smithsonian's "Don't worry
  about silences", Poynter's "Don't break the silence with a new question", Essential Partners' pauses.
  **Stammering** (STAMMA: "Try not to do this", on finishing sentences; do not say "slow down" or
  "relax"): the plainest case where a cooperative habit is experienced as intrusion.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why an intrusive interruption costs more than its seconds (it takes the
  floor mid-unit, which is how Anderson and Leaper's third level defines it; in the clinics studied,
  interrupted patients' opening lists went unfinished, and the older figure for how rarely they got
  back to them is Beckman and Frankel's, gated, G5); why silence helps
  (the speaker gets the transition place back; Essential Partners' account that listeners concentrate
  better when a pause is coming, labelled practitioner).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a patient's opening in a clinic, stopped at 11 seconds, and what
  Langewitz's numbers say it would have cost to let her finish, with the setting named; then the
  course's reading of what transfers to a friend's story. (b) wrinkle: two colleagues, one who
  overlaps with enthusiastic "yes, and" and one who goes quiet when overlapped, each reading the other
  wrongly; and a third who stammers, whose sentences the first keeps finishing. Gap: the reader
  classifies four overlaps in a short transcript before the checkpoint.
- **Misconceptions:** "men interrupt women far more" as settled (contested; the meta-analytic
  picture); overlap is interruption, and interruption is always rude; a pause means they've finished;
  "if I let them talk they'll go on forever" (Langewitz); finishing someone's sentence helps; the
  Italians and the Japanese as overlap cultures (Stivers, from lesson 1).
- **Practice:** "try one move": in two ordinary conversations, when the other person stops, wait a
  breath before replying, and write down whether they went on; a journal entry classifying three
  overlaps the reader was part of; an analysis of one public recorded panel discussion, counting
  intrusive interruptions by speaker and saying what the count can't show (Tannen's point).
- **Images:** SVG bar chart of Anderson and Leaper's effects (overall .15; intrusive .33; mixed-gender
  .11; same-gender .24), with "negligible" and "small" bands labelled as Cohen's conventions, and the
  caption naming 43 studies of adults, mostly middle-class European American samples.
- **Sources:** synthesis essentials 7, misconceptions (gender, overlap); Part B §1.5, §3.1 to §3.3,
  (b)2 and 3, (c) C2, C3, (d) D1, D2, D14; Part E §2.1, §2.2, §6.2, §6.7, (a)1 and 2, (d)1 and 2;
  Part E §5.2, §5.4.
- **Connects:** lesson 1 (overlap statistics); lesson 4 (stage hogging); Personal Safety lesson 8,
  "Talking it down", for talk with someone threatening, which this lesson does not cover; leads to
  lesson 6 (a question asked into the space), lesson 15 (style and culture, stammering recalled).
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's rules 1a to 1c (an interruption is a self-selection at a place that

### 6. Asking better questions (~80 min; Tier A, light neutrality pass)

- **Objectives:** explain what the question-asking experiments found about follow-up questions and
  liking, including what the askers expected and what onlookers thought; classify questions as
  follow-up, full-switch, mirror, introductory, partial-switch or rhetorical, and as open or closed;
  rewrite a counterfeit, leading, stacked or "any" question into one that invites a real answer;
  decide when not to ask, and explain why asking too many questions can fail.
- **Core ideas:** **Huang et al. 2017** (one group's programme, audited, decision 17; statistics from
  the corrected results, G2): Study 1, 199 online text-chat pairs of strangers, "at least nine" or "at
  most four" questions; partners liked high askers more (d about .35), through perceived
  responsiveness; Study 2A replicated (about .27); askers did not predict it (r = -.02); outside
  observers did not like high askers more; follow-up questions carried the effect, full-switch
  questions went with lower responsiveness; the question types' shares (40.51% follow-up...); the
  speed-dating data correlational (4.51 follow-ups per person per date); the authors' own boundary,
  too many questions without reciprocal disclosure. Mental Fitness lesson 2's table for what .35
  means (about 64%, the course's arithmetic). Brooks and John 2018: "too many questions can also
  create a stilted dynamic" [V]. **Open and closed** (Samaritans' "what's been going on for you this
  week?" for "have you had a bad week?"; NHS's how, what, where, when; Headlee's "What was that like?"
  for "Were you terrified?"; StoryCorps' yes/no items that open a story in context, as the caution
  against rigid rules). **One at a time** (Smithsonian; Poynter's "This isn't multiple choice").
  **"Why"** (the FBI model avoids "why" as "interrogatory"; Acas's open-question example is "Why did
  you leave?"): a real disagreement between traditions, left as one. **"Some" and "any"** (Heritage
  et al. 2007, single study, setting named: 20 family physicians, 224 visits; 90.3% against 53.1%;
  unmet concerns cut from 37% to 9%; visit length unchanged; the authors' negative-polarity mechanism;
  "may generalize" to "Do you have any questions?", the authors' suggestion, not a finding). **When a
  question isn't one** (Adler's counterfeit questions from the 13th-edition outline: questions that
  trap, tag questions, statements in disguise, hidden agendas, questions seeking a "correct" answer,
  unchecked assumptions; Acas on leading and multiple questions; Essential Partners' "a challenge in
  disguise"). **When not to ask** (Gordon's roadblock 11, "questioning, probing", as practitioner lore
  in tension with Huang, Part C §6.5; the review's point that interrogating questions backfire, G14;
  the right to refuse an answer, the Oral History Association).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why follow-ups work where switches don't: they show the asker heard,
  understood and cares (responsiveness, the authors' mediator); why "any" suppresses answers (the
  authors' account: "any" signals an expected "no").
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a returning traveller; "Did you have a good holiday?", then "What
  was the best part?", then a follow-up on the answer, with each question typed and the full-switch
  alternative shown. (b) wrinkle: Heritage's clinic and then a team meeting that ends "Any questions?"
  to silence: the reader is shown what the study did and did not test, and the lesson leaves the
  meeting version as the authors left it, an open suggestion. Gap: the reader types six questions from

- **Misconceptions:** to be liked, talk about interesting things about yourself (Huang; lesson 4's
  Tamir and Mitchell); more questions are always better; open questions are always better than closed
  (StoryCorps; teach-back in lesson 8 wants specific ones); "Any questions?" invites questions;
  people know that asking makes them liked (they didn't predict it).
- **Practice:** a rewrite set of eight questions (a trap, a tag, a stacked pair, a leading one, an
  "any", a "why" to a grieving friend, a closed opener, a switch where a follow-up fits); "try one
  move": in one ordinary conversation, ask two follow-ups on what the other just said, and journal
  what happened.
- **Images:** SVG bar of Heritage's two conditions (90.3%, 53.1%), captioned with the study's sample
  and setting.
- **Sources:** synthesis finding 5, essentials 8; Part A §1.1; Part B §4.1, §4.2, (b)6, (d) D10, D11;
  Part C §6.5, §2.5 (Puzzle 7, G14); Part E §1.1, §1.7, §2.3, §4.1, §4.4, §5.1 to §5.4, §6.2, (a)3,
  (b)5, (d)3.

### 7. Ask, don't guess (~75 min; Tier A, full neutrality pass)

- **Objectives:** explain what the perspective-taking experiments found about accuracy, and why
  asking beat imagining; predict how much of a feeling you are showing, using the illusion of
  transparency, and say what follows for the speaker and the listener; explain what empathic accuracy
  is measured as and what raises it; compare the positions on whether empathy can be taught and
  whether more of it makes better support.
- **Core ideas:** **perspective taking and perspective getting** (Eyal, Steffel and Epley 2018, one
  group's programme of 25 experiments: imagining another's view made people slightly less accurate
  overall, d = -0.23, significant in only 4 of 17 comparisons in 1 to 15 and not reliable across
  replications; no gain in predicting partners' preferences; pretest participants expected it to
  help; in Experiment 25, 104 couples, asking the partner raised accuracy sharply, d = 1.85 in the
  full-information condition; "Increasing interpersonal accuracy seems to require gaining new
  information rather than utilizing existing knowledge about another person" [V]). **The illusion of
  transparency** (Gilovich, Savitsky and Medvec 1998: liars expected 48.8% of observers to catch them,
  25.6% did, about chance; foul-drink tasters expected 4.91 of 10 to see their disgust, 3.56 did;
  observers "performed no better than chance" on disgust; corrected 2026-09-26, see SOURCES "Gate
  closures, lesson 07"). **Empathic
  accuracy** (Ickes 1993: a similarity score between a person's listed thoughts and another's
  inferences, not a percentage of thoughts read; male friends 29.8 against strangers 19.9, from
  shared knowledge; feedback on the other's actual thoughts "accelerated the rate" of improvement;
  self-rated empathy did not predict accuracy). **Empathy as a default and a choice** (McAuliffe et al.
  2020: "imagine" instructions did not raise empathic concern above no instructions, g = .08, while
  "remain objective" lowered it, g = .45: people empathise by default; Zaki: empathy is "a choice that
  we make" [V]; Weisz et al. 2021, abstract, 292 first-year students: a malleable-mindset intervention
  raised accuracy for others' positive emotions and friends reported eight weeks later). **Can it be
  taught** (Teding van Berkhout and Malouff 2016, abstract: 18 RCTs, g = 0.63, 0.51 after
  trim-and-fill, longest follow-up six months, not in everyday relationships; the digital-training
  contrast not used, decision 15). **Is more better** (contested 12, partly value): Bloom 2017's four
  senses (cognitive empathy, contagion, emotional empathy, compassion) and his case against emotional
  empathy as a "spotlight"; Zaki's reply that empathy can be widened; Bloom's agreement that it is
  flexible and his worry that flexibility makes it manipulable. Understanding People owns the rest.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why imagining fails: it swaps egocentric projection for other guesses that
  are no more accurate (Eyal et al.'s own finding); why asking works: it adds information the guesser
  did not have; why feedback helps accuracy (Ickes), which is the reason lesson 8's check exists.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: two flatmates predicting each other's answers to five preference
  questions, once by imagining and once after asking, set out like Experiment 25 with its result. (b)
  wrinkle: the speaker's side: someone sure their annoyance in a meeting was obvious, and the
  colleagues who saw nothing; what Gilovich's numbers say, what the speaker should do (say it), and
  what the listener should not assume (that no sign means no feeling). Gap: the reader predicts which
  condition won before the predict block opens.
- **Misconceptions:** put yourself in their shoes and you'll know what they think; "I can tell how
  people feel" (Ickes); "my feelings are obvious"; empathy is fixed, some people just have it; more
  empathy always makes better support (preview of lesson 10, where Bloom's Box 3 returns).
- **Practice:** a perspective-getting exercise with a willing friend: write predictions of their
  answers to five questions (a favourite meal, how they'd spend a free Saturday...), then ask, and
  score (decision 1); a journal entry on one moment this week when the reader assumed what someone
  felt, and what asking would have cost.
- **Sources:** synthesis finding 9, essentials 11; Part C §7.1 to §7.7, (a)4 and 6, (b)4, 5, 7, 8,
  (c) C3, (d)6, 10; Part C §5 (measures converge weakly).
- **Connects:** lesson 6 (the question as the tool); lesson 3 (self-ratings); leads to lesson 8 (the
  check that supplies the feedback Ickes found helps), lesson 10 (Bloom on support), lesson 14 (the
  face and the lie), lesson 15 ("ask how they want to be spoken with").
- **Spaced review of:** Mental Fitness lesson 2 (d and g); lesson 3's point that self-report is weak
  evidence of listening.
- **Neutrality:** the Bloom and Zaki debate in each author's own words (Bloom 2017 read in full; Zaki
  on Edge read in full); neither called right; Bloom not described as "against caring" (Part C "may
  not say").
- **Cut first if long:** McAuliffe (to one sentence); Weisz (to one sentence); the Ickes therapy-tape
  study.

### 8. Saying it back (~75 min; Tier A, light neutrality pass)

- **Objectives:** explain Rogers and Farson's idea of listening for total meaning and their test of
  understanding, and why they said technique without the attitude fails; rewrite a statement as a
  simple and as a complex reflection, and name a feeling at the right intensity; apply a check of
  understanding in both directions: restating what you heard until the speaker agrees, and using
  teach-back to check what you explained; compare the practitioners' shared advice with the dissent
  against performed attention.
- **Core ideas:** **Rogers and Farson** (the 1987 excerpt of the 1957 booklet, read in full; an essay
  for managers, not a study): "total meaning", content plus feeling, with the machinist "finally
  finished with that damned lathe setup"; respond to feelings; note all cues; understanding tested by
  "reflecting in your own words what the speaker seems to mean", to the other's "satisfaction" (the
  source's "sic" kept or the phrase paraphrased); the ground rule of restating before replying;
  technique without the attitude "will be empty and sterile" [V]; judgment "whether critical or
  favorable" and reassurance both block. **Rogers 1957**: reflecting feelings "is by no means an
  essential condition" [V]; "Feeling may be 'reflected' in a way which communicates the therapist's
  lack of empathy" [V]. **Reflections** (SAMHSA TIP 35, citing Miller and Rollnick 2013, G-note:
  decision 9's clinical label): a reflection as "your best guess"; simple and complex, with TIP 35's
  "nagging" and "pressuring" pair (both are simple reflections in Exhibit 3.6, not a simple and a
  complex; corrected at the lesson 08 review, 2026-09-26); the fixing reflex saved for lesson 10. **Naming a feeling**
  (VitalTalk's NURSE, oncology: "turn down the intensity a notch"; "stop short of suggesting you
  understand everything (you don't)"; the FBI model's emotional labelling: even a wrong label shows
  effort, as the practitioners claim). **The practitioners agree** (Part E (a)4: SHUSH "Say it back";
  988 "Summarize What You Hear"; R U OK? "ask if you have understood them properly"; Calgary-Cambridge
  item 14, which "invites patient to correct"; PON's "Have I captured your main points?"; the natural
  "Y'mean X?" of lesson 2). **Teach-back, the other direction** (AHRQ Tool 5: "you are checking how
  well you explained something, not testing the patient"; "Do you understand?" and "Does that make
  sense?" are "NOT teach-back questions"; "If they parrot your words back to you, they may not have
  understood"; chunk and check; VitalTalk's "Does that make sense...?" as the small, real
  contradiction between two clinical traditions; Talevski 2020 is on the unread line, so the evidence
  sentence is AHRQ's own framing or left out). **The dissent** (Headlee: performed attention "is
  crap" [V], with her "There is no reason..." line if lesson 4 didn't use it; PON: active listening
  "does not mean sitting patiently", "Nor does it simply involve nodding" [V]).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why a restatement is a check rather than a courtesy: it gives the speaker
  something to correct, which is the feedback lesson 7 showed raises accuracy (Ickes); a correction is
  the check working, which is this course's reading of Rogers' "to the other's satisfaction".
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a friend says, "I've rewritten this report three times and she

### 9. Does active listening work? (~85 min; Tier B)

- **Objectives:** explain why "active listening" has meant different things in different studies,
  and why results do not carry between definitions; compare what the paraphrase experiments and the
  high-quality listening experiments found, including what each compared against; state what the
  four-lab test and the workplace meta-analysis add, and what a correlation of .39 does and does not
  mean; explain the Gottman controversy over active listening in conflict, in both sides' terms.
- **Core ideas:** **four definitions** (Part C §5: paraphrase only; paraphrase plus questions,
  feeling reflections, checks and immediacy; any warm response to a partner's negativity; the
  speaker's perception of attentive, empathic, non-judgmental listening). **The paraphrase
  experiments** (G1; read levels in the sentence): Weger, Castle and Emmett 2010 (180 undergraduates,
  paraphrase against simple acknowledgement: the listener seemed more likeable, the speaker did not
  feel more understood or more satisfied); Weger et al. 2014 (115 people, strangers, active listening
  against advice against acknowledgement: felt more understood with active listening; advice as
  satisfying; small effects; trust the abstract over the REACH summary's contradiction); Bodie et al.
  2015 (full text: 41 trained listeners against 130 untrained, undergraduates' real upsetting
  problems; small effects on felt emotional awareness and on feeling better, R² = .14 and .13; nothing
  for problem-solving; "not a panacea" [V]; seven of eight confederates female). **The high-quality
  listening programme** (one group's programme, several preregistered): Itzchakov, Kluger and Castro
  2017 and Itzchakov, DeMarree et al. 2018 (abstracts: less social anxiety, clearer and less extreme
  attitudes); the HBR studies (112 undergraduates, distracted against undistracted; 114 business
  undergraduates, good, moderate or poor listeners; three field studies of 180 workers); Itzchakov et
  al. 2024 (disagreement: less polarisation); **the contrast is usually a deliberately distracted
  listener** (decision 4). **The four-lab test** (DeMarree et al. 2026, preprint, abstract: Israel,
  Singapore, the UK and the US, N = 1,407: telling one person to listen to learn "did not influence
  perceived listening quality or polarization outcomes in any sample"; the correlational path held in
  all four). **The meta-analysis** (Kluger et al. 2024, abstract unless G1 closes: 664 effect sizes;
  perceived listening and work outcomes r̄ = .39; "75% of the literature relied on self-reports";
  without self-report-only data, listening and performance r̄ = .21; correlational). **The
  reconciliation**, SOURCES' own: the positive programme manipulates the whole stance (attention
  against distraction), the sceptical studies isolate the technique, and both can be true; the field's
  review calls paraphrase "a risky intervention" and the field "conceptually fragmented" (Itzchakov
  and Bodie 2026). **Conflict** (contested 2): Gottman et al. 1998 (130 newlywed couples; active
  listening sequences about 4 seconds of 900; "hardly ever occurred" and did not predict outcomes;

  Stanley, Bradbury and Markman 2000 (abstract: "premature at best"; correlational data cannot test a
  training method); Gottman's own later blueprint, which asks each partner to state the other's
  position "to their partner's satisfaction" [V]; no claim that he recanted (Part C gate 4); Kim,
  Capaldi and Crosby 2007's failed replication of Gottman's models, which did not test the active
  listening component. Detail belongs to Relationships and Family and Conflict Resolution.

  listening" and "paraphrase beats acknowledgement" are different claims, and only one of them is
  about a technique (Mental Fitness lesson 2's lesson, applied); why an instruction may fail where a
  stance succeeds (the four-lab result against the trained-listener studies, the course's reading of
  SOURCES' reconciliation).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: Weger 2010 read as a design: who, what was compared, what was
  measured, what one can conclude, and what one can't (anything about emotional topics; the topic was

- **Misconceptions:** paraphrasing always makes people feel understood (Weger 2010); happy couples

- **Practice:** a claim to evaluate, written by the drafter and labelled as invented ("a two-hour

### 10. When someone brings you a problem (~85 min; Tier A, full neutrality pass; crisis callout)

- **Objectives:** rank supportive responses by how far they acknowledge and legitimise the other's
  feelings, and rewrite a low-ranked one; explain when advice helps and when it backfires, using what
  the advice studies found about solicitation, politeness, feasibility and timing; explain how help
  that implies incompetence can make stress worse; apply Ask-Offer-Ask or a support-first sequence to
  a described conversation.
- **Core ideas:** **person-centred support** (Burleson's nine levels, as Jones, Bodie et al. 2018
  describe them: condemning or challenging the feelings at the bottom, distracting and explaining in
  the middle, explicitly acknowledging, elaborating and helping someone see how the feelings fit a
  wider context at the top; High and Dillard 2012, abstract: across 23 studies, more person-centred
  messages are rated more effective and do more good, the effect weaker with trained strangers, G1
  fallback for sizes). **Advice** (contested 4; MacGeorge, Feng and Guntzviller 2016: unsolicited
  advice threatens face and is evaluated worse; content (effective, feasible, few drawbacks) drives
  use, politeness drives perceived quality; MacGeorge et al. 2016 with 244 students and their friends:
  adviser characteristics had no direct effect, and an "unexpectedly large percentage" received no
  advice; Feng 2014, vignettes, 572 US and 540 mainland Chinese participants: advice after emotional
  support and problem inquiry rated best in both, Americans valuing the preliminaries more; Feng and

  (Bolger, Zuckerman and Kessler 2000: 68 couples, one partner facing the New York bar exam; support

  guiding and directing; Samaritans' three withholdings; 988's "do not offer glib reassurance";

- **Mechanism to explain:** why unsolicited advice lands badly (face threat and the implied message of

  why sequence matters (Feng's support, then inquiry, then advice).

### 12. Strangers, small talk and going deeper (~80 min; Tier A, full neutrality pass)

- **Objectives:** explain what the experiments with commuters and strangers found about what people
  expect from talking to strangers and what happens, and where the barrier lies; explain the
  deep-talk experiments' finding and its proposed reason; state what the small-talk study found, what
  its replication found, and what that teaches about single findings; identify who was studied in
  each, and what that limits.
- **Core ideas:** **undersociality** (Epley and Schroeder 2014, nine experiments, Chicago trains, buses,
  taxis and a waiting room: commuters told to talk had a more positive commute, no less productive;
  others predicted the opposite; the mistaken preference "stems partly from underestimating others'
  interest in connecting" [V]; cab riders who talked routinely were calibrated. Schroeder, Lyons and
  Epley 2022, London rail, 383 completed: talking was better, but these commuters expected that; the
  barrier was starting. Kardas, Schroeder and O'Brien 2022, five preregistered experiments, N = 1,093:
  people expected enjoyment to fall as a conversation went on; it held or rose. Atir, Wald and Epley
  2022, abstract: people learned more than they expected. Epley et al. 2022's name for the pattern).
  **Depth** (Kardas, Kumar and Epley 2022, twelve experiments, US samples: people overestimated
  awkwardness and underestimated connection, more for deep talk, d = 2.23 against 0.55 in Experiment
  2; the proposed reason, expecting others to care less about your disclosures; within-person, deep
  partners felt closer and preferences shifted, d = .60; changing expectations changed topic choice).
  **The small-talk finding** (Mehl et al. 2010: 79 undergraduates, the EAR recorder, happiest had a
  third as much small talk; Milek et al. 2018: four samples, N = 486, small talk unrelated to life
  satisfaction, r = .05, while substantive conversation still went with it, r = .23; "The failure to
  replicate the original small-talk effect is theoretically and practically important" [V]; all
  correlational). **The 36 questions** (Aron et al. 1997, abstract: gradually escalating disclosure
  produced more closeness than small-talk tasks in a lab; not "fall in love"; G20). **What people talk
  about** (Szala et al. 2025: about 85% social in 535 Polish conversations; Dunbar's two thirds as they
  describe it; "gossip" in the broad sense). **Compliments and thanks** get one line (Kumar and Epley
  2018; Zhao and Epley 2021) and are Social Skills'. **Limits** (open concern 2): one network of labs,
  US and UK samples, WEIRD; the one independent direct replication is Liu et al.'s Study 4a through
  Transparent Replications (decision 15); privacy norms differ, which is partly a value question.
  The course's line, SOURCES' own: small talk and depth are not rivals, and people in these studies
  tended to stop short of the depth they would have enjoyed.
- **Mechanism to explain:** the proposed mechanism across the programme: people underestimate others'
  interest in them (the authors'; tested by manipulating expectations in Kardas 7a and 7b); why small
  talk may be the road to depth rather than its opposite (Aron's escalation; openings as necessary
  work, lesson 13's pre-closings in reverse), labelled as SOURCES' reading.

  after 2010 should conclude. (b) wrinkle: a commuter who dreads starting and a stranger who would
  rather read; the London finding that the barrier is the start; and what the evidence does not
  license (talking to anyone who doesn't want to, or going deep with someone who has signalled no,
  lesson 2's dispreferred shapes). Gap: the reader predicts Kardas's expected and actual awkwardness
  before the predict block.
- **Misconceptions:** small talk makes you unhappy; strangers don't want to be talked to; deep
  questions will be awkward; the 36 questions make people fall in love; conversations get duller the
  longer they go; these findings describe everyone everywhere.

- **Connects:** lesson 4 (Tamir and Mitchell); lesson 6 (questions as the way in); Personal Safety

### 13. How it ends, and how it went (~70 min; Tier A, light neutrality pass)

- **Objectives:** state what the study of conversation endings found about how often conversations
  end when both people want, and how far off the mismatch runs; explain how pre-closings let a
  conversation end without either person refusing the other, and apply them; state what the
  liking-gap studies found, for whom it is largest and how long it lasted; explain why both findings
  are the same kind of misjudgement, and what they do and do not license.
- **Core ideas:** **endings** (Mastroianni, Gilbert, Cooney and Wilson 2021; D3). Study 1: 806 online
  participants recalling their latest conversation, mostly with intimates; 66.51% felt ready to end at
  some point; on average people wished it had been 24.08% longer, yet the absolute gap averaged 56.01%
  of its length. Study 2: 252 strangers in 126 pairs in a lab; 1.59% ended when both wanted; 29.37%
  when one did; in 46.83% both wanted to end sooner, in 9.52% both wanted longer; guesses of when the
  partner wanted to stop were off by 63.84% of the conversation. Never "15.6%". The authors'
  "coordination problem". **Pre-closings** (Schegloff and Sacks 1973, observed in recordings): a
  conversation is closed, not stopped; "well", "okay" at a topic's end offer the other a turn to raise
  something new; the terminal exchange. Built from lesson 2's preference: a pre-closing lets the other
  person decline to close without anyone saying no. **The liking gap** (Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom and
  Clark 2018): after first conversations people underestimated how much the partner liked them; the
  gap was 1.14 points for the shyest, 0.55 average, 0.25 (not significant) for the least shy; it held
  for short and long conversations; coders could see the liking signals participants missed; the
  mediator, harsher thoughts about one's own performance; among 102 Yale suitemates it lasted "at all
  time points except for the final one" [V], so "for several months" (D4); Mastroianni, Cooney,
  Boothby and Reece 2021 found it in groups and teams, the same research group. No independent
  replication cited (decision 15).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why nobody says "I'm ready to go": both findings are about information
  each person hides to be kind (the authors' "coordination problem"); the liking gap's mediator is the
  one Boothby tested (self-critical thoughts), and the course gives no other.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: the end of a phone call between two friends, marked up: a topic
  winds down, a pre-closing ("Right, well..."), an offered turn, one new topic raised and answered, a
  second pre-closing, the terminal exchange. (b) wrinkle: after a first long chat with a new
  neighbour, someone replays their own awkward moments and concludes it went badly: the liking gap
  says the neighbour probably liked them more than they think, and the lesson also says what it does
  not say (it is an average; it doesn't tell you this neighbour's mind; lesson 7's "ask, don't guess"
  still applies). Gap: from Mastroianni's figures, the reader works out, for 100 conversations like
  Study 2's, how many ended when both wanted, how many when one did, and how many overran for both,
  before the checkpoint.
- **Misconceptions:** they'll tell you when they want to go; everyone wants out early (in Study 1 the
  average person wanted more); "they didn't like me much"; the liking gap means everyone liked you;
  ending a conversation first is rude (a pre-closing is the ordinary way).
- **Practice:** journal the next five endings the reader is part of: who pre-closed, how, and whether
  a new topic came in; "try one move": offer an honest pre-closing earlier than usual in one
  conversation you'd like to end, and see what the other person does with it.
- **Images:** SVG of Mastroianni's Study 2 (both wanted and did 1.59%; one wanted 29.37%; both wanted

### 14. What body language can and cannot tell you (~85 min; Tier B)

- **Objectives:** explain where the 7-38-55 figures came from and when their author says they apply;
  state what the deception research found about detecting lies from behaviour, including experts,
  eye contact and microexpressions, and what does better; compare the positions on reading emotion
  from faces, and state what thin slices can and cannot predict; evaluate claims about mirroring and
  power posing against their replications.
- **Core ideas:** **Mehrabian** (his own page, quoted: "Total Liking = 7% Verbal Liking + 38% Vocal
  Liking + 55% Facial Liking" and "Unless a communicator is talking about their feelings or attitudes,
  these equations are not applicable."; two small 1967 laboratory studies of single spoken words and
  posed photographs, G6; Lapakko 1997's abstract on applications that "overlook important
  limitations"; Zenger and Folkman 2016's "80%" as a specimen of the myth in a business magazine).
  **Lies** (Bond and DePaulo 2006, a meta-analysis of 206 documents and 24,483 judges: about 54%,
  47.55% of lies and 61.34% of truths judged correctly, a truth bias; video-only worse; experts no
  better, d = -.025; Bond and DePaulo 2008, abstract: "the best judges are no more accurate than a
  stochastic mechanism would produce" [V]; DePaulo et al. 2003: 158 cues, median |d| = 0.10, eye
  contact d = 0.01, gaze aversion d = 0.03, "none of the measures of looking behavior supported the
  widespread belief" [V]; the Global Deception Research Team 2006: gaze aversion named by 63.66% in 58
  countries, the top belief in 51, expressed "in every one of the 75 countries we studied" [V];
  Hartwig and Bond 2011, abstract: people "rarely rely on the wrong cues", the cues are weak; Luke et
  al. 2023: the one item 80% of experts agreed on is that gaze aversion is not generally diagnostic;
  microexpressions in 2% of expressions and in truth tellers too (Porter and ten Brinke 2008, via Vrij
  et al. 2019); Ekman's METT no better than a bogus training in Jordan et al. 2019, abstract, N = 90;
  NLP eye cues tested three ways by Wiseman et al. 2012, nothing; SPOT and the Reid manual's eye-contact
  line through Denault et al. 2020, with GAO as they and Vrij report it; what does better: content and
  questions, Vrij et al.'s verbal cues averaging d = 0.30, Hauch et al.'s content-cue training,
  Levine's truth-default theory at its abstract). **Faces** (contested 9, one section, Understanding
  People owns the theory: Barrett et al. 2019, expected configurations in about 19% of emotional
  events, r = .32, "a scowling facial configuration is an expression of anger, but not the expression
  of anger" [V]; Keltner et al. 2019, "upwards of 20 emotions" in multimodal patterns; Elfenbein and
  Ambady 2002's above-chance recognition across cultures; the common ground both sides state: faces
  carry social information, context matters). **Thin slices** (Ambady and Rosenthal 1992: r = .39
  across 38 results; under half a minute did as well as five; not a verdict on a person). **Gestures**
  ("few, if any, body movements that have invariant meaning", Harrigan as quoted by Denault et al.; no
  study of crossed arms found). **Mimicry** (Chartrand and Bargh 1999, abstract; Hale and Hamilton
  2016's preregistered null, N = 40, and their review's "may be fragile"). **Power posing** (Carney,
  Cuddy and Yap 2010, N = 42; Ranehill et al. 2015, N = 200: felt power yes, hormones and behaviour
  no; Carney's own "I do not believe that 'power pose' effects are real" [V]; Simmons and Simonsohn

  g = 0.45, expansive versus neutral 0.06).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why demeanour fails: the cues barely differ between liars and truth
  tellers (Hartwig and Bond's conclusion: not bad observers, weak signals); why a "lie cue" misleads

  rate labelled as the course's arithmetic); why content and questions help (liars and truth tellers

- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: the formula applied to "Your train leaves at 6:10 from platform 4"

  candidates lie and looking away is equally common in liars and truth tellers, how many of those who
  look away are lying (the course's arithmetic); then what would help (ask for checkable detail).
  Gap: the reader does the natural-frequency count before the checkpoint.
- **Misconceptions:** 93% of communication is nonverbal; liars avoid eye contact; looking up and to the

- **Practice:** a consensual "two truths and a lie" round with friends or family, keeping score of

### 15. Different habits: culture, autism, age and hearing (~85 min; Tier B)

- **Objectives:** explain how cultures differ in turn timing and in how a listener shows attention,
  with what was measured, in whom and how large; explain why national labels ("high-context", a
  country's score) are weak guides to the person in front of you; compare the two readings of
  communication difficulty between autistic and non-autistic people, including the 2025 registered

  physiology, but Japanese participants rated direct gaze as angrier and less approachable; Hessels

### 16. Listening to someone who disagrees with you (~85 min; Tier B)

- **Objectives:** explain why acknowledging a view is not agreeing with it, and rewrite an

  judged by the reader's own journal, with the limits said.

## Neutrality notes

The course is a standards 3.4 domain. Its failure modes are not ideological in the usual sense; they
are **one population speaking for humanity, one profession's rule of thumb taught as a finding, a
contested question closed in the direction that suits a skills course, and identity-laden groups
(women and men, cultures, autistic people, older and disabled people, partisans) described in terms
they would not recognise**. Every reviewer checks: every finding carries its population and setting
(decision 3); every effect its comparison (decision 4); every practitioner model its profession and
goal (decision 9); every contested question both sides at full strength with no verdict (decision
12); decision 13's language; and the count test (citations, last words, whose errors are named) on
every contested block.

- **Gender and interruption** (lesson 5). Contested empirical. Dominance, style and timing views each
  in their own terms; Zimmerman and West as a pioneering, small study; Anderson and Leaper's sizes and
  moderators; no claim about individuals.
- **Culture** (lessons 1, 2, 15). Largely established that the turn-taking system is shared with small
  felt variation; contested how far variation goes and whether national typologies help. What was
  measured, in whom, how large; no national character; the typologies' own case said to be unread.
- **Autism** (lesson 15). Contested empirical with a value dimension. The deficit and double-empathy
  readings each in proponents' terms; 2020 and 2025 together; autistic people's own accounts
  (Trevisan) quoted as their accounts; decision 13's language.
- **Political dialogue** (lesson 16). Contested empirical on effects; value on purpose. No side on any
  issue; organisations in their own words; the retraction as the notice gives it; example topics
  without party signal.
- **Suicide** (lesson 11). Established for the settings studied; untested for friends; the services'
  advice as theirs; the cautious position at its strongest; nothing that assesses risk.
- **Active listening and the couples controversy** (lesson 9). Contested empirical on both questions;
  Gottman and PREP each in their own terms; Gottman's later blueprint reported, no recantation
  claimed.
- **Empathy** (lesson 7), **advice** (lesson 10), **depth** (lesson 12), **second stories** (lessons 4
  and 10), **listening as technique** (lesson 3), **faces and lie detection** (lesson 14): each
  classified as SOURCES classifies it, both positions given, the value element named as value.
- **Popular authors** (lessons 3, 4, 14, 17). Headlee, Murphy, Duhigg, Carnegie, Nichols and Straus,
  Zenger and Folkman: their arguments stated as they would state them, credited where the evidence
  agrees, no sneering.
- **Integrity** (lessons 6, 16). The Gino co-authorships handled by decision 17 and gate G3; nothing
  said about any researcher beyond what a primary record shows.
- **Religion.** Nothing in SOURCES bears on any tradition; the course makes no claim either way.

The course-wide audit (decision 14) reads across all seventeen lessons for how often the Epley
network, the Itzchakov and Kluger programme and US undergraduates speak for "people", and whether
the misconception lists over-represent one side of any contested question.

## What downstream courses will need from this one

**Public Speaking** can assume the Mehrabian correction and the lie-detection evidence (lesson 14),
and links back rather than reteaching them; it owns delivery and critical listening to a speech.
**Understanding People: Psychology Fundamentals** can assume effect sizes read with their comparison
(through Mental Fitness lesson 2 and this course), the perspective-taking and illusion-of-transparency
findings, the face debate at the level of "can you read a face?", and the replication stories of
small talk, power posing and mimicry as cases; it owns emotion theory, empathy's psychology in full
and the replication crisis as a subject. **Social Skills and Etiquette** can assume the stranger,
depth, liking-gap and endings findings and the question-asking evidence, and builds its occasions and
manners on them without repeating them. **Conflict Resolution** can assume acknowledgement is not
agreement, restating to the other's satisfaction, receptive language (if G3 closed), the structured
dialogue practices, and the Gottman controversy in outline; it owns interests and positions,
*Difficult Conversations*, mediation and de-escalation beyond Personal Safety lesson 8. **Relationships
and Family** can assume person-centred support, advice timing, invisible support and the active
listening controversy; it owns Gottman's research in full and parenting. **Cognitive Biases and Clear
Thinking** can assume the liking gap, the endings mismatch, undersociality and the illusion of
transparency as worked examples, and links back. **Persuasion and Influence** and **Negotiation**
inherit the course's line that listening to understand and listening to influence are different goals,
and take the FBI stairway's end and Voss from there. **Teaching and Mentoring** can assume teach-back
as a check of one's own explanation. **Introduction to Psychology courses later on the Core**, and any
statistics course, can use lesson 9 and lesson 12 as cases of a finding, its comparison and its
replication.
