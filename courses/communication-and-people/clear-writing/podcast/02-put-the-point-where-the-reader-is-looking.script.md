S1: And it opens by doing something to you rather than telling you something. A paragraph with no title, read once at normal speed. Deal with everything in the space before you start, because afterwards it is far harder. Take out whatever will come out, and protect whatever will not. Attend to any irregularities early: they will be much more obvious at the end than they are now. Always work downward. Use less material than feels right, since two light passes give a better result than one heavy one. Wait properly between passes.

S2: I understood every word of that and I have no idea what it was about.

S1: Which is exactly the lesson's line. Now the title is, painting a room.

S2: Oh. Take out whatever will come out is the furniture. The irregularities are cracks and nail holes.

S1: Working downward is ceiling before walls, so you aren't dripping onto finished work. The two light passes are coats, the waiting is drying time. And nothing about the paragraph changed. Three words of context arrived before it instead of after it.

S2: I'd have blamed the vocabulary.

S1: You'd have been wrong, and the lesson checks. Every word is ordinary English, every sentence is short, and it scores sixty nine on the Flesch reading ease scale, which is comfortably plain English. It was still unreadable. A formula counts syllables and sentence lengths, and the problem was in neither.

S2: So what is the principle underneath it?

S1: Gopen and Swan, in a nineteen ninety paper called The Science of Scientific Writing, state it as the sixth of seven structural principles. In general, provide context for your reader before asking that reader to consider anything new. And keep the in general. It's theirs, and the same paper says none of these reader expectation principles should be considered rules.

S2: Why does withholding context cost so much? Is the reader just mildly annoyed?

S1: No. A reader who doesn't yet know where a passage is going can't file anything. Every fact arrives and has to be held, unsorted, in case it matters. Attend to any irregularities early is a burden until you know it's a room being painted, and then it costs nothing at all, because it drops into a slot that was already there.

S2: So you've made them carry the whole document at once.

S1: And they won't do it for long. Lesson one's finding was that a reader takes in about a fifth of an average page, and the sharper version is that readers get through half the information only on pages of about a hundred and eleven words or fewer. Spend those first hundred words giving them nothing to hold on to and you've spent the part you could count on.

S2: How much context does a reader need? I can imagine overcorrecting into three paragraphs of background.

S1: The painting passage needed three words. The reader usually needs a frame, not a briefing. The frame lets them file everything after it. The briefing is another thing to hold. And it applies at three scales. The document: a reader should be able to say what this is and what it wants from them inside the first hundred words. The section, which is mostly a job for headings. And the paragraph, which should usually declare its business in its first sentence.

S2: Is the heading advice just taste?

S1: GOV.UK's guidance on structure asks for headings that are descriptive and frontloaded, and tells writers to avoid generic headings like Introduction. Put the most important information first, it says. The quicker you get to the point, the greater the chance your users will see the information you want them to. And at every scale the question is the same. Can the reader file what comes next.

S2: There's a worked email in this lesson. Give me the damage.

S1: Two hundred and thirty five words, and the decision arrives at word two hundred and eleven. It opens with, following our discussion last week and having reviewed the figures from Q two as well as the projections that finance circulated on Monday, and goes on like that for three more paragraphs before saying the launch should move from May to July.

S2: So a reader giving it the attention they give an average page has stopped in the second paragraph, and everything they read was preamble.

S1: They know there was a discussion and that the regional teams have concerns. They don't know anyone is proposing to move anything. The rewrite is ninety one words. It opens, we should move the launch from May to July, here's why. The decision begins at word three and is complete by word nine.

S2: So the whole rewrite fits inside what the original spent clearing its throat. What came out?

S1: A hundred and forty four words, and every one was hedging or throat clearing. Following our discussion last week went, because the reader was at the discussion. I wanted to reach out went, because it describes the email rather than saying anything. As you will remember, on balance, I think the sensible course of action is probably, and unless there is something I have missed.

S2: And the facts?

S1: Moved, not cut. The regional teams, the summer cover, the Q two figures and the launch date are all still there. They arrive after the reader knows what they're for. That's the difference between cutting and front loading.

S2: Now, I've been waiting for this. Is there a case where the point should not come first?

S1: Three, and the lesson says they aren't rare. The first is bad news that needs its reason to be understood. A letter refusing a benefit claim. Buried, it's two sentences of thanking you for your application and reviewing your circumstances, and then the refusal turns up at the end of the third. Front loaded with no reason, it's, your claim has been refused, you can appeal within one month.

S2: That's honest, but I'd stop reading and start reacting.

S1: Which is the problem. Many readers never reach the part about appealing. The fix is one sentence of reason next to the decision. Your claim has been refused, because your savings are above the sixteen thousand pound limit. If that figure is wrong, you can appeal within one month and we will look again. One sentence of reason, not four paragraphs of context, and the deadline is still in the first thirty words.

S2: And the other two?

S1: An argument whose conclusion would be rejected on sight. If your reader believes the opposite, opening with the conclusion invites them to spend the document arguing instead of reading, so the premises genuinely come first. Which is still principle six, by the way: the premises are the context that makes the conclusion intelligible. And third, narrative, where the withholding is the point. A joke front loaded is not a joke. Neither is a mystery.

S2: Are those three exceptions research?

S1: No, and the lesson says so in the open. They're the drafter's working judgement, drawn from the principle rather than from a study, and the course has not read research on the claim about softening bad news or the claim about arguing readers. Treat them as reasoning you can check against your own readers.

S2: Last thing. What do people get wrong about this?

S1: That front loading means being blunt, which is unrelated: we should move the launch from May to July, and here's why, is front loaded and perfectly courteous. That a summary at the end does the same job, when a summary at the end helps a reader who finished and context at the start helps a reader read. And that having an executive summary is enough, when a great many executive summaries say what the document covers, which is a table of contents in prose and gives the reader nothing to file.
