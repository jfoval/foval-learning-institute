S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Writing Clearly, lesson nine, the last one. Revising, and finding out whether it worked.

S1: It opens by saying what you now have and what you still don't. You've taken a document through eight lessons. You know where its point should go, what its sentences should look like, which words are doing nothing, and how to shape it for someone scanning. You still don't know whether anyone understands it.

S2: And that sounds like a small gap.

S1: It's the whole thing. Every judgement so far has been made by the one person who can't read the document fresh. You know what it was supposed to say, and your own knowledge fills every hole as you reread. Lesson four used that to explain why you can't feel a long wait between a subject and its verb.

S2: So the lesson starts by separating two words.

S1: Revising and editing, which get used interchangeably. Revising is structural. What is this document for, is that still what it's doing, is the argument in the right order, does that section need to exist. Revising can delete a third of a draft. Editing is local. The sentence, the word, the comma. Everything lessons three to six taught you.

S2: And revising comes first.

S1: And the reason isn't taste. Edit first and you'll spend an hour polishing sentences in a section you're about to cut. That work isn't merely wasted. It makes the cut harder, because nobody likes deleting a paragraph they've just perfected.

S2: Is there research behind the distinction?

S1: Nancy Sommers, in a nineteen eighty two report to the National Institute of Education. She examined fifteen composition textbooks and found they yield twenty three terms used synonymously with revision. What those books teach equates revision with cleanliness. To groom, to polish, to order, and to tidy up one's writing. And they place it as that interlude after you finish writing the first draft and before you type the paper.

S2: Both halves wrong.

S1: And between them they describe what most people do. Her own study was eight first year students and seven experienced adult writers, each writing three compositions, rewriting each twice, and interviewed three times.

S2: That's fifteen people. I don't want to hear a law about students and experts out of fifteen people.

S1: And the lesson says so before it reports anything. Fifteen writers can't establish a fact about students and experts in general, and it doesn't claim one. What a study that size gives you is a distinction worth having and a vocabulary for it, which you check against yourself.

S2: All right. What did she find?

S1: Her central finding, in her words. The student writers have operational procedures for revising and reasons to explain their procedures, but they have not codified or synthesized the procedures into a theory of the revision process. The experienced writers, on the other hand, have a codified set of principles about how their revision process works.

S2: So they weren't lazy. They had procedures.

S1: What they lacked was any account of what revision is for, so they couldn't tell which procedure a draft needed. And the second half is sharper. For the experienced writers revision was not a stage but rather a process that occurred throughout their writing. Their first drafts were already the results of an elaborate revision process. She says the evidence calls for a rejection of the linear stage theory, and proposes instead that writers sense, tolerate and resolve dissonance.

S2: Hold on. Doesn't that contradict revise before you edit?

S1: The lesson takes that head on. Sommers is describing how composing happens. Revise before you edit is advice about a draft already in front of you, and its whole content is, don't polish sentences in a section you're about to cut.

S2: She has two of her writers in their own words, I think.

S1: One student's strategy is to use a thesaurus to improve the sophistication of his writing. One of the experienced writers has two named activities instead. Rewriting means the deep structural changes, the content. Revising means the surface changes, word and phrases. She isn't more talented in that quotation. She has a distinction he doesn't have, and it tells her what to do next.

S2: Now. The step almost nobody takes.

S1: Ask a real person what they think it says. The US federal plain language guide sets this out as paraphrase testing. Their own scoping: with only six to nine participants, paraphrase testing will not take a lot of time.

S2: How do you run one?

S1: Find between six and nine people. Give them a section, not the whole document. Ask them to tell you in their own words what it means. Write down what they say, in their words, and don't correct them. Then ask what they'd do next. Those last two steps are the course's wording rather than the guide's.

S1: And never ask, was that clear, because a question answerable with yes or no gets you a yes and tells you nothing.

S2: And the hard one is writing it down.

S1: Someone misreads your sentence and every instinct says explain it. The moment you explain, the evidence is gone. You now have a reader who understands your document because you told them what it says, which isn't a thing you can ship. The misreading is the finding.

S1: Here's a worked one, from a council tax letter. If your circumstances change in a way that affects your entitlement, you must tell us within one month of the change. Where a change is not reported within that period, any resulting overpayment is recoverable, and a penalty may be applied.

S2: And the reader said?

S1: So if something changes I should let them know, and if I don't they might fine me. I'd probably ring them when I got round to it. It sounds like the sort of thing where they'd understand if you were a bit late.

S1: Fluent, confident, and about eighty percent right. What she's lost is the one month, the thing the section exists to communicate. She converted a deadline into a general obligation.

S2: So which sentence did that?

S1: The deadline sits at the end of a long subordinate clause opening on, if your circumstances change, so by the time she reached, within one month, she'd been holding an unresolved condition for most of the sentence. That's lesson four. And the stress position, where lesson five says a reader puts their weight, is occupied by, a penalty may be applied. Which is why the penalty survived and the deadline didn't.

S1: So the rewrite follows from the diagnosis. Tell us within one month if your circumstances change. If you tell us later than that, you may have to pay back anything you were overpaid, and you may be charged a penalty. The deadline is in the first six words, and the penalty has moved to after the thing the reader has to do.

S2: Did it test better?

S1: The second reader paraphrased it as, I've got a month to tell them, and if I miss it I pay it back and maybe a fine. That's the test passing, and it took eleven minutes.

S2: What if I can't find six people?

S1: The guidance works with six to nine, and you do learn more from six than from one. The lesson's own advice, flagged as a judgement rather than a finding, is do it with one rather than not at all. Nothing in the guidance says one is sufficient. The step people actually skip is the first one.

S2: Last thing. Why not just use a readability score instead?

S1: A formula counts syllables per word and words per sentence, so a better score means those counts went down, not that a reader understood more. Lesson one gave the deeper reason: difficulty is a relation between a text and a reader, and a formula sees one side of it. Which doesn't make formulas useless. A score that suddenly jumps is a signal worth investigating. A signal is not an outcome.

S2: The full lesson has both of those writers at length, Sommers's coding scheme for your own revisions, the responses that look right and aren't, and the course project: one document taken all the way through, with a record of what your reader said. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: That's the end of Writing Clearly. Thanks for listening, and go and ask somebody what your document says.
