S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Writing Clearly, lesson eight. Rules and where they come from.

S1: This one turns round and looks at the course itself. Everything so far has asked you to trust it. Readers take in about a fifth of a page. Readers emphasise what arrives last.

S2: And meanwhile I've been told all my life never to split an infinitive.

S1: Never start a sentence with but. Never use the passive. Those usually arrive with no evidence, and often with real conviction. So the lesson names the difference first. A rule like, readers emphasise what arrives last, is a description. Somebody looked at what readers do and reported it, and you can check. A rule like, never split an infinitive, is a prohibition. It tells you what not to do and offers nothing to check.

S1: And the first kind can be wrong, which is a virtue, because a claim that could be wrong is one you can test. Gopen and Swan are careful about this. None of these reader expectation principles should be considered rules, they write.

S2: Start with the passive, because everybody thinks they know it.

S1: A passive clause takes the thing acted upon and makes it the subject, and the test is whether you could add, by someone, without breaking the sentence. Here's Pullum on what's happened to that distinction. It is typical for college graduates today to be unable to distinguish active from passive clauses. They often equate the grammatical notion of being passive with the semantic one of not specifying the agent of an action. They think a bus exploded is passive because it doesn't say whether terrorists did it.

S1: But a bus exploded is about as active as a sentence gets. The bus is the subject and it's doing the exploding. What people react to is that nobody is blamed, which is a different complaint, often fair, and nothing to do with voice.

S2: All right. The book and the attack.

S1: The Elements of Style began as a slim volume William Strunk printed privately for his Cornell students in nineteen eighteen. E B White, who'd taken the class in nineteen nineteen, revised and expanded it in nineteen fifty nine. And the lesson is fair to its defenders: they hold it's a compact set of maxims that has helped generations of novices, and that a beginner who follows it writes better than one who follows nothing.

S2: And Pullum?

S1: His essay marked the fiftieth anniversary in two thousand and nine. He's head of linguistics at Edinburgh and co author of The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language. The book, he writes, does not deserve the enormous esteem in which it is held by American college graduates. Its advice ranges from limp platitudes to inconsistent nonsense. Its enormous influence has not improved American students' grasp of English grammar, it has significantly degraded it.

S1: And the lesson insists on two things that get cut when that is quoted alone. First, he isn't attacking the style advice. What he objects to, he says, is not the style advice in Elements, which might best be described the way the Hitchhiker's Guide describes Earth. Mostly harmless. And, it doesn't hurt to lay such well meant maxims before novice writers.

S1: And second, he says which half of the case is his. After noting that writing tutors simply red circle everything that looks like a passive, he writes that that overinterpretation is part of the damage Strunk and White have unintentionally done, but it is not what I am most concerned about here. What concerns me is that the bias against the passive is being retailed by a pair of authors so grammatically clueless that they don't know what is a passive construction and what isn't.

S2: So the charge is competence, and a response that concedes the tutors and disputes the competence has answered the half he disclaimed.

S1: Two parts. Of the four pairs under, use the active voice, he says a staggering three out of four are mistaken diagnoses. And the stronger half, that the book can't keep its own rules on the same page as it states them. He's careful to say he isn't nitpicking their style, and that White often wrote beautifully. Write with nouns and verbs, not with adjectives and adverbs, they insist, and then in the very next sentence comes a negative passive clause containing three adjectives.

S2: Can this course check that?

S1: No. Those sentences are White's nineteen fifty nine text, which the course hasn't read. So it takes the half it can check, Strunk's nineteen eighteen, out of copyright and available in full.

S2: And what does opening it show?

S1: That Pullum has read it too, and says so. He quotes the book explicitly saying it does not mean that the writer should entirely discard the passive voice, which is frequently convenient and sometimes necessary, and he grants the topic dependence point lesson five was built on.

S2: So what does the primary text add?

S1: The sentence that introduces the disputed examples. Many a tame sentence of description or exposition can be made lively and emphatic by substituting a verb in the active voice for some such perfunctory expression as, there is, or, could be heard. So Strunk names the faults himself, and neither is the passive. Which fits Pullum's own hedge: he writes presumably fingered as passive, inferring it from where the examples sit under an active voice heading. Both readings are available from the page, and his is the natural one for a reader who trusts headings.

S2: Does it correct the critic anywhere?

S1: Pullum writes that Strunk's original never even mentioned split infinitives. It does. The nineteen eighteen glossary has an entry.

S2: So where does that leave it?

S1: Messier than either side would like, which is the point. His central charge rests mainly on the same page self contradictions, quoted from a text this course hasn't read, and they look serious. On the examples, the nineteen eighteen wording complicates the charge without refuting it, and he hedged it himself. Two of his sharpest hits, on none and on which, belong to White rather than Strunk. One incidental claim is wrong. And the damage he documents is real and isn't in dispute.

S1: Now, the method, which is the actual lesson. Notice what moved the argument. Not authority, not intuition. The text, opened, by someone willing to publish what they found. Pullum does the same on the none rule. He searches three books by writers publishing while Strunk taught, finds plural agreement in each, and asks the question worth stealing. Is the intelligent student supposed to believe that Stoker, Wilde and Montgomery didn't know how to write?

S1: Four steps. State the rule precisely, so it could be true or false. Find a corpus, and Project Gutenberg is free. Count, both ways. Then say what the numbers can and cannot show.

S2: And the counts?

S1: Mark Liberman checked half a dozen of Twain's books and found roughly seven sentence initial howevers for each three placed after the subject. In five books by Henry James the ratio was one to fifteen.

S2: So the rule is dead.

S1: That's the step the lesson is most careful about. On the descriptive view, which this course works from, a rule contradicted by good writers of its period is not a rule of English. But there's a prescriptive reply and it isn't silly. A standard written variety isn't the sum of what fluent people write. It's maintained deliberately, by editors and style guides and teaching, so strangers can read each other without friction. On that view Twain's usage is evidence about Twain, not a repeal.

S2: So what does the count actually settle?

S1: The narrow question, either way. Is this rule a description of how careful writers of the period wrote? If not, anyone presenting it as a fact about English is wrong. Whether you should follow it anyway, in a house that has chosen it, is a separate question the numbers don't touch. Keep the two apart and you can be evidence led without being rude to your copy editor.

S2: The full lesson puts the five charges in a table with whose text each one lands on, and sets an exercise where you test a rule you were taught, including reporting anything that goes against you. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.
