S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Writing Clearly, lesson five. Topic position and stress position.

S1: This is the lesson that finishes the sentence. It opens with a paragraph where every sentence is grammatical, none is longer than fifteen words, and there are no buried actions or long waits between subject and verb. It passes every test the course has given you so far. The council commissioned a structural survey in January. Corrosion in the main support beams was found by the survey. About two million pounds is the cost of replacing those beams. Four hundred thousand pounds remains in this year's capital budget. Closure until at least next spring is the consequence.

S2: I can get the facts out. A bridge, a survey, some rust and a shortage of money. But it was work.

S1: And the effort is out of proportion to the content. Now the same paragraph again. The council commissioned a structural survey in January. The survey found corrosion in the main support beams. Replacing those beams would cost about two million pounds. That cost is far more than the four hundred thousand pounds left in this year's capital budget. So the bridge stays closed until at least next spring.

S1: Not one fact added or removed. Forty nine words and fifty three, so the second is barely longer. What changed is the order, and a handful of linking words.

S2: Say what changed, precisely.

S1: Every sentence after the first begins with something you already had. Sentence two opens with, the survey. Sentence three, replacing those beams. Sentence four, that cost. Sentence five opens with, so, which points at everything before it.

S2: And in the first version?

S1: Every sentence after the first opens with something new. Corrosion, two million pounds, four hundred thousand pounds, closure. A fresh object four times in a row, and the reader has to work out what each has to do with the last.

S1: Ten words are new in the second version and six have gone, and almost all are linking rather than carrying a fact. That cost, far more than, so, the bridge. The facts are word for word the same.

S2: Give me the names.

S1: Gopen and Swan. On the beginning of a sentence: the information that begins a sentence establishes for the reader a perspective for viewing the sentence as a unit. Readers expect a unit of discourse to be a story about whoever shows up first. They call that the topic position.

S2: And that is not where the important thing goes.

S1: It is not. It's where you tell the reader what this sentence is about, and how it connects to the one before. Then the end. It is a linguistic commonplace, they write, that readers naturally emphasize the material that arrives at the end of a sentence. That's the stress position, where the reader places their weight.

S2: And the principle joining them?

S1: The given and new contract. Put in the topic position the old information that links backward. Put in the stress position the new information you want the reader to emphasize. Old at the front, new at the end, and every sentence a bridge from something the reader has to something they don't. With their own caveat, though. None of these reader expectation principles should be considered rules.

S2: So what exactly was wrong with the choppy version?

S1: Sentence two ended on, by the survey. The survey was old information sitting in the stress position, where the reader places weight. Meanwhile corrosion, the news, was at the front, where the reader wanted orientation. Every sentence after the first had its information exactly backwards.

S1: And not one was unclear on its own. Which is why, make your sentences clearer, is useless advice for a choppy paragraph. Cohesion isn't a property of sentences. It's a property of the joins, and they live at the two ends.

S2: What about transition words? However, therefore, furthermore. Don't those fix it?

S1: Bolt them onto the first version and it isn't better. It's longer, and now it's choppy prose that has been told it is connected. A transition word announces a relation. The topic position creates one. Which isn't a ban on transitions. Use them where the relation genuinely isn't obvious from the order.

S2: Now, you've been promising me something about the passive since lesson three.

S1: This is where it settles. In nineteen eighteen, in The Elements of Style, Strunk gave this pair, inside the rule headed, use the active voice. The dramatists of the Restoration are little esteemed today. And, modern readers have little esteem for the dramatists of the Restoration.

S2: First is passive, second is active. And he says use the second.

S1: He does not. He says the first would be the right form in a paragraph on the dramatists of the Restoration, the second in a paragraph on the tastes of modern readers. And then this. The need of making a particular word the subject of the sentence will often, as in these examples, determine which voice is to be used.

S2: That's the topic position.

S1: Described in nineteen eighteen, seventy two years before Gopen and Swan named it. And Strunk gets his credit. One paragraph earlier in the same rule he writes that this rule does not, of course, mean that the writer should entirely discard the passive voice, which is frequently convenient and sometimes necessary.

S2: So the man whose rule is headed use the active voice says that.

S1: What's misleading isn't Strunk. It's the heading, read alone by people who never got to the paragraph underneath, and a century of advice that repeated the heading and dropped the rest.

S1: So the honest position is not avoid it, and not a replacement rule either. The passive is what lets you put something other than the actor in the topic position. If your paragraph is about the dramatists, they go at the front, and the passive is how you get them there.

S1: A test case. A paragraph about a patient's recovery. The consultant discharged her on the fourth day, or, she was discharged on the fourth day.

S2: The second, because the paragraph is about her.

S1: And the passive is what puts her there. The first shifts the story to the consultant, and a reader will take the next sentence to be about him. If the paragraph were about his decisions, the first would be right. A rule that bans one takes away the version you need whenever the paragraph is about the patient.

S2: And the other side gets a hearing?

S1: Strunk's own reason stands: the active voice is usually more direct and vigorous than the passive. And GOV.UK tells government writers to use it because it is more direct and puts the focus on the user, which for most sentences most of the time is right.

S2: Last thing. What if the two positions fight? The old information is also the thing I most want to land.

S1: A paragraph has just said the fire door was propped open. Then: the propped door was what the inspector wrote up. Old information at the front, so the link holds, but it ends on, wrote up, which isn't what you wanted emphasised.

S2: So turn it round. What the inspector wrote up was the propped door.

S1: Now the door lands, and the sentence opens on the inspector, who hasn't been mentioned and isn't what the paragraph is about. Both versions cost something.

S1: The lesson's own judgement, flagged as a judgement rather than something Gopen and Swan say, is that the topic position usually wins, because a reader who has lost the thread can't be made to emphasise anything. But there's usually a better move. Split it. The inspector wrote up the propped door. That door is now the only thing standing between the building and a prohibition notice.

S2: Two topic positions, two stress positions, and the door in both.

S1: A sentence trying to do two jobs often just needed to be two.

S2: The full lesson has the diagram of the contract and a paragraph to repair by moving information only, with the warning that goes with it: a repair is not allowed to add a causal claim that wasn't there. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.
