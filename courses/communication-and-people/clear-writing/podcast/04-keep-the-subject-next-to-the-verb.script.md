S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Writing Clearly, lesson four. Keep the subject next to the verb.

S1: And it opens with a sentence you're meant to read once, at normal speed, noticing what your mind does. The application, which had been submitted in March, revised twice at the request of the highways officer, and held while the flood risk assessment was recommissioned, was refused.

S2: I met the subject almost straight away and then I was just holding it.

S1: You met it in the second word. Then you waited twenty four words before the sentence told you what the application did, and all that time you were holding the application in your head, unresolved, through three separate pieces of history.

S2: And the fix is just moving things?

S1: Same words. The application was refused. It had been submitted in March, revised twice at the highways officer's request, and held while the flood risk assessment was recommissioned. Nothing has gone. The March date, the two revisions, the highways officer and the flood risk assessment are all still there. You got the verb four words in instead of twenty eight.

S2: So what's the principle?

S1: Gopen and Swan state it as the first of their seven structural principles, and the lesson gives their wording exactly. Readers expect a grammatical subject to be followed immediately by the verb. Anything of length that intervenes between subject and verb is read as an interruption, and therefore as something of lesser importance.

S2: That second half is doing something the first half isn't.

S1: It is, and the lesson makes a point of it. They aren't only saying the interruption costs the reader something. They're saying the reader discounts it, files it as a footnote to the main event. Which is worth knowing before you put twenty words of your best material in there.

S2: And the usual caveat.

S1: The one this course quotes every time. None of these reader expectation principles should be considered rules. It's a description of what readers do, not a prohibition.

S2: Why does the wait cost anything at all? I can hold four words.

S1: Because a subject on its own is an unfinished thought. You can't do anything with, the application, until you know what happened to it, so you hold it, and everything arriving before the verb has to be held alongside it, because it might turn out to matter to whatever the verb says.

S2: So the cost isn't flat.

S1: One or two words cost almost nothing. Twenty four cost a great deal, and they cost it on top of the ordinary work of reading twenty four words. The reader is doing two jobs at once and one of them is bookkeeping. And the lesson is honest that the rising cost is its own reasoning from the word interruption, not a measured curve from a study.

S2: How does this sit with lesson three?

S1: It's the other half of it. Lesson three got the right character into the subject slot. If you move the character to the front and then make the reader wait thirty words for the verb, you've spent the gain. They're halves of one move.

S2: Can I just listen for it in my own writing?

S1: No, and there's a specific reason. You already know what your sentence means. When you reread, the application, which had been submitted in March, your mind supplies, was refused, before you get there, because you wrote it. The wait costs you nothing, so you can't feel it.

S2: Same problem as rereading your own work in lesson one.

S1: Same answer, too. Replace the judgement with a count. Find the main subject, find its verb, count the words in between. That number is the size of the wait you're asking for.

S2: Is there a threshold?

S1: Nobody has measured a cutoff, and the lesson says anyone who gives you one is selling something. What it offers is the drafter's working rule of thumb, reasoned from the principle. Under about five words the gap is invisible. Five to ten is usually fine. Ten to twenty is where readers start to notice. Past twenty you're asking for real work.

S2: And I should treat those as?

S1: A way to sort your own sentences, not as findings. And the lesson makes a nice point here. The number that matters isn't the boundary. It's that you now have a figure for each sentence, so you can rank them and fix the worst three instead of rewriting everything.

S2: Work an example.

S1: Here's a Friday afternoon email. The new supplier, who was selected after a procurement exercise that ran for most of last year and involved eleven bidders, has missed its first delivery date.

S2: Subject is the new supplier. Verb is has missed. And the gap is, what, eighteen words?

S1: Eighteen, from who to bidders. And if you'd picked, was selected, as the verb, that's the verb of the interruption. The sentence's own verb is the one that tells you what the supplier did.

S2: So how many ways are there to close a gap like that?

S1: Three, and you can derive them from the shape of the problem rather than remembering a list. The interruption is in the wrong place, so either it moves or the sentence does. It can go to the front, it can go to the end, or the sentence splits in two and the interruption becomes a sentence of its own.

S2: And I'd assume those are interchangeable.

S1: That's the thing. They aren't, and the reason is Gopen and Swan's second clause arriving in practice. Each one leaves something different at the end of the sentence, and the end of a sentence is where a reader puts their weight.

S2: Take them one at a time.

S1: Move the interruption to the front. After a procurement exercise that ran for most of last year and involved eleven bidders, the new supplier has missed its first delivery date. The gap is gone, and what lands last is the missed delivery. So that version is about the failure, and the procurement exercise has become scene setting.

S2: And to the end?

S1: The new supplier has missed its first delivery date, after a procurement exercise that ran for most of last year and involved eleven bidders. Also gone, but now what lands last is the year long exercise with eleven bidders. That version is quietly about the waste, and a reader finishes it thinking about the procurement rather than the delivery.

S2: And the split.

S1: The new supplier has missed its first delivery date. It was selected after a procurement exercise that ran for most of last year and involved eleven bidders. Two sentences, two stress positions, and both facts land. Usually the safest choice when both halves matter, and the one most writers reach for last.

S2: So if I'm writing to the procurement team to ask what went wrong with the process?

S1: You use the second. You want them to finish the sentence thinking about the procurement exercise, because that's what your email is about. If you were writing to operations about a supply problem you'd use the first. If you were writing a report that has to record both fairly, you'd split it.

S2: Which means the question can't be answered by looking at the sentence.

S1: It depends on what you want the reader to walk away with, which is a question about the document. That's the idea lesson five is built on, and this is the first place it bites.

S2: Now, I have a suspicion this is going to turn into, write shorter sentences.

S1: The lesson heads that off with a pair. Thirty six words. The council agreed to fund the repairs, which had been costed at ninety thousand pounds by an independent surveyor, and asked the housing team to begin work in the spring, before the next round of inspections. Then eighteen words. The decision, given the circumstances and the timescale involved and the other matters raised at the meeting, stands.

S2: The short one is harder. I had to carry, the decision, the whole way.

S1: Through fifteen words of vagueness to reach, stands. The long one has its subject and verb together at the front, twice, and everything after arrives in order, a clause at a time, with nothing held. Half the length and considerably harder.

S2: So the length rule is a proxy.

S1: GOV.UK's guidance says to try to split up sentences that are over twenty five words long, and that's sensible precisely because it's a rule of thumb about where to look. Long sentences are where big gaps tend to live. But splitting a long sentence with no gap gains nothing, and a short sentence with a gap passes the length check untouched.

S2: Which is the readability formula problem from lesson one, at a smaller scale.

S1: The countable thing is a signal. The thing you care about is what the reader has to hold.

S2: Does an interruption ever earn its place?

S1: Sometimes, and there's a pattern. The report, dated four March, recommends closure. Two words, and they tell the reader which report, which is something they need before the verb can mean anything. Or, the tenant, not the landlord, is responsible for the internal decoration. Three words, doing the most important work in the sentence, which is ruling out the thing the reader probably assumed.

S2: And the pattern?

S1: A short interruption that tells the reader which thing the subject is usually earns its place. A long one that gives history usually doesn't.

S2: What do people get wrong?

S1: A few things. That short sentences are clear sentences, when the eighteen word example is the counterexample. That you should vary your sentence length, which is advice about rhythm rather than clarity, and shouldn't talk you into leaving a twenty word gap for the sake of the music. That reading it back is a test, when you already know what the verb is going to be. And that parentheses are the problem, when the punctuation is innocent. A twenty word parenthesis is a problem, and so is a twenty word relative clause, and so is a twenty word list. Look for the distance, not the marks.

S2: The full lesson has the chart of the gap, four worked cases on when an interruption earns its place, and an exercise where you count every gap on a page of your own writing. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Go and count a page.
