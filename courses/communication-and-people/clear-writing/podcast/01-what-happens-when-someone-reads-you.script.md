S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're starting Writing Clearly, with its first lesson. What actually happens when someone reads you.

S1: It opens with a scene. A friend forwards you a nine hundred word email from her landlord and asks what it means. Somewhere in the sixth paragraph there's a date, and the date is in nine days. Everything else is context. She had already read it twice and missed the date both times.

S2: So the landlord wrote it badly.

S1: The email isn't badly spelled. It's written in the order the landlord thought about the problem, which isn't the order the reader needed to meet it. That mismatch is what the whole course is about.

S2: So before any advice about how to write, what does the evidence say happens on the other end?

S1: In two thousand and eight Jakob Nielsen published an analysis of how little people read online. The data came from Weinreich and colleagues, who instrumented twenty five users' browsers and recorded extended information about everything they did during normal web use. After cleaning it covered forty five thousand, two hundred and thirty seven page views. Not a laboratory task.

S2: And what came out of it?

S1: Two numbers. A page visit takes about twenty five seconds whatever is on the page. And each additional hundred words adds about four point four seconds.

S2: Hold on. Twenty five seconds of reading, for any page?

S1: That's the trap, and Nielsen's own reading of the number is why. He says people spend some of their time understanding the page layout and navigation features, as well as looking at the images. The twenty five seconds isn't reading. It's arriving.

S2: So the words themselves only buy four point four seconds a hundred.

S1: And Nielsen assumed two hundred and fifty words a minute, which is twenty four seconds per hundred. So every extra hundred words gets under a fifth of the time it needs. A page ten times as long gets roughly twice the attention, most of it still the arrival cost. The average page in his data was five hundred and ninety three words, and his conclusion, in his own words, is this. On average, users will have time to read twenty eight percent of the words if they devote all of their time to reading. More realistically, users will read about twenty percent of the text on the average page.

S2: The if is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

S1: And it's the part that gets dropped when the figure is quoted. One more line from the same analysis. Users read half the information only on those pages with a hundred and eleven words or less. Now do the landlord's email. Nine hundred words. Time given is twenty five seconds plus four point four for each of nine hundreds, about sixty five seconds. Time needed is nine hundred divided by two hundred and fifty, two hundred and sixteen seconds.

S2: So sixty five over two hundred and sixteen. That's about thirty percent, and that's the ceiling.

S1: Before a single second goes on working out where the paragraphs start. Something like a fifth is what a reader who hasn't decided the document matters will actually take in. And the date was in paragraph six.

S2: So past the first hundred words or so, you're not deciding whether the reader misses something. You're deciding what.

S1: And the landlord made that decision without noticing.

S2: I've heard that twenty percent figure used to justify some silly things. Can I cut a hospital letter to two sentences?

S1: No, and Nielsen says two things that limit his own data. His participants had above average intelligence, with several being university employees. And he treated the very long pages as beside the point. Pages with a huge word count are probably not real pages anyway, he wrote, more likely academic papers or terms and conditions.

S2: So it's capable people skimming ordinary web pages.

S1: It isn't a law about reading. Think how differently one person reads in a day. They skim a news page over breakfast and take in a fifth. Then a letter comes from the hospital about their mother, and they read every word twice and read it again down the phone. What changed is that the second document was about something they needed. So the twenty percent is a default for a reader who hasn't yet decided you matter, which is most documents most of the time. The wrong use is cutting a surgical consent form to two sentences.

S2: Okay. The budget tells me I have less room than I thought. What goes in the room?

S1: That depends on who's reading, and most writers answer with a category. Residents of the borough is a category. It can't change a sentence of your letter. Compare it with, people who owe the new charge, who need to know by when, and what happens if they can't pay. That version tells you what the first paragraph is, what the second is, and what can be cut. And the test is simple. If knowing the answer wouldn't change a sentence, you haven't answered it.

S2: What are the common ways people get the audience wrong?

S1: Two. The first is writing for the approver. Documents get shaped by what a manager or a legal team will accept, but the approver wants nothing wrong in it and the reader wants to know what to do. Optimise for the first and you get nine hundred words of context with a date in paragraph six.

S2: And the second?

S1: Writing for yourself six months ago. You write, submit form C T two by the deadline, because you know what that form is and when the deadline is, and your own knowledge fills the gaps as you reread it. A reader meeting it cold has to find out both before they can act.

S2: Which is why rereading your own work is a bad test, I take it.

S1: And why the course ends by handing it to another person.

S2: Last thing. There's a suspicion that plain writing is condescending. Is there anything real behind that?

S1: The UK government publishes its writing standards in the open, and its guidance on clear language gives a reason that isn't about elegance. One in six adults in England have very poor literacy skills. And on the condescension point the same guidance reports the research in one sentence. People with higher levels of literacy prefer plain English because it allows them to understand the information as quickly as possible.

S2: So the most capable readers want it most.

S1: A specialist doesn't read a plain sentence and feel patronised. They get it and move on to the part they needed their expertise for. And it isn't one country's taste. The Plain Writing Act of two thousand and ten in the United States established the requirement that content for the public is written for its specific audience.

S2: And readability scores? The lesson is more sceptical than I expected.

S1: The formulas count syllables per word and words per sentence, and a formula that knows nothing about the reader can't tell you whether the reader understood. You can improve a score by chopping a sentence in half at the comma and change nothing about what anyone takes away. A two thousand and twenty one study by Gooding, Berzak, Mak and Sharifi measured how five hundred and eighteen readers scrolled through texts, and found that reader background influences both how people read and what makes a text difficult for them. Difficulty is a relation between a text and a reader, not something the text carries on its own.

S2: The full lesson has the two lines drawn on a chart, the arithmetic worked out, and an exercise where you measure something you actually wrote. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Go and find out what your readers really got.
