S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Writing Clearly, lesson six. Concision that is actually teachable.

S1: It opens with a paragraph of the kind that circulates in every organisation, a hundred and eleven words long. It begins, it should be noted that, due to the fact that the previous system was not able to handle the volume of applications that we were receiving during the course of the busy summer period, a decision was taken by the management team in order to procure a replacement.

S2: I can hear the meeting it came out of.

S1: It goes on, and here's the whole of it at forty six words. The old system could not handle the summer volume of applications, so the management team bought a replacement. If the new one is not fully working by the end of September, we may have to go back to the old one for a while. Nobody wants that.

S2: Nothing lost?

S1: Every fact survives. The old system, the volume, the summer, the management team, the replacement, September, the temporary reversion, and that nobody wants it. Fifty nine percent of the words are gone.

S2: You said, not fully working, not, not working.

S1: Deliberate. The original said, not fully and completely operational. A system that isn't fully operational may still be partly operational, which is a different fact from one that isn't working at all. Fully and completely is a doubled pair, so one goes. But fully is not padding, and dropping it would have changed what the sentence claims.

S2: Everyone has heard, omit needless words.

S1: The most quoted writing advice there is, and routinely quoted at a quarter of its length. The quarter that gets quoted is the least useful part. Here's the whole first paragraph of Strunk's rule thirteen, from nineteen eighteen. Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts. This requires not that the writer make all his sentences short, or that he avoid all detail and treat his subjects only in outline, but that he make every word tell.

S2: That last sentence changes it.

S1: And almost nobody quotes it. He's answering the misreading his own headline invites, sixty years before anyone accused him of it. He isn't telling you to be brief. He's telling you to make every word tell. And the rule gives a substitution list. The question as to whether becomes whether. He is a man who becomes he. In a hasty manner becomes hastily. Owing to the fact that becomes since.

S2: So where does the objection come from?

S1: Geoffrey Pullum, in an essay called Fifty Years of Stupid Grammar Advice. Many are useless, he writes, like, omit needless words. The students who know which words are needless don't need the instruction.

S1: Read fairly, it lands on the headline and not on the rule. Four words tell you what the finished state looks like and nothing about how to reach it, rather like telling someone to sculpt an elephant by removing everything that doesn't look like an elephant. Strunk knew that, which is why he wrote three more paragraphs and a list. The headline travelled, and the rule stayed behind.

S2: And Pullum himself?

S1: The lesson gives him his qualifications, because they get dropped when he's quoted. In the same essay he calls the style advice mostly harmless, and writes that even so, it doesn't hurt to lay such well meant maxims before novice writers. His quarrel is with the book's grammatical claims, which lesson eight takes up.

S2: So the answer is to name the kinds.

S1: Don't cut by feel. Name the kind of every cut, and you can do it again next week and on someone else's writing. The lesson is upfront that the list is its own, assembled from its sources and the pattern of the examples. Strunk's is shorter, Williams's longer. Six is few enough to remember.

S1: Doubled words, two joined by and where either does the job. Full and complete. Each and every. First and foremost. Already implied, where the sentence has said it once in another word. Period of time. Advance planning. Revert back.

S1: Third, metadiscourse, writing about the writing. It should be noted that. I would like to say that. It is important to note that. Cut the whole phrase. Then a phrase for a word: due to the fact that becomes because, in the event that becomes if, at this point in time becomes now, in order to becomes to.

S1: Two left. Negative for positive, where a not has a word of its own. Was not able to becomes could not. Did not remember becomes forgot. Not many becomes few. And hedge stacking, two or three hedges where one is honest. There is a possibility that we may possibly becomes we may.

S2: I notice none of that is about long words.

S1: None of the six is about long words, which is worth saying, because people think concision is a vocabulary problem. Replacing utilise with use is worth doing and saves you two letters. Replacing, there is a possibility that we may possibly need to, with, we may, saves you nine words.

S2: Are any of the six really about something else?

S1: Both are usually symptoms. Metadiscourse piles up when a writer is nervous about the content and warms up before saying it. Hedges stack when the writer isn't sure of the claim. Make the cut, then ask what the writing is being cautious about, because that's often the real problem.

S2: Is there a seventh kind?

S1: One row in the worked table isn't on the list, and it's the biggest. A decision was taken by the management team in order to procure a replacement becomes, the management team bought a replacement. That's a buried action from lesson three, and it returns the most words for one change.

S2: Now, the lesson says it couldn't have come any earlier in the course. Why?

S1: Here it is. Take two sentences from lesson five's bridge paragraph. Replacing those beams would cost about two million pounds. That cost is far more than the four hundred thousand pounds left in this year's capital budget.

S2: And a concision pass finds, that cost is far more than, and cuts it.

S1: Six words repeating something the reader had half a second ago. Cut it and you get, only four hundred thousand pounds is left in this year's capital budget. Five words shorter, both figures intact, no fact lost. And the paragraph is worse.

S2: Because the second sentence now opens on a fresh number.

S1: The chain from lesson five is broken, and the reader has to work out the relation between two million and four hundred thousand for themselves. Repetition in the topic position is not padding. It is the join. It looks exactly like padding, which is why concision passes damage prose so often.

S2: So how do I tell them apart?

S1: Ask what the repeated words are doing. If they're carrying the reader from the previous sentence into this one, they're load bearing. If they're just there, they're not.

S2: Last thing. What do people get wrong?

S1: That shorter is always better, when the target isn't fewer words, it's no words that fail to carry something. And that you should cut every adjective and adverb, which isn't even Strunk's. Write with nouns and verbs was added in the nineteen fifty nine revision, and this course takes that wording from Pullum rather than from the revision, which it hasn't read. Adjectives that carry information aren't padding. A twelve page report and a report are different facts.

S2: And the one about never repeating a word?

S1: That one does real damage. It sends writers to a thesaurus for a different word for the same thing, and now the reader has to work out whether the scheme in this sentence is the same as the programme in the last one. Use the same word for the same thing, every time.

S2: The full lesson cuts that paragraph line by line with every kind named, and sets an exercise where you cut something of your own by a third and tally which kind is yours. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Go and find out which kind is yours.
