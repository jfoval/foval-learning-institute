S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Writing Clearly, lesson seven. Shape at the document level.

S1: It starts by noticing something the course has been assuming. Lesson one established that a reader takes in about a fifth of an average page, and gets through half the information only on pages of about a hundred and eleven words or fewer. Everything since has answered that at the level of sentences and paragraphs, which assumes the reader is reading.

S2: And often they aren't.

S1: They're looking. They've opened a document to find one thing, they're moving down it at speed, and prose isn't what they're using to find their way. Which is the part most writing courses do not teach, on the grounds that it belongs to somebody else. The US federal plain language guide doesn't. One of its four parts is Design for understanding, a quarter of the whole subject, and its opening claim is blunt. Dense, cluttered writing deters people from taking the time to read.

S1: Start with headings. The test is short, and it disqualifies most of the headings you've written. A heading has to give a reader enough to decide whether to stop.

S2: Background. Context. Introduction. Next steps.

S1: Every one a label that would fit almost any document in the world, so none tells a reader anything about this one. The federal guidance asks for heading terms that are clear and descriptive, and descriptive means it describes this content, not the kind of content.

S2: Give me the before and after.

S1: Background becomes, why the old system was replaced in April. Next steps becomes, what we need from you by the fourteenth of March. Considerations becomes, three things that could delay this. Analysis becomes, costs came in eleven percent over budget.

S2: And reading only the second column, I know roughly what the document says.

S1: Which you can't do with the first.

S2: GOV.UK has a list of criteria, I think.

S1: Four. Headings should be descriptive, frontloaded, active and removable. The fourth is the one worth stopping on.

S2: Removable? That sounds like the opposite.

S1: Their wording is, the content should still make sense with the headings removed. Both have to be true at once. Strip them and the prose still has to work, because plenty of readers, including anyone listening, will meet it that way. Keep them and they still have to tell the story on their own, because that's what a skimmer gets. Fail the first and you've put content in your headings. Fail the second and you've put labels there.

S2: Is there a case where the category labels are right?

S1: There is. Introduction, Methodology, Findings, Discussion, Conclusion. In a scientific paper those are a convention readers know how to use, and someone looking for the method knows where to go. They work because the structure is standardised across thousands of papers.

S2: And the failure is importing them somewhere with no convention.

S1: Internal reports, proposals, briefing notes. There, Findings tells a reader nothing, because they've no idea what kind of finding to expect. So a category heading only works when the category is a convention your reader knows the shape of. Otherwise it's a word occupying the most valuable line on the page.

S2: Right. Prose, list, table. Isn't that just taste?

S1: Each makes a different claim about the content, and choosing the wrong one tells the reader something untrue. Prose says, these things are related and the relations matter. Because, therefore, although, unless. Prose is the only form that can carry an argument, because an argument is made of joins.

S1: A list says these items are parallel and independent. Each stands on its own and could be reordered without loss. The federal guide says to use lists for easy scanning. A table says this has two dimensions. Every item is described by the same properties and the reader wants to compare across them. Their wording is, where appropriate, use tables to make complex relationships clear, and the operative word is relationships, plural.

S2: So the question is?

S1: What relationship does this content actually have. Not what would look tidier. And one more distinction. A bulleted list claims the items are parallel and unordered. A numbered list claims they're in a sequence that matters.

S2: The lesson works a real note, doesn't it.

S1: A consultation note about a parking scheme, and every word of it is fine. Four parts. The response figures, the objections, the proposals, and the committee timing.

S1: The objections become a table: three things of one kind, each with a count, and the question is which is biggest. The proposals become a bulleted list: three parallel changes that could be reordered without loss. The response figures stay as prose, because four times what we normally see, and a third came in during the final week, are related to each other and to the coverage in the local paper. And the committee timing stays as prose, because it holds a condition and a consequence, and a list would cut that join.

S2: And what's new in the shaped version?

S1: The four headings, and nothing else. The prose sections are the note's own sentences unchanged, and the table cells and bullets are its own phrases lifted out.

S1: The note said, the main objection, raised in two hundred and sixty responses, was the cost of a residents' permit. The table row says, the cost of a residents' permit, two hundred and sixty. So, the main objection, raised in, was, has gone. So has, the second most common, and, a smaller number.

S2: Those were doing work.

S1: They told the reader which count was biggest and that the three were ranked. In the table the structure does that, because the rows are in order and the numbers are in a column the eye runs down. A table deletes the words the prose needed to carry the comparison, and carries it itself.

S2: And the counts add up?

S1: Two hundred and sixty, a hundred and fourteen and thirty eight come to four hundred and twelve, the number of responses. Nobody is counted twice.

S2: You promised me that bullets can destroy an argument.

S1: The original. We should delay the launch until July. The regional teams have flagged capacity problems over the summer, and although the Q two figures are strong, they are strong in exactly the regions that would be short staffed. A May launch would therefore put our thinnest support cover against our highest demand.

S2: And bulleted?

S1: Four bullets. Regional teams have flagged capacity problems over the summer. Q two figures are strong. The strong regions are the ones that would be short staffed. A May launch puts thin support cover against high demand.

S2: Every fact is still there.

S1: The argument is not. Three words are missing, and none of them is a fact, which is why a bullet by bullet check finds nothing missing. Although, exactly, and therefore.

S2: Say what each was doing.

S1: Although told you the strong Q two figures cut against the case and were about to be answered. Exactly told you the third fact was the answer. Therefore told you the fourth was the conclusion. Without them, four claims sit side by side as if they were four separate observations of equal weight, and the reader has to work out which is the objection and which is the reply.

S2: So the list is a false statement about the content.

S1: And the falsehood is invisible, because it lives in the formatting rather than in any sentence.

S2: Last thing. What do people get wrong?

S1: That headings are for long documents, when a three hundred word email with two informative headings is easier to act on than the same email in one block. The threshold isn't length, it's whether the reader might want to go straight to one part. And that formatting is the designer's job, when deciding three things are a table and three others are a list is deciding what relationship the content has.

S2: And adding headings at the end?

S1: A heading written last is written by someone describing a section they've finished rather than telling a reader what's in it, and that's the frame that produces Background. Write them as you go.

S2: The full lesson has the note in both forms and an exercise where you restructure a document without changing a sentence. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Go and read your own headings on their own.
