# Outline: Writing Clearly

*Stage 2, written 2026-09-08 from `research/SOURCES.md`. Sequence for how ideas build, not how
textbooks list chapters. This replaces the four placeholder lessons that are currently live; nothing
from them is carried over except the four topics, which survive as parts of lessons 2, 6, 3 and 8.*

## The shape of the course, and why

The field has two traditions and the course has to choose. The **rule tradition** tells writers what
not to do. The **reader-expectation tradition** describes what readers actually do with structure, and
lets the writer draw their own conclusions. Gopen and Swan and Williams are the second; *The Elements
of Style* is read as the first, and lesson 8 shows why that reading is half unfair and half deserved.

**This course teaches the second tradition**, and the sequence follows from it. A reader-expectation
course cannot open with rules, because it has not yet earned the reader's belief that reading is a
process with regularities. So lesson 1 is evidence: this is what happens to your words when someone
else meets them. Everything after that is a claim about readers, tested on the reader themselves.

Nine lessons. The current four total 1,806 words; these should each be what the teaching needs, which
on the evidence of the courses already at standard means five to eight thousand.

**A constraint that shapes every lesson.** This is the course about writing well. Every sentence of it
is a claim about how to write, tested against itself. A flabby lesson here is worse than a flabby
lesson anywhere else on the site, and the voice pass on this course is not optional polish.

## Lesson sequence

### 1. What actually happens when someone reads you (~55 min)

- **Objectives:** explain what the evidence says about how much of a page a reader takes in, and under
  what conditions; identify who a given piece of writing is actually for; state the difference between
  a text being difficult and a reader finding it difficult.
- **Core ideas:** Writing is a transaction with someone who is not obliged to continue. Clarity is not
  politeness or simplicity; it is the difference between being understood and not. Plain writing is not
  writing down.
- **Mechanism to explain:** the reading budget. Nielsen's regression is the concrete form: about 25
  seconds of attention on an ordinary page plus 4.4 seconds for each additional 100 words, so on an
  average page a visitor has time for about 28% of the words and realistically reads about 20%. Show
  the arithmetic on a real 800-word page.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: take the same 60-word notice and mark which 20% a skimming reader
  would get under the F-shaped scan, twice, with the point in different places. (b) with a wrinkle: a
  case where the number does *not* apply, using Nielsen's own caveats. His participants had
  "above-average intelligence, with several being university employees," and very long pages were
  excluded. A contract, a textbook, or a letter from a doctor is read differently. **The wrinkle is the
  lesson: a statistic about web pages is not a law about reading.**
- **Misconceptions to address:** "Plain writing is dumbing down" (answered by GOV.UK's own finding that
  "people with higher levels of literacy prefer plain English because it allows them to understand the
  information as quickly as possible"); "my readers are like me"; "if they care, they will read it
  properly."
- **Practice:** take something the learner wrote in the last month, count the words, apply the
  arithmetic, and mark the sentence they would most want a 20% reader to land on. Keep it: this
  document is the spine of the course project.
- **Sources:** Nielsen (2008), with caveats; GOV.UK clear language, including the one-in-six literacy
  figure; Gooding et al. (2021) for reader background affecting perceived difficulty; Plain Writing Act
  2010 and the GOV.UK mandate for the fact that this is a legal obligation somewhere, not a taste.
- **Connects:** leads to lesson 2, which is what to do about the 20%.
- **Chart:** the reading-budget line, words against seconds, with the 20% and 28% bands marked. Drawn
  from Nielsen's own figures, labelled with the sample and the caveat.

### 2. Put the point where the reader is looking (~55 min)

- **Objectives:** apply front-loading at the level of the document, the section and the paragraph;
  rewrite a buried-point email so the decision is in the first sentence; explain why context must come
  before the thing it contextualises.
- **Core ideas:** Front-loading. Gopen and Swan's principle 6, "provide context for your reader before
  asking consideration of anything new," which is the reason front-loading works rather than a
  restatement of it. The inverted pyramid as a special case, not a law.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why a reader who does not know where a passage is going has to hold
  everything in suspense, and what that costs them. This is the honest version of "get to the point."
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: the five-clause preamble email, rewritten. (b) with a wrinkle: a
  case where the point must *not* come first, such as bad news that needs its reason first to be
  understood rather than merely resented, or an argument whose conclusion would be rejected out of hand
  without its premises. **Front-loading is a default, not a rule**, and the wrinkle is where the course
  earns trust.
- **Misconceptions to address:** "the inverted pyramid is always right"; "front-loading means being
  blunt"; "a summary at the end is the same thing."
- **Practice:** front-load three real documents the learner has to hand, one of which should be one
  where they conclude it is wrong to do so, with the reason written down.
- **Sources:** Gopen and Swan principle 6; GOV.UK on front-loading and headings; carries lesson 1's
  reading budget forward.
- **Connects:** builds on lesson 1; the paragraph-level version is completed in lesson 5, once topic
  and stress position exist.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's reading budget, applied to a longer document.

### 3. Characters and actions (~65 min)

- **Objectives:** identify the real character and the real action in a sentence; rewrite a sentence so
  the character is the subject and the action is the verb; recognise a nominalisation and decide
  whether to undo it.
- **Core ideas:** **The highest-yield sentence-level move in the whole course.** Williams' two
  principles, which are Gopen and Swan's 3 and 5: make central characters the subjects of verbs, and
  articulate the action of every clause in its verb. Nominalisation as the standard failure.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why a buried action is hard to read. A sentence whose verb is *was made*,
  *occurred* or *is* forces the reader to reconstruct who did what from the nouns. Show the
  reconstruction happening.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: Strunk's own pair from 1918, "A survey of this region was made in
  1900" against "This region was surveyed in 1900". **Point out that both are passive.** Strunk is not
  attacking the passive here; he is attacking an action buried in a noun, which is the same point
  Williams makes sixty years later. This example does a second job: it prepares lesson 8. (b) with a
  wrinkle: an official sentence with three nominalisations and no character named at all, where undoing
  it forces the writer to decide who is responsible, and where that is exactly why it was written that
  way.
- **Misconceptions to address:** "nominalisations are always bad" (they are how you make an idea the
  subject of a sentence, which is often what you want); "this is just a rule about -ion words." GOV.UK's
  "-ion and -ment" guidance is a cheap detector, not a definition, and the lesson should say so.
- **Practice:** a set of ten sentences to diagnose, character and action named for each before any
  rewriting. Then rewrite five. Then find three in the learner's own document from lesson 1.
- **Sources:** Williams lessons 2 and 3; Gopen and Swan principles 3 and 5; Strunk 1918 rule 10's
  nominalisation paragraph; GOV.UK on -ion and -ment.
- **Connects:** builds on nothing before it, which is why it can come this early; leads to lesson 4,
  which is the same sentence seen from the reader's position rather than the writer's.

### 4. Keep the subject next to the verb (~50 min)

- **Objectives:** explain what a reader does when a subject and its verb are separated; find the gap in
  a long sentence and close it; decide when an interruption is worth its cost.
- **Core ideas:** Gopen and Swan's principle 1, in their words: "Readers expect a grammatical subject
  to be followed immediately by the verb. Anything of length that intervenes between subject and verb
  is read as an interruption."
- **Mechanism to explain:** the reader is holding the subject in memory, waiting. Every word of the
  interruption is carried at a cost. This is why long sentences by good writers are easy and long
  sentences by bad writers are not, which is the honest answer to "keep sentences short."
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a 40-word sentence with a 22-word interruption, fixed three
  different ways (move it to the front, move it to the end, split the sentence), with what each version
  emphasises. (b) with a wrinkle: a long sentence that is perfectly readable because subject and verb
  are adjacent, set against a 20-word sentence that is not because they are not. **Length is a proxy;
  structure is the thing.**
- **Misconceptions to address:** "short sentences are clear sentences"; "vary your sentence length" as
  advice without a reason. GOV.UK's 25-word rule is a place to look, not a definition of clarity, and
  the lesson quotes it as such.
- **Practice:** measure subject-to-verb distance in a page of the learner's own writing; fix the worst
  three. A count, not an impression.
- **Sources:** Gopen and Swan principle 1; GOV.UK on sentence length.
- **Connects:** builds on lesson 3; with lesson 5 it completes the sentence.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 3's characters and actions, since the subject you are keeping near the
  verb should be the character.

### 5. Topic position and stress position (~70 min)

- **Objectives:** identify the topic and stress positions of a sentence; apply the given-new contract
  to a paragraph that feels choppy and explain what changed; predict which word a reader will emphasise.
- **Core ideas:** **The deepest idea in the course.** Gopen and Swan's principles 2, 4 and 7 and the
  given-new contract in their exact words: "Put in the topic position the old information that links
  backward; put in the stress position the new information you want the reader to emphasize." This is
  what cohesion actually is.
- **Mechanism to explain:** "Readers expect a unit of discourse to be a story about whoever shows up
  first," and "readers naturally emphasize the material that arrives at the end of a sentence." So the
  writer is not choosing word order for elegance; they are choosing what the reader will think the
  passage is about and what they will take away.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a five-sentence paragraph whose sentences are individually fine and
  which reads as a list of unrelated facts, fixed only by moving old information to the front of each
  sentence. Nothing deleted, nothing added. (b) with a wrinkle: Strunk's own 1918 pair, "The dramatists
  of the Restoration are little esteemed to-day" against "Modern readers have little esteem for the
  dramatists of the Restoration," with his own explanation that "the need of making a particular word
  the subject of the sentence will often determine which voice is to be used." **The passive is
  sometimes the right choice, and the reason is the topic position.** Pullum's split-infinitive point
  belongs here too: "tend to completely polarize the faculty" stresses the polarising, and to stress
  completeness you write "polarize the faculty completely."
- **Misconceptions to address:** "never use the passive"; "put the important word first" (usually
  wrong: the stress position is the end); "transitions are what makes writing flow" (transition words
  are a patch over a topic-position problem).
- **Practice:** take the choppy paragraph from the worked example and a second one from the learner's
  own writing; mark the topic and stress position of every sentence; rewrite without adding a word.
- **Sources:** Gopen and Swan principles 2, 4, 7 and the given-new contract; Pullum on stress and the
  split infinitive; Strunk 1918 rule 10.
- **Connects:** completes the sentence work of 3 and 4; makes the paragraph-level half of lesson 2
  finally explicable.
- **Spaced review of:** lessons 2, 3 and 4, all of which are special cases of this.
- **Chart:** a sentence drawn as a bar with topic and stress positions marked, the same content shown
  in two orders with what the reader takes away from each.

### 6. Concision that is actually teachable (~55 min)

- **Objectives:** name the specific kinds of redundancy in a passage; cut a document by a third without
  losing content; explain why "omit needless words" is not usable advice on its own.
- **Core ideas:** Concision as a set of identifiable patterns rather than a virtue. Strunk's maxim in
  full, because it is better than its reputation: "Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should
  contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a
  drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts."
- **Mechanism to explain:** why the maxim fails as instruction even though it is true. Pullum's
  objection is the honest one and the lesson should quote it: "The students who know which words are
  needless don't need the instruction." So the lesson supplies what the maxim omits, which is the list.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a 120-word paragraph reduced to 70 with each cut labelled by kind
  (doubled words, what the sentence already implies, metadiscourse, a phrase for a word, negatives
  turned positive). (b) with a wrinkle: a passage where cutting makes it worse, because the redundancy
  was doing cohesive work that lesson 5 explains. **Repetition of the old information in the topic
  position is not padding.**
- **Misconceptions to address:** "shorter is always better"; "cut every adjective and adverb"; "never
  repeat a word" (the thesaurus reflex, which lesson 9 names as the classic novice error).
- **Practice:** cut one of the learner's own documents by a third, labelling every cut by kind, then
  give the original and the cut version to another person and ask which is clearer. The count is not
  the point; the labels are.
- **Sources:** Williams lesson 8 (Concision); Strunk 1918 rule 13; Pullum's objection to it; GOV.UK's
  word-choice list.
- **Connects:** depends on lesson 5, because you cannot tell padding from cohesion without it.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 5's given-new contract, from the opposite direction.

### 7. Shape at the document level (~55 min)

- **Objectives:** structure a document so a skimming reader can find what they need; write headings
  that carry information; decide when a list or a table beats a paragraph.
- **Core ideas:** Design is part of writing, not decoration applied afterwards. A quarter of the US
  federal plain-language guide is "Design for understanding," and the current course has nothing on it.
- **Mechanism to explain:** the skimming reader from lesson 1 does not read the document, they navigate
  it. Headings, lists and tables are the navigation. A heading that says "Background" tells them
  nothing; a heading that says "Why the launch moved to July" tells them whether to stop.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a 600-word all-prose policy note restructured with informative
  headings and one table, with the same words. (b) with a wrinkle: a document made worse by
  bullet points, where a list destroys the argument's connective tissue because the relations between
  the items were the content. **A list is a claim that the items are parallel and independent.**
- **Misconceptions to address:** "bullet points are always clearer"; "headings are for long documents";
  "formatting is the designer's job."
- **Practice:** restructure a real document, then write its headings as a standalone list and check
  whether that list alone tells the story.
- **Sources:** digital.gov, Design for understanding; GOV.UK on headings and front-loading; carries
  lesson 1's evidence.
- **Connects:** scales lessons 2 and 5 up from the sentence and paragraph to the document.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 2's front-loading, at document scale.

### 8. Rules, and where they come from (~70 min)

- **Objectives:** distinguish a rule that describes how readers process prose from one that asserts a
  prohibition; check a usage rule against evidence yourself; explain what a passive is and when it is
  the right choice.
- **Core ideas:** **The course's contested question, taught as a live disagreement and as a method.**
  Two traditions, one famous book, one famous attack on it, and what happens when you open the book.
- **Mechanism to explain:** how to test a usage claim. Pullum's own method: take the rule, take a
  corpus of the era, and count. He uses "none of us" in Wilde, Stoker and Montgomery and finds plural
  agreement in all three. Project Gutenberg is the corpus and the learner can repeat it in ten minutes.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: the passive. What one actually is, why most people who ban it cannot
  identify one (Pullum: it is "typical for college graduates today to be unable to distinguish active
  from passive clauses"), and Pullum's own pair, "The bill was paid by me" against "The bill was paid by
  an anonymous benefactor." (b) with a wrinkle, and this is the heart of the lesson: **the Strunk
  episode, worked in front of the reader.** Pullum charges that three of the four example pairs under
  "Use the active voice" are not passives. Open Strunk 1918. The three sentences are there, and they are
  introduced by Strunk himself as examples of "some such perfunctory expression as *there is*, or *could
  be heard*". He did not call them passives. Meanwhile the *none* rule and the anti-*which* rule that
  Pullum demolishes **are not in the 1918 book at all**, so they belong to White. Both men are partly
  right about different things: the organisational failure is real and the damage Pullum documents is
  real, but the charge of grammatical incompetence does not follow from these examples.
- **Misconceptions to address:** "never split an infinitive"; "never start a sentence with however, or
  but, or and"; "never use which for a restrictive clause"; "the style book contains the rules of
  English." Each gets the evidence, not an assertion.
- **Practice:** pick a writing rule the learner was taught, find its source, and test it against a
  corpus. Write down what you found, including if it held up.
- **Sources:** Pullum (2009) in full, including his concession that the style advice is "mostly
  harmless"; Strunk (1918) in full; Gopen and Swan's caveat that "none of these reader-expectation
  principles should be considered 'rules.'"
- **Connects:** re-reads lessons 3, 5 and 6 as descriptions rather than commandments.
- **Spaced review of:** the whole sentence sequence, 3 to 6.
- **Neutrality note:** see below. This is the one lesson in the course where a named living scholar and
  two dead authors are in dispute, and each must be given his own case in his own words.

### 9. Revising, and testing it on a real reader (~60 min)

- **Objectives:** distinguish revising from editing and say what each is for; run a paraphrase test on
  your own writing; explain what a readability score does and does not measure.
- **Core ideas:** Revision is structural and comes first; editing is sentence-level and comes second.
  And then the step almost nobody teaches: **find out whether it worked.**
- **Mechanism to explain:** paraphrase testing, from the federal guide. Give a reader a section, ask
  them to say in their own words what it means, and listen for the gap between that and what you meant.
  The guide's own scoping: individual interviews, best for short pieces, and "with only 6 to 9
  participants, paraphrase testing will not take a lot of time." Usability testing for longer documents
  and forms "where finding the right information is important"; controlled comparative studies only
  after the small methods.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a transcript of a paraphrase test where the reader's summary is
  fluent, confident and wrong, with the sentence that misled them identified. (b) with a wrinkle: a
  passage that a readability formula scores well and a real reader cannot use, because formulas count
  syllables and words per sentence and know nothing about the reader. Gooding et al. is the honest
  citation: reader background changes what makes a text feel difficult, so difficulty is a relation, not
  a property. **Splitting a sentence in half improves the score without changing the meaning conveyed.**
- **Misconceptions to address:** "revision means finding better words" (the classic novice error; the
  Sommers finding, *if the gap below is closed*); "a grade-level score measures clarity"; "if I
  understand it, it is clear."
- **Practice:** run one paraphrase test on one page, with one real person, and write down the gap. This
  is the exercise the learner will remember.
- **Sources:** digital.gov Test for understanding; Sommers (1980) **subject to the gap note**; Gooding
  et al. (2021); Williams' distinction between clarity and grace.
- **Connects:** closes the course; the learner's document from lesson 1 has now been through every
  lesson.
- **Spaced review of:** everything, by applying it to one document.

## Assessments

### Course-end test blueprint

Twenty items, at least twelve of them application rather than recall, per standards 4.3. Options within
about 15 characters of each other, all four answer indices used, no adjacent repeat, at least two
options per item carrying no absolute.

| Objective area | Items | Kind |
|---|---|---|
| The reading budget and who a document is for (L1) | 2 | 1 recall, 1 application |
| Front-loading, and when not to (L2) | 2 | both application, one of them a case where front-loading is wrong |
| Characters and actions; nominalisation (L3) | 4 | 1 identification, 3 rewriting judgements |
| Subject-verb distance (L4) | 2 | both application |
| Topic and stress position; given-new (L5) | 4 | 1 identification, 3 application including one passive-is-correct item |
| Concision by kind (L6) | 2 | 1 labelling a cut, 1 where cutting is wrong |
| Document shape (L7) | 2 | 1 heading quality, 1 list-versus-prose judgement |
| Rules and evidence (L8) | 1 | application: which of these claims could be checked against a corpus |
| Revision and testing (L9) | 1 | application: what a paraphrase test would reveal |

Every distractor must be a real error someone makes, not a filler. The passive items in particular
should punish the "any *be* is passive" reflex, since that is the misconception the course exists to
correct.

### Project: one document, taken all the way

**Deliverable:** one real document of the learner's own, 400 to 1,200 words, submitted in four parts.

1. The original, untouched, with the date it was written.
2. A diagnosis, one page: who the reader is and what they need; where the point currently sits; three
   sentences whose character and action are buried; the topic and stress positions of one paragraph
   that does not cohere; the document's headings listed on their own.
3. The rewrite.
4. A paraphrase-test record: one real reader, what they said the document meant in their own words,
   the gap, and what was changed as a result. **If nothing needed changing, say so and say how you
   know.**

**Rubric:** diagnosis names specific sentences rather than impressions (the commonest failure); the
rewrite's changes are traceable to named principles; at least one place where the learner decided a
principle did not apply here, with the reason; the paraphrase-test record reports what the reader
actually said, including if it was inconvenient.

## Neutrality notes

This is **not** a sensitive domain under standards 3.4, so the Stage 4 neutrality pass is skipped for
lessons 1 to 7 and 9, with a line in REVIEWS.md recording that. Two exceptions:

- **Lesson 8 is a genuine dispute between named people** and needs a Part 3.2 check, not a full audit:
  does Strunk get his own case in his own words, does Pullum get his concession that the style advice is
  "mostly harmless" and not only his harshest sentence, and is the reader left able to state each
  position as its holder would? The research file already flags that White's 1959 edition has not been
  read, so the lesson must not attribute to White anything not verified in a text someone opened.
- **Lesson 1's literacy statistics** describe real people and must not be written in a way that treats
  low literacy as stupidity. GOV.UK's own framing, that plain English serves high-literacy readers too,
  is the tone to follow.

## Open concerns, and what will be hard to do well

- **Two research gaps still block two lessons.** Sommers (1980) is unread and lesson 9's central
  misconception rests on it. No primary critique of readability formulas was reachable and lesson 9's
  second worked example needs one. Close both before drafting lesson 9, or drop those claims.
- **Lesson 5 is the hardest lesson in the course** and the one most likely to come out as abstract
  hand-waving. It needs the paragraph that visibly repairs itself with nothing added or deleted, and if
  that example is not genuinely convincing the lesson fails. Build the example first, then the lesson.
- **Lesson 8 could easily become a hit piece or an apologia.** The material is entertaining and the
  temptation is to enjoy Pullum's prose at Strunk's expense. The test is whether a reader who loves
  *The Elements of Style* finishes it feeling fairly treated.
- **Every exercise asks for the learner's own writing.** That is right, and it means the course cannot
  check the work. The project's paraphrase test is the only real feedback loop, which is why it is a
  requirement and not a suggestion.
- **The course must practise what it teaches.** A reviewer should run lesson 5's own test on lesson 5's
  own paragraphs. If the course's prose fails its own diagnosis, the diagnosis is what learners will
  remember.
