# Sources: Writing Clearly

*Stage 1 research, written 2026-09-08, on a course that has been live since the site launched with four
placeholder lessons averaging 452 words. Nothing in those four lessons was built from research; this file
is the first research the course has had, and the lessons are being replaced rather than improved. For
each source: what it is good for and what it establishes. Where a source was not actually opened, this
file says so, because the pipeline's worst recurring defect is citing books nobody read.*

## Primary sources

- **George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing," *American Scientist* 78
  (November–December 1990), 550–558.** Full text read at
  [wstomv.win.tue.nl](https://wstomv.win.tue.nl/quotes/science-of-scientific-writing.html). **This is the
  spine of the course.** Its move is to stop giving rules about writing and start describing what readers
  do with structure. The claims it establishes, in the authors' words:
  - "The information that begins a sentence establishes for the reader a perspective for viewing the
    sentence as a unit: Readers expect a unit of discourse to be a story about whoever shows up first."
  - "It is a linguistic commonplace that readers naturally emphasize the material that arrives at the
    end of a sentence." (the *stress position*)
  - "Readers expect a grammatical subject to be followed immediately by the verb. Anything of length
    that intervenes between subject and verb is read as an interruption."
  - The given/new contract: "Put in the topic position the old information that links backward; put in
    the stress position the new information you want the reader to emphasize."

  Their seven structural principles, which are the best candidate for the course's own backbone:
  (1) follow a grammatical subject as soon as possible with its verb; (2) place in the stress position
  the new information you want emphasised; (3) place the person or thing whose story a sentence is
  telling at the beginning; (4) place appropriate old information in the topic position for linkage and
  context; (5) articulate the action of every clause or sentence in its verb; (6) provide context before
  asking consideration of anything new; (7) ensure relative emphases of substance coincide with
  structural expectations for emphasis.

  **The caveat matters as much as the principles, and must survive into the lessons:** "None of these
  reader-expectation principles should be considered 'rules.'" A course that turns them into a checklist
  has misread its own main source.

- **Geoffrey K. Pullum, "50 Years of Stupid Grammar Advice," *The Chronicle of Higher Education*, 17
  April 2009.** Full text read. Pullum is head of linguistics and English language at Edinburgh and
  co-author of *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*. What it establishes, precisely:
  - Of the four pairs of examples Strunk and White offer under "Use the active voice," **three are not
    passive at all.** "At dawn the crowing of a rooster could be heard" is correctly identified. "There
    were a great number of dead leaves lying on the ground" "has no sign of the passive in it anywhere."
    "It was not long before she was very sorry that she had said what she had" "contains nothing that is
    even reminiscent of the passive construction." And in "The reason that he left college was that his
    health became impaired," *impaired* is an adjective, not a passive; "'Become' doesn't allow a
    following passive clause."
  - The evidence against the "none takes a singular verb" rule, gathered from books Strunk's own
    contemporaries wrote: Wilde's *The Importance of Being Earnest* ("None of us are perfect"), Stoker's
    *Dracula* ("I think that none of us were surprised"), Montgomery's *Anne of Avonlea* ("None of us
    ever do"). This is a teachable method, not just a fact: **you can check a usage rule against a corpus
    yourself**, and Pullum names Project Gutenberg as the corpus.
  - The "which" rule: "There was never a period in the history of English when 'which' at the beginning
    of a restrictive relative clause was an error," and White "not only added the anti-'which' rule to
    the book but also revised away the counterexamples that were present in his old professor's original
    text."
  - The split-infinitive claim is descriptively backwards: tucking the adverb before the verb
    *de-emphasises* it, so "tend to completely polarize the faculty" stresses the polarising, and
    "polarize the faculty completely" is the way to stress completeness. This is the same stress-position
    fact Gopen and Swan teach, arriving from a different direction, and the two sources agreeing is worth
    showing the reader.

  **The qualifier that must not be trimmed** (defect 9): Pullum is not attacking the style advice. He
  says explicitly that what he objects to "is not the style advice in *Elements*, which might best be
  described the way The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy describes Earth: mostly harmless," that some
  recommendations are "vapid," "tautologous" or "useless" but that "it doesn't hurt to lay such
  well-meant maxims before novice writers." His charge is against the *grammar* claims: "Its enormous
  influence has not improved American students' grasp of English grammar; it has significantly degraded
  it." A lesson that quotes the second sentence without the first misrepresents him.

- **William Strunk Jr., *The Elements of Style* (privately printed, 1918).** Public domain, read in
  full at [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134.txt). Reading it
  changed this file's contested question, which is the argument for the rule that produced it.

  Strunk's stated aim is modest: the book "aims to give in brief space the principal requirements of
  plain English style," concentrating on "essentials, the rules of usage and principles of composition
  most commonly violated." It does not claim to be a grammar.

  **Rule 10, "Use the active voice," does not say what its critics report it saying.** Strunk states the
  qualification himself, in 1918, in the rule's own third paragraph: "This rule does not, of course, mean
  that the writer should entirely discard the passive voice, which is frequently convenient and sometimes
  necessary." He then gives the pair "The dramatists of the Restoration are little esteemed to-day" and
  "Modern readers have little esteem for the dramatists of the Restoration," and says which is right
  depends on what the paragraph is about: "The need of making a particular word the subject of the
  sentence will often, as in these examples, determine which voice is to be used." **That is the topic
  position, seventy-two years before Gopen and Swan named it.** Strunk gets the reader-expectation point
  right and files it under a rule that reads like a prohibition, which may be the most useful single
  fact in this whole file.

  **On Pullum's central charge, the primary source substantially exonerates Strunk.** Pullum says three
  of the four example pairs under "Use the active voice" are "mistaken diagnoses" of the passive. Three
  of those four sentences do appear in the 1918 rule 10. But they appear in a final paragraph that Strunk
  introduces this way: "Many a tame sentence of description or exposition can be made lively and emphatic
  by substituting a verb in the active voice for **some such perfunctory expression as *there is*, or
  *could be heard*.**" He is not calling them passives. He names the fault himself, and it is the
  existential *there were* and the limp *could be heard*, which is a real and teachable fault.

  So the honest account is that **both men are partly right, and about different things**. The examples
  do sit inside a rule headed "Use the active voice," so a reader can easily take them as passives, and
  by Pullum's account generations of writing tutors did. That is a failure of organisation, and its
  consequences are exactly what Pullum documents. But it is not evidence that Strunk could not tell a
  passive from an adjective, because Strunk did not say those sentences were passive.

  **Two of Pullum's specific charges land on White, not Strunk, and the 1918 text is the proof.**
  The rule that *none* takes a singular verb, which Pullum demolishes with Wilde, Stoker and Montgomery,
  **is not in the 1918 edition at all**. Neither is the prohibition on *which* in restrictive relative
  clauses; Strunk's rule 3 simply distinguishes restrictive from non-restrictive clauses and punctuates
  them, using "The candidate who best meets these requirements will obtain the place" against "The
  audience, which had at first been indifferent, became more interested." Pullum says White added the
  anti-*which* rule and revised away his old professor's counterexamples. The 1918 text is consistent
  with that.

  One example pair Pullum does not mention is worth teaching on its own, because it is the nominalisation
  problem stated plainly in 1918: "A common fault is to use as the subject of a passive construction a
  noun which expresses the entire action, leaving to the verb no function beyond that of completing the
  sentence." His examples: "A survey of this region was made in 1900" against "This region was surveyed
  in 1900"; "Mobilization of the army was rapidly effected" against "The army was rapidly mobilized."
  **Note that both of Strunk's preferred versions are themselves passive.** He is not attacking the
  passive there at all; he is attacking actions buried in nouns, which is Williams' and Gopen and Swan's
  central point.

## Canonical textbooks

- **Joseph M. Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*** (originally *Style: Ten Lessons in
  Clarity and Grace*, 1981; 13th edition 2021, with Gregory G. Colomb and Joseph Bizup). The standard
  university text for this subject. **Contents read, book not read.** Structure: Part One, Style as
  Choice (Lesson 1, Correctness); Part Two, Clarity (Actions, Characters, Cohesion and Coherence,
  Emphasis); Part Three, Clarity of Form (Framing documents and sections); Part Four, Grace (Concision,
  Shape, Elegance); Part Five, Ethics. Its two central principles, as consistently reported: **make
  central characters the subjects of verbs, and use verbs to name the actions those characters are
  involved in.** That is the same idea as Gopen and Swan's principles 3 and 5, which is strong evidence
  it is the real core of the field rather than one author's hobby-horse.

  **The reception fact worth the course's attention: Pullum recommends Williams.** The linguist who
  called *Elements* the work of "idiosyncratic bumblers" endorses a different style book. That resolves
  the contested question below better than any argument: the complaint is not that writing advice is
  impossible, it is that advice should describe how readers process prose rather than assert invented
  rules.

- **Strunk and White, *The Elements of Style*** (1959; Strunk privately 1918). Cannot be summarised here
  responsibly: see the gap note above. It has sold millions and is, in Pullum's account, "just about all
  of the grammar instruction most Americans ever get," so the course cannot ignore it and must not
  characterise it second-hand.

## Expert bodies and official guidance

- **GOV.UK, "Use clear language"**
  ([guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/)).
  The best source of *concrete, testable* rules, from an organisation that writes for an entire country
  and measures whether people succeed. Plain English is mandatory across GOV.UK. Its specific rules:
  split sentences over 25 words; paragraphs of no more than 5 sentences; prefer "buy" to "purchase" and
  "help" to "assist"; watch words ending in *-ion* and *-ment*, which "tend to make sentences longer and
  more complicated" (this is the nominalisation problem, stated as a rule of thumb an ordinary writer can
  apply); avoid government jargon, which is "too vague and can lead to misinterpretation"; use the active
  voice because it "is more direct and puts the focus on the user"; avoid negative contractions, because
  "many users find negative contractions hard to read, or misread them."

  Two findings the course should teach rather than bury. The reason given is not elegance but access:
  **"1 in 6 adults in England have very poor literacy skills."** And the objection every clever writer
  raises is answered directly: **"people with higher levels of literacy prefer plain English because it
  allows them to understand the information as quickly as possible."** Plain writing is not writing down.

- **Digital.gov / plainlanguage.gov, the Plain Language guide series** (US federal). Four parts:
  Principles of plain language; Writing for understanding; Design for understanding; Test for
  understanding. Legal footing: **the Plain Writing Act of 2010** "established the requirement that
  content for the public is written for its specific audience." Best for: the fact that plain language is
  a legal obligation in one large government and a mandate in another, which is the answer to "is this
  just style?"

  Its **Test for understanding** section is the part most courses omit and this one should not. Methods,
  as the guide sets them out: **paraphrase testing** (individual interviews; ask a reader to say in their
  own words what a section means; "with only 6 to 9 participants, paraphrase testing will not take a lot
  of time"), best for short pages and survey questions; **usability testing**, best for longer documents
  and forms "where finding the right information is important"; and **controlled comparative studies** at
  large scale, which the guide says to attempt only after the small-scale methods.
  *(Read via the guide's own summaries of these pages; open each page before drafting the lesson that
  uses them.)*

## Key research

- **Jakob Nielsen, "How Little Do Users Read?" Nielsen Norman Group, 5 May 2008.** Reanalysis of
  Weinreich et al., who "instrumented 25 users' browsers and recorded extended information about
  everything they did"; 45,237 page views after cleaning. Findings: about 25 seconds per page baseline
  plus **4.4 seconds for each additional 100 words**, so on an average page "users will have time to read
  28% of the words" and "more realistically, users will read about 20% of the text." Establishes the
  empirical basis for front-loading the point.

  **Nielsen's own caveats must travel with the number**, because this statistic is quoted everywhere
  without them: participants had "above-average intelligence, with several being university employees,"
  and pages with very high word counts were excluded from the analysis. It is a finding about ordinary
  web pages skimmed by capable readers, not a law about all reading.

- **Sian Gooding, Yevgeni Berzak, Tony Mak and Matt Sharifi, "Predicting Text Readability from Scrolling
  Interactions" (2021), arXiv:2105.06354.** 518 participants. Abstract only. Two findings the course can
  use: reader interaction with a text differs measurably by text difficulty and can predict readability;
  and **reader background influences both reading patterns and what makes a text feel difficult.** The
  second is the honest correction to any "this text scores grade 8" claim: difficulty is a relation
  between a text and a reader, not a property of the text.

- **Nancy Sommers, "Revision Strategies of Student Writers and Experienced Adult Writers," *College
  Composition and Communication* 31 (December 1980).** **Not read; see the gap note.** As reported: 20
  first-year students and a group of experienced writers (academics, editors, journalists) each wrote
  three essays through successive drafts. The reported finding that matters for the editing lesson is
  that students treat revision as "a rewording activity" because they take the word as the unit of
  discourse, using a thesaurus as "a harvest of lexical substitutions," while experienced writers work
  with a codified sense of shape and argument. If that holds up on reading, it is the misconception the
  editing lesson exists to break.

## What the field considers essential

The order in which these build, drawn from Gopen and Swan's seven principles, Williams' part structure,
and the GOV.UK rules:

1. **Writing is a transaction with a reader who is not obliged to continue.** The Nielsen numbers, the
   GOV.UK literacy figures, and the plain-language mandates all say the same thing from different
   directions. This is the "why", and it is evidence, not exhortation.
2. **Put the point where the reader is looking for it.** Front-loading at the level of the document, the
   section and the paragraph. Gopen and Swan's principle 6 (context before anything new).
3. **Characters as subjects, actions as verbs.** Williams' core, Gopen and Swan's principles 3 and 5.
   This is the single highest-yield sentence-level move and the one the current placeholder course
   entirely lacks. Nominalisation is its shadow, and GOV.UK's "-ion and -ment" rule is the cheap test.
4. **Subject next to verb.** Gopen and Swan's principle 1.
5. **Topic position and stress position.** The given/new contract; principles 2, 4 and 7. This is the
   idea that explains cohesion, why paragraphs feel choppy, and why the split-infinitive intuition is
   backwards.
6. **Concision, correctly understood.** Not "omit needless words," which Pullum rightly calls useless
   advice ("the students who know which words are needless don't need the instruction"), but the
   specific, teachable kinds of redundancy Williams catalogues.
7. **Shape at the document level.** Headings, lists, tables, and design as part of writing, which is a
   full quarter of the federal guide and is absent from the current course.
8. **Revision as a distinct activity from editing**, and the misconception that separates novices from
   experts.
9. **Testing what you wrote on a real reader.** Paraphrase testing is cheap enough that a single learner
   can do it, and no popular writing course teaches it. This is the course's strongest chance to teach
   something the reader will not have met elsewhere.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Never use the passive."** Wrong twice over. Most people who repeat it cannot identify a passive
  (Pullum: it is "typical for college graduates today to be unable to distinguish active from passive
  clauses," and they often think "a bus exploded" is passive because it does not name an agent), and the
  passive is the right choice when the agent is unknown, unimportant, or newer to the discourse than the
  patient. Pullum's test case: "The bill was paid by me" is inane, "The bill was paid by an anonymous
  benefactor" is "perfectly natural."
- **"Short sentences are clear sentences."** Length is a proxy. Gopen and Swan's whole argument is that
  structure, not length, decides whether a sentence is readable; GOV.UK's 25-word rule is explicitly a
  rule of thumb about where to look, not a definition of clarity.
- **"A readability score measures clarity."** Formulas count syllables and words per sentence, which are
  surface proxies. Gooding et al.'s finding that reader background changes perceived difficulty is the
  clean statement of why. Splitting a sentence in half raises the score without changing what a reader
  understands.
- **"Revision means finding better words."** The Sommers finding, if it holds on reading.
- **"Plain writing is writing down to people."** Answered directly by GOV.UK's finding about high-literacy
  readers.
- **"The rules in the famous style book are the rules of English."** They are not, and several are
  demonstrably at odds with the usage of the era in which they were written.

## Contested questions and the positions on each

**1. What should a writer do with *The Elements of Style*?** *(Contested, Part 3.1: a live disagreement
among informed people. Reading the 1918 primary source moved this question and the lesson should show
that, because the move is the method.)*

- *The book's defenders* hold that it is a compact set of maxims that has demonstrably helped generations
  of novices, that its style advice is sound, and that its brevity is its virtue.
- *Pullum's position*, in his own terms: the style advice is "mostly harmless" and some of it is
  worthless but not damaging; the grammar is "atrocious," is often contradicted by the authors' own prose
  on the same page, and has "significantly degraded" a nation's grasp of its own grammar. His sharpest
  charge is that three of the four example pairs under "Use the active voice" are not passives at all.
- *What the 1918 text shows.* Strunk states the "don't discard the passive" qualification himself. He
  chooses between active and passive on what the paragraph is about, which is the topic-position
  principle. And the three sentences Pullum calls misdiagnosed passives are introduced by Strunk as
  examples of "some such perfunctory expression as *there is*, or *could be heard*", which is a
  different and defensible complaint. Meanwhile the *none* rule and the anti-*which* rule, two of
  Pullum's strongest hits, are **absent from 1918** and so belong to White.
- *Where that leaves it.* The organisational failure is real and its consequences are real: putting
  those examples under a heading that says "Use the active voice" taught a lot of people to red-circle
  anything containing *be*. Pullum's account of the damage stands. His account of Strunk's competence
  does not, on this evidence, for the 1918 book. **White's edition has not been read and the question of
  what he changed is open**; that is the gap to close.
- **The course's position, and the reason:** teach the reader-expectation tradition, which describes
  what readers do, rather than the rule tradition, which asserts what writers must not do. Say plainly
  that this is a choice between schools, and note that Pullum, the fiercest critic of the rule tradition,
  recommends Williams. Then show the reader this whole episode as a worked example of the course's own
  method: a famous book, a famous attack on it, and what happens when you open the book yourself.

**2. Do readability formulas measure anything useful?** *(Contested, with the expert view running against
popular practice.)* The criticism found repeatedly in the literature is that formulas rest on surface
proxies (characters or syllables per word, words per sentence) chosen for statistical correlation rather
than any theory of comprehension, and that they ignore layout, prior knowledge, motivation and the
reader entirely. **This research did not reach a primary peer-reviewed statement of that critique that
could be opened and read** (the strongest candidates were paywalled or unparseable), so the lesson must
either cite Gooding et al.'s reader-background finding, which was read at abstract level, or someone must
close this gap before the lesson is drafted. Do not cite the critique to a source nobody opened.

**3. Is plain language an obligation or a preference?** Mostly settled in the institutional world and
worth stating as such: mandatory across GOV.UK, and required in the US by the Plain Writing Act of 2010.
The contested part is scope, not principle: whether it applies to law, medicine and technical writing to
the same degree.

## Open concerns about scope

- **The course is currently four lessons averaging 452 words with no citations, no exercises beyond a
  single block each, no charts and no links.** It is not being improved; it is being replaced. The
  outline should assume nothing from it except the four topics, and those four do not cover the essential
  list above: nothing on characters and actions, nothing on topic and stress position, nothing on document
  shape, nothing on testing.
- **Recommended length: about nine lessons**, following the essential list. That matches Williams' own
  proportions (four of his lessons are on clarity alone) and gives the course a real project.
- **`course.yaml` needs work.** `level: All levels` is not one of the three levels the taxonomy allows
  (the map has it as Foundation); `estimated_hours: 1.6` was already wrong and is now stale after the
  reading-time pass. The outcomes list is close to right but omits the two biggest ideas in the field.
- **This is not a sensitive domain under standards 3.4**, so the neutrality pass can be skipped per the
  Stage 4 tiering, with one exception: question 1 above is a genuine dispute between named living and
  dead people, and the lesson that handles it should be checked for whether Strunk and White get their
  own case made in their own words. That is a Part 2 and Part 3.2 problem, not a full audit.
- **The course is about writing in English and should say so**, and should be careful that its advice on
  word choice ("buy" not "purchase") is British and American English alike. The GOV.UK source is British;
  the digital.gov source is American; they agree on substance, which is worth saying.
