# Reviews: Writing Clearly, lesson 6 — Concision that is actually teachable

## 2026-09-11 — Lesson 06 — depth, fact-check, neutrality, pedagogy, voice and media, Tier A
**Reviewer:** one subagent, fresh context, all passes. Given `course-wide.md` first.

**Verdict on the draft:** send back. The lesson's thesis was false of the source it was built on,
and six of the eight counts it states about passages printed beside them were wrong.

**CW-01:** clean on statistics. **CW-02:** six for six on "no Go deeper", "zero contractions",

### Findings

**Fact-check**

1. [critical] **F1.** The lesson quoted Strunk's rule 13 and stopped one sentence early. The rule
   continues: "**This requires not that the writer make all his sentences short, or that he avoid
   all detail and treat his subjects only in outline, but that he make every word tell.**" That is
   Strunk pre-empting the lesson's own first misconception, "Shorter is always better", which the
   lesson presented as something his readers invented.
   **And worse.** On the strength of that trim the lesson asserted, in bold, that "'Omit needless
   words' describes the finished state and says nothing about how to get there", and then "the
   rest of this lesson supplies what the maxim leaves out, which is the list." Rule 13 **is** a
   list. It gives "the question as to whether" to "whether", "there is no doubt but that" to "no
   doubt", "used for fuel purposes" to "used for fuel", "he is a man who" to "he", "in a hasty
   manner" to "hastily", a whole section on "the fact that", and more. Two of this lesson's six
   kinds are sitting in it. The lesson's thesis was false of the rule, and true only of the
   four-word headline. Verified in the Gutenberg text.
2. [critical] **F2.** "This is Strunk's 'write with nouns and verbs' turned into a rule." The
   string does not occur anywhere in the 1918 text; it was grepped. It is White's, from the 1959
   revision, which `SOURCES.md` records as **not read and not citable**, and which the same file
   warns about in terms: "Two of Pullum's specific charges land on White, not Strunk."
3. [major] **F3.** Pullum trimmed of his concession, for the second lesson running. The lesson
   quotes his "Many are useless" and stops. In the same essay: "mostly harmless", and "Even so, it
   doesn't hurt to lay such well-meant maxims before novice writers."
4. [major] **F4.** "Every fact survives", of a rewrite that turned "not fully and completely
   operational" into "not working". Partly operational is not the same as not working, and the
   table filed the change under "doubled words" as though only redundancy went.
5. [major] **F5, CW-05.** Six of eight stated counts wrong: 108 words (111), 48 words (46), 55
   percent (58.6), "nine words from thirty-one" (eight from thirty-two), "five words that repeat"
   (six). And the predict block said "cut this by about half" over a model answer that cuts three
   quarters. All recomputed.
6. [major] **F6.** "the thesaurus reflex turns out to be the single clearest marker separating
   novice revisers from experienced ones", unsourced and in the lesson's own voice. Sommers's
   report describes **one** student, in a study of eight students and seven experienced writers,
   and its actual novice/expert contrast is local versus global revision. `SOURCES.md` marks the
   secondary summaries' phrasings as "not citable".
7. [medium] **F7.** "readers... are not reading but looking, which lesson 1 established is most
   readers most of the time." Lesson 1 establishes no such proportion.
8. [minor] **F8.** GOV.UK cited "for the plainer-word substitutions", which the lesson does not
   contain, in a lesson that says "None of these is a rule about long words."
9. [minor] **F9.** The Sources line claimed rule 13 was "quoted in full above". It was not.

**Depth**

11. [major] **D2.** The six kinds are this course's own construction, and only a clause at the end

12. [medium] **D3.** The worked example uses a seventh kind, buried actions, and the lesson says

13. [medium] **D4.** A table row that cuts nothing: "the previous system" to "the old system",

14. [minor] **D5.** "the most quoted sentence in the history of writing advice", of four sentences.

### Resolutions

1. F1 — fixed, and it improves the lesson. The section is renamed "What the famous advice actually
   says". Rule 13's first paragraph is quoted in full with the "make every word tell" sentence in
   bold, seven of Strunk's own substitution pairs are printed, and the thesis is rewritten to the
   one that survives contact with the source: **the four-word headline is not instruction, the rule
   underneath it is, and what travelled for a century was the headline.** Pullum's objection is
   then read as landing on the headline, which is what it says. The lesson now ends that section
   with "this lesson isn't supplying what Strunk left out, it's doing what he did", which is both
   true and a better frame than the old one.
2. F2 — fixed. The misconception now says the advice is not Strunk's, attributes it to the 1959
   revision in Pullum's words, and says this course has not read that revision. Sources repeats it
   and records that the string is absent from the 1918 text.
3. F3, N1 — fixed together. Pullum's "mostly harmless" and "it doesn't hurt to lay such well-meant
   maxims before novice writers" are quoted, with a line saying his quarrel is with the
   grammatical claims rather than with telling a beginner to cut. With F1's restoration, both
   sides are now in their own words.
4. F4 — fixed. The rewrite reads "not fully working", the table row reads "fully working", and a
   new paragraph makes the near-miss into the lesson's opening discipline: *fully and completely*
   is a doubled pair, *fully* is not padding, and cutting it would have changed the claim.
5. F5 — all six recounted and corrected, and recorded in Sources per CW-05. The predict header now
   asks "How much of this can go, and what kind is each cut?" and the answer says three quarters.
6. F6 — fixed. The claim is now what the study supports: one student in a study of fifteen
   described his revision strategy as reaching for a thesaurus, offered as a habit to watch for in
   yourself rather than a measured difference between novices and experts.
7. F7, F8, F9 — fixed.
8. D1 — fixed, four items.
9. D2 — fixed. "**This list is mine**" now appears in the body where the kinds are introduced, with
   a line saying Strunk's is shorter and Williams's longer, and why six is the right number to
   carry.
10. D3 — fixed. Buried actions are named as a seventh kind to tally, the "nothing left over" claim
    now reads "nothing left over that the six kinds and lesson 3 can't account for", and the
    exercise asks for seven columns.
11. D4, D5 — fixed by dropping the row and the superlative.
12. P1 — fixed. Three items rebuilt on fresh cases: a manager circulating the four-word maxim, a
    newsletter about a phone system, and a colleague's report containing "on an ongoing basis".
    Four of six now apply the idea to something new.
13. P2, P3, P4 — fixed. The predict's passage moves into the body of the block, the heading becomes
    "Down the paragraph, cut by cut", and step 1 of the exercise now says a third is a working
    target rather than a law and names this lesson's own 59 percent.
14. P5 — re-measured, and **the rewrite broke it again**, in a new way: keys came out C C C B C A,
    with D never used and two adjacent repeats. Two items reordered. Final: C A D B C A, all four
    used, no adjacent repeat, "always longest" 0 percent, "always shortest" 17 percent.
15. V1, V2, V3 — fixed. 81 contractions, all four "Here is" openers rewritten, morals reduced.
16. V4 — fixed. "Take 45 minutes over the cutting" and `minutes: 75`, measured. Step 6, which needs
    another person, is now explicitly a later task rather than part of the sitting.
17. V5 — fixed. A third block, a predict after the six kinds, asking the reader to name the kind
    for three phrases before reading on.
