**Fact-check**

2. [critical] **F2.** Strunk's own qualification cut, in the direction that helps the lesson's
   argument. One paragraph above the Restoration pair, inside the same rule, he writes: "This rule
   does not, of course, mean that the writer should entirely discard the passive voice, which is
   frequently convenient and sometimes necessary." The lesson omitted it and then wrote that the
   pair is "filed under a rule heading that reads like a prohibition on the passive" and that
   "choosing the active would break the paragraph to satisfy a rule". Strunk says the opposite
   himself, in the rule. Verified in the 1918 Gutenberg text.
3. [critical] **F3.** Gopen and Swan's "None of these reader-expectation principles should be

4. [major] **F4.** The Pullum block quote is altered: "de-emphasizes" Britishised, and "a sentence

   its author wrote. Verified by extracting the text of the PDF.
5. [major] **F5.** The split-infinitive stress claim is attributed to "*The Elements of Style*" in
   a run of paragraphs otherwise all about Strunk 1918, so a reader takes it as Strunk's. It is
   **White's 1959 addition**. Pullum, in the same essay the lesson quotes: "Strunk's original
   version never even mentioned split infinitives. White added both the above remark..." Strunk
   1918's only mention says the construction "is in disfavor" and makes no claim about stress,
   which this course has now read directly. `SOURCES.md` records White's revision as not read and
   not citable.
6. [major] **F6.** "The choppy paragraph at the top of this lesson took you **measurably** longer
   to read than the repaired one." Nothing was measured and no reader was timed. The repaired
   version is also the longer of the two. CW-01's shape exactly.

   "The trust introduced a new booking system in April", which ends on "in April".
8. [major] **F8.** The checkpoint instructs "add or remove nothing but connective words" and its
   own model answer adds "preceded by", "also brought" and "a sharp rise", converting the
   original's "rose in the same period" into a causal claim, which the next paragraph then builds
   on ("becomes an argument").
9. [medium] **F9.** "Every sentence in the second version begins with something you already had"
