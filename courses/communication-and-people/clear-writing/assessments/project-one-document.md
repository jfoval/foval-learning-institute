The test measures whether you can recognise the moves. This project measures whether you can make them on writing that matters to you, and then find out from another human being whether any of it worked. That last part is what separates this from every writing exercise you have done before, and it is the part people skip.

Budget about four hours, spread over a week rather than done in one sitting. The gap matters. On Tuesday you will see things in your own prose that were invisible on Sunday, for the same reason lesson 4 gave: you cannot feel a wait when you already know what is coming.

## Choose the document

One real document of your own, between 400 and 1,200 words, that somebody actually had to act on. An email that asked for something, a report, a proposal, a letter, a set of instructions, a funding application.

Three rules about the choice.

**It must be real and already sent.** Not something written for this project. The whole exercise depends on the document having been produced under the conditions you actually write under.

**Somebody must have had to do something with it.** Writing whose only job was to exist cannot be tested, because there is no right answer for a reader to arrive at.

**Not your best piece.** Choose one you were slightly uneasy about, or one that produced a reply asking a question you thought you had answered. That reply is a finding, and it is the closest thing to free evidence you will get.

If you have been carrying a document since lesson 1, use that one.

## Part 1: the original, untouched

Submit it exactly as it went out, with the date it was written and one line saying who received it and what you needed them to do.

Do not tidy it first. The urge to fix a typo before anyone sees the original is strong and it defeats the purpose, which is to have an honest before.

## Part 2: the diagnosis

One page, and this is the part that carries the most weight in the rubric. **Name specific sentences.** A diagnosis that says "the tone is a bit formal" or "it could be clearer" is not a diagnosis; it is an impression, and impressions are what the whole course was built to replace.

Cover these six, in writing:

1. **The reader.** Who they are and what they came needing. Test it against lesson 1: if knowing this would not change a sentence, you have not answered it.
2. **Where the point currently sits**, counted in words from the start. Then the reading budget arithmetic from lesson 1 for the document's length.
3. **Three sentences whose character and action are buried.** Quote each one and fill in the two columns from lesson 3, character and action, in ordinary words, before proposing any rewrite.
4. **The worst gap between a subject and its verb.** Quote the sentence and give the number.
5. **One paragraph that does not cohere.** Quote it, then list the opening words of each of its sentences, as lesson 5 taught. Say which sentence breaks the chain.
6. **Your headings, listed on their own.** If the document has none, say what they would be. Then say whether that list alone tells the story.

## Part 3: the rewrite

Rewrite the document. Then, underneath, a short table with one row per significant change:

| What I changed | Which principle | What it cost |
|---|---|---|

The third column is not decoration. Most changes cost something: a front-loaded decision loses a gentle opening, a cut sentence takes a join with it, a split sentence loses a rhythm. Saying what a change cost is how you show you made a judgement rather than applied a rule.

**At least one row must be a place where you decided a principle did not apply here.** Every lesson in this course had a wrinkle, and a rewrite that applied nine principles nine times without exception has almost certainly damaged something. If you genuinely cannot find one, say so and say why, and expect that to be the weakest part of your submission.

## Part 4: the paraphrase test

One reader. Not a colleague on the same project, not someone who helped write it, and not someone who will try to be encouraging.

Follow lesson 9. Section by section, ask them to tell you in their own words what it means, and what they would do if they received it. **Write down what they say in their words. Do not correct them.** Then submit:

- What you asked, and what they said, in their words. Verbatim where you can.
- Every gap between what they said and what you meant, with the sentence you think caused each one.
- What you changed as a result.
- **If nothing needed changing, say so and say how you know.** That is a legitimate result, and it is much more convincing when you can point to the questions you asked. Before you record it, check that you asked for a paraphrase rather than for a verdict.

## Rubric

Marked on five things, in this order of weight.

**1. The diagnosis names sentences, not impressions.** This is the commonest failure and the heaviest. Every claim in part 2 should quote the text it is about. A diagnosis you could paste onto somebody else's document is not a diagnosis of yours.

**2. Changes are traceable to named principles, with their costs.** Anyone can improve a document by fiddling with it. The point is to be able to say why each change is an improvement, in terms that will still work next month on a different document.

**3. At least one considered exception.** A place where you decided front-loading was wrong, or a nominalisation should stay, or a repetition was a join rather than padding, with the reason. This is the single clearest signal that the course has been understood rather than memorised, because every principle in it is a default.

**4. The paraphrase test reports what happened, including the inconvenient parts.** A record in which the reader understood everything perfectly is either a lucky document or, far more often, a test where the writer explained as they went. If your reader misread something, that is the most valuable line in your submission.

**5. The rewrite is better, and you can say how you know.** Note the order. This is last, because a rewrite that is better by accident teaches you nothing, and the first four are what make it repeatable.

## What a strong submission looks like

The diagnosis is uncomfortable to write. It quotes three or four sentences the writer was previously happy with and says exactly what is wrong with each.

The change table has a row that reads roughly: *moved the deadline to the first sentence; front-loading, lesson 2; cost is that the letter now opens with a demand and the thanks come afterwards, which I judged worth it because two people missed the deadline last time.*

There is an exception row, and it is specific.

The paraphrase record contains at least one sentence beginning "she thought it meant", followed by something the writer did not mean.

And the final paragraph says what the writer learned about their own writing rather than about this document. That is the part that transfers, and it is why the project is worth four hours.
