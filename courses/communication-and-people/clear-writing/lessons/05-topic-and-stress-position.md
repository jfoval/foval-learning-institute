Read this paragraph. Every sentence in it is grammatical, none is longer than fifteen words, and there are no buried actions or long waits between subject and verb. It has passed every test this course has given you so far.

> The council commissioned a structural survey in January. Corrosion in the main support beams was found by the survey. About two million pounds is the cost of replacing those beams. Four hundred thousand pounds remains in this year's capital budget. Closure until at least next spring is the consequence.

Read it and you can extract the facts. You can also feel that it is hard work, and that the effort is out of proportion to the content, which is a bridge, a survey, some rust and a shortage of money.

Now the same paragraph again.

> The council commissioned a structural survey in January. The survey found corrosion in the main support beams. Replacing those beams would cost about two million pounds. That cost is far more than the four hundred thousand pounds left in this year's capital budget. So the bridge stays closed until at least next spring.

Not one fact has been added or removed. The two versions are 49 and 53 words, and the second is barely longer: two of its sentences lost two words each and one gained seven. What did change is the order, and a handful of words that are links rather than facts.

:::predict What changed between the two versions?
Every sentence after the first begins with something you already had.

Sentence two opens with **the survey**, which sentence one just gave you. Sentence three opens with **replacing those beams**, which sentence two just gave you. Sentence four opens with **that cost**, from sentence three. Sentence five opens with **so**, which points at everything before it.

In the first version, every sentence after the first opens with something new: corrosion, two million pounds, four hundred thousand pounds, closure. The reader is handed a fresh object four times in a row and has to work out what each one has to do with the last.

Ten words are new in the second version and six have gone, and almost all of them are doing the linking rather than carrying a fact. "That cost", "far more than", "so", "the bridge": every one of those exists to attach a sentence to the one before it. The facts, the survey and the corrosion and the two figures and the closure, are word for word the same.
:::

That is the whole idea, and it has a name.

## The two positions

Gopen and Swan's account of this in ["The Science of Scientific Writing"](https://wstomv.win.tue.nl/quotes/science-of-scientific-writing.html) is the clearest statement in the literature, and the two claims are worth having exactly.

On the beginning of a sentence:

> "The information that begins a sentence establishes for the reader a perspective for viewing the sentence as a unit: Readers expect a unit of discourse to be a story about whoever shows up first."

They call this the **topic position**. It is not where you put the important thing. It is where you tell the reader what this sentence is *about*, and by extension how it connects to the one before.

On the end:

> "It is a linguistic commonplace that readers naturally emphasize the material that arrives at the end of a sentence."

This is the **stress position**. It is where the reader places their weight, and where they will still be when the sentence stops.

And then the principle that joins them, which they call the given and new contract:

> "Put in the topic position the old information that links backward; put in the stress position the new information you want the reader to emphasize."

Old at the front. New at the end. Every sentence a bridge from something the reader has to something they don't.

Their own caveat sits directly under the numbered list those principles come from, and it applies here as much as anywhere: **"None of these reader-expectation principles should be considered 'rules.'"** What follows is a description of what readers do with order, not an instruction you owe obedience to. The section on conflicts below is where that matters most.

## Why the choppy version was choppy

Go back to the first paragraph and you can now say exactly what was wrong with it.

Sentence two ended on "by the survey". The survey was old information, and it was sitting in the stress position, where the reader was placing weight on it. Meanwhile "corrosion", which was the news, was at the front, where the reader was looking for orientation and instead got a fresh object.

Every sentence after the first had its information exactly backwards. Not one of them was ungrammatical, unclear, or too long. They were each pointing the reader's attention at the wrong end.

That's why "make your sentences clearer" is useless advice for a choppy paragraph. Each sentence was already clear. **Cohesion isn't a property of sentences. It's a property of the joins between them,** and the joins live at the two ends.

:::callout Why transitions do not fix this
The standard repair for a choppy paragraph is to bolt on however, therefore, in addition, furthermore. Try it on the first version and you will see what it buys you.

> The council commissioned a structural survey in January. **Subsequently,** corrosion in the main support beams was found by the survey. **Furthermore,** about two million pounds is the cost of replacing those beams.

It is not better. It is longer, and now it is choppy prose that has been *told* it is connected. A transition word announces a relation; the topic position creates one. Use transitions where the relation is genuinely not obvious from the order, and stop using them to paper over sentences that are facing the wrong way.
:::

## The wrinkle: this is why the passive is sometimes right

You've been carrying an argument since lesson 3, and this is where it settles.

In 1918, Strunk gave this pair in [*The Elements of Style*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134.txt), inside the rule headed "Use the active voice":

> The dramatists of the Restoration are little esteemed to-day.
>
> Modern readers have little esteem for the dramatists of the Restoration.

The first is passive. The second is active. And Strunk doesn't tell you to use the second. He says:

> "The first would be the right form in a paragraph on the dramatists of the Restoration; the second, in a paragraph on the tastes of modern readers. The need of making a particular word the subject of the sentence will often, as in these examples, determine which voice is to be used."

Read that last sentence again. **The need to make a particular word the subject determines the voice.** That's the topic position, described in 1918, seventy-two years before Gopen and Swan named it.

Give Strunk the credit here, because this course has been hard on rule 10 and it's easy to leave the wrong impression. He isn't caught out by this example. One paragraph earlier, in the same rule, he writes:

> "This rule does not, of course, mean that the writer should entirely discard the passive voice, which is frequently convenient and sometimes necessary."

So the man whose rule is headed "Use the active voice" says in the rule itself that the passive is frequently convenient and sometimes necessary, and then shows you a case where it's the right choice. What's misleading isn't Strunk. It's the heading, read on its own by people who never got to the paragraph underneath, and a century of advice that repeated the heading and dropped the rest.

So the honest position on the passive isn't "avoid it", and it isn't a replacement rule either. It's this: **the passive is what lets you put something other than the actor in the topic position.** If your paragraph is about the dramatists, the dramatists go at the front, and the passive is how you get them there.

Be fair to the other side, which has a real case. Strunk's own reason for the rule stands: "The active voice is usually more direct and vigorous than the passive." GOV.UK tells government writers to use the active voice because it "is more direct and puts the focus on the user", and for most sentences most of the time that's right, which is why it survives as a default. The claim in this section is narrower than "prefer the passive". It's that the choice is decided by what the paragraph is about, and that a writer who can't reach for the passive has lost the ability to write a paragraph about the thing something was done to.

:::predict A paragraph is about a patient's recovery. Which of these two sentences belongs in it, and why?
(a) "The consultant discharged her on the fourth day."

(b) "She was discharged on the fourth day."

**(b)**, the passive one. The paragraph is about her, so she belongs in the topic position, and the passive is what puts her there. Version (a) shifts the story to the consultant, and a reader will take the next sentence to be about him.

If the paragraph were about the consultant's decisions, (a) would be right and (b) would be the mistake. Neither sentence is better in the abstract, which is the whole point: a rule that bans one of them takes away the version you need whenever the paragraph is about the patient.
:::

There's a second consequence, and it settles something people argue about endlessly. *The Elements of Style* says the split infinitive "should be avoided unless the writer wishes to place unusual stress on the adverb." Geoffrey Pullum, in ["50 Years of Stupid Grammar Advice"](https://pullum.ppls.ed.ac.uk/50years.pdf), points out that this has the stress exactly backwards:

> "Tucking the adverb in before the verb actually de-emphasizes the adverb, so a sentence like 'The dean's statements tend to completely polarize the faculty' places the stress on polarizing the faculty. The way to stress the completeness of the polarization would be to write, 'The dean's statements tend to polarize the faculty completely.'"

He's applying the stress position, and he says so: the book's own earlier section, "Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end", already implies it.

Two things to keep straight about whose claim this is. Pullum notes that the split-infinitive remark is **White's**, added in the 1959 revision: "Strunk's original version never even mentioned split infinitives." This course has read Strunk's 1918 text, where the only mention of the construction says it "is in disfavor" and makes no claim about stress at all. So this is not a case of Strunk contradicting himself, and the 1918 book this lesson has otherwise been quoting is not the book making the error.

And Pullum isn't the wholesale opponent he is sometimes made into. In the same essay he calls the style advice "mostly harmless" and says White "often wrote beautifully". His quarrel is with the grammatical claims, not with the enterprise, which is worth knowing before lesson 8 takes up his argument at length.

## What to do when they conflict

Sooner or later the two positions pull against each other. The old information that links backward is also the thing you most want emphasised.

Take a case. A paragraph has just told the reader that the fire door was propped open. The next sentence has to say that this is what the inspector found, and "the propped door" is both the old information and the thing you want to land:

> The propped door was what the inspector wrote up.

Old information is in the topic position, so the link holds, but the sentence ends on "wrote up", which is not what you wanted emphasised. Turn it round:

> What the inspector wrote up was the propped door.

Now the door lands, and the sentence opens on the inspector, who has not been mentioned and is not what the paragraph is about. The reader gets a fresh object where they were looking for orientation. Both versions cost something.

When you have to choose, my own rule, and it's a judgement rather than something Gopen and Swan say, is that the **topic position usually wins**. A reader who has lost the thread can't be made to emphasise anything, so orientation comes first.

But there's usually a better move than choosing, and it's the one to reach for: split the sentence.

> The inspector wrote up the propped door. That door is now the only thing standing between the building and a prohibition notice.

Two sentences, two topic positions, two stress positions, and the door is in both. A sentence trying to do two jobs often just needed to be two sentences.

:::checkpoint Can you repair this paragraph by moving information only, adding and removing nothing but connective words?
> "A new booking system was introduced by the trust in April. Waiting times fell by nine days over the following quarter. Staff training had been completed in March. Complaints about the phone line rose sharply in the same period."

One repair:

> "In April the trust introduced a new booking system. That system had been preceded by staff training, completed in March. Over the following quarter, waiting times fell by nine days. In the same period, complaints about the phone line rose sharply."

What changed and why. Sentence one puts the date at the front as orientation and ends on the new booking system, which is the news and the thing every later sentence points back at. Sentence two opens on "that system" and lands on the training. Sentence three opens with the time frame and lands on "nine days", the figure worth remembering. Sentence four opens on "in the same period", which points back at that quarter, and lands on the sharp rise in complaints.

Notice what the repair does not do. It doesn't say the system caused the complaints. The original says the two happened in the same period and no more, and a repair that moves information is not allowed to add a causal claim that wasn't there. That temptation is worth naming, because it's where this move goes wrong: arranging sentences into a chain can make a sequence look like an argument. Keep the chain and leave the claim alone.

If your repair differs from mine, that's expected. Check it against one question: does every sentence after the first begin with something the reader already has?
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Never use the passive."** The passive is how you control the topic position. Banning it costs you the ability to write a paragraph about the thing that had something done to it, which is a great many paragraphs.

**"Put the important word first."** This is the single most common misreading of this material, and it is backwards. The front of a sentence is for orientation. The end is for emphasis. If you front-load the important word, the reader arrives at the full stop with their weight on whatever happened to be lying around at the end.

**"Front-loading a document means front-loading every sentence."** Lesson 2 was about where the point of a *document* goes, and the reason was the reading budget. This lesson is about the internal shape of a *sentence*, and the reason is how readers process order. They are different scales with different rules, and this is the one place in the course where the advice at two levels genuinely differs.

**"Transitions make writing flow."** They label flow. Cohesion comes from the joins, and transitions sit on top of the joins rather than making them.

**"This is stylistic."** Gopen and Swan's claim is about what readers expect of order, not about a stopwatch, and nothing here has been timed. But you read both versions of the bridge paragraph, with the same facts in them, and one of them cost you more. That's the evidence available, and it's the evidence that matters, because it's the same evidence your reader will have.

## Practice

:::exercise Mark the ends of your own sentences
Allow 40 minutes for this, and do it slowly. It's the exercise in this course that changes how you read.

1. Take three consecutive paragraphs from the document you have been carrying since lesson 1.

2. For every sentence, underline the first few words and the last few words. Just those. You are marking the topic and stress positions.

3. Read only the underlined openings, in order, ignoring everything else. **If that list of openings tells a coherent story, the paragraph coheres.** If it reads as a set of unrelated objects, you have found your problem, and you have found it without judging any sentence.

4. Now read only the underlined endings, in order. Ask whether those are the words you would choose to have a reader remember. Very often two or three of them will be dates, references, or someone's job title that drifted to the end because that was where it fitted.

5. Repair one paragraph by moving information only. No new facts, no deletions, connective words allowed. Then run step 3 again on the repair.

6. Finally, find one sentence in your own writing where the passive is the right choice, and write one line explaining why in terms of what the paragraph is about. If you cannot find one, look for a paragraph about a thing rather than a person, because that is where they live.
:::

## Where this goes next

You've now met the whole of the sentence. Lesson 3 gave you its subject and its verb, lesson 4 the distance between them, and this lesson its two ends. Everything else in this course is either larger than a sentence or about deciding what to cut.

Cutting is next, and this lesson has just made it possible. Lesson 6 is about concision, and it couldn't have come earlier, because until you can see the topic position you can't tell padding from the repetition that holds a paragraph together. The commonest way to wreck a passage is to cut the words that were doing the linking.
