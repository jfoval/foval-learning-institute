> Deal with everything in the space before you start, because afterwards it is far harder. Take out whatever will come out, and protect whatever will not. Attend to any irregularities early: they will be much more obvious at the end than they are now, and by then very little can be done about them. Always work downward. Going the other way means repeating what you have already finished. Use less material than feels right, since two light passes give a better result than one heavy one, which gathers and runs. Wait properly between passes, even though the waiting is the hardest part of it. When everything has settled, put back what you moved and clean what you used straight away, because a day later that is a much bigger job.

You understood every word. You could not tell anyone what it was about.

:::predict Now read it again, knowing that the title is "Painting a room". What changed?
Everything, and instantly. Taking out what will come out is the furniture. The irregularities are the cracks and nail holes you fill before you start, which are invisible now and glaring under fresh paint. Working downward is ceiling before walls, so you are not dripping onto finished work. The two light passes are coats. The waiting is drying time. And cleaning what you used straight away is the brushes, which are easy tonight and ruined tomorrow.

Nothing about the paragraph changed. Not one word was rewritten. The only thing that arrived was three words of context, and they arrived before the passage instead of after it. That is the whole of this lesson.
:::

Every word in that paragraph was in ordinary English. Every sentence was short. A readability formula would have scored it well, which is lesson 1's point about formulas making a second appearance. And it was still unreadable, because the reader had nowhere to put anything.

## Context first, then the thing it contextualises

Gopen and Swan state this as the sixth of their seven structural principles in ["The Science of Scientific Writing"](https://wstomv.win.tue.nl/quotes/science-of-scientific-writing.html), and they put it plainly: **"provide context for your reader before asking consideration of anything new."**

That sentence is doing more work than it looks. It is not advice about being organised. It is a claim about what a reader is physically doing while they read you.

Here is the mechanism. A reader who does not yet know where a passage is going cannot file anything. Every fact arrives and has to be held, unsorted, in case it turns out to matter. "Attend to any irregularities early" is a burden until the moment you know it is a room being painted, at which point it costs nothing at all, because it drops into a slot that was already there.

So the cost of withholding context is not that the reader is mildly annoyed. It is that you have made them carry your whole document at once. And they will not do it for long, because of the reading budget from lesson 1: they have about 130 words before they decide whether this is worth it, on the [reading budget](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/) lesson 1 built, and you have spent those 130 words giving them nothing to hold on to.

:::callout Two words, not two paragraphs
Notice how little context that passage needed. Three words, before it. This is worth remembering when you are tempted to write three paragraphs of background: the reader usually needs a frame, not a briefing. The frame is what lets them file everything that comes after. The briefing is what they have to hold.
:::

## Front-loading at three levels

Front-loading is the practical form of principle 6, and it applies at three scales. It is the same move each time, for the same reason.

**The document.** The reader should be able to state what this is and what it wants from them before they have read 130 words. In an email that means the request or the decision in the first sentence or two. In a report it means an opening that says what was found, not what was done.

**The section.** Each section should say early what it is for. This is mostly a job for headings, which [GOV.UK's guidance](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/) treats as navigation rather than decoration, and lesson 7 takes headings properly, but the principle is here: a section that opens with three sentences of preamble has recreated the whole problem in miniature.

**The paragraph.** A paragraph should usually declare its business in its first sentence. Not because of a rule about topic sentences, but because a reader who does not know what a paragraph is about is doing the washing-clothes exercise again, at small scale, forty times a document.

The pattern to notice is that the reason never changes. At every level, the question is whether the reader can file what comes next.

## Worked example: the email with five clauses of throat-clearing

Here is the kind of thing that arrives every day.

> Following our discussion last week and having reviewed the figures from Q2 as well as the projections that finance circulated on Monday, and taking into account the feedback from the regional teams who raised a number of concerns about capacity during the summer period, I wanted to reach out regarding the launch date and share some thoughts about how we might want to proceed given everything we now know.

Sixty-nine words. Nothing has been said. The reader has crossed half their attention budget and does not yet know whether this email needs a reply, a decision, or nothing.

Now the same email, front-loaded:

> **We should move the launch from May to July. Here is why.**
>
> The regional teams flagged capacity problems over the summer, and the Q2 figures back them up: we would be launching into our thinnest support cover of the year. Finance's Monday projections do not change the case either way.
>
> If you want to keep May, the thing to solve is summer cover. Let me know by Thursday and I will put options together.

Twelve words to the decision. The reasons are all still there, and one of them (finance) is explicitly marked as not load-bearing, which the original hid inside a list. And the last paragraph does something the original never did: it tells the reader what to do.

:::predict What was actually cut between those two versions, and what was only moved?
Almost nothing was cut. "Following our discussion last week" went, because the reader was at the discussion. "I wanted to reach out" went, because it describes the email rather than saying anything. "Share some thoughts about how we might want to proceed" went, because the second version does that instead of announcing it.

Everything else was moved. The regional teams, the Q2 figures, the finance projections and the launch date are all still in the second version. What changed is that they now arrive after the reader knows what they are for. That is the difference between cutting and front-loading, and this course does both, in different lessons.
:::

## The wrinkle: when the point should not come first

If this lesson stopped here you would have a rule, and rules applied without their exceptions are what lesson 8 is about. Front-loading is a default. There are cases where it is wrong, and they are not rare.

**Bad news that needs its reason to be understood.** "Your application has been refused" as a first sentence is honest, and it is also the sentence after which many readers stop reading and start reacting, which means they never reach the part about appealing. The usual advice is to soften it with praise, which is worse: it buries the decision and reads as insincere. What works is to put the one reason that makes the decision intelligible next to the decision itself, at the top. Not four paragraphs of context. One sentence, so the reader understands rather than only resents.

**An argument whose conclusion would be rejected on sight.** If you are writing to someone who currently believes the opposite of your conclusion, opening with the conclusion invites them to spend the rest of the document arguing with you instead of reading it. Here the premises genuinely do come first, because the reader has to agree to each step before they will accept where the steps lead. Notice this is still principle 6: you are providing the context that makes the conclusion intelligible before asking consideration of it.

**Narrative, where the withholding is the point.** A joke front-loaded is not a joke. Neither is a mystery. This course is about writing that has to be understood, not writing that has to be enjoyed, and the two have different rules. It is worth knowing which one you are doing.

:::checkpoint For each of these, decide whether to front-load, and say what the deciding factor was.
**A letter telling a tenant their rent is going up by 6% from June.** Front-load. The reader needs the number and the date above everything else, and they will be looking for exactly those two things. Withholding them is not tact; it is making an anxious person hunt.

**A proposal to a board that has rejected this idea twice before.** Do not front-load the proposal itself. The deciding factor is that the reader arrives disagreeing, so the conclusion needs its premises to be intelligible. Front-load what has changed since the last rejection, which is the context that makes a third hearing reasonable.

**A safety notice about a chemical spill procedure.** Front-load, hard. The deciding factor is that this reader is not reading, they are looking, under pressure, for one instruction. This is really a design problem, and lesson 7 takes it up.

**An email to a colleague explaining why a shared deadline slipped.** This one is genuinely a judgement call, and the deciding factor is what you want from them. If you want them to do something, front-load the ask. If the email exists so they are not blindsided, the reason and the fact can arrive together, because there is no action to bury.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"The inverted pyramid is always right."** Journalists put the most important information first, then detail, then background, and it works because a news reader may stop at any point. It is one good instance of front-loading, not the definition of it. A document whose reader will finish it has different needs.

**"Front-loading means being blunt."** The two are unrelated. "We should move the launch from May to July, and here is why" is front-loaded and perfectly courteous. Bluntness is a matter of what you say about people; front-loading is a matter of what order the reader meets things in.

**"A summary at the end does the same job."** It does not, and the reason is in this lesson's first paragraph. A summary at the end helps a reader who finished. Context at the start helps a reader read. The passage at the top of this lesson, with its title printed underneath instead of above, is still unreadable.

**"Front-loading means putting the conclusion first."** Sometimes, and sometimes not. The principle underneath is context before the new thing. In an argument, the conclusion is the new thing and the premises are the context, which is why an unwelcome conclusion belongs after the reasons rather than before them.

**"My document has an executive summary, so it is front-loaded."** Only if the summary says what was found and what should happen. A great many executive summaries say what the document covers, which is a table of contents in prose and gives the reader nothing to file.

## Practice

:::exercise Front-load three real documents, and find one that should not be
Use the document you kept from lesson 1, plus two more you actually sent.

1. For each one, write down in a single sentence what the reader most needed to know. Not what the document is about. What they needed.

2. Find where that sentence currently appears, counted in words from the start. Write the number down.

3. Rewrite the opening so it arrives within the first 130 words, and preferably within the first 25. Move things; do not delete yet. Deleting is lesson 6, and doing both at once makes it impossible to tell which change helped.

4. Now the part that matters most. **One of your three should be a case where you conclude front-loading is wrong.** Find it, or find a fourth document where it is. Write down which of the three exceptions applies, and what you would put first instead.

5. Take the front-loaded version of one document and read only its first 130 words aloud, then stop. Ask yourself whether a reader who stopped there would do the right thing. That question is the whole test.

If you cannot find any document where front-loading is wrong, that is worth noticing too. It probably means everything you write is a request or a report, which is true of most working writers, and it means the default is safe for you.
:::

## Where this goes next

You now know where the point should go. You do not yet know how to write the sentence that carries it, and that is the harder problem: an email can be perfectly front-loaded and still be exhausting to read, because the trouble is inside the sentences rather than in their order.

The next lesson goes down a level, to the single move that does more for a sentence than any other. Then lesson 5 comes back to arrangement, at the scale of the paragraph, and explains something this lesson has only asserted: why a passage can feel disconnected even when every sentence is in a sensible order.
