Read this sentence once, at normal speed, and notice what your mind does.

> The application, which had been submitted in March, revised twice at the request of the highways officer, and held while the flood risk assessment was recommissioned, was refused.

You met the subject in the second word. Then you waited. Twenty-four words later the sentence finally told you what the application did, and by then you had been holding "the application" in your head, unresolved, through three separate pieces of history.

Now the same content, same words, rearranged:

> The application was refused. It had been submitted in March, revised twice at the highways officer's request, and held while the flood risk assessment was recommissioned.

Nothing has gone. The March date, the two revisions, the highways officer and the flood risk assessment are all still there. You just got the verb four words in instead of twenty-eight.

## What the reader is doing while they wait

Gopen and Swan state this as the first of their [seven structural principles](https://wstomv.win.tue.nl/quotes/science-of-scientific-writing.html), and their wording is worth having exactly:

> "Readers expect a grammatical subject to be followed immediately by the verb. Anything of length that intervenes between subject and verb is read as an interruption, and therefore as something of lesser importance."

Take the second half of that sentence with the first. They aren't only saying the interruption costs the reader something. They're saying the reader discounts it, files it as a footnote to the main event. That's worth knowing before you put twenty words of your best material in there, and it's why the three fixes below aren't interchangeable.

And take their caveat with the principle, as this course does every time it quotes them: **"None of these reader-expectation principles should be considered 'rules.'"** What follows is a description of what readers do, not a prohibition.

The mechanism is the same one from lesson 2, at a smaller scale. A subject on its own is an unfinished thought. The reader can't do anything with "the application" until they know what happened to it, so they hold it, and everything arriving before the verb has to be held alongside it, because it might turn out to matter to whatever the verb says.

That's why the cost isn't flat. One or two words of interruption cost almost nothing. Twenty-four words cost a great deal, and they cost it on top of the ordinary work of reading twenty-four words. The reader is doing two jobs at once, and one of them is bookkeeping. That rising cost is this course's reasoning from Gopen and Swan's "interruption", not a measured curve from a study; take it as a way of thinking about the gap rather than as a finding.

:::callout This is the other half of lesson 3
Lesson 3 got the right character into the subject slot. This lesson is about what happens next. If you move the character to the front and then make the reader wait thirty words for the verb, you've spent the gain. The two moves are halves of one move, and it's worth checking both at the same time.
:::

## Measure it, do not feel it

You can't do this by ear on your own writing, and here's the reason.

You already know what your sentence means. When you reread "The application, which had been submitted in March..." your mind supplies "was refused" before you get there, because you wrote it. The wait costs you nothing, so you can't feel it. That's the same problem lesson 1 raised about rereading your own work, and it has the same answer: replace the judgement with a count.

**The count.** Find the main subject. Find its verb. Count the words in between. That number is the size of the wait you're asking for.

The chart below draws that count for the sentence at the top of this lesson, in both versions.

:::callout There is no threshold, and here is the honest version of one
Nobody has measured a cutoff, and anyone who gives you one is selling something. What this course can offer is the drafter's working rule of thumb, reasoned from the principle rather than drawn from a study: under about five words the gap is invisible, five to ten is usually fine, ten to twenty is where readers start to notice, and past twenty you're asking for real work.

Treat those as a way to sort your own sentences, not as findings. The number that matters isn't the boundary. It's that you now have a figure for each sentence, so you can rank them and fix the worst three instead of rewriting everything.

## One sentence, three fixes, three meanings

Take this, from the sort of email that arrives on a Friday afternoon.

> The new supplier, who was selected after a procurement exercise that ran for most of last year and involved eleven bidders, has missed its first delivery date.

:::predict Before you read the three fixes: name them. How many ways are there to close a gap like this, and what are they?
Three, and you can derive them from the shape of the problem rather than remembering a list. The interruption is in the wrong place, so either it moves or the sentence does. It can go to the front, it can go to the end, or the sentence can split in two and the interruption becomes a sentence of its own.

That's the whole set. What the next three sections add isn't a fourth option. It's that the three are not interchangeable, because each one leaves something different sitting at the end of the sentence.
:::

Subject: the new supplier. Verb: has missed. Gap: eighteen words. Count them, because that's the method and it's worth doing once on a sentence somebody else wrote.

There are three ways to close it, and they aren't interchangeable. Each one puts something different at the end of the sentence, and the end of a sentence is where a reader puts their weight. That's Gopen and Swan's second clause arriving in practice: whatever sits in the interruption gets read as the lesser thing, so which material you move is a decision about what the sentence is for.

**Fix one, move the interruption to the front.**

> After a procurement exercise that ran for most of last year and involved eleven bidders, the new supplier has missed its first delivery date.

The gap is gone. What lands last is the missed delivery, so this version is about the failure. The procurement exercise has become scene-setting.

**Fix two, move the interruption to the end.**

> The new supplier has missed its first delivery date, after a procurement exercise that ran for most of last year and involved eleven bidders.

Also gone, but now what lands last is the year-long exercise with eleven bidders. This version is quietly about the waste, and a reader will finish it thinking about the procurement rather than the delivery.

**Fix three, split it.**

> The new supplier has missed its first delivery date. It was selected after a procurement exercise that ran for most of last year and involved eleven bidders.

Two sentences, two stress positions, and both facts land. This is usually the safest choice when both halves matter, and it is the one most writers reach for last.

:::predict You are writing to the procurement team to ask what went wrong with the process. Which fix do you use, and why?
Fix two. You want the reader to finish the sentence thinking about the procurement exercise, because that is what your email is about, and the end of the sentence is where they will place their weight.

If you were writing to the operations team about a supply problem, you would use fix one, so the missed delivery lands. If you were writing a report that has to record both fairly, you would split it.

Notice that the question "which fix?" cannot be answered by looking at the sentence. It depends on what you want the reader to walk away with, which is a question about the document. That is the idea lesson 5 is built on, and this is the first place it bites.
:::

## The wrinkle: length is a proxy, not the fault

Two sentences. One is thirty-six words and easy. One is eighteen words and hard.

> **Thirty-six words.** The council agreed to fund the repairs, which had been costed at ninety thousand pounds by an independent surveyor, and asked the housing team to begin work in the spring, before the next round of inspections.

> **Eighteen words.** The decision, given the circumstances and the timescale involved and the other matters raised at the meeting, stands.

The long one has its subject and verb together at the front, twice ("the council agreed", "asked"). Everything after that arrives in order, and the reader can take it a clause at a time without holding anything.

The short one makes you carry "the decision" through fifteen words of vagueness to reach "stands". It's half the length and considerably harder.

This matters because sentence length is the thing people measure, and it is a proxy. [GOV.UK's guidance](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/) says to "try to split up sentences that are over 25 words long", which is sensible advice precisely because it is a rule of thumb about where to look. Long sentences are where big gaps tend to live. But splitting a long sentence with no gap gains nothing, and a short sentence with a gap will pass the length check untouched.

This is the same lesson as the readability formulas in lesson 1, arriving at a smaller scale. The countable thing is a signal. The thing you actually care about is what the reader has to hold.

:::checkpoint When does an interruption earn its place? Decide for each, then open this.
**"The report, dated 4 March, recommends closure."** Earns it. Two words, and they tell the reader which report, which is something they need before the verb can mean anything.

**"The application, which had been the subject of considerable correspondence with the parish council over the preceding eighteen months, was refused."** Does not earn it. The correspondence is background, and the reader can have it after they know the outcome.

**"The tenant, not the landlord, is responsible for the internal decoration."** Earns it. Three words, and they are doing the most important work in the sentence, which is ruling out the thing the reader probably assumed.

**"Mrs Okafor, who has lived at the property since 2011 and has never previously been in arrears, has now missed two payments."** A judgement call. The clause is long enough to cost something, and it is also the whole reason the sentence is worth writing, since the point is that this is out of character. Splitting it would work: "Mrs Okafor has missed two payments. She has lived at the property since 2011 and has never previously been in arrears." Whether that is better depends on whether you want the reader to meet her history before the bad news or after it, which is lesson 2's question about bad news, showing up inside a single sentence.

The pattern across all four: **a short interruption that tells the reader which thing the subject is usually earns its place. A long one that gives history usually does not.**
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Short sentences are clear sentences."** Length correlates with the fault and isn't the fault. The eighteen-word example above is the counterexample to carry around.

**"Vary your sentence length."** Common advice, usually given with no reason attached, and it's really advice about rhythm rather than clarity. Rhythm matters, and it isn't what this lesson is about. Don't let a rule about variety talk you into leaving a twenty-word gap for the sake of the music.

**"I read it back and it sounded fine."** It will. You know what the verb is going to be. That's the commonest reason writers miss gaps in their own work, and it's why the exercise below asks for a number.

**"Parentheses are the problem."** The punctuation is innocent. A twenty-word parenthesis between subject and verb is a problem, and so is a twenty-word relative clause, and so is a twenty-word list, none of which uses brackets. Look for the distance, not the marks.

**"This contradicts putting the character first."** It completes it. Get the right subject, then let it act promptly. If you can only do one, do lesson 3, because a sentence with the wrong subject can't be rescued by putting its verb close to it.

## Practice

:::exercise Count the gaps in a page of your own writing
Allow 45 minutes for this. Use the document you've been carrying since lesson 1, or a fresh page of anything you wrote for someone else.

1. Take one page, about 300 to 400 words. For every sentence, find the main subject and its verb and count the words between them. Write the number in the margin. Do all of them, including the ones that are obviously fine, because you need the distribution and not just the outliers.

2. Rank them. You'll usually find that two or three sentences carry most of the trouble and the rest are already fine. That's normal, and it's why this is worth measuring rather than rewriting everything.

3. Take your worst sentence and fix it **all three ways**: interruption to the front, interruption to the end, and split into two. Write out all three versions in full. Don't choose yet.

4. Now choose, and write one line saying why. The question isn't which sounds best. It's what you want the reader to be thinking about when the sentence ends.

5. Fix the next two worst, one way each.

6. Last, check your three rewrites against lesson 3. Is the subject of each one a character, and is the verb its action? If you've closed a gap but left an abstraction sitting in the subject slot, you've done half the job.
:::

## Where this goes next

Two facts about readers, and they turn out to be one fact. Readers want a character doing something, and they want the doing to arrive quickly. Both are about the front of the sentence.

The next lesson is about the other end, and it is the deepest idea in the course. You have already brushed against it twice: in the three fixes above, where the choice depended on what landed last, and in lesson 2, where a whole document had to be arranged around what the reader meets first. Lesson 5 gives you the principle underneath both, and with it the reason a paragraph of perfectly good sentences can still read like a list of unrelated facts.
