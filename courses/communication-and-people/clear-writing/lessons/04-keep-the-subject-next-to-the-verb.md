Read this sentence once, at normal speed, and notice what your mind does.

> The application, which had been submitted in March, revised twice at the request of the highways officer, and held while the flood risk assessment was recommissioned, was refused.

You met the subject in the second word. Then you waited. Twenty-four words later the sentence finally told you what the application did, and by then you had been holding "the application" in your head, unresolved, through three separate pieces of history.

Now the same content, same words, rearranged:

> The application was refused. It had been submitted in March, revised twice at the highways officer's request, and held while the flood risk assessment was recommissioned.

Nothing has gone. The March date, the two revisions, the highways officer and the flood risk assessment are all still there. You just got the verb four words in instead of twenty-six.

## What the reader is doing while they wait

Gopen and Swan state this as the first of their [seven structural principles](https://wstomv.win.tue.nl/quotes/science-of-scientific-writing.html), and their wording is worth having exactly:

> "Readers expect a grammatical subject to be followed immediately by the verb. Anything of length that intervenes between subject and verb is read as an interruption."

The mechanism is the same one from lesson 2, at a smaller scale. A subject on its own is an unfinished thought. The reader cannot do anything with "the application" until they know what happened to it, so they hold it, and everything that arrives before the verb has to be held alongside it, because it might turn out to be relevant to whatever the verb says.

This is why the cost is not flat. One or two words of interruption cost almost nothing. Twenty-four words cost a great deal, and they cost it on top of the ordinary work of reading twenty-four words. The reader is doing two jobs at once, and one of them is bookkeeping.

:::callout This is the other half of lesson 3
Lesson 3 got the right character into the subject slot. This lesson is about what happens next. If you move the character to the front and then make the reader wait thirty words for the verb, you have spent the gain. The two moves are halves of one move, and it is worth checking both at the same time.
:::

## Measure it, do not feel it

Here is why you cannot do this by ear on your own writing.

You already know what your sentence means. When you reread "The application, which had been submitted in March..." your mind supplies "was refused" before you get there, because you wrote it. The wait costs you nothing, so you cannot feel it. This is the same problem lesson 1 raised about rereading your own work, and it has the same answer: replace the judgement with a count.

**The count.** Find the main subject. Find its verb. Count the words in between. That number is the size of the wait you are asking for.

There is no magic threshold, and anyone who gives you one is selling something. But as rough guidance: under about five words is invisible, five to ten is usually fine, ten to twenty is where readers start to notice, and past twenty you are asking for real work. What matters more than the number is that you now have one, and you can rank your sentences by it and fix the worst.

## Worked example: one sentence, three fixes, three meanings

Take this, from the sort of email that arrives on a Friday afternoon.

> The new supplier, who was selected after a procurement exercise that ran for most of last year and involved eleven bidders, has missed its first delivery date.

Subject: the new supplier. Verb: has missed. Gap: twenty-two words.

There are three ways to close it, and they are not interchangeable. Each one puts something different at the end of the sentence, and the end of a sentence is where a reader puts their weight.

**Fix one, move the interruption to the front.**

> After a procurement exercise that ran for most of last year and involved eleven bidders, the new supplier has missed its first delivery date.

The gap is gone. What lands last is the missed delivery, so this version is about the failure. The procurement exercise has become scene-setting.

**Fix two, move the interruption to the end.**

> The new supplier has missed its first delivery date, after a procurement exercise that ran for most of last year and involved eleven bidders.

Also gone, but now what lands last is the year-long exercise with eleven bidders. This version is quietly about the waste, and a reader will finish it thinking about the procurement rather than the delivery.

**Fix three, split it.**

> The new supplier has missed its first delivery date. It was selected after a procurement exercise that ran for most of last year and involved eleven bidders.

Two sentences, two stress positions, and both facts land. This is usually the safest choice when both halves matter, and it is the one most writers reach for last.

:::predict You are writing to the procurement team to ask what went wrong with the process. Which fix do you use, and why?
Fix two. You want the reader to finish the sentence thinking about the procurement exercise, because that is what your email is about, and the end of the sentence is where they will place their weight.

If you were writing to the operations team about a supply problem, you would use fix one, so the missed delivery lands. If you were writing a report that has to record both fairly, you would split it.

Notice that the question "which fix?" cannot be answered by looking at the sentence. It depends on what you want the reader to walk away with, which is a question about the document. That is the idea lesson 5 is built on, and this is the first place it bites.
:::

## The wrinkle: length is a proxy, not the fault

Here are two sentences. One is thirty-eight words and easy. One is nineteen words and hard.

> **Thirty-eight words.** The council agreed to fund the repairs, which had been costed at ninety thousand pounds by an independent surveyor, and asked the housing team to begin work in the spring, before the next round of inspections.

> **Nineteen words.** The decision, given the circumstances and the timescale involved and the other matters raised at the meeting, stands.

The long one has its subject and verb together at the front, twice ("the council agreed", "asked"). Everything after that arrives in order, and the reader can take it a clause at a time without holding anything.

The short one makes you carry "the decision" through fourteen words of vagueness to reach "stands". It is half the length and considerably harder.

This matters because sentence length is the thing people measure, and it is a proxy. [GOV.UK's guidance](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/) says to "try to split up sentences that are over 25 words long", which is sensible advice precisely because it is a rule of thumb about where to look. Long sentences are where big gaps tend to live. But splitting a long sentence with no gap gains nothing, and a short sentence with a gap will pass the length check untouched.

This is the same lesson as the readability formulas in lesson 1, arriving at a smaller scale. The countable thing is a signal. The thing you actually care about is what the reader has to hold.

:::checkpoint When does an interruption earn its place? Decide for each, then open this.
**"The report, dated 4 March, recommends closure."** Earns it. Two words, and they tell the reader which report, which is something they need before the verb can mean anything.

**"The application, which had been the subject of considerable correspondence with the parish council over the preceding eighteen months, was refused."** Does not earn it. The correspondence is background, and the reader can have it after they know the outcome.

**"The tenant, not the landlord, is responsible for the internal decoration."** Earns it. Three words, and they are doing the most important work in the sentence, which is ruling out the thing the reader probably assumed.

**"Mrs Okafor, who has lived at the property since 2011 and has never previously been in arrears, has now missed two payments."** A judgement call. The clause is long enough to cost something, and it is also the whole reason the sentence is worth writing, since the point is that this is out of character. Splitting it would work: "Mrs Okafor has missed two payments. She has lived at the property since 2011 and has never previously been in arrears." Whether that is better depends on whether you want the reader to meet her history before the bad news or after it, which is lesson 2's question about bad news, showing up inside a single sentence.

The pattern across all four: **a short interruption that tells the reader which thing the subject is usually earns its place. A long one that gives history usually does not.**
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Short sentences are clear sentences."** Length correlates with the fault and is not the fault. The nineteen-word example above is the counterexample to carry around.

**"Vary your sentence length."** Common advice, usually given with no reason attached, and it is really advice about rhythm rather than clarity. Rhythm matters, and it is not what this lesson is about. Do not let a rule about variety talk you into leaving a twenty-word gap for the sake of the music.

**"I read it back and it sounded fine."** It will. You know what the verb is going to be. This is the single most common reason writers miss gaps in their own work, and it is why the exercise below asks for a number.

**"Parentheses are the problem."** The punctuation is innocent. A twenty-word parenthesis between subject and verb is a problem, and so is a twenty-word relative clause, and so is a twenty-word list, none of which uses brackets. Look for the distance, not the marks.

**"This contradicts putting the character first."** It completes it. Get the right subject, then let it act promptly. If you can do only one, do lesson 3, because a sentence with the wrong subject cannot be rescued by putting its verb close to it.

## Practice

:::exercise Count the gaps in a page of your own writing
Use the document you have been carrying since lesson 1, or a fresh page of anything you wrote for someone else.

1. Take one page, about 300 to 400 words. For every sentence, find the main subject and its verb and count the words between them. Write the number in the margin. Do all of them, including the ones that are obviously fine, because you need the distribution and not just the outliers.

2. Rank them. You will usually find that two or three sentences carry most of the trouble and the rest are already fine. That is normal, and it is why this is worth measuring rather than rewriting everything.

3. Take your worst sentence and fix it **all three ways**: interruption to the front, interruption to the end, and split into two. Write out all three versions in full. Do not choose yet.

4. Now choose, and write one line saying why. The question is not which sounds best. It is what you want the reader to be thinking about when the sentence ends.

5. Fix the next two worst, one way each.

6. Last, check your three rewrites against lesson 3. Is the subject of each one a character, and is the verb its action? If you have closed a gap but left an abstraction sitting in the subject slot, you have done half the job.
:::

## Where this goes next

You have now met two facts about readers that both turn out to be about the same thing. They want a character doing something, and they want the doing to arrive quickly. Both are about the front of the sentence.

The next lesson is about the other end, and it is the deepest idea in the course. You have already brushed against it twice: in the three fixes above, where the choice depended on what landed last, and in lesson 2, where a whole document had to be arranged around what the reader meets first. Lesson 5 gives you the principle underneath both, and with it the reason a paragraph of perfectly good sentences can still read like a list of unrelated facts.
