Lesson 1 established that a reader takes in about a fifth of an average page, and gets through half the information only on pages of about 111 words or fewer. That finding has been driving everything since, but it has been driving it at the level of sentences and paragraphs, which quietly assumes the reader is reading.

Often they are not. They are looking. They have opened a document to find one thing, and they are moving down it at speed, and prose is not what they are using to navigate.

What they are using is the part of writing that most writing courses do not teach, on the grounds that it belongs to somebody else. The US federal plain-language guide does not treat it that way: one of its four parts is [Design for understanding](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/design), a quarter of the whole subject, and its opening claim is blunt. **"Dense, cluttered writing deters people from taking the time to read."**

## Headings are the document a skimmer actually reads

Here is the test for a heading, and it disqualifies most of the headings you have written.

**A heading has to give a reader enough to decide whether to stop.**

Now look at the ones everybody uses. *Background. Context. Introduction. Next steps. Considerations. About this document.* Every one of them is a category label. Every one of them would fit almost any document in the world, which means none of them tells a particular reader anything about this one.

The federal guidance asks for two things: "use heading levels consistently and appropriately to create hierarchy", and "write heading terms that are clear and descriptive". The second is the one people fail. Descriptive means it describes *this content*, not the kind of content.

| Category label | Same section, described |
|---|---|
| Background | Why the old system was replaced in April |
| Next steps | What we need from you by 14 March |
| Considerations | Three things that could delay this |
| Analysis | Costs came in eleven percent over budget |
| About this scheme | Who can apply, and who cannot |

Read down the right-hand column on its own. You now know roughly what that document says, which is a thing you cannot do with the left-hand column at any length.

:::predict A report has these headings: Introduction, Methodology, Findings, Discussion, Conclusion, Appendices. Is that wrong?
Not necessarily, and this is worth being careful about. In a scientific paper those headings are a convention that readers know how to use, and a reader looking for the method knows exactly where to go. The labels are doing navigational work precisely because the structure is standardised across thousands of papers.

The failure is when those headings are imported into a document with no such convention behind it, which is where most of them live: internal reports, proposals, briefing notes, project updates. There, "Findings" tells a reader nothing, because they have no idea what kind of finding to expect.

So the rule is not that category headings are banned. It is that a category heading is only doing work when the category is a convention your reader already navigates by. Otherwise it is a word occupying the most valuable line on the page.
:::

## Prose, list, or table

These three are not stylistic choices. Each one makes a different claim about the content, and choosing the wrong one tells the reader something untrue.

**Prose** says: these things are related, and the relations matter. Because, therefore, although, unless. Prose is the only form that can carry an argument, because an argument is made of joins.

**A list** says: these items are parallel and independent. Each one stands on its own and could be reordered without loss. The federal guide's advice is to "use lists for easy scanning", and that is exactly what parallel independent items are good for.

**A table** says: this has two dimensions. Every item is described by the same set of properties, and the reader will want to compare across them. The guidance is to "where appropriate, use tables to make complex relationships clear", and the operative word is relationships, in the plural.

The choice follows from asking one question: **what relationship does this content actually have?** Not what would look tidier.

:::callout One extra distinction inside lists
A bulleted list claims the items are parallel and unordered. A numbered list claims they are in a sequence that matters.

Numbering a set of unordered options implies a priority that is not there, and a reader will look for it. Bulleting a set of steps that must happen in order throws away the part that mattered most. Neither is a formatting preference; each is a claim about the content.
:::

## Worked example: same words, restructured

Here is a note as it arrived. Every word of it is fine.

> The consultation on the parking scheme closed on 30 April. We received 412 responses, which is roughly four times what we normally see, and about a third of them came in during the final week after the local paper covered it. The main objection, raised in 260 responses, was the cost of a residents' permit. The second most common, in 140 responses, was the proposed boundary, particularly on the east side. A smaller number, 38, objected to the enforcement hours. We are proposing to reduce the permit charge from £90 to £60, to move the eastern boundary to exclude Mill Lane, and to leave the enforcement hours as consulted. The revised scheme will go to committee on 12 June. If members want a different option on the permit charge, we would need to know before 28 May in order to model it.

Now the same words, given shape. Nothing is rewritten.

> **The consultation closed on 30 April with 412 responses, four times the usual**
>
> About a third arrived in the final week, after the local paper covered it.
>
> **What people objected to**
>
> | Objection | Responses |
> |---|---|
> | Cost of a residents' permit | 260 |
> | The proposed boundary, particularly the east side | 140 |
> | Enforcement hours | 38 |
>
> **What we propose to change**
>
> - Reduce the permit charge from £90 to £60
> - Move the eastern boundary to exclude Mill Lane
> - Leave the enforcement hours as consulted
>
> **What we need from members, and by when**
>
> The revised scheme goes to committee on 12 June. If members want a different option on the permit charge, we need to know **before 28 May** so it can be modelled.

Three things happened, and each was a decision about content rather than about looks.

The three objections became a **table** because they are one kind of thing measured on one axis, and the reader's question is which is biggest. The three proposals became a **bulleted list** because they are parallel and independent, and reordering them would lose nothing. The last section stayed as **prose** because it contains a condition and a consequence, which is a relation, and a list would have severed it.

And the headings tell the story on their own. Read only the four of them and you know what the note says.

## The wrinkle: bullet points can destroy an argument

Bullets are the default in a great many workplaces, applied to everything, and here is what they cost when applied to the wrong thing.

Original:

> We should delay the launch until July. The regional teams have flagged capacity problems over the summer, and although the Q2 figures are strong, they are strong in exactly the regions that would be short-staffed. A May launch would therefore put our thinnest support cover against our highest demand.

Bulleted:

> We should delay the launch until July.
> - Regional teams have flagged capacity problems over the summer
> - Q2 figures are strong
> - The strong regions are the ones that would be short-staffed
> - A May launch puts thin support cover against high demand

Every fact survives. The argument does not.

In the original, *although* tells you the second fact cuts against the case and is being answered. *Exactly* tells you the third fact is the answer. *Therefore* tells you the fourth is the conclusion. In the bulleted version those three words are gone, and four claims sit side by side as if they were four separate observations of equal weight. A reader now has to reconstruct which one is the objection and which is the reply.

**A list is a claim that the items are parallel and independent.** When they are not, the list is a false statement about the content, and the falsehood is invisible because it lives in the formatting rather than in any sentence.

:::checkpoint For each of these, decide the form, and say what relationship the content has.
**The five documents someone must attach to an application.** A bulleted list. Parallel, independent, order does not matter, and the reader is going to use it as a checklist.

**How three pension options compare on charges, access and risk.** A table. Two dimensions, three options against three properties, and the reader's whole purpose is to compare across.

**Why a project was late.** Prose. The reasons interact, some caused others, and one of them is probably the real one while the rest are context. All of that lives in the joins, and a list would flatten it into a set of equally weighted excuses.

**The steps to reset a password.** A numbered list. Parallel in form, strictly ordered in content, and the numbering is the part the reader uses when they lose their place halfway.

**A single caveat that applies to everything above it.** Prose, and probably one sentence. A one-item bulleted list is a bullet that has nothing to be parallel to, which is a common tic and always worth deleting.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Bullets are always clearer."** They are clearer for parallel independent items and worse for everything else. The bulleted argument above is the standard demonstration.

**"Headings are for long documents."** A 300-word email with two informative headings is easier to act on than the same email in one block. The threshold is not length; it is whether the reader might want to go to one part.

**"Formatting is the designer's job."** Deciding that three things are a table and three others are a list is deciding what relationship the content has. Nobody applying formatting afterwards can make that decision for you, because it is a claim about meaning.

**"A table is for numbers."** A table is for two dimensions. Plenty of good tables contain no numbers at all, including the heading table earlier in this lesson.

**"White space is wasted space."** The guide's own claim is that dense, cluttered writing deters people from reading at all, so space that gets someone to start is not waste.

**"I will add the headings at the end."** Headings written last tend to be category labels, because by then you are describing sections rather than telling the reader what is in them. Write them as you go and rewrite them at the end.

## Practice

:::exercise Restructure without rewriting, then read the headings alone
Take the document you have carried since lesson 1, or a longer one if that has become short.

1. **Change no sentences in this exercise.** You may move text, split it, and add headings. If you catch yourself rewriting, you are avoiding the exercise, because the point is to show how much can be gained without touching the prose.

2. Go through and mark every stretch where the content is a set of parallel independent items, and every stretch where it has two dimensions. Convert the first kind to lists and the second to tables. Leave everything with a *because* or an *although* in it as prose.

3. Give every section a heading that would let a reader decide whether to stop. Then check each one against the test: could this heading sit unchanged on somebody else's document about something else? If yes, it is a category label, and rewrite it.

4. **Now the test.** Write out your headings, in order, on their own, with nothing else. Read them. Do they tell the story? A reader who saw only those, which is close to what a skimming reader gets, would come away with what?

5. If the heading list does not tell the story, the problem is usually one of two things. Either a heading is a label, or the document's sections are not the shape of its argument. The second is more common and more useful to find.

6. Last, count. How many words did you move, and how many did you change? If the second number is not close to zero, do step 1 again on a fresh copy.
:::
