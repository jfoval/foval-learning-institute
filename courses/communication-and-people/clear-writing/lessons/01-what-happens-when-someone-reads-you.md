A friend forwards you a 900-word email from her landlord and asks what it means. You read it. Somewhere in the sixth paragraph there is a date, and the date is in nine days, and everything else in the email is context. Your friend had already read it twice and missed the date both times.

The landlord is not a bad person and the email is not badly spelled. It is written in the order the landlord thought about the problem, which is not the order your friend needed to meet it. That mismatch is what this course is about, and it costs people money, appointments, medication and jobs.

Before any advice about how to write, it is worth knowing what actually happens on the other end. Not what ought to happen. What the evidence says does.

## Nobody is obliged to keep reading

Start with the only measurement of ordinary reading that most writers have ever heard of, and then take it apart, because it is quoted far more often than it is understood.

In 2008 Jakob Nielsen published [an analysis of how little people read online](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/). The underlying data came from a study by Weinreich and colleagues, who "instrumented 25 users' browsers and recorded extended information about everything they did" as those people went about their normal web use. After cleaning, the analysis covered 45,237 page views. That is not a laboratory task; it is a record of people doing whatever they were already doing.

Two numbers came out of it. A page visit starts with a fixed cost of roughly **25 seconds**, whatever is on the page. And each additional 100 words buys about **4.4 more seconds** of attention.

Sit with the second number, because it is the strange one. It does not say people give more time to longer pages in proportion. It says a page ten times as long gets nowhere near ten times the attention. Nielsen's conclusion was that on an average page "users will have time to read 28% of the words," and that "more realistically, users will read about 20% of the text."

:::predict Before you read on: what do you expect the 4.4 seconds to buy?
An adult reading carefully manages somewhere around 250 words a minute, which is 24 seconds per 100 words. The reader is being offered 4.4 seconds for work that takes 24. So each extra hundred words is funded at a little under a fifth of its cost. That is where the 20 percent comes from, and it is why the shortfall grows with every paragraph rather than staying constant.
:::

## The two lines, and where they cross

Put the two rates on the same axes and the finding stops being a slogan.

One line is the time a reader would **need** in order to read every word, at 250 words a minute. The other is the time the reader actually **gives**, at 25 seconds plus 4.4 seconds per 100 words. Both come straight out of the numbers above.

The lines cross at about 130 words. Below that, a reader has more time than your document needs, and the writing hardly has to work. Above it, the gap opens and never closes.

That is the sentence worth keeping. **A reader will finish about 130 words. Past that, you are not deciding whether they miss something. You are deciding what.** A landlord who buries a date in paragraph six has made that decision without noticing they were making it.

:::callout Where these lines come from
The 25 seconds and the 4.4 seconds are Nielsen's, from the browsing data. The 250 words a minute is a standard figure for adult reading of ordinary prose, and the crossing point moves if you change it: at 200 words a minute the lines cross at about 160 words, at 300 they cross at about 110. The shape does not change, and the shape is the point.
:::

## Where the number does not apply

Here is the part that gets left out when this finding is quoted, and leaving it out turns a useful estimate into a bad rule.

Nielsen says two things about his own data that limit it. The participants "had above-average intelligence, with several being university employees." And the analysis excluded pages with very high word counts, such as academic papers and terms and conditions, which he judged "probably not 'real' pages anyway."

So the finding is about **capable people skimming ordinary web pages.** It is not a law about reading.

Think about how differently the same person reads in a single day. They skim a news page over breakfast and take in a fifth of it. Then a letter arrives from the hospital about their mother, and they read every word of it twice, then read it again to someone on the phone. Same reader, same eyes. What changed is that the second document was about something they needed.

This is not a hole in the research. It is the research telling you what question it answered. The correct use of the 20 percent figure is as a **default assumption for a reader who has not yet decided your document matters to them**, which describes most documents most of the time. The correct use is not to cut a surgical consent form to two sentences.

:::checkpoint Which of these should you plan for using the 20 percent assumption, and which should you not?
An internal announcement about a change to the expenses system: **use it.** Nobody woke up wanting to read this, and it competes with everything else in the inbox.

A letter telling someone their benefit claim has been refused and how to appeal: **do not use it.** This reader will read every word, several times, possibly while distressed. Length is not the enemy here; ambiguity is, and so is making them hunt for the deadline.

A product page for a kettle: **use it.** Classic skimming, and the reader is comparing rather than reading.

The instructions on a fire door: **neither.** Nobody is reading this at all until the moment they are, and then they need one instruction, visible at a glance, from three metres away. This is a design problem before it is a writing problem, which is lesson 7.
:::

## Who the document is actually for

The reading budget tells you that you have less room than you thought. It does not tell you what to put in the room. For that you have to know who is reading, and most writers answer that question with a category rather than a person.

"Residents of the borough" is a category. It cannot change a single sentence of your letter. Compare it with: *people who owe the new charge, who need to know by when, and what happens if they cannot pay.* That version tells you what the first paragraph is, what the second paragraph is, and what can be cut.

The test for whether you have named your audience properly is simple. **If knowing the answer would not change a sentence, you have not answered it.**

Two failures are worth naming because they are so common they feel like normal practice.

The first is writing for the approver. A great many documents are shaped by what a manager, a legal team or a communications lead will accept, and this pressure is real, but the approver is not the reader and the two want different things. The approver wants nothing wrong in it. The reader wants to know what to do. A document optimised entirely for the first is how you get nine hundred words of context and a date in paragraph six.

The second is writing for yourself six months ago. It is genuinely hard to remember not knowing something. You write "submit form CT2 by the deadline" because you know what CT2 is and when the deadline is, and the sentence looks complete to you because your own knowledge quietly fills the gaps as you reread it. This is why rereading your own work is such a poor test of whether it is clear, and it is why this course ends with getting an actual other person to tell you what they think it said.

## Plain does not mean simple, and it is not optional

There is a suspicion that writing plainly is a kind of condescension, that it means writing down to people. It is worth answering, because it stops good writers from doing the thing that would help most.

The UK government publishes its writing standards in the open. [Its guidance on clear language](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/) gives a reason for the policy that is not about elegance at all: **"1 in 6 adults in England have very poor literacy skills."** For a government, writing a form that a sixth of the population cannot use is not a stylistic failure. It is a failure to deliver the service.

Then the same guidance answers the condescension objection directly, and this sentence is worth memorising: **"people with higher levels of literacy prefer plain English because it allows them to understand the information as quickly as possible."**

That is the whole answer. A specialist does not read your plain sentence and feel patronised. They read it, get it, and move on to the part they actually needed their expertise for. Complexity in the writing does not signal complexity in the thought; it just spends the reader's attention on the wrong thing.

None of this is one country's taste, either. In the United States the [Plain Writing Act of 2010](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language) "established the requirement that content for the public is written for its specific audience", and plain English is mandatory across the whole of GOV.UK. Two governments, two drafting traditions, the same conclusion.

## Difficulty is not a property of your document

One more idea, and it undermines something you have probably been told to trust.

There is a large industry built on scoring text for reading level. Paste a page in, get a grade. The scores come from formulas that count surface features: syllables per word, words per sentence. They are quick, and they are the reason people believe difficulty is something a document has, like a word count.

In 2021 Gooding, Berzak, Mak and Sharifi ran [a study of 518 readers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.06354) that measured how people scrolled through texts of different difficulty. Two findings matter here. Reading behaviour differs measurably with text difficulty, so difficulty is real and measurable. And **reader background influences both how people read and what makes a text feel difficult to them.**

Put those together and difficulty turns out to be a relation between a text and a reader, not a property of the text. The pension page that is transparent to someone in financial services and impenetrable to everyone else has not got two difficulties. It has one text, meeting different readers.

This has a sharp practical consequence. A formula that knows nothing about the reader cannot tell you whether the reader understood. It can only tell you about syllables. You can improve a score by chopping a sentence in half at the comma and changing nothing whatever about what a reader takes away.

:::predict A colleague reports that a rewrite moved the page from "grade 12" to "grade 8". What have you learned about the rewrite?
That the words got shorter, the sentences got shorter, or both. Nothing else follows. You have not learned whether a reader can now find the deadline, whether the main point moved to the front, whether the technical term that actually blocked people is still there, or whether the shorter sentences broke the connections between the ideas and made the passage harder to follow. The score went down. The question of whether anyone understands it better is untouched, and lesson 9 is about how to answer it properly.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"If they cared, they would read it properly."** They might. But you do not get to choose whether your reader is tired, interrupted, on a phone with 4% battery, or reading in their second language. Writing that only works on an attentive reader is writing that fails exactly when it matters.

**"Shorter is always better."** The findings above are about attention, not about length as a virtue. A 130-word notice that omits the appeal deadline has not saved the reader anything. Later lessons are almost entirely about making the same content easier to take in, which is a different move from having less content.

**"My readers are like me."** They know less about your subject, care less about it, and arrived from somewhere you did not anticipate. The whole of lesson 9 exists because this belief survives everything except meeting an actual reader.

**"A readability score measures clarity."** It estimates one input to clarity by counting proxies. When the estimate and a real reader disagree, the reader is right.

**"Plain writing is for people who struggle."** Answered above, by the people whose job it is to write for a whole country.

## Practice

:::exercise Measure one thing you wrote
Do this on something real. An email you sent, a report, a message to a group. It should be at least 300 words, and it should be something someone actually had to act on.

1. Count the words. Most editors will do it; otherwise paste it somewhere that will.

2. Work out the two numbers from this lesson. Time the reader gives: 25 seconds, plus 4.4 seconds for every 100 words. Time the document needs: the word count divided by 250, times 60. Write both down in seconds.

3. Divide the first by the second. That is roughly the share of your document a distracted reader had time for.

4. Now the part that matters. Read your own document and mark the **one sentence** you would most want a reader to land on if they read only that share of it. Just one. Then find where it currently sits: how many words in, and how far down the screen on a phone.

5. Write one line naming the reader. Not a category. Who they are, what they came needing, and what they will do next if they understand you. Then check your line against the test in this lesson: if knowing this would not change a sentence, you have not answered it yet.

**Keep this document.** It is the one you will carry through the whole course, and it is the thing you will hand to another person in lesson 9.
:::

## Where this goes next

You now have the constraint. A reader gives you a fixed and small amount of attention, they give it before deciding whether your document deserves more, and what they find hard depends on who they are rather than on a score.

Everything after this is a response to that constraint, and the responses get progressively deeper. The next lesson is the most obvious one: if the reader only gets part of your document, put the part that matters where they will be. After that the course goes below the level of arrangement and into the sentence itself, where the real work is.
