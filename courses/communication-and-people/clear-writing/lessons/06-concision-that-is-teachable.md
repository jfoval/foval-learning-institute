A paragraph of the kind that circulates in every organisation. It's 111 words.

> It should be noted at the outset that, due to the fact that the previous system was not able to handle the volume of applications that we were receiving during the course of the busy summer period, a decision was taken by the management team in order to procure a replacement. In the event that the new system is not fully and completely operational by the end of September, there is a possibility that we may possibly need to revert back to the old one on a temporary basis for a short period of time. It is important to note that this would not be a desirable outcome for anyone concerned.

And here it is at 46 words, with nothing removed but words.

> The old system could not handle the summer volume of applications, so the management team bought a replacement. If the new one is not fully working by the end of September, we may have to go back to the old one for a while. Nobody wants that.

Every fact survives. The old system, the volume, the summer, the management team, the replacement, September, the temporary reversion, and the fact that this would be unwelcome are all still there. Fifty-nine percent of the words are not.

Note "not fully working" rather than "not working". The original said "not fully and completely operational", and a system that isn't *fully* operational may still be partly operational, which is a different fact from one that isn't working at all. *Fully and completely* is a doubled pair and one of them goes; *fully* is not padding, and dropping it would have been a cut that changed what the sentence claims. That distinction is the whole discipline of this lesson.

## What the famous advice actually says

Strunk's version of this, in [*The Elements of Style*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134.txt) of 1918, is the most quoted piece of writing advice there is. It's also routinely quoted at a quarter of its length, and the quarter that gets quoted is the least useful part.

The whole of rule 13's first paragraph reads:

> "Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts. **This requires not that the writer make all his sentences short, or that he avoid all detail and treat his subjects only in outline, but that he make every word tell.**"

The analogy is exactly right. A machine part that does nothing isn't neutral; it's weight, and something has to carry it. And the bolded sentence, which almost nobody quotes, is Strunk answering the misreading his own headline invites, sixty years before anyone accused him of it. He is not telling you to be brief. He is telling you to make every word tell.

Then there's the part that surprises people who only know the four words. Rule 13 doesn't stop at the principle. It gives you a list:

> the question as to whether → **whether**
>
> there is no doubt but that → **no doubt**
>
> used for fuel purposes → **used for fuel**
>
> he is a man who → **he**
>
> in a hasty manner → **hastily**
>
> owing to the fact that → **since**
>
> in spite of the fact that → **though**

That is instruction, not a slogan, and two of this lesson's six kinds are sitting in it already.

So where's the objection? Geoffrey Pullum puts it this way in ["50 Years of Stupid Grammar Advice"](https://pullum.ppls.ed.ac.uk/50years.pdf):

> "Many are useless, like 'Omit needless words.' (The students who know which words are needless don't need the instruction.)"

Read fairly, that lands on the **headline** and not on the rule. Four words on their own tell you what the finished state looks like and nothing about how to reach it, rather like telling someone to sculpt an elephant by removing everything that doesn't look like an elephant. Strunk knew that, which is why he wrote three more paragraphs and a substitution list. What happened over a century is that the headline travelled and the rule stayed behind.

Pullum is careful about this too, and the care usually gets dropped when he's quoted. In the same essay he calls the style advice "mostly harmless" and writes: "Even so, it doesn't hurt to lay such well-meant maxims before novice writers." His quarrel is with the book's grammatical claims, which lesson 8 takes up, not with the idea of telling a beginner to cut.

So this lesson isn't supplying what Strunk left out. It's doing what he did, at more length and with the kinds named, because a list you can hold in your head is what makes the cut repeatable.

## The six kinds

Don't cut by feel. Name the kind of every cut, and you'll be able to do it again next week and on someone else's writing.

**This list is mine.** It was assembled from the sources at the foot of this lesson and from the pattern of the examples; Strunk's list is shorter, Williams's catalogue is longer. Use this one because six is few enough to remember at the moment you need it.

| Kind | What it is | Example, and the cut |
|---|---|---|
| **Doubled words** | Two words joined by *and* where either would do | *full and complete* → **complete**. Also *each and every*, *first and foremost*, *hopes and aspirations* |
| **Already implied** | The sentence has said it once in another word | *period of time* → **period**. Also *advance planning*, *revert back*, *large in size*, *in the month of May* |
| **Metadiscourse** | Writing about the writing | *It should be noted that*, *I would like to say that*, *It is important to note that*. Cut the whole phrase |
| **A phrase for a word** | Four words doing one word's job | *due to the fact that* → **because**. Also *in the event that* → **if**, *at this point in time* → **now**, *in order to* → **to** |
| **Negative for positive** | A not that has a word of its own | *was not able to* → **could not**. Also *did not remember* → **forgot**, *not many* → **few** |
| **Hedge stacking** | Two or three hedges where one is honest | *there is a possibility that we may possibly* → **we may**. Also *it seems likely that it could perhaps* |

None of these is a rule about long words, and every cut above leaves the vocabulary alone.

:::predict Name the kind for each of these three, before you read on. "In the event that", "each and every", "did not remember".
*In the event that* is a phrase for a word, and the word is *if*. Four words to one.

*Each and every* is a doubled pair joined by *and*, where either half carries the whole meaning. Pick one.

*Did not remember* is a negative for a positive, and the positive is *forgot*. Three words to one.

If you found those easy, that's the point of naming kinds. The hard part of concision isn't spotting that something is wrong, it's knowing what to do about it, and a named kind comes with its own fix attached.
:::

:::callout Two of these are not really about concision
*Metadiscourse* and *hedge stacking* are usually symptoms of something else. Metadiscourse piles up when a writer is nervous about the content and warms up before saying it. Hedges stack when the writer is not sure of the claim. If you find a lot of either, the cut is worth making, and it is also worth asking what the writing is being cautious about, because that is often the real problem with the document.
:::

## Down the paragraph, cut by cut

The 111-word paragraph again, with every cut named. Work down it and you'll see nothing left over that the six kinds and lesson 3 can't account for.

| Original | Kind | Result |
|---|---|---|
| It should be noted at the outset that | metadiscourse | *(gone)* |
| due to the fact that | phrase for a word | so |
| was not able to | negative for positive | could not |
| the volume of applications that we were receiving | already implied, since a volume you handle is one you receive | the volume of applications |
| during the course of the busy summer period | phrase for a word, twice over | the summer |
| a decision was taken by the management team in order to procure | buried action, from lesson 3 | the management team bought |
| In the event that | phrase for a word | If |
| fully and completely operational | doubled words | fully working |
| there is a possibility that we may possibly need to | hedge stacking | we may have to |
| revert back | already implied, since revert means to go back | go back |
| on a temporary basis for a short period of time | doubled and a phrase for a word | for a while |
| It is important to note that this would not be a desirable outcome for anyone concerned | metadiscourse plus a negative for a positive | Nobody wants that. |

Notice the row that isn't one of the six. "A decision was taken in order to procure" is a buried action from lesson 3, and undoing it is a concision move as well as a clarity move. Count it as a seventh kind when you tally your own cuts, because it's the one that returns the most words for a single change. That overlap isn't a coincidence: an action buried in a noun always needs extra words to hold it up.

:::predict How much of this can go, and what kind is each cut?
> "At this point in time, it would appear that there may possibly be a need for us to give consideration to a review of the current arrangements that are presently in place."

One answer: **"We may need to review the current arrangements."** Eight words from thirty-two, which is three quarters of it gone.

The cuts, in order. *At this point in time* is a phrase for a word, and the word is *now*, which then turns out to be implied by the present tense and goes entirely. *It would appear that there may possibly be a need for us to* is hedge stacking, three hedges deep, and becomes *we may need to*. *Give consideration to a review of* is a buried action twice over, and becomes *review*. *That are presently in place* is already implied by *current*, and *current* and *presently* are themselves a doubled pair, so one of them goes.

If you kept a hedge, that can be right. If you genuinely are unsure, *we may need to review* keeps one and is honest. What you shouldn't keep is all three.
:::

## The wrinkle: some repetition is holding the passage together

This lesson couldn't have come any earlier, and here's why.

Take two sentences from lesson 5's bridge paragraph:

> Replacing those beams would cost about two million pounds. That cost is far more than the four hundred thousand pounds left in this year's capital budget.

A concision pass will find "That cost is far more than" and see six words repeating something the reader was told half a second ago. Cutting looks obvious:

> Replacing those beams would cost about two million pounds. Only four hundred thousand pounds is left in this year's capital budget.

Five words shorter, both figures intact, no fact lost. And the paragraph is worse, because the second sentence now opens on a fresh number instead of on something the reader already has. The chain from lesson 5 is broken, and the reader has to work out the relation between two million and four hundred thousand for themselves.

**Repetition in the topic position is not padding. It is the join.** It looks exactly like padding, which is why concision passes damage prose so often, and why "cut anything you've already said" is a dangerous rule for anyone who hasn't met the given and new contract.

So ask what the repeated words are doing. If they're carrying the reader from the previous sentence into this one, they're load-bearing. If they're just there, they're not.

:::checkpoint Which of these repetitions is padding and which is the join?
**"The trust introduced a new booking system in April. *The system* had been in development for two years."** A join. It puts old information in the topic position, and cutting it to "It had been in development for two years" would be fine too, since *it* does the same job in fewer words. Either keeps the link.

**"The report recommends closure. *The report* was commissioned in January. *The report* runs to ninety pages."** Padding, in the second and third instances, and something worse: three sentences in a row opening on the same words is a list, not a chain. This wants restructuring, not cutting.

**"We received your application on 3 March. *Your application* is now with the assessment team."** A join, and worth keeping over *it* in this case, because the letter is about the reader's application and the phrase is doing reassurance as well as linkage.

**"The committee met on Tuesday. *At that meeting on Tuesday*, the following matters were discussed."** Padding. *At that meeting* would be the join; adding *on Tuesday* again repeats what the reader has, without carrying anything forward.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Shorter is always better."** A passage that's been cut below the point where it makes sense is shorter and useless. The target isn't fewer words; it's no words that fail to carry something. Strunk says this himself in the rule, which is the sentence nobody quotes: not that you make all your sentences short, "but that he make every word tell."

**"Cut every adjective and adverb."** This one isn't Strunk's. "Write with nouns and verbs, not with adjectives and adverbs" was added in the 1959 revision, and Pullum notes that the very next sentence of the book breaks it. This course hasn't read that revision and takes the wording from Pullum. Either way the advice is wrong: adjectives that carry information aren't padding. *A twelve-page report* and *a report* are different facts.

**"Never repeat a word."** This one causes real damage. It sends writers to a thesaurus to find a different word for the same thing, and now the reader has to work out whether the *scheme* in this sentence is the same as the *programme* in the last one. Use the same word for the same thing, every time. Lesson 9 comes back to it: in Nancy Sommers's study of student and experienced revisers, one student described his whole revision strategy as reaching for a thesaurus. It's a recognisable habit, though that was one writer in a study of fifteen, so take it as a thing to watch for in yourself rather than as a measured difference between novices and experts.

**"Concision is about vocabulary."** Nearly every cut in this lesson's table left the vocabulary untouched. Replacing *utilise* with *use* is worth doing and saves you two letters. Replacing *there is a possibility that we may possibly need to* with *we may* saves you nine words.

**"I'll know padding when I see it."** You won't, in your own writing, for the reason lesson 4 gave: you know what you meant, so every word looks necessary. Name the kind. If you can't name the kind, you're guessing.

## Practice

:::exercise Cut something of yours by a third, and label every cut
Take 45 minutes over the cutting. Use the document you've carried since lesson 1, in whatever state lessons 2 to 5 have left it. Step 6 needs another person, so it happens later.

1. Count the words. Write the number at the top. Your target is two thirds of it. A third is a working target, not a law: this lesson's own paragraph lost nearly sixty percent, and a document that's already been through lessons 2 to 5 may have much less to give.

2. Work through it once, cutting only what you can name. Keep a tally as you go, in seven columns: the six kinds, plus buried actions from lesson 3. **If you can't say which kind a cut is, don't make it yet.** Put a question mark in the margin and move on.

3. Now go back to the question marks. For each, read the sentence without the words and ask what a reader has lost. If the answer is nothing, cut it and work out afterwards which kind it was. If the answer is a link back to the previous sentence, put it back, and mark it as a join.

4. Look at your tally. Most people find one kind dominates, and knowing which one is yours is worth more than this whole exercise. Metadiscourse tends to mean you're warming up before you say things. Hedge stacking tends to mean you're not sure. Phrases for words tends to mean you learnt to write in an institution.

5. Check your cuts against lesson 5. Read only the opening words of each sentence, in order, as you did there. If the chain broke anywhere, you cut a join.

6. Then the test that settles it, whenever you can get it. Give the original and the cut version to one person, in that order, and ask which is clearer and where they got lost. If the shorter one is harder, you've cut joins, and step 5 will tell you where.
:::

## Where this goes next

You have now finished the sentence and the paragraph. Everything so far has been about what happens inside a passage of prose.

The next lesson goes up a level, to the parts of a document that aren't prose at all: headings, lists and tables. Those are what a reader uses when they're not reading but looking, and lesson 1's figures are about exactly that reader, the one who takes in about a fifth of a page they haven't yet decided to commit to. It's the quarter of the subject that writing courses usually leave to designers.
