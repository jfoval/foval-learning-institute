A letter about a planning application contained this sentence. It's grammatical, correctly spelled, and only twenty words long.

> Determination of the application was undertaken following consideration of representations received during the consultation period by officers with delegated authority.

Read it twice and you can work out roughly what happened. That work you just did is the subject of this lesson, and the point is that you shouldn't have had to do it.

Now the same event, in eleven words:

> Officers with delegated authority decided the application after reading the responses.

One thing did get dropped, and it's worth naming: the consultation period. Everything else is still there. The representations became "responses", the delegated authority survived intact, and the determination became "decided". If the period matters, put it back, and the sentence still runs to fourteen words: "after reading the responses sent during the consultation". What changed isn't that anything was cut. It's that somebody is now doing something.

## The move that does most of the work

Joseph Williams built his book *Style* around two principles, as everyone who has read it reports, including the sources this course has read. Gopen and Swan reach the same place from the reader's side in [their seven structural principles](https://wstomv.win.tue.nl/quotes/science-of-scientific-writing.html), where they appear as numbers three and five. Put together they say:

**Make the main character of your sentence its grammatical subject, and put that character's action in the verb.**

Before you take that as a rule, take their own warning with it. The same paper says **"None of these reader-expectation principles should be considered 'rules.'"** They're describing what readers expect, not issuing prohibitions, and a description of an expectation can be worth violating on purpose. Lesson 8 is about the difference, and this lesson's last section is about the cases where you should.

A teacher of writers and two describers of readers landing in the same place is better evidence than either alone. Be careful how much you make of it, though: Gopen and Swan wrote nine years after Williams, and this course hasn't read Williams's own text to see whether he's behind them. What you can say is that the principle turns up independently of any one author's taste, which is more than most writing advice can claim.

The mechanism is easy to state. A reader meets a sentence expecting a story, and a story needs somebody doing something. If your subject is a person or a thing that acts, and your verb is the acting, the reader gets the story for free. If not, they have to assemble it from the nouns, and they will, and it costs them.

That's what happened to you in the planning sentence. "Determination was undertaken" gave you a subject that isn't a character and a verb that isn't the action. The real action, deciding, was sitting inside the noun *determination*. The real character, the officers, was parked at the very end behind the word *by*. You reconstructed the sentence in your head, and every sentence like it spends the reader's attention on assembly instead of on what you're telling them. That's the cost, and lesson 1 is about how little of it you have.

## Diagnose before you rewrite

Almost everyone starts rewriting straight away, shuffling words until it sounds better. That produces an improvement you can't repeat.

Do it in two steps instead, and write the first step down.

**Step one, name the character and the action.** Who or what is doing something, and what are they doing? Answer in ordinary words, not in the sentence's own vocabulary.

**Step two, put them in the slots.** Character becomes the subject. Action becomes the verb.

Diagnose these five before rewriting any of them.

| Sentence | Character | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Determination of the application was undertaken by officers. | the officers | deciding |
| There was a failure of communication between the two departments. | the two departments | not telling each other |
| The implementation of the new policy will take place in April. | somebody unnamed | starting the policy |
| An assessment of the risks has been carried out. | somebody unnamed | assessing the risks |
| Our expectation is that completion of the works will occur by June. | we, and the builders | expecting, and finishing |

Look at the third and fourth rows. The character came out as "somebody unnamed", and that isn't a failure of the diagnosis. It *is* the diagnosis. Those sentences don't say who is implementing or who assessed, and you can't put a character in the subject slot until you decide who it is. That's the whole point of the harder example below.

:::predict Diagnose this one before you rewrite it. "A reduction in waiting times was achieved through the introduction of a new booking system." Character? Action?
The character is whoever runs the service, which the sentence never names. There are two actions, and both are buried in nouns. *Reduction* is really "cut" or "brought down", and *introduction* is really "brought in" or "started using".

So a rewrite has to answer a question the original ducked. If it's the hospital, then: "The hospital cut waiting times by bringing in a new booking system." If nobody knows who, the honest version says so, or names the service rather than a person: "Waiting times fell after the new booking system started." Notice the second version drops the claim that the booking system *caused* the fall, which the original asserted with the word *achieved*. That isn't a side effect of tidying. Undoing a buried action often exposes a causal claim that was resting on nothing.
:::

## Nominalisation, and why the -ion rule is only a detector

The noun that swallowed the verb has a name. A **nominalisation** is a verb or an adjective turned into a noun: *decide* becomes *decision*, *fail* becomes *failure*, *implement* becomes *implementation*, *reduce* becomes *reduction*.

[GOV.UK's guidance](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/) gives working writers a cheap detector for these. It says to watch words ending in *-ion* and *-ment*, because they "tend to make sentences longer and more complicated".

Take that for exactly what it is. It's a place to look, not a verdict. *Government*, *equipment*, *condition* and *region* all end that way and none of them is a buried verb. And plenty of nominalisations end some other way: *failure*, *analysis*, *growth*, *belief*, *refusal*. The ending finds you candidates faster than reading can, which is why it's worth knowing. Then you check each one by asking whether there's a verb hiding in it and a person who should be doing it.

:::callout The quick sweep
Search a document for "tion" and for "ment", and read each hit. Most will be innocent. The ones that aren't will usually be sitting next to a limp verb, because a buried action leaves the verb slot with nothing to do, and something has to fill it. Watch for *was made*, *was undertaken*, *took place*, *occurred*, *was carried out*, *there was*, and plain *is*. Those verbs are the fingerprints.
:::

## The pair that is not about the passive

Slow down for this one, because it corrects something almost everybody has been taught.

In 1918, William Strunk wrote in [*The Elements of Style*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134.txt) that "a common fault is to use as the subject of a passive construction a noun which expresses the entire action, leaving to the verb no function beyond that of completing the sentence." His example pair:

> A survey of this region was made in 1900.
>
> This region was surveyed in 1900.

He prefers the second. Now look carefully at both.

:::predict What is the difference between those two sentences? Be precise before you open this.
**Both of them are passive.** "Was made" is passive; "was surveyed" is passive. Neither names who did the surveying.

So whatever Strunk was objecting to, it was not the passive voice, even though this passage sits inside the rule headed "Use the active voice". What changed between the two versions is that *survey* stopped being a noun and became the verb. The action moved into the verb slot, and the limp *was made* disappeared because it was only ever there to hold the sentence together.

His other pair makes the same point: "Mobilization of the army was rapidly effected" against "The army was rapidly mobilized". Both passive. One has its action in a verb.
:::

That's a useful thing to carry, and lesson 8 comes back to it properly. A great deal of advice that arrives labelled "avoid the passive" is really about buried actions, and the two get confused because they often show up together. They're separate problems with separate fixes, and if you only know the passive rule you'll fix the wrong one.

Be fair to the other side while you're here. The same GOV.UK page this lesson took its detector from does tell writers to "use the active voice", and it gives a reason: the active "is more direct and puts the focus on the user". That's a defensible position and it isn't what this section is arguing against. The claim here is narrower, that the *particular* fault Strunk named is about buried actions rather than about the passive, and that treating the two as one thing makes you fix the wrong one. Whether "avoid the passive" survives contact with the evidence as a general rule is lesson 8's question.

## The harder example: when the fix forces a decision

Reports about things that went wrong are full of sentences like this.

> Following identification of the discrepancy, escalation was delayed pending clarification of the reporting requirements.

Three nominalisations, no character anywhere. Diagnose it and every row of the table comes out "somebody unnamed".

Try to rewrite it and you hit a wall at once, because you can't write "X delayed escalating" without knowing who X is. The sentence isn't merely badly arranged. **It's arranged so that the question doesn't come up.**

Sometimes that's carelessness and sometimes it isn't. A sentence with no character can't be checked against anyone, and the version that names one can. Compare:

> The duty manager knew about the discrepancy on Tuesday and did not escalate it until Friday, because the reporting rules were unclear.

Same events. Now there's a person, two dates, and a reason. Every one of those is checkable, and one of them may be wrong, which is exactly what the original protected.

:::checkpoint You rewrite a sentence to name who acted, and you're asked to put it back the way it was. What should you conclude, and what should you do?
Conclude that the vagueness was probably doing work. Not necessarily dishonest work. Sometimes an organisation genuinely hasn't established who acted, and naming a person prematurely would be worse than saying nothing. Sometimes a document is deliberately written so that no individual is exposed, and that's a decision above your pay grade rather than a writing error.

What to do is ask which it is, in those words. "Do we not know who, or do we know and not want to say?" The first has an honest fix, which is to write that it hasn't been established. The second isn't a writing problem and you should stop treating it as one.

What you shouldn't do is quietly restore the original and file it. You've found the one sentence in the document that somebody cares about.
:::

## When to leave it alone

Undoing every nominalisation is a worse rule than the one it replaces, and it's the commonest overcorrection once this idea lands.

**When the abstraction is genuinely the subject.** "Inflation fell to three percent" is a sentence about inflation. Rewriting it as "prices rose more slowly" changes what the sentence is about. If your topic really is the decision, the failure or the reduction, then the noun belongs in the subject slot, and lesson 5 will show you the deeper reason why.

**When the noun is a fixed term.** *Planning permission*, *risk assessment*, *performance review* are names of things. Unpacking them into verbs produces a sentence that's technically clearer and unrecognisable to the people who have to act on it.

**When it links two sentences.** If you've just written about the committee deciding, then opening the next sentence with "That decision..." puts old information first, which is doing a job. Lesson 5 is entirely about this, and it's the commonest reason a nominalisation earns its place.

**When naming the character would be false precision.** If it genuinely isn't known who assessed the risks, "somebody assessed the risks" invents a certainty the writer doesn't have. Write that it isn't recorded.

## What people get wrong

**"Nominalisations are bad."** They're a normal and necessary part of English. The problem isn't the noun; it's a sentence whose action has left the verb, leaving *was made* or *took place* behind. Diagnose before you judge.

**"This is a rule about words ending in -ion."** That's a detector with a good hit rate and plenty of false positives, both ways. The test is whether there's an action buried and a character displaced.

**"Passive voice is the real problem here."** Strunk's own pair disproves it, since both his sentences are passive and he preferred one of them. Buried actions and the passive are different faults that travel together.

**"Rewriting is the first step."** Rewriting is the second step. Naming the character and the action in ordinary words is the first, and skipping it is why people's rewrites are inconsistent.

**"Being concrete means using shorter words."** Concreteness is about whether a reader can picture who is doing what. "Utilise" for "use" is a vocabulary problem and a small one. "Determination was undertaken" is a structural problem and a large one, and every word in it is short.

## Practice

:::exercise Diagnose ten, rewrite five, then find three of your own
Allow 50 minutes for this. Do the diagnosis in writing: the temptation is to fix these in your head, and if you do, you won't be able to repeat it on your own writing next week.

1. Make two columns, character and action, and diagnose all ten of these. Ordinary words only.

   (a) There was considerable disagreement among the trustees regarding the proposal.

   (b) Notification of the change was not received by residents until March.

   (c) An investigation into the cause of the outage is currently ongoing.

   (d) The provision of support to new members is the responsibility of the secretary.

   (e) Improvement in the results was observed after the intervention.

   (f) Inflation reached its highest level since 1982.

   (g) A decision on the appeal will be made in due course.

   (h) Cancellation of the service occurred due to a shortage of drivers.

   (i) The risk assessment was completed prior to commencement of the works.

   (j) Our recommendation is that consideration be given to a review of the policy.

2. Two of the ten should be left mostly as they are, and one of those two is only half a case. Find both, say which half, and write one line each saying why.

3. Rewrite five of the remaining eight, character in the subject, action in the verb. For any where the character isn't stated, write what you'd need to find out before you could rewrite it honestly.

4. Now take the document you've been carrying since lesson 1. Search it for "tion", for "ment", and for the limp verbs listed in the callout above. Find three sentences with a buried action. Diagnose each in writing, then rewrite it.

5. Read your three rewrites next to the originals, and check one thing. Did you change what the sentence claims? If a rewrite says something the original didn't, either the original was hiding something, which is worth noting, or you've gone too far.
:::

:::checkpoint Which two did you mark to leave, and did you spot the one that is only half a case?
The two to leave are (f) and (i).

**(f) Inflation reached its highest level since 1982.** The abstraction is the subject of the sentence and the verb already carries an action. Rewriting it into "prices rose faster than at any time since 1982" isn't wrong, but it changes the topic from inflation to prices, and if the document is about inflation that's a loss.

**(i) The risk assessment was completed prior to commencement of the works.** This is the half case, and it's meant to be. *Risk assessment* is a fixed term and should stay. *Commencement* isn't, and "before the works started" is plainly better. If you marked the whole sentence for rewriting, or left the whole thing alone, reread it and find the seam.

If you picked (g) as one to leave, that's a defensible reading, since "a decision will be made" is a fixed formula. But notice it's the sentence in the list that most conspicuously refuses to say who decides, which is the harder example again.
:::

You now have the move that does most of the work at the sentence level. The next lesson is its companion and takes about half the time. Once you've got the character into the subject slot, the reader wants the verb almost immediately, and a great many otherwise well-built sentences fail on that one point.
