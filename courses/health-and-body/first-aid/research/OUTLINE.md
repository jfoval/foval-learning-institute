# Outline: First Aid and CPR

*Stage 2, 2026-09-24. Built from `research/SOURCES.md` (the synthesis and Parts A to D), after
`npm run taught`. Sequenced for how the ideas build and for lives saved. **A sensitive domain
(medicine, standards 3.4) and a safety-critical course**: every lesson gets the five-reviewer Stage
4 and the neutrality audit, and every number is fact-checked against the guideline text, not
against SOURCES.*

## How many lessons, and why

**Sixteen.** SOURCES suggested fifteen and said twelve was possible only by merging lessons that
would each run well past an hour. Fifteen was one too few: its first lesson carried the bystander
literature, scene safety, the call, the primary survey, consent, the law in three jurisdictions and
the course's scope, which is two sittings. So the first minutes are split in two, **why people
don't act (and why the reasons don't hold)** and **danger, the call and recognising cardiac
arrest**, and recognition moves into the call lesson, where the 2025 guidelines now put it: ERC and
RCUK tell you to call for any unresponsive person and check breathing while the call connects (Part
A 2.2), so recognition and the call are one act. Compressions then get a lesson of their own.

1. **You are the first responder.** Why people freeze and what the evidence says; the fears that
   stop people (being sued, doing harm, touching a stranger) against the numbers; consent; the law
   in the US and UK, not legal advice; what a certificate is for; how this course labels its
   instructions.
2. **Danger, the call, and recognising cardiac arrest.** Scene safety; the responsiveness check;
   the call (numbers, location, speakerphone, the call handler as coach); call-first against
   recognise-then-call; gasping, the panting athlete and the brief seizure; no pulse check; when in
   doubt, start; the minimum if you read nothing else.
3. **Pushing on the chest.** Every compression number and the reason for it; hand position;
   surface and the bed; pregnancy; swapping and stopping; what CPR does to ribs and what it does
   to someone not in arrest.
4. **Breaths, and what CPR can and cannot do.** 30:2 and how to give a breath; the compression-only
   trials and what "weak, very low certainty" means; survival figures, the television myth, and who
   gets bystander CPR.
5. **The defibrillator.** What an AED treats and what it will not; the steps; pads, bras and
   modesty; minutes to first shock; where AEDs are, registries and volunteer apps; AED law.
6. **Children, babies and drowning.** The US/UK difference in sequence, ratio and infant
   technique; paediatric pads; why breaths matter more here; drowning as a hypoxic arrest.
7. **Breathing but not awake.** The recovery position and who it is not for; opioid overdose and
   naloxone; fainting and counter-pressure.
8. **Choking.** Mild and severe; 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts; infants, children, late
   pregnancy, large bodies, wheelchairs, self-rescue; when they go unresponsive; suction devices.
9. **Severe bleeding and shock.** Direct pressure; tourniquets and their history; packing and
   haemostatic dressings; embedded objects; public-access kits; shock and the leg-raise
   disagreement.
10. **Burns, cuts and small injuries.** Cooling (20 minutes in the UK, 5 to 20 in the US); covering;
    when to go to hospital; chemical and electrical burns; cuts and grazes; nosebleeds; eye
    injuries; knocked-out teeth; amputated parts.
11. **Bones, joints, head and spine.** Fractures as found; sprains and the RICE to PEACE and LOVE
    argument; head injury red flags; spinal injury without collars.
12. **Heart attack and stroke.** Recognition including the presentations people dismiss; call
    first; aspirin (the dose table, the grade, the stroke exception); FAST and BE-FAST.
13. **Anaphylaxis and asthma.** Adrenaline first, two devices, the second dose, lie down; device
    differences and nasal adrenaline; the asthma puff schedules and the person's own plan.
14. **Seizures, low blood sugar and poisoning.** Protect, time, side; when to call; 15 to 20 g of
    sugar and nothing by mouth if not fully awake; poison lines, no vomiting, carbon monoxide.
15. **Heat, cold, bites and stings.** Heat exhaustion against heatstroke and cold-water immersion;
    hypothermia and frostbite; stings and snakebite.
16. **After the emergency, and staying ready.** The rescuer's aftermath; someone in a mental health
    crisis; how skills fade and how to keep them; classes and certificates; how the guidelines are
    made and changed. The project.

**Why this order.** The field's own long courses open with the bystander and the law (Red Cross
*Responding to Emergencies*, Part D 1.1) and HSE's syllabus opens with the first aider's role and
the scene (Part D 1.3), so lessons 1 and 2 do. Then the order is by lives saved and by what builds
on what: cardiac arrest (2 to 6) before anything else, because it is where a bystander changes the
outcome most (Hasselqvist-Ax 2015, adjusted OR 2.15) and because recognition, compressions and the
recovery position are reused in almost every later lesson. **Most arrests happen at home** (73.4%
US, 70 to 80% UK, Part D 7), so the worked examples in lessons 2 to 7 are split between a family
member at home and a stranger in public, and the home case comes first. The HSE annual refresher,
the field's statement of what matters most (assessment, unconscious casualty, CPR, bleeding,
shock, Part D 1.3), is complete by lesson 9. Injuries (10, 11) come before medical emergencies
(12 to 15) because shock (9) and the unresponsive casualty (7) are prerequisites for both, and
the medical lessons are grouped by what the bystander does rather than by organ system.

**Why not fewer.** Children and drowning cannot fold into the adult lessons without doubling each
with a second country's sequence (Part A 2.4, open concern 2). Breaths cannot fold into
compressions, because lesson 4 is where the course teaches how to read a guideline's grade, on the
one question (breaths or not) where the grade is the whole story. **Why not more.** Wound care
beyond first response, dental and eye injuries, and bites are short sections because the sources
are short and the bystander's job is short. Environmental emergencies share a lesson: the
bystander's actions for heat and cold are few and the wilderness versions belong elsewhere.

**Lesson length.** Most land between 65 and 85 measured minutes, remembering measured minutes
include exercises and the Sources list. **Three are planned at 85**: 6 (two countries' sequences
for two age groups, plus drowning), 9 (the tourniquet case and its history, plus shock) and 14
(three conditions). Each earns it by carrying safety-critical steps that a reader arriving from a
search engine needs in one place. **Lesson 10 has a named seam**: if its draft passes 95 minutes,
split it into "Burns" and "Cuts and small injuries", which renumbers the course to seventeen; the
outline's cross-references use lesson titles as well as numbers so a split is a find-and-replace.

## What is deliberately left out, and said so in lesson 1

- **Certification.** The course is not a certificate and cannot be one (Part D 5; HSE L74 para 62,
  AHA Heartsaver's hands-on requirement). Said in lesson 1 and in every lesson's callout
  (decision 4).
- **Anything a professional does.** Pulse checks, advanced airways, oxygen, pulse oximetry,
  rescue medicines such as buccal midazolam, glucagon by anyone not trained on it, cervical
  collars, splinting beyond improvised support. Oxygen appears once, in lesson 12, only so a reader
  knows why guidance differs (Part C 14), and glucagon once, in lesson 14, as "if prescribed and
  you have been shown how".
- **Wilderness and disaster medicine.** Emergency Preparedness and Wilderness Survival (both much
  later on the Core) own remote evacuation, grid-down care, disaster kits and wilderness wound
  management. Lesson 15 teaches heat, cold and bites at the level of a bystander who can call an
  ambulance.
- **Childbirth.** In the Red Cross long course (Part D 1.1); not researched. Named and left out.
- **Chest injuries and the open chest wound.** HSE FAW content, and a topic in ERC 2025 First Aid,
  but not read in detail (the ERC chapter's abstract only lists it, Part B 0). Named and left out.
- **Mental health beyond first response.** Mental Fitness (Term 2) owns anxiety, low mood and
  treatment. Lesson 16 gives ERC and RCUK 2025's first aid response to someone with suicidal
  thoughts and points back to Mental Fitness's crisis callout.
- **Addiction.** Habits and Addiction (later) owns it. Lesson 7 teaches recognising an opioid
  overdose and giving naloxone, and nothing about drug use itself.
- **Exercise screening.** Strength and Fitness owns when to stop training. Lessons 2 and 12 pick up
  from its callout (chest pain, fainting, collapse during exercise) and treat the person.
- **Kitchen fire.** Cooking Fundamentals lesson 2 owns fire prevention and the pan fire. This course
  owns burn first aid and picks up where that lesson stops (see "Boundaries").
- **Legal advice.** Lessons 1 and 5 carry the law as general information, labelled every time
  (decision 9).
- **Standards 2.5's note**, "education, not personalised advice", is carried by the callout on
  every lesson, which is stronger than once per course and is the point of decision 4.

## Twelve decisions, settled here so no lesson re-opens them

**1. Honest objectives: nothing learned from a page proves a hands-on skill.** Every objective
uses a verb a reader can be tested on from a page: **state, describe, decide, recognise, sequence,
explain, compare, identify**. No objective says "perform", "give CPR", "clear choking" or "apply a
tourniquet", because a reader cannot be shown to meet a depth, a force or a tightness without a
manikin and an instructor (SOURCES open concern 6; HSE L74 para 62 "should be assessed by direct
observation"; ILCOR EIT 2025: training effects "may not translate to real-life situations", Part D
4). What the course *can* build is the knowledge that makes a class faster and an emergency less
frightening, and the willingness to try: very short videos raised attempted CPR from 76.5% to over
99% in Bobrow 2011 (Part D 4), which is the finding that justifies a reading course existing at
all. `course.yaml` outcomes are reworded to match (done at this stage).

**Every lesson that teaches a physical skill (3 to 11, 13) ends by pointing at a hands-on class
and at a free practice tool**: BHF RevivR and RCUK Lifesaver for CPR and the AED (Part D 6, R6 and
R7), the ACS Stop the Bleed online course and its in-person skills session for bleeding (R14). The
where-to-train list is taught once, in lesson 16, and pointed to from the others.

**Exercises never ask a reader to do a physical technique on a living person.** Compressions go
into a cushion or a firm pillow (RevivR's own method, Part D 6 R6); back blows, thrusts, the
recovery roll and a tourniquet are rehearsed as a spoken or written sequence, or on a willing
adult only for positioning with no force (the recovery position's arm and knee placement, never a
roll onto a hard floor, never a thrust, never a tightened band). **No exercise ever asks a reader
to dial an emergency number to practise.** Every rehearsal of the call is spoken aloud to yourself
or a friend. The drafter writes these limits into each exercise, not into a footnote.

**2. Two countries' guidance, side by side, neither called wrong.** "US" means AHA 2025 (CPR,
choking) or AHA/American Red Cross 2024 (first aid) unless stated; "UK/Europe" means RCUK 2025,
the UK adaptation of ERC 2025 and the citable European source while the ERC chapters are
unverified (gate G1). Where they differ, the lesson gives **a short two-column table or an "In the
US / In the UK and Europe" pair**, never interleaved through the prose (SOURCES open concern 2), the
same convention as Cooking Fundamentals decision 1. Where they agree, one sentence says so and
names both, because agreement is information too (the adult numbers, lesson 3).

- **Neither side is wrong.** Most differences are weak recommendations on very low certainty
  evidence, resolved differently for teachability (SOURCES finding 3). Where a body gives its
  reason (the AHA's "consistency" for back blows first, Part B 2.2; ERC's hypoxic argument for five
  breaths first, Part A 5C), the lesson gives it in the body's words. Where no reason is given, the
  lesson says so rather than inventing one.
- **Which to follow.** Lesson 1 tells the reader: follow the guidance of the country you are in,
  which is the one your call handler and your class will use; readers elsewhere follow their
  national resuscitation council. The course shows these two because both were read.
- **Where a public page lags its own country's guideline**, the lesson teaches the guideline and
  names the page, dated: BHF's "hands-only ... since the Covid-19 pandemic" (lesson 4), St John's
  bed-to-floor (lesson 3), the NHS child choking page's "any order" and two fingers (lesson 8), the
  American Red Cross infant page's two fingers (lesson 8), the NHS cuts page's elevation (lessons 9
  and 10), the NHS and St John heatstroke wet sheet (lesson 15), the ERC lay book's 10% a minute (lesson 5). **Each is said once, plainly, without
  mockery**: pages lag, (St John's legs-up for bleeding was listed here and is
  not a lag: RCUK 2025 has no shock-position section for it to lag, lesson 9's Stage 4, 2026-09-25), and the October 2025 guidelines came out eleven months before this course.

**3. Versioning: every guideline-based instruction carries its guideline and year in the text.**
The convention is a parenthesis after the instruction: **"(RCUK 2025)"**, **"(AHA 2025, COR 1)"**,
**"(AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 2b)"**, **"(ILCOR 2025, weak recommendation, very low certainty)"**. A
later update is then a search for the body and year, not a re-reading of the teaching. Rules:

- **One instruction, one attribution.** No paragraph mixes two guidelines' numbers in one
  sentence without naming each.
- **Dated figures are quarantined**, one sentence each with its date or data year: CARES 2025
  (10.5%), England 30-day survival (9.5%, RCUK 2025 epidemiology), The Circuit's AED count, the 84%
  of US states requiring school CPR (end of 2024), the GAO overdose-law count (gate G10), naloxone's
  legal status (US OTC since March 2023; UK supply regulations 2015, 2019, 2024), nasal adrenaline
  approvals (FDA August 2024; MHRA 2025 and the children's dose by June 2026), the ASA's switch to
  BE-FAST (between January 2025 and 2026), UK AIR/MART asthma advice (2024 onward), the American
  Red Cross burns page's change (during 2026), the RCUK suction-device statement (July 2026).
- **"Current" is always dated.** Never "the current guidance is"; always "RCUK's 2025 guidelines
  say".
- **Re-check trigger.** Guidance moves between full cycles: ILCOR published CoSTR updates in 2022,
  2024 and 2025 among the documents read, ERC issued errata on 21 September 2026, and three public
  bodies changed their advice during 2025 and 2026 (SOURCES open concern 3). The course is
  re-checked **when ILCOR publishes its next CoSTR summary**, and whenever the AHA, AHA/Red Cross,
  ERC or RCUK publishes a new guideline, focused update or erratum touching a topic taught here.

**Proposed standing item for `docs/QUEUE.md`** (for the orchestrator to add; this session touches
only the course folder):

> **First Aid and CPR: re-check on new guidance (standing).** When ILCOR publishes its next CoSTR
> summary, or the AHA, AHA/Red Cross, ERC or RCUK publishes a guideline, focused update or erratum,
> search the course's lessons for that body's name and year, compare each attributed instruction
> with the new text, and update or re-date it. Also once a year: re-read the public pages each
> lesson names as lagging (OUTLINE decision 2) and drop the note where the page has caught up.
> Unread ERC errata 111314 and 111316 (21 September 2026) are the first instance.

**4. A safety callout on every lesson, word for word, directly after the opening paragraph, and a
check enforces it.** The pattern is Nutrition's and Strength and Fitness's (`NUTRITION_SAFETY` and
`TRAINING_SAFETY` in `scripts/build.mjs`). The text:

> :::callout Before you need this
> This course is education, not a certificate, and not medical advice about anyone's own situation. In an emergency, call your local emergency number first (911 in the US, 999 or 112 in the UK, 112 across the EU), put the phone on speaker, and do what the call handler tells you. Reading can teach you what to do and in what order. It can't teach your hands how hard to push or how a back blow should feel, so take a hands-on class with a manikin and an instructor, and practise between classes. Each instruction here names the guideline and year it comes from, and guidance changes.
> :::

Sources for each clause: the numbers (911.gov, NHS "When to call 999", the European Commission's
112 page, St John DR ABC "999 or 112", Part D 1.5); speakerphone (AHA 2025 Part 7, ERC via RCUK
2025, Part A 2.2, Part D 1.5); follow the call handler (911.gov "Do not hang up until the
call-taker instructs you to do so", the British Red Cross on following the 999 operator's
instructions, Part D 1.5 and Part B 3.7); not a certificate and hands-on assessment (HSE L74 para
62, AHA Heartsaver's hands-on skills session, Part D 1.2, 1.3, 5); practise between classes (skills
decay within 3 months, Riggs 2019; ILCOR 2025 strongly recommends feedback devices, Part D 4);
"not medical advice" (standards 2.5). **For the orchestrator:** the check goes beside
`TRAINING_SAFETY` as `FIRST_AID_SAFETY`, keyed on `cdir.name === "first-aid"`, with the callout's
sentences added to `REPETITION_EXEMPT` as Strength's were. It uses "the call handler" for both
countries' terms (UK "call handler", US "call-taker" or "dispatcher"); lessons use whichever term
the source uses and say once, in lesson 2, that they are the same job.

**5. The course's question: which guideline, which year, and how strongly?** Memory added the
sample, Mental Fitness what the comparison group got, Nutrition "instead of what?", Strength and
Fitness "trained whom, for how long, measured on what?", Cooking "what is this rule protecting?".
**A first aid instruction is a recommendation by a named body, in a given year, at a stated
strength**, and most are weak on very low certainty evidence (SOURCES finding 4). That is not a
reason to ignore them: it is the reason two countries can differ without either being wrong, the
reason a page can change in a year, and the reason "doing something beats doing nothing" is the
one finding stated as established. Introduced in lesson 1 (how to read the parenthesis), taught
properly in lesson 4 with Sleep's grades as the spaced-review hook (Sleep taught comparing
recommendation strengths across three guidelines), used in every lesson after, and joined to the
institute's claim sort in lesson 16.

**The grading key, taught in lesson 4 and not before in any detail:** AHA class of recommendation
(COR 1 "should", 2a "is reasonable", 2b "may be considered", 3: No Benefit, 3: Harm) and level of
evidence (A, B-R, B-NR, C-LD, C-EO); ILCOR's strong or weak recommendation and high to very low
certainty, plus the good practice statement (expert consensus with no systematic review). **The one
point that must land:** "weak, very low certainty" describes the evidence behind a choice between
options, not whether to act, and the finding underneath almost every such recommendation is that
acting beats not acting (Part A 3). Lessons 2 and 3 print the labels in the parenthesis with a
one-line forward pointer ("lesson 4 explains these labels"); lesson 1 says only that each
instruction names who says it, when, and how strongly.

**6. Read-level discipline and the unread line.** A lesson may not cite, name or quote anything on
the `<!-- unread: -->` line, and the build fails on the words. Concretely:

- **No lesson names the 1968 bystander experiments or their authors** (Darley, Latané) or gives the
  85% and 31% figures. The bystander effect is taught from Fischer 2011 (abstract), Philpot 2020
  (full) and the Genovese correction (Manning 2007, New York Times 2016). Gate G8.
- **ERC chapters by article number** (110771, 110753, 110752) and ERC 2021 First Aid (Zideman) are
  cited only after gate G1. Until then, **RCUK 2025 and the ERC lay book** are the European
  sources.
- **"Time is brain"**: the ASA's "nearly 2 million" or 1.9 million brain cells a minute is given
  attributed to the ASA as its figure, never to Saver.
- **Anaphylaxis posture** is cited to RCUK 2021, MHRA 2023 and the NHS; Pumphrey 2000 (abstract)
  for the fatal-reaction timings; the 2003 letter and Mullins are not named.
- **The First Aid Manual** is not cited for content (catalogue record only). HSE L74's statement
  that UK training follows it is citable as HSE's statement.
- **The Red Cross Scientific Advisory Council** note on ABC and CAB is not cited; lessons do not
  claim the Red Cross and the AHA agree on sequence beyond what their read pages say (gate G15).
- **[V-img] quotations** from the AHA/Red Cross 2024 tables are re-read against the image before
  they reach a lesson (gate G3). **[T] quotations** (the GAO report, Part D 3.1) are re-checked
  (gate G10). **ILCOR 2025 quotations** carry the accepted-manuscript caveat or are re-read against
  the published text (gate G5).
- **Abstract-level studies** (Hasselqvist-Ax, Blewer, Garcia, Kragh, Teixeira, Riggs and the rest)
  may be cited for what the abstract says and nothing more.
- **Snippet-only items are never taught as findings**: the what3words "85%", the NHS Act FAST
  relaunch details, the MHRA EURneffy approval day, the AHA Part 4 alert-app survival summary.

**7. Units and numbers.** Each figure in its source's own unit first with the conversion in
brackets: RCUK "5 to 6 cm (2 to 2.4 in)"; AHA "2 inches (5 cm)"; temperatures in both scales;
blood sugar as "below 70 mg/dL (US) or below 4 mmol/L (UK)"; aspirin in mg as each source gives it.
**Compression rate, depth, ratios and the cooling times are the numbers a fact-checker checks
first**, against the guideline text, in every lesson that states them.

**8. Anatomy is taught where it is needed, because nothing earlier teaches it.** Human Anatomy and
Physiology is much later on the Core, and `npm run taught` shows no earlier course covering the
heart, circulation or airway. Each lesson explains only the mechanism its instructions depend on,
from its sources: why compressions must be deep and must recoil (lesson 3), what ventricular
fibrillation is and why only a shock treats it (lesson 5), why arrests from drowning and overdose
are hypoxic and why that makes breaths matter (lessons 4, 6, 7), why lying flat helps in
anaphylaxis (venous return, RCUK 2021, lesson 13), why a cold burn helps and why ice harms (lesson
10). **No mechanism is added from general knowledge**: where SOURCES gives none, the lesson gives
the instruction and says the source gives no mechanism. No "brain damage begins in four minutes"
or similar popular figures unless a read source prints one.

**9. The law is general information, labelled every time.** Lesson 1 and lesson 5 (AED immunity)
each carry, in the body at the point of use: "This is general information, not legal advice. The
law differs by state and by country." (Part D 3.) The duty-to-rescue question is a value question
(decision in "Neutrality notes"): described, never prescribed.

**10. Images: real, licensed, or none.** Standards 4.5 forbid AI imagery and decorative pictures;
the guidelines' own figures are copyrighted (SOURCES open concern 5). The plan per lesson is below.
Three kinds of media are allowed:

- **Wikimedia Commons photographs and diagrams**, hotlinked from upload.wikimedia.org, each checked
  for its licence and **for its technique against the 2025 guidance before use**. A search of
  Commons on 2026-09-24 found candidates for every physical skill (named per lesson below); none
  has been checked for licence or accuracy. Several may show superseded technique (two-finger
  infant compressions, "look, listen and feel", abdominal thrusts only), and **a picture showing
  superseded technique is used only when the caption says so and the point is the change**
  (lesson 16's history of the guidelines).
- **Official videos**, embedded privacy-enhanced, **only after someone has watched each one against
  2025 guidance** (gate G4). None has been watched.
- **Inline SVG charts from real data and SVG diagrams of a sequence** (the order of actions as a
  flow), in the site's colours, labelled directly. A sequence diagram is structure, which
  standards 4.5 allow; **no drawn illustration of a body, a hand or a technique**, which would be
  generated imagery standing in for a photograph.

**11. Boundaries with other Core courses, settled so no lesson re-opens them.**

- **Cooking Fundamentals** (T3, earlier). Its lesson 2 teaches the pan fire, the oven fire and a
  first response to a burn (20 minutes under cool running water in the UK; the American Red Cross's
  5 to 20 minutes), and says "First Aid and CPR, later this term, takes it up". Its lessons 1 and 12
  say this course treats **cuts** and burns. **This course owns burn first aid and cuts.** Lesson 10
  opens by naming what the reader met in Cooking lesson 2 (the kitchen burn), re-teaches cooling
  properly with the US/UK difference and ILCOR's "no duration can be recommended", and adds what
  Cooking left out (covering, when to go to hospital, chemical and electrical burns, children and
  over-cooling). It does not re-teach fire. **Cuts are a promise**: gate G6 must close, or its
  fallback must be written, and lesson 10 must say plainly what it covers.
- **Strength and Fitness** (T3, earlier). Its callout lists chest pain, pressure in the neck, jaw
  or arms, dizziness, palpitations, fainting and disproportionate breathlessness, and three of its
  lessons say "First Aid and CPR ... starts from the warning signs in the callout". **This course
  honours that in two places**: lesson 2's recognition section uses **the person who collapses
  while exercising** (the panting athlete, the brief seizure-like episode, Part A 2.1) as a worked
  example, and lesson 12 opens from the same symptom list as a heart attack recognition case.
  Fainting while exercising is one of the NHS's call-999 triggers (lesson 7). It does not re-teach
  exercise screening or exertional rhabdomyolysis.
- **Mental Fitness** (T2). Lesson 16 gives the ERC and RCUK 2025 response to someone with suicidal
  thoughts and points to Mental Fitness. **The crisis numbers are Mental Fitness's**, word for word
  where they overlap (988; Samaritans 116 123), so the institute never prints two versions.
- **Personal Safety, Home Repair and Maintenance, Car Basics** (T3, after this). This course
  teaches scene safety for a rescuer (traffic, electricity, water, fire, violence, carbon monoxide)
  and stops there; it does not teach self-defence, wiring or roadside procedure.
- **Habits and Addiction, Medical Literacy, Human Anatomy** (later). See "What downstream courses
  need".
- **Emergency Preparedness, Wilderness Survival** (much later). See "Left out".

**12. Stage 4 and fact-check discipline for this course.** Every lesson: the five-reviewer Stage 4,
the neutrality audit, and a fact-check of every number and every attributed instruction against the
guideline text (not SOURCES). Every lesson's Sources block gives the read level for each item, and
every guideline item names the section read and, for the AHA and the 2024 first aid guideline, the
Wayback snapshot ID from SOURCES. **The quiz items on safety steps follow decision 2**: each names
the country whose rule it asks about, or has one correct answer under both.

## Stage 1 gates

Each gap SOURCES names is either planned around or a gate a drafter must close before the lesson
that needs it, with the fallback to write if it cannot be closed. **A closed gate is recorded in
SOURCES.md with its read level, and any unread entry it clears is removed from the unread line in
the same commit.** Most of these need an ordinary browser and nothing else (SOURCES open concern
4); none needs John.

## Lesson sequence

Every lesson opens with its reason to care, then the callout (decision 4), and ends with
Connections, Go deeper, Sources and the quiz. Predicts and checkpoints: two or three of each per
lesson, at least one of them a decision on a new scenario.

### 1. You are the first responder (~65 min)

- **Objectives:** explain why people hesitate to help in an emergency and what the evidence says
  about bystanders, including the Kitty Genovese correction; state what the evidence shows about
  the risk of being sued and the risk of doing harm, in the US and the UK, as general information
  and not legal advice; decide how to get consent from a responsive person and what applies to an
  unresponsive one; say what this course covers, what a certificate is for, and how to read the
  guideline label beside each instruction.
- **Core ideas:** most cardiac arrests happen at home (73.4% US, AHA CPR Facts and Stats; 70 to
  80% UK, RCUK 2025), so the first rescuer is usually family (Part D 7); the bystander effect as a
  real individual-level effect that weakens in dangerous emergencies (Fischer 2011, g = -0.35) and
  the crowd-level finding that someone intervened in 90.9% of filmed public conflicts (Philpot
  2020, with its caveat that these were conflicts, not collapses) (Part D 2.2); the 38 witnesses
  story as false by the New York Times' own 2016 account (Part D 2.1); why people say they wouldn't
  act (BHF 2025 poll: 51% of people living with a partner cite fear of causing harm; public
  perceptions of the sex gap, Perman 2019; barriers in deprived communities, Uny 2023) with polls
  labelled as indicative (Part D 2.3); the law: US Good Samaritan statutes with California
  §1799.102 as the worked text (good faith, no pay, not grossly negligent), Murphy 2020's 3 suits
  over giving CPR in thirty years (gate G12), the three duty-to-assist states and what satisfies
  them (calling counts) (Part D 3.1); UK: no duty to rescue, a duty of care once you volunteer, no
  reported successful claim (RCUK law booklet, and the RCUK FAQ discrepancy resolved in the
  booklet's favour), SARAH 2015 in England and Wales only, necessity and implied consent (Part D
  3.2); harm to someone given CPR who wasn't in arrest (under 2%, Part A 2.8), previewed and taught
  in lesson 3; consent (Red Cross Check, Call, Care: "obtain consent"; RCUK's implied consent and
  necessity); a certificate is a workplace instrument (OSHA 1910.151 "adequately trained"; HSE
  certificates three years; Red Cross two years) and **nobody needs one to help** (AHA 2025 COR 1:
  all lay rescuers should give compressions; RCUK: untrained AED use is justified) (Part D 5); the
  course's label convention (decisions 3 and 5, the short version); scope and what is left out.
- **Mechanism to explain:** diffusion of responsibility, as Fischer describes it, and why danger and
  clarity reduce it (an obvious emergency is recognised faster); why the legal risk runs the other
  way from the fear (the law protects good-faith helpers, and in cardiac arrest, as RCUK puts it,
  making things worse is "virtually impossible, since without intervention death is inevitable").
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a parent finds their father collapsed in the kitchen: no
  stranger, no consent question, no liability question, and the lesson walks through why none of
  the usual fears applies, ending at "call" (lesson 2). (b) wrinkle: a woman collapses at a bus stop
  with six people around her; each fear in turn (someone else will, I'll be sued, I'll hurt her,
  I'd have to touch her chest), each answered with its number, and the one tactic the evidence
  supports: point at one person and give them a job (Part D myths, "take charge and give people
  jobs").
- **Misconceptions:** "someone else will help"; "38 people watched and did nothing"; "you'll be
  sued"; "you need a certificate to help"; "you might make it worse" (in cardiac arrest); "in the UK
  you must help by law" (no duty; the duty-to-assist examples are three US states).
- **Practice:** write down, for your own country and state, which emergency number you would call,
  whether your jurisdiction has a duty to assist from those named in the lesson, and one sentence
  you would say to get a specific bystander to act; then sort six statements about helping into
  "supported by the evidence here", "not supported" and "a value question".
- **Images:** none needed. One SVG: the US legal counts (3 against 167) as a simple labelled bar,
  only if gate G12 closes or the counts are printed from the abstract (they are: the chart is
  allowed on the abstract's counts).
- **Sources:** Part D 2, 3, 5, 7; Part A 2.8; SOURCES "Boundaries".
- **Connects:** Logic and Argument (a claim and its evidence), Mental Fitness (fear as ordinary,
  not a flaw), Personal Finance (insurance and liability as risk judgement). Leads to lesson 2,
  where the reader acts.
- **Spaced review of:** Sleep's recommendation grades, previewed for decision 5; Memory's sample
  question, on the Philpot caveat (conflicts, not collapses).
- **Neutrality:** the duty-to-rescue debate is a value question, described (see "Neutrality
  notes"); disparities in who receives CPR are reported as data in lesson 4, not here.

### 2. Danger, the call, and recognising cardiac arrest (~80 min)

- **Objectives:** sequence the first minute for a collapsed person under US and UK/European
  guidance, from checking for danger to starting CPR; state what a call handler needs from you
  and how your location reaches them; recognise cardiac arrest, including agonal gasping and the
  brief seizure-like episode, and explain why a lay rescuer doesn't check for a pulse; decide
  whether to call the emergency number or a non-emergency line for a described situation.
- **Core ideas:** danger first (St John DR ABC: "Before approaching the casualty, always make sure
  the area is safe."; traffic, electricity, fire, water, violence, and carbon monoxide as a hidden
  danger, Part C 9) (Part D 1.4, SOURCES safety-critical); response (shake the shoulders, ask "Are
  you OK?"); **call-first against recognise-then-call**, the two-column table: RCUK 2025 "Call 999
  for any unresponsive person ... then assess breathing while waiting for the call to be
  answered", with ERC's reason (dispatchers recognise most arrests), against AHA 2025 (recognise,
  then a lone rescuer with a phone calls and starts CPR at once, COR 1) and the AHA's note that a
  large study found no survival difference between CPR-before-call and call-before-CPR (Part A
  2.2); in practice both say speakerphone and start (Part A 4); the numbers (911; 999 or 112; 112
  across the EU) (Part D 1.5); what the call handler asks (911.gov: location first; NHS: postcode or
  landmarks), AML sending your phone's location automatically in the UK, what3words as a fallback
  the call handler can text you (Part D 1.5); "Do not hang up until the call-taker instructs you";
  the call handler as coach (dispatch CPR: ILCOR strong recommendation, very low certainty; AHA
  2025 T-CPR COR 1, LOE A for compression-only instructions to adults) (Part A 2.2); no phone,
  alone (ERC via RCUK or the lay book, gate G1: leave to call as quickly as possible); send a
  helper for an AED (St John); **recognition**: unresponsive and not breathing normally (AHA 2025
  COR 1, C-LD), agonal gasping as a sign of arrest present in up to 40 to 60% (AHA) and ERC's lay
  words for it (gasping, snoring, gurgling), no pulse check for lay rescuers (AHA 2025 COR 1; lay
  accuracy 47% at 5 s and 73% at 10 s), the panting athlete and the brief seizure-like episode
  (gate G1), "If there is any doubt, assume cardiac arrest and start CPR" (RCUK 2025); **the
  minimum if you read nothing else**: call on speaker, push hard and fast in the centre of the
  chest, don't stop until help takes over; the slogans as the same order in three sets of words
  (Check, Call, Care; DR ABC; Check, Call, CPR) (Part D 1); 999 against NHS 111, and the NHS's
  while-you-wait list (lights on, door open, call again if the person changes) (Part D 1.5).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why gasping happens and why it misleads (the AHA's definition: slow,
  irregular gasps "ineffective for ventilation"); why the 2025 European guidance moved the call
  earlier (the dispatcher recognises the arrest better than the bystander does, so confirming it
  yourself costs time and adds little); why the pulse check was dropped (lay accuracy near a coin
  toss at 5 seconds).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: the father on the kitchen floor from lesson 1, walked through
  second by second in both countries' sequence, with the call transcript (what you say, what you're
  asked, in the order 911.gov and the NHS give). (b) wrinkle: **a man collapses during a five-a-side
  game** (Strength and Fitness's callout, picked up here): he twitches for a few seconds and then
  makes loud, slow gasps, and his friends roll him on his side because "he's breathing". The lesson
  shows why each observation points to arrest, and what the call handler would have said.
- **Misconceptions:** "gasping means they're breathing"; "check for a pulse first"; "wait until
  you're sure"; "a seizure means it isn't cardiac arrest"; "download what3words or they won't find
  you"; "look up the nearest defibrillator on a website" (defibfinder.uk says it is not for
  emergencies); "hang up once you've given the address".
- **Practice:** write your home's call script (address or landmark, what happened, callback
  number) and **say it aloud to yourself or a friend; never dial to practise**; find where AML is
  or isn't relevant to your phone and country from the lesson; then three scenario cards (a person
  asleep on a park bench who won't wake; a person who fainted and is talking; a collapsed person
  making snoring sounds) sequenced step by step under your country's guidance.
- **Images:** an SVG flow of the first minute, two columns (RCUK 2025 and AHA 2025), with each box
  carrying its attribution. Commons candidate for "a defibrillator in a public cabinet with its
  sign" (`AED - Automated external defibrillator in compartment with sign above.jpg`, G16) is held
  for lesson 5, not used here.
- **Sources:** Part A 2.1, 2.2, 4; Part D 1.4, 1.5; Part C 9 (carbon monoxide).
- **Connects:** lesson 1's fears, now answered by the steps. Leads to lesson 3 (what "push" means)
  and lesson 5 (the AED the helper fetches). Forward to lesson 14 for seizures that are not arrest.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's give-a-job tactic, now with the job named ("you, call 999 on
  speaker; you, find a defibrillator").
- **Video:** AHA "Official 2012 Hands-Only CPR Instructional Video" (69 s; two steps unchanged) and
  the RCUK "Become ResusReady" film, both after G4.

### 3. Pushing on the chest (~70 min)

- **Objectives:** state the compression rate, depth, recoil and hand position for an adult that
  the AHA and RCUK 2025 guidelines give, and explain the reason for each number; decide what to do
  when the person is on a bed, is pregnant or is large; describe when to swap and when to stop;
  state what the evidence shows about rib fractures in real CPR and about injury to someone given
  CPR who wasn't in arrest.
- **Core ideas:** the adult numbers, identical everywhere and **Established** (SOURCES finding 2):
  100 to 120 a minute; at least 5 cm (2 in), not more than 6 cm (2.4 in); full recoil; minimal
  pauses; heel of the hand on the lower half of the breastbone, other hand on top, arms straight,
  shoulders over the chest (the Part A 2.3 table, each with its AHA COR/LOE); clothing (thin
  clothing need not come off for compressions, BHF; ERC allows moving it to find the landmark);
  **the bed**: RCUK 2025 do not move a person from a bed to the floor, push deeper; AHA 2025 a
  firm surface if it does not delay CPR, torso at about knee level (COR 1, B-NR, new); St John's
  page still says move to the floor, the table and decision 2's note (Part A 2.3); larger bodies,
  same technique, may need more force (AHA COR 1; ERC 2025); **pregnancy** beyond about 20 weeks:
  manual left uterine displacement while compressing (AHA 2025 COR 1, C-LD; RCUK 2025) (Part A
  2.10); swap about every 2 minutes because depth falls after 90 to 120 seconds (AHA 2025 2a, B-R;
  St John one to two minutes) (Part A 2.6); when to stop (help takes over, signs of life, you're
  exhausted, St John; "Do not interrupt CPR unless ...", RCUK); harms: rib fractures in about 55%
  of people given CPR (Van Wijck 2024), more injury above 6 cm depth (Hellevuo 2013) as the reason
  for the cap, and under 2% injured among people given CPR who weren't in arrest (White 2010,
  Haley 2011, Williamson 2024) (Part A 2.8); the rescuer's risk (infection risk low; PPE reasonable
  if immediately available, AHA 2a, and it may delay CPR).
- **Mechanism to explain:** each number's reason as the sources give it: depth moves enough blood,
  the 6 cm cap limits injury (Hellevuo), recoil lets the chest refill before the next push, pauses
  cost what the compressions built, fatigue shows up as shallow compressions before the rescuer
  feels it (AHA's 90 to 120 seconds). The drafter uses only mechanisms SOURCES or the guideline
  text supply (decision 8).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: an adult on the floor, set up and compressed for two minutes,
  every choice with its number and its reason, then a swap with a second rescuer. (b) wrinkle: an
  older woman collapses in bed at night; her husband has a UK first aid certificate from 2019 and
  remembers "move them to the floor". The lesson gives RCUK 2025's answer, the AHA's, and St John's
  page, and asks what a lone, elderly rescuer should do (answer: start on the bed, push deeper,
  under both RCUK and the AHA's "does not delay" condition).
- **Misconceptions:** "breaking ribs means you did it wrong"; "you'll hurt someone who's only
  fainted"; "you need to take all their clothes off"; "always move them to the floor"; "you can
  compress in the recovery position" (AHA: effective CPR cannot be performed in that position).
- **Practice:** RevivR (free, about 15 minutes, uses a cushion and the phone camera for feedback) or
  **two minutes of compressions into a firm cushion to a metronome app set at 110**, counting aloud
  and noting when your arms tire; never on a person (decision 1). Then two scenario decisions (a
  pregnant woman at about seven months; a man on a waterbed).
- **Images:** Commons candidates (G16): `Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation Adult.jpg`, `Chest
  compressions.gif`, `NASA astronaut Jessica Meir practices cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR)
  (jsc2025e070047).jpg` (a 2025 NASA photograph, likely public domain, of hand position on a
  manikin). Choose one, checked for hand position.
- **Sources:** Part A 2.3, 2.6, 2.8, 2.10.
- **Connects:** lesson 2's "push hard and fast", now exact. Leads to lesson 4 (what to do in the
  pauses) and lesson 5 (when the AED says stop). Forward to lesson 6 (children's depth as a
  fraction of the chest).
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's fear of harm, now with the three studies behind "under 2%".
- **Video:** American Red Cross "How to Perform Hands-Only CPR" and AHA "How to save a life:
  Hands-Only CPR", after G4.

### 4. Breaths, and what CPR can and cannot do (~75 min)

- **Objectives:** describe how to give a rescue breath and state the adult ratio, and decide when a
  lay rescuer should give breaths and when compressions alone; explain what a guideline's class of
  recommendation and level of evidence, and ILCOR's strength and certainty, do and do not tell you;
  compare what the compression-only trials found with the argument for breaths, in each side's
  terms; state real survival figures from cardiac arrest, compare them with television, and state
  what the data show about who receives bystander CPR.
- **Core ideas:** 30:2 for the trained, able and willing (ILCOR 2025 weak, very low certainty; AHA
  2025 2a, B-R; RCUK 2025) and continuous compressions otherwise (ILCOR good practice statement;
  AHA COR 1); how to give a breath (head tilt, chin lift, about 1 second, enough to make the chest
  rise, "a regular rather than a deep breath"; if the chest doesn't rise after 2 attempts, back to
  compressions) (Part A 2.4); **the grading key** (decision 5); the compression-only evidence:
  SOS-KANTO, Svensson 2010, Rea 2010 (dispatcher-instruction RCTs), Hüpfl 2010's meta-analysis
  (14% against 12%, RR 1.22, NNT 41) and Iwami 2015's rise in bystander CPR from 34.6% to 47.3% as
  compression-only spread in Japan, against the case for breaths (oxygen runs out; overdose and
  asphyxial arrests, 1 in 6 to 1 in 3 US arrests per the AHA; the RCTs predate the overdose era;
  observational data favour breaths) (Part A 3, 5A); where it stands: compressions for everyone,
  breaths for the trained and willing, both far better than nothing (Established); the COVID
  modifications withdrawn by ERC and RCUK 2025, and BHF's page still saying hands-only is "the
  recommended way ... since the Covid-19 pandemic" (decision 2) (Part A 2.12); the British Red
  Cross teaching the public hands-only while St John and RCUK teach 30:2 to the trained (a UK
  internal difference, Part D 1.4); face shields; survival: CARES 2025 10.5% to discharge (US),
  England 9.5% at 30 days, **definitions differ** (Part A 3); the CARES breakdown (public 20.9%,
  home 8.9%; with bystander CPR 12.9% against 7.7%; bystander AED 32.9%); bystander CPR associated
  with about twice the survival (Hasselqvist-Ax 2015, adjusted OR 2.15; RCUK "two to four-fold");
  Denmark's rise (Wissenberg 2013, 21.1% to 44.9% bystander CPR, authors say causality uncertain);
  television (Diem 1996: 75% immediate, 67% apparent discharge; Portanova 2015: 69.6%) (Part A 6);
  who receives CPR: in public, women less often than men (39% against 45%, Blewer 2018), no gap at
  home; Black and Hispanic people less often at home and in public in every neighbourhood type
  (Garcia 2022) (Part A 3).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why breaths matter more in some arrests (a sudden cardiac arrest starts
  with oxygenated blood; a drowning or overdose arrest starts without it, AHA's own sentence in
  Part A 2.9); why an association study cannot settle what a trial could, and why the trials that
  exist were about dispatcher instructions, not trained rescuers; why an odds ratio of 2.15 is not
  "twice the survival" exactly, taught plainly because Statistics for Citizens comes later.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: reading one recommendation line, "We suggest that bystanders who
  are trained, able, and willing, give chest compressions with rescue breaths ... (weak
  recommendation, very low–certainty evidence)", word by word: who, what, how strong, how sure, and
  what it doesn't say. (b) wrinkle: two readers disagree: one says hands-only is best because the
  RCTs favour it; the other says breaths are best because of overdose. The lesson shows what each
  trial population was (dispatcher-guided, untrained callers, adult arrests mostly of cardiac
  cause), why both readers are partly right, and why the guidelines split the answer by who the
  rescuer is.
- **Misconceptions:** "CPR usually works, like on TV"; "blow hard" (BHF "blow hard for a second",
  flagged as imprecise); "it isn't CPR without mouth-to-mouth"; "hands-only is the recommended way
  since COVID"; "a weak recommendation means it doesn't matter"; "always tilt the head and blow
  first" (adults: compressions first everywhere).
- **Practice:** grade four real recommendation lines from the course's sources (the student
  translates each into a plain sentence and says what it does not claim); then decide, for three
  rescuers (an untrained teenager, a nurse off duty, a parent who did a class five years ago), what
  each should do for an adult, with the guideline and year.
- **Images:** SVG bar chart: survival on television (Diem 1996, Portanova 2015) against CARES 2025
  and England 30-day, with the definitions footnoted; SVG bar chart of the CARES 2025 breakdown by
  location and bystander action. Both from real data, both labelled with source and year.
- **Sources:** Part A 2.4, 2.12, 3, 5, 6; Part D 1.4.
- **Connects:** Sleep (comparing recommendation strengths), Mental Fitness (what the comparison
  group got, applied to the compression-only trials), Nutrition (observational against
  experimental). Leads to lesson 5 (the AED) and lesson 6 (children, where breaths matter most).
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's home-versus-public point, now with Blewer's numbers; lesson 3's
  harm figures as an example of very low certainty that is still enough to act on.
- **Neutrality:** disparities are reported with the studies' own framing (association, not
  measured cause); Perman 2019's themes are public perceptions, labelled so.

### 5. The defibrillator (~65 min)

- **Objectives:** explain what an AED treats and why it will not shock a heart that doesn't need
  it; sequence using an AED during CPR, including pad placement and what to do about a bra;
  explain why every minute to the first shock matters, with the figure the AHA gives; state how to
  find an AED where you live and what the law says about using one, as general information.
- **Core ideas:** anyone can use one (RCUK 2025); the steps: switch on, follow the prompts, pads on
  bare skin, keep compressing while a second person places the pads, nobody touches during
  analysis and shock, restart compressions at once (ERC via RCUK or the lay book, gate G1) (Part A
  2.5); semi-automatic and fully automatic devices; "Defibrillation should not be delayed to
  provide additional CPR" (gate G1; fallback AHA 2025 §9.2 COR 1); pad positions (front-side
  standard, front-back alternative, follow the manufacturer's diagram: ILCOR 2025 good practice
  statement); **bras and modesty**: ILCOR 2025 "it may not always be necessary to remove a bra ...
  Pads must be placed on bare skin", modesty second to life (gate G1 for ERC's sentence; ILCOR
  carries it) and the public-AED sex gap (CARES 2025: 11.7% against 13.4%) (Part A 2.5, 3); what
  an AED treats: VF and pulseless VT, not asystole; only about 18% of US arrests start shockable
  (CARES), so most arrests are not shockable and CPR is what keeps them alive (Part A 6); minutes:
  AHA 2025 (ARREST registry) first-shock success 93% within 6 minutes and 75% beyond 16, and "Every
  minute of delay to first shock was associated with a 6% decreased probability of survival to
  discharge"; ERC's 3 to 6% (gate G1); the ERC lay book's 10% as the older popular figure (decision
  2) (Part A 2.5, 5I); **where AEDs are**: The Circuit (UK national register visible to 999 call
  handlers; defibfinder.uk not for emergencies) and PulsePoint (US, community by community,
  public responders notified only of public events) (Part D 1.5); locked cabinets (ILCOR advises
  against; theft under 2%) (Part A 2.5); volunteer apps (Ringh 2015: bystander CPR 62% against 48%;
  ILCOR strong recommendation, very low certainty; survival effects inconsistent) (Part A 2.5, Part
  D 1.5); **the law**: US Cardiac Arrest Survival Act, 42 U.S.C. §238q (civil immunity for AED use,
  a floor where states are silent), RCUK: untrained use justified, labelled per decision 9 (Part D
  3); drowning: CPR with breaths before the AED (AHA COR 1), forward to lesson 6; children's pads,
  forward to lesson 6.
- **Mechanism to explain:** ventricular fibrillation as chaotic electrical activity that pumps no
  blood, which a shock can stop so a normal rhythm can restart; why an AED analyses before it
  shocks and "will not discharge if it does not detect appropriate conditions" (ERC lay book); why
  compressions resume straight after a shock (the heart doesn't pump well at once; the drafter
  checks the guideline gives this reason before printing it, else states the instruction alone).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: an office collapse with two rescuers and an AED from reception,
  narrated prompt by prompt. (b) wrinkle: a woman in a sports bra at a gym; one rescuer hesitates
  over exposing her chest. The lesson gives ILCOR's good practice statement, the CARES gap, and the
  steps that keep both the pads and her dignity (bare skin in the right place, others asked to
  turn away, a coat over her afterwards), and then the AED advises no shock: what now? (continue
  CPR; the prompt is not a sign she is fine).
- **Misconceptions:** "an AED can restart a flat line"; "you need training to use an AED"; "you
  could shock someone who doesn't need it"; "stop CPR while you fetch it" (a helper fetches it; a
  lone rescuer's order is lesson 2's); "cut or remove the bra every time"; "each minute costs 10%"
  (the AHA's figure is 6%).
- **Practice:** find the nearest public AED to your home and to your workplace using your country's
  public locator (The Circuit's defibfinder.uk in the UK; a local PulsePoint community if yours has
  one), and note the hours it is accessible; then sequence an AED use from five shuffled prompts;
  then decide for three scenarios (wet ground by a pool; a man with a medication patch where a pad
  goes; the AED says "no shock advised").

### 6. Children, babies and drowning (~85 min)

- **Objectives:** sequence CPR for a child and for a baby under AHA 2025 and under RCUK 2025, and
  say exactly where they differ; state the compression depth for a child and a baby and describe
  the infant techniques each guideline recommends; decide which AED setting or pads to use for a
  small child; explain why breaths matter more in children and in drowning, and sequence the
  response to a drowned person as a lay rescuer and as a trained one.
- **Core ideas:** paediatric boundaries (AHA: until puberty; ERC/RCUK: 0 to 18, infant under 1,
  "when in doubt, use directions for adults") (Part A 2.4); **the difference table**: US compressions
  first (C-A-B, AHA 2b, C-LD), 30:2 one rescuer, 15:2 two; UK/Europe five rescue breaths first,
  then 15:2 if trained in paediatric BLS or 30:2 otherwise, and call handlers use 30:2 with the five
  breaths for untrained callers (RCUK 2025) (Part A 2.4, 4); each side's reason in its own words
  and both sides' admission that the evidence is minimal (Part A 5C); depth: one third of the
  chest, about 5 cm for a child and 4 cm for an infant (AHA), never more than 6 cm at any age
  (RCUK) (Part A 2.3); **infant technique**: AHA 2025 one hand (heel) or two-thumb encircling,
  two-finger technique eliminated (COR 1, B-NR); RCUK two-thumb encircling (Part A 2.3); children
  one or two hands; breaths: mouth-to-mouth-and-nose for infants; why breaths matter (Kitamura
  2010: in non-cardiac arrests aged 1 to 17, conventional CPR 7.2% against 1.6% favourable outcome;
  no difference in cardiac-cause arrests; compression-only still beats nothing) (Part A 3); "it is
  far better to use the adult CPR sequence for the resuscitation of a child than to do nothing"
  (RCUK FAQ, Part D 3.2); single rescuer: call and start CPR before fetching an AED (RCUK); **AED**:
  AHA attenuator and paediatric pads under 8 years, adult AED "may be considered" if none (2b);
  RCUK paediatric mode under 25 kg, adult mode if nothing else, front-back pads under 25 kg (Part A
  2.5, 5E); paediatric survival (CARES: infants lowest) (Part A 3); **drowning**: a hypoxic arrest
  (AHA's sentence); don't enter the water unless trained, reach or throw (RCUK, ERC); ILCOR 2025:
  compressions first for lay people, breaths first for trained duty-to-respond rescuers; RCUK first
  aid and BHF: trained, give five breaths first; AHA: breaths and compressions, trained may start
  with breaths (2b); CPR with breaths before the AED (AHA COR 1); in-water breaths only for trained
  rescuers with flotation (gate G2) (Part A 2.9, 5D); drowning prevention as a pointer (Part C 15).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why a child's arrest is usually hypoxic and an adult's usually cardiac,
  and what that implies for the first action; why a depth is given as a fraction of the chest for
  children; why an attenuator or paediatric mode lowers the dose.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a four-year-old found unresponsive and not breathing, sequenced
  twice, once under each guideline, in the two-column format. (b) wrinkle: a six-month-old pulled
  from a paddling pool at a family barbecue in Spain, with an American parent trained under AHA and
  a British grandparent trained under RCUK. Which sequence, which number (112), which infant
  technique? The lesson's answer: either guideline's sequence beats nothing; do not argue over it;
  the call handler's instructions decide.
- **Misconceptions:** "two fingers for a baby" (removed by the AHA in 2025, not RCUK's either);
  "never use an adult AED on a child"; "you can't do CPR on a baby without hurting it"; "jump in to
  save a drowning person"; "compression-only is fine for children as well as adults" (breaths
  recommended, compression-only if unable or unwilling).
- **Practice:** the two-column table left half blank for the reader to fill from memory, then
  checked; an infant-hand-position rehearsal **on a doll, a rolled towel or a cushion only**;
  decisions for three scenarios (a 12-year-old showing signs of puberty in the US; a 20 kg
  six-year-old and an AED with no child mode; a teenager pulled from a lake by a lifeguard).

### 7. Breathing but not awake (~75 min)

- **Objectives:** decide whether an unresponsive person needs CPR, the recovery position or to be
  left as found, and describe how to put an adult or a child in the recovery position; recognise
  an opioid overdose and sequence the response, including naloxone, under US and UK guidance;
  explain what a physical counter-pressure manoeuvre does for someone about to faint and state when
  a faint needs an emergency call.
- **Core ideas:** the recovery position is for the person who is unresponsive or drowsy and
  breathing normally, not for agonal breathing and not after trauma (ERC/RCUK 2025) (Part A 2.7);
  the ERC/RCUK technique step by step (near arm out, far hand to cheek, far knee bent, roll towards
  you, head tilted, check breathing regularly; either arm position, per an RCT in volunteers); the
  AHA's position (gate G7); no side preference except pregnancy (left side) (Part A 2.7, 2.10);
  the weak evidence base (34 studies, mostly healthy volunteers) (Part A 2.7); check breathing
  continuously and start CPR if it stops being normal (lesson 2 revisited); the AHA/Red Cross 2024
  shock-positioning recommendations for who lies on their back and who goes on their side (gate
  G3); **opioid overdose**: recognition (slow, irregular or absent breathing, extreme drowsiness,
  pinpoint pupils, via RCUK or the lay book, gate G1); the US table: call, CPR with breaths if not
  breathing normally (AHA 2025 COR 1), naloxone must not delay CPR or the call, "Opioid antagonists
  are extremely unlikely to benefit ... in cardiac arrest who is not receiving CPR", and the AHA's
  note that this differs from naloxone's prescribing information; observe afterwards because the
  opioid can outlast naloxone (Part A 2.11); the UK/Europe column: ERC start CPR and give nasal
  naloxone; RCUK "Administer naloxone if you are trained"; **legal status**: US OTC 4 mg nasal
  spray since March 2023 (FDA); UK prescription-only but supplied without prescription by named
  services (2015; nasal added February 2019; widened December 2024), "Anyone can use available
  naloxone to save a life in an emergency" (DHSC); overdose Good Samaritan laws (gate G10); 1 in 6
  to 1 in 3 US arrests related to overdose (AHA) (Part A 2.11); **fainting**: physical
  counter-pressure manoeuvres (ILCOR 2025 strong recommendation, low to very low certainty; lower
  body preferred; meta-analysis of 11 trials, risk halved, AHA supportive text); lie down with legs
  raised or sit with head between knees (NHS); the AHA/Red Cross 2024 leg raise for fainting with no
  trauma (2b, gate G3); NHS call-999 triggers (not awake within a minute, chest pain or
  palpitations, fainted while exercising or lying down, seizure, injury) and more than 28 weeks
  pregnant on the side (Part C 8).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why an unconscious person on their back can block their own airway and
  why the side position lets fluid drain, as the sources give it (the drafter checks RCUK or ERC for
  the wording; else the instruction alone); why naloxone reverses opioid breathing depression but
  not cardiac arrest, and why the person can stop breathing again; why tensing the legs can stop a
  faint (it pushes blood back from the legs, per the AHA's supportive text if it says so; else the
  instruction and the trial result only).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a man found drowsy after a night out, breathing normally (quiet
  and regular; never slow or snoring, which are signs of arrest: Stage 4, 2026-09-25), put in the recovery position and watched, with the moment his breathing changes
  and the rescuer rolls him back and starts CPR. (b) wrinkle: a young woman found in a public
  toilet, barely breathing, pinpoint pupils, a naloxone kit on the wall. The lesson sequences it
  under AHA 2025 (call, breaths and compressions if not breathing normally, naloxone without
  delaying either) and RCUK 2025, and says what to watch for after she wakes.
- **Misconceptions:** "put anyone who's unconscious in the recovery position" (not agonal
  breathing, not trauma); "naloxone first, then CPR" (AHA: not if it delays CPR); "naloxone is
  dangerous if it isn't an opioid overdose" (the drafter checks a read source before printing any
  claim on this; if none, the misconception is left out); "once they wake up they're fine"; "sit a
  fainting person up quickly"; "a faint is never serious".
- **Practice:** the recovery position rehearsed **on a willing adult lying on a carpet or bed, for
  arm and knee placement only, rolled slowly**, or talked through step by step; a decision sheet
  of six unresponsive people (breathing normally, gasping, after a fall from a ladder, after a
  seizure, a suspected overdose, 30 weeks pregnant), each with the action and its source.
- **Images:** Commons candidates (G16): `Recovery position.svg`, `Recovery position maneuver.jpg`,
  `Recovery Position.JPG`; choose one matching ERC/RCUK. No overdose images.
- **Sources:** Part A 2.7, 2.10, 2.11, 4; Part C 8; Part D 3.1 (overdose laws).
- **Connects:** lesson 2 (breathing normally or not), lesson 4 (overdose as a hypoxic arrest).
  Leads to lesson 11 (why trauma changes positioning) and lesson 14 (after a seizure). Forward to
  Habits and Addiction.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 4's breaths argument (the overdose era), lesson 1's law (overdose
  Good Samaritan laws as a second kind of protection).
- **Video:** St John "The Recovery Position" (2016, check G4); CDC "How to Use Naloxone Nasal
  Spray" (US product, labelled so).

### 8. Choking (~80 min)

- **Objectives:** distinguish mild from severe choking and decide what to do for each; sequence
  the response to a choking adult or child over one and to a choking baby under AHA 2025 and RCUK
  2025; decide what changes for late pregnancy, a person too large to reach round, a wheelchair
  user and someone alone; sequence what to do when a choking person becomes unresponsive, and
  state what the guidelines say about suction devices.
- **Core ideas:** mild (coughing, making sounds: let them cough and watch, AHA COR 1) against
  severe (can't cough, speak or breathe) (Part B 2.1); **the 2025 change**: AHA now teaches
  repeated cycles of 5 back blows then 5 abdominal thrusts (COR 1, B-NR), so the US and UK teach
  the same sequence; its evidence (no RCTs; Dunne 2024, 709 cases) and its candid reason
  (consistency with infant and child guidance); the press release's statement that the previous
  AHA guidelines had no adult choking recommendation (Part B 2.2); RCUK's "up to" five and "up to"
  five, when to call (the text and the algorithm differ; teach calling as soon as it is severe,
  speakerphone), no blind finger sweeps (AHA COR 3: Harm), medical review after thrusts (RCUK,
  explicit; not a numbered AHA recommendation) (Part B 2.2); technique (St John "Cough it out, hit
  it out, squeeze it out"; heel of the hand between the shoulder blades, leaning forward; fist
  between the belly button and the bottom of the chest, pull sharply inwards and upwards; the Red
  Cross's navel landmark) (Part B 2.2); chest thrusts for late pregnancy and people too large to
  encircle (AHA COR 1, C-EO); wheelchairs (Red Cross) (Part B 2.3); self-rescue (Red Cross: call
  even if you can't speak, thrusts on yourself or against a chair back; expert advice only) (Part
  B 2.4); **unresponsive**: CPR, compressions can expel the object, remove a visible object when
  giving breaths (Part B 2.5); **infants and children**: children 5 and 5 abdominal; infants 5 back
  blows and 5 chest thrusts, never abdominal thrusts; infant chest-thrust technique three ways (AHA
  2025 heel of one hand; RCUK two-thumb encircling; NHS, British Red Cross and the American Red Cross
  infant page still two fingers) (decision 2); head down along the forearm or thigh; the NHS child
  page's "It doesn't matter which order" as a page that conflicts with every guideline read; the
  unconscious choking child (US compressions first, UK five breaths first); every baby given chest
  thrusts seen in hospital (St John) (Part B 2.6); **suction devices** (LifeVac, Dechoker): ILCOR
  withdrew its 2020 "suggest against" and makes no recommendation; AHA 2b, insufficient evidence;
  RCUK July 2026 no recommendation either way, established treatments first; the evidence (Dunne
  2023 self-reported, industry-assisted; Dunne 2025 a manikin trial) (Part B 2.7).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why an effective cough beats anything a rescuer can do (high airway
  pressure, AHA); why back blows go first (the sources give consistency and the Dunne cohort, not a
  physical mechanism: the lesson says so); why abdominal thrusts can injure (case reports of aortic
  dissection and gastric rupture) and why that is the reason for medical review; why infants get
  chest thrusts, not abdominal (organ injury, AHA).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a colleague choking on a sandwich at lunch, sequenced cycle by
  cycle under either country's guidance (they now match). (b) wrinkle: a grandmother alone with a
  choking eight-month-old, who remembers two fingers from a 2019 leaflet; the lesson gives RCUK's
  two-thumb and the AHA's heel of one hand, says which her country teaches, and what she does
  when the baby goes limp (her country's CPR sequence from lesson 6, with the call on speaker).
- **Misconceptions:** "abdominal thrusts first" (older US teaching); "finger-sweep the throat";
  "hold a choking baby upside down by the feet" (British and American Red Cross: not effective);
  "slap a coughing person on the back"; "once it's out they're fine"; "it doesn't matter which
  order"; "buy a suction device instead of learning the technique".
- **Practice:** sequence three cases from shuffled steps (a pregnant woman at 36 weeks; a large man
  you can't reach round; a toddler); rehearse back-blow and thrust hand positions **on yourself,
  without force, or in the air**, never on another person; write what you would do if you choked
  alone at home.

  people.jpg`, `Abdominal thrusts against choking.jpg`, `AbdominalThrust HeimlichManoeuvre.svg`.
  Check each for technique; use at most two. No infant still unless it shows current technique for
  the country it is attributed to.
- **Sources:** Part B 2.
- **Connects:** lesson 6's paediatric sequences (the unresponsive choking child). Leads to lesson

### 9. Severe bleeding and shock (~85 min)

- **Objectives:** recognise life-threatening bleeding and sequence its control under AHA/Red Cross
  2024 and RCUK 2025, from direct pressure to a tourniquet; describe where and how a tourniquet goes
  on, and explain why a bystander never loosens it; explain how the evidence on tourniquets changed
  and what it still does not show; recognise shock and decide how to position a person with it
  under each country's guidance.
- **Core ideas:** recognition (pooling, spurting, bleeding despite pressure, AHA/Red Cross 2024; the
  Red Cross's "about half of what a soda can contains"; "Bleeding emergencies can claim a life in
  3-5 minutes", Stop the Bleed) (Part B 3.1, 3.7); direct pressure first (AHA COR 1; ILCOR strong,
  very low certainty; RCUK), then a pressure dressing; the soaked-dressing conflict (the Red Cross
  says don't remove the pad, St John and the British Red Cross say replace it, no guideline
  addresses it: continuous firm pressure is the point) (Part B 3.2); pressure points (ILCOR strong
  against; AHA "uncertain", 2b; pulses return within 20 to 40 seconds) and elevation (not in any
  guideline read; still on the NHS cuts page; not called harmful) (Part B 3.3); **tourniquets**:
  AHA/Red Cross 2024 COR 1, B-NR; commercial probably better than improvised (2a), improvised at
  least 2 in wide (2a, C-EO); ILCOR weak, very low certainty; placement: RCUK 5 to 7 cm above,
  Stop the Bleed 2 to 3 inches, never over a joint, "a hand's width or less above the wound" as the
  lesson's summary (labelled as the course's own); tighten until bleeding stops, it hurts, write the
  time, never release it (RCUK, Stop the Bleed, St John); the "(if trained)" on the American Red
  Cross page against the guideline's lack of a training condition and the UK's PAcT kits "designed
  to be used by ANY person" (Part B 3.4, 3.7); **the history** (Richey 2007: Civil War misuse, the
  First World War instruction to remove tourniquets, the Second World War review, and the hesitancy
  they left; the reversal from Iraq and Afghanistan, Kragh 2009's 90% against 10% survival when
  applied before shock, no amputations solely from tourniquets; civilian Teixeira 2018 adjusted OR
  5.86 and Scerbo 2017; the White House-convened Stop the Bleed) (Part B 3.4); packing and
  haemostatic dressings (AHA 2a; ILCOR weak; RCUK "ideally a haemostatic dressing"; packing for the
  neck, armpit and groin; no haemostatic dressing in the PAcT kit) (Part B 3.5); embedded objects
  (don't pull them out) (Part B 3.6); **shock**: signs (pale, cold, clammy, fast weak pulse, fast
  breathing, confusion, thirst); nothing to eat or drink; keep warm; **the leg-raise table**:
  AHA/Red Cross 2024 raise the feet 6 to 12 in only if no trauma (2b), back down if it hurts; St John
  and the British Red Cross raise the legs, including for bleeding, except a suspected leg, hip or
  pelvic fracture (Part B 5; gate G3 for the table wording).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why pressure works on the bleeding point and a pressure point doesn't
  (collateral flow restores the pulse in seconds, per the AHA's volunteer data); why a loose
  tourniquet can make bleeding worse (it blocks veins but not arteries, from Part B 13.3's caution;
  the drafter confirms a read source states it before printing, else "loose application can
  increase venous bleeding" attributed to the source that does); why wide beats narrow; why
  "tourniquet as a last resort" came to be believed and why it reversed.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a deep cut to the forearm from a broken window, controlled with
  pressure and a dressing, and the moment the rescuer judges pressure has failed. (b) wrinkle: a
  thigh wound at a road collision, no tourniquet, a belt and a pen to hand. The lesson gives the
  AHA's improvised-tourniquet conditions, St John's improvisation "following the instruction of
  Ambulance control", the case for and against in each side's terms (Part B 13.4), and why the
  call handler's instruction decides.
- **Misconceptions:** "pressure points stop bleeding"; "loosen a tourniquet now and then";
  "tourniquets cost the limb"; "only trained people may use a tourniquet"; "raise the legs of
  anyone in shock"; "give a person in shock a drink".
- **Practice:** a sequencing exercise for three wounds (a scalp cut, an arm wound spurting through
  a towel, a groin wound); locate the nearest bleed kit or PAcT kit in a place you go often (many
  UK venues display them); the ACS Stop the Bleed online course (free) as the recommended practice,
  with its in-person skills session for the hands-on part; **no exercise tightens a tourniquet on
  anyone**.
- **Images:** Commons candidates (G16): `Tourniquet application.jpg`, `Tourniquet.jpeg`, the US Air
  Force photograph of a tourniquet being applied (US government work, likely public domain);
  choose one showing a windlass tourniquet above a wound and not on a joint.
- **Sources:** Part B 3, 5, 13.3 to 13.6, 14.
- **Connects:** lesson 1 (the law, and fear of doing harm, now with a tourniquet). Leads to lesson
  10 (small wounds) and lesson 11 (open fractures with bleeding). Forward to Personal Safety (public
  places, violence) as a pointer.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 4's grading key on "COR 1 on observational data" against ILCOR's
  "weak, very low certainty" for the same thing, which is the clearest case in the course of two
  grading systems reading one evidence base differently.

### 10. Burns, cuts and small injuries (~85 min; seam: split before "Cuts" if the draft passes 95)

- **Objectives:** sequence first aid for a thermal burn under US and UK guidance, including how
  long to cool and what to cover it with, and explain why each country's figure is what it is;
  decide which burns need hospital care, and sequence first aid for chemical and electrical burns;
  describe first aid for a cut or graze, a nosebleed, something in the eye, a knocked-out tooth and
  an amputated part, and say when each needs help.
- **Core ideas:** **opens from Cooking Fundamentals lesson 2**, naming what it taught and what this
  lesson adds (decision 11); cooling: immediately, running water (ILCOR strong recommendation, very
  low certainty); **the duration table**: UK 20 minutes within 3 hours (NHS, British Burn
  Association, St John, British Red Cross) against US 5 to 20 minutes (AHA/Red Cross 2024, 2b; the
  American Red Cross page since 2026, dated), ILCOR "a specific duration of cooling cannot be
  recommended"; watch small children for over-cooling ("Cool the burn but warm the patient", BBA)
  (Part B 4.1); ice (BBA and St John no; the Red Cross FAQ no; the AHA's narrow fallback of wrapped
  ice for superficial burns when no clean water exists) (Part B 4.1, 4.5); after cooling: remove
  jewellery before swelling; don't remove anything stuck; cover with cling film laid on, not
  wrapped (UK) or a clean dry non-adherent dressing (US); no blisters burst, no sticky plasters;
  pain relief (Part B 4.2); the two moments kept apart (gate G13); when to get care (AHA/Red Cross
  2024: full thickness, larger than the palm, face, hands, feet, genitals; smoke inhalation signs;
  NHS 999 or A&E list, 111 for a child under 5) (Part B 4.3); chemical burns (brush off powder,
  then water: at least 15 minutes US, at least 20 BBA, about an hour NHS; never neutralise;
  protect yourself); eye splashes (15 minutes AHA/Red Cross 2024, at least 20 St John); electrical
  (power off first; small burns can hide internal injury; be ready for CPR) (Part B 4.4); **cuts and
  grazes** (gate G6); **nosebleeds**: sit, lean forward, pinch the soft part 10 to 15 minutes (AHA
  COR 1; NHS), St John's three 10-minute periods and 999 after 30, help if it won't stop, and for
  people on blood thinners (AHA 2a) (Part B 3.8); **eye**: don't rub, cover with a cup for an
  embedded object, rinse dust out (AHA/Red Cross 2024) (Part B 9.1); **knocked-out tooth**:
  permanent teeth only, hold by the crown, brief rinse, try to replant, dentist now; storage table

  saliva; never tap water, AHA COR 3: Harm) (Part B 9.2); **amputated part**: ILCOR 2025 good

- **Mechanism to explain:** why cooling helps and why grease and ice harm (greases hold heat, the
  British Red Cross; ice damages skin); why cling film suits a burn (it doesn't stick; the lesson

  nosebleed (blood into the airway or stomach, the Red Cross and St John); why a tooth's root must
  not dry or sit in tap water (the drafter uses the AHA's reason if it gives one).

- **Misconceptions:** "butter, toothpaste or ice on a burn"; "pop the blister"; "tilt the head back

### 11. Bones, joints, head and spine (~75 min)

- **Objectives:** describe first aid for a suspected fracture and decide when it needs an emergency
  call; compare RICE with PEACE and LOVE for a sprain, and state what the evidence shows about ice
  and anti-inflammatories; recognise the signs after a head injury that need an emergency call
  under NHS and NICE guidance and AHA/Red Cross 2024; decide what to do for a suspected spinal
  injury, including when moving the person comes first.
- **Core ideas:** **fractures**: every AHA/Red Cross 2024 recommendation is expert opinion (C-EO)
  because no first aid studies exist; a limb that is blue, purple or pale needs an ambulance (COR
  1); support as found, don't straighten unless transport requires it; cover open wounds; bleeding
  first (lesson 9); support above and below, don't reset a dislocation, remove rings (British Red
  Cross, St John) (Part B 6.1); **sprains**: AHA/Red Cross 2024 cold 20 to 30 minutes per
  application with a barrier (2a), cold helps pain but hasn't been shown to speed recovery,
  compression for comfort (2b); ILCOR "either ... or no application" of a compression bandage;
  the cold-duration spread (British Red Cross 10 max; NHS, St John and American Red Cross 20; AHA
  20 to 30); PRICE (NHS) and PEACE and LOVE (Dubois and Esculier 2020, an editorial by two
  physiotherapists, not a guideline); the NSAID disagreement (NHS ibuprofen; PEACE and LOVE
  against as standard care) (Part B 6.2, 13.7, 13.8); **head injury**: concussion means off the
  field until assessed (AHA COR 1, B-NR; RCUK); severe signs call the emergency number; the NHS
  call-999 list (knocked out and not woken, can't stay awake, seizure, fall of more than 1 metre or
  5 stairs, clear fluid from the ears or nose, and the rest) and the NICE NG232 ambulance and
  emergency-department triggers, including blood thinners and vomiting; someone stays with them for
  24 hours; no need to keep them awake if tired (NHS); painkillers: NHS paracetamol at home after a
  minor injury against the American Red Cross "not advised" (Part B 7); **spinal**: suspect after
  falls, dives, crushes, collisions (RCUK); keep still, let an alert person hold their own neck
  (RCUK); no collars for first aiders (AHA COR 3: Harm; ILCOR weak against, with the scoping
  review's harms and the room it leaves for trained ski patrollers and lifeguards); airway first,
  always; move for danger, the airway or CPR; the reversal of immobilise-everyone and the AHA's own
  sentence that the old practices were "driven more by legal considerations and unproven theories"
  (Part B 8).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why a first aider supports a fracture instead of straightening it (pain,
  further injury; the AHA's 2a reasons); the argument inside PEACE and LOVE (inflammation as part of
  repair) in its authors' terms, and the NHS's case for pain relief; why collars were dropped
  (ILCOR: impaired breathing and swallowing, raised intracranial pressure, no rise in cord injury
  where protocols changed).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a teenager's wrist after a fall from a skateboard, supported,
  cooled and taken to hospital, with the "if you can move it, it isn't broken" myth answered. (b)
  wrinkle: a cyclist lies on the road after a collision, awake, neck painful, traffic still moving.

  changes if he vomits or stops breathing normally (airway and CPR come first; the log-roll is for

  injury, whatever happens"; "put a collar on them"; "keep a person with a head injury awake all
  night"; "heat on a fresh sprain" (the American Red Cross calls it a myth); "ice speeds healing".
- **Practice:** an improvised support rehearsal on yourself (a folded towel under a forearm, a
  jumper as a sling); a head-injury triage of six cases under NHS and AHA criteria; a short written

### 12. Heart attack and stroke (~75 min)

- **Objectives:** recognise a heart attack, including the presentations people most often dismiss,
  and sequence what a bystander does; state what each major body says about aspirin for chest
  pain, including dose, strength and exceptions, and explain why none recommends it for a
  suspected stroke; recognise a stroke using FAST, and compare FAST with BE-FAST in each side's
  terms; explain why calling at once matters even when symptoms pass.
- **Core ideas:** **opens from Strength and Fitness's callout** (decision 11): its list of symptoms
  is the heart attack list; recognition (chest pain or pressure spreading to the arms, neck, jaw,
  back or stomach; breathlessness; sweating; nausea; light-headedness) and the atypical
  presentations in women, people with diabetes and older people (AHA/Red Cross 2024; the NHS's
  "feeling like you have indigestion"; the American Red Cross on women's earlier symptoms) (Part C
  3); call first, don't drive (AHA/Red Cross 2024 COR 1; about 1 in 300 driven by private car
  arrested en route) (Part C 3); sit on the floor, knees bent, back supported; help with their own
  GTN; **the aspirin table** (Part C 3's table, rebuilt per decision 2, with gate G1 and G3
  applied): US 162 to 325 mg, "may encourage", COR 2b, and "reasonable to wait for EMS" if
  uncertain; UK 300 mg; ERC 150 to 500 mg; each body with its own verb (encourage, assist, administer, give, offer: Stage 4, 2026-09-25); not if
  allergic; St John's under-16 exclusion; the evidence (two observational studies; no study of the
  risk in the first aid setting; aortic dissection as the harm case) (Part C 3); **stroke**: call at
  once (AHA/Red Cross 2024 COR 1); FAST (NHS, RCUK, St John, the British Red Cross; the AHA/Red
  Cross 2024 guideline names it); note the time; call even if symptoms go away (ASA; NHS within 24
  hours); nothing to eat or drink (St John); don't drive to A&E (NHS); low blood sugar as a mimic
  (AHA 2a, forward to lesson 14); FAST not validated in children (Part C 2); **FAST or BE-FAST**:
  the ASA's public message switched to BE-FAST between January 2025 and 2026 (dated); Aroor 2017
  (14.1% missed by FAST, 4.4% with BE-FAST, one centre, retrospective, and its authors'
  "validated in a prospective study" condition); the ASA's own randomised online study finding FAST
  better remembered and the same intent to call (unpublished, labelled so); ILCOR and ERC find no
  evidence to prefer either (Part C 2); **no aspirin for stroke**: phrased as "no guideline
  recommends it, and a stroke can be a bleed" (Part C 2); the ASA's brain-cell figure attributed
  to the ASA (decision 6); oxygen: not in stroke (AHA 3: No Benefit), and why the UK and US differ
  on oxygen by first aiders (one paragraph, Part C 14).
- **Mechanism to explain:** a heart attack is a blocked artery starving heart muscle, which can
  lead to cardiac arrest (lesson 2 revisited); a stroke is either a blockage or a bleed, and nobody
  can tell which without a scan, which is why aspirin is for chest pain only. The drafter checks
  the stroke-types sentence against a read source (NHS stroke page or ASA) before printing it.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a 58-year-old man with crushing chest pain after carrying
  shopping upstairs: call, sit him down, aspirin under his country's rule, watch for collapse. (b)
  wrinkle: a 62-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes who has felt breathless and sick with a sore jaw
  since lunch, "probably indigestion", and an hour later her words come out wrong for two minutes
  and then clear. Two emergencies, one call; the lesson works through what to say to the call
  handler, and why neither "it passed" nor "she's had aspirin before" changes the plan.
- **Misconceptions:** "it's just indigestion"; "wait and see if it passes"; "drive them to hospital
  yourself, it's quicker"; "give aspirin for any chest pain, or for a stroke"; "no crushing chest pain,
  no heart attack" (corrected 2026-09-25 from "women have the same symptoms as men", which would
  have listed the BHF's stated position as a misconception); "FAST catches every stroke".
- **Practice:** say the FAST check aloud on a willing family member (face, arms, speech) and time
  how long it takes; decide aspirin for six people (a 15-year-old in the UK; a man on warfarin; a
  woman allergic to aspirin; an alert man with chest pain after a fall from a ladder; a man with
  classic chest pain; a woman with sudden weakness on one side), with the source for each.
- **Images:** the ASA "Spot a stroke F.A.S.T." poster is reproduced in the AHA/Red Cross 2024
  guideline and is copyrighted: link, don't embed. No Commons image planned.
- **Sources:** Part C 2, 3, 14.
- **Connects:** Strength and Fitness's callout; lesson 2 (arrest as the thing a heart attack can
  become). Leads to lesson 13. Forward to Medical Literacy (reading a mnemonic's sensitivity).
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 4's grading key on COR 2b; lesson 1's give-a-job tactic.
- **Video:** NHS Act FAST (UK) and AHA "B.E. F.A.S.T. to spot a stroke" (US, 2026 message), American
  Red Cross and BHF heart attack videos, after G4.
- **Neutrality:** FAST against BE-FAST and bystander aspirin are contested, each side in its terms;
  the ASA is described as changing its message, not criticised for it.

### 13. Anaphylaxis and asthma (~80 min)

- **Objectives:** recognise anaphylaxis and sequence the response, including when to give the
  second dose under US and UK guidance; describe how to use the common adrenaline devices,
  including their different hold times, and explain why posture matters; explain why
  antihistamines are not the emergency treatment; sequence helping someone through an asthma attack
  with their reliever, and explain why the person's own action plan comes first.
- **Core ideas:** recognition (airway, breathing or circulation problems, usually with skin signs,
  after a trigger) and ILCOR's candour that first aiders shouldn't be expected to recognise it
  reliably without repeated training (Part C 4); **adrenaline first, even if in doubt** (MHRA 2023);
  help them use their own device (AHA/Red Cross 2024 COR 1); outer thigh, through clothing; hold
  times by device (EpiPen 3 seconds, US label and UK leaflet; Jext 10 seconds; "follow the
  instructions printed on the device" as the rule); call the emergency number; **second dose
  table**: UK/Europe at 5 minutes if no improvement, other thigh (RCUK 2025, MHRA, NHS); US if no
  response and EMS more than 5 to 10 minutes away (2b); carry two (MHRA; AHA kit "minimum 1,
  recommended 2") (Part C 4); **posture**: lie down, legs raised, sit up only if breathing is hard,
  never stand or walk even if better (RCUK 2021, MHRA 2023, NHS); the AHA/Red Cross 2024 guideline
  gives no posture recommendation; the evidence is expert opinion on fatal case series (Pumphrey
  2000: median time to arrest 30 minutes for food, 15 for venom, 5 for a drug given in hospital;
  adrenaline before arrest in only 14% of the fatal cases) (Part C 4, decision 6); antihistamines
  third line (RCUK 2021); nasal adrenaline (neffy, FDA August 2024; EURneffy, MHRA 2025; the
  children's dose by June 2026; approval on pharmacokinetic studies in healthy adults) (Part C 4);
  schools in England may keep spare auto-injectors since 2017 (Part C 4); **asthma**: sit up;
  help with their own reliever (AHA/Red Cross 2024 COR 1); spacer preferred, improvised from a
  500 mL bottle or paper cup (2a); UK blue reliever 1 puff every 30 to 60 seconds up to 10; AIR or
  MART inhalers 1 puff every 1 to 3 minutes up to 6 (NHS, Asthma + Lung UK, 2024 onward); call if
  worse, no better after the maximum, or no inhaler; the person's plan first (Part C 5).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why lying flat helps in anaphylaxis (RCUK 2021: keeping the person flat
  maximises venous return; standing has preceded collapse and death); why adrenaline is the
  treatment and antihistamines are not (the drafter uses RCUK 2021's wording for what each does, or
  gives the instruction alone); why a spacer helps (more of the dose reaches the lungs, if a read
  source says so; else the AHA's recommendation alone).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a colleague with a peanut allergy whose lips swell and who starts
  to wheeze after a pastry; she can't get the device out; the rescuer helps, lays her down, calls,
  and watches the clock for the second dose. (b) wrinkle: a teenager at a UK football tournament
  stung by a wasp, whose EpiPen works, who feels better and wants to stand up and go back to his
  friends; his coach from the US says "he's fine". The lesson gives the MHRA's "do not stand up
  even if someone encourages you to", the two countries' second-dose rules, and why "fine" at 6
  minutes is not the end.
- **Misconceptions:** "antihistamine first, EpiPen if it gets worse"; "stand up and walk it off";
  "one auto-injector is enough"; "all auto-injectors are held for 10 seconds"; "you need to be
  trained to use someone's EpiPen"; "take 10 puffs of any inhaler" (not for AIR or MART).
- **Practice:** find the hold time and the injection site on the printed instructions of a real or
  trainer device if one is in your home (never fire a live device); write a one-card plan for a
  household member with a known allergy or asthma (their device, where it lives, the second-dose
  rule for your country); decide for four scenarios (a person unsure whether it's anaphylaxis; a
  child whose own reliever is AIR; a person on a flight; a person who fainted after a sting).
- **Images:** Commons candidates (G16): `Epipen design (cropped).jpg`, `Adrenalina autoinyector1.JPG`
  (check licences; a device photograph shows the blue safety cap and orange tip better than words).
- **Sources:** Part C 4, 5; decision 6 on Pumphrey.
- **Connects:** Cooking Fundamentals lesson 4 (allergens and cross-contact, prevention); lesson 9
  (shock positioning, now reversed for a different reason). Leads to lesson 14.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 7's positioning decisions; lesson 4's grading key on "contested in
  weight, not direction".
- **Video:** MHRA auto-injector campaign and British Red Cross anaphylaxis (UK); Asthma + Lung UK
  asthma attack, after G4.

### 14. Seizures, low blood sugar and poisoning (~85 min)

- **Objectives:** sequence first aid for a convulsive seizure and decide when to call, under US and
  UK guidance; recognise low blood sugar in a person with diabetes and sequence the response,
  including what to give, how much and when never to give anything by mouth; sequence the response
  to a suspected poisoning, including carbon monoxide, and explain why making someone vomit is
  wrong.
- **Core ideas:** **seizures**: protect from injury, time it, don't restrain, nothing in the mouth
  (AHA/Red Cross 2024 COR 3: Harm for both); a person cannot swallow their tongue (Epilepsy
  Foundation; AHA supportive text); **side position table**: turn during the seizure (CDC, Epilepsy
  Foundation, AHA) against after the jerking stops (NHS, Epilepsy Action); both use the recovery
  position once it's over; **call criteria table** (AHA: first seizure, more than 5 minutes,
  repeated without recovery, in water, injury, breathing trouble, infant under 6 months, pregnancy,
  not back to baseline in 5 to 10 minutes; NHS and Epilepsy Action add three in 24 hours; St John
  unresponsive over 10 minutes); febrile seizures (antipyretics don't stop or prevent them, AHA
  COR 3: No Benefit; the NHS "do not ... sponge" against St John's "strip them of a single layer",
  a UK inconsistency named); rescue medicine only by someone trained who follows the person's plan;
  filming can help diagnosis (St John) (Part C 6); **low blood sugar**: awake and able to swallow:
  15 to 20 g of glucose, tablets preferred (ILCOR strong recommendation for tablets), sugary drink or
  sweets if not; recheck at 10 to 15 minutes; call if no better; not awake or can't swallow:
  nothing by mouth (AHA COR 3: Harm), recovery position, call; glucagon only if prescribed and you
  know how; the child who won't cooperate (sugar under the tongue, ILCOR weak); the units (below 70
  mg/dL, below 4 mmol/L) and the US 15-15 rule; the British Red Cross's logic that sugar given to a
  conscious person whose sugar is actually high is unlikely to harm (Part C 7); **poisoning**: US
  Poison Help 1-800-222-1222; UK 999 if unconscious, not breathing, seizing, or struggling to
  breathe, otherwise NHS 111; don't induce vomiting (NHS; ipecac abandoned, AACT/EAPCCT 2013);
  nothing to eat or drink unless a poison centre or clinician says so (the dilution disagreement,
  reconciled) (Part C 9); activated charcoal as a hospital treatment, not first aid; chemicals on
  skin and eyes (back to lesson 10); **carbon monoxide** (colourless, odourless, symptoms better
  outside, get out, rescuer safety, alarms) (Part C 9); the opioid overdose back-reference (lesson
  7).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why nothing goes in a seizing or drowsy person's mouth (choking and
  aspiration, the AHA's supportive text); why sugar is safe to try in a conscious person and
  dangerous in an unconscious one (swallowing, choking); why vomiting is induced no longer
  (choking, and no benefit shown, per the sources read).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a man has a tonic-clonic seizure in a supermarket; the rescuer
  clears space, cushions his head, times it, and puts him on his side under each country's timing;
  it stops at 2 minutes and he is known to have epilepsy: call or not? (b) wrinkle: a colleague with
  type 1 diabetes becomes confused and sweaty mid-afternoon, then says he's fine and refuses juice;
  twenty minutes later he's drowsy and can't swallow properly. The lesson sequences the change from
  "encourage oral glucose" to "nothing by mouth, recovery position, call", and why a hypo can look
  like drunkenness or a stroke (back to lesson 12).
- **Misconceptions:** "they can swallow their tongue"; "hold them down" or "put something between
  their teeth"; "give a sugary drink to anyone who collapses"; "make them sick"; "give milk for a
  poisoning" (named only if a read source addresses it; otherwise the rule "nothing unless told"
  carries it); "a seizure always needs an ambulance" (not for a known epilepsy seizure that follows
  its usual pattern, per the NHS and Epilepsy Action criteria).
- **Practice:** a seizure log template (start, end, what you saw) filled in from a described case;
  a scenario sort of eight people (conscious hypo, unconscious hypo, febrile seizure, first seizure,
  a child who drank bleach, a family with headaches in winter that ease at work, a person who took
  too many tablets and is alert, a seizure in a swimming pool), each with the call decision and the
  source; save your country's poison line in your phone.
- **Images:** none planned; no photographs of seizures.
- **Sources:** Part C 6, 7, 9.
- **Connects:** lesson 2 (the seizure-like start of cardiac arrest, contrasted), lesson 7 (the
  recovery position), lesson 12 (hypoglycaemia as a stroke mimic). Leads to lesson 15. Forward to
  Home Repair and Maintenance (carbon monoxide alarms).
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 2's call-or-111 decision; lesson 7's positioning.
- **Video:** Epilepsy Action "CARE" (UK), Epilepsy Foundation "Stay Safe Side" (US), St John
  diabetic emergency and poisoning videos, after G4.
- **Neutrality:** people with epilepsy and diabetes are described as people with a condition and a
  plan, not as patients to be managed; no sensational language.

### 15. Heat, cold, bites and stings (~75 min)

- **Objectives:** distinguish heat exhaustion from heatstroke and sequence the response to each,
  including why cooling comes before transport for heatstroke; sequence the response to
  hypothermia and to frostbite, and explain why rubbing and rapid rewarming are wrong; describe
  first aid for a bee or wasp sting and a snakebite, and compare the US and UK advice on
  snakebite.
- **Core ideas:** **heat**: exhaustion (sweating, clammy, dizzy, thinking clearly; better within 30
  minutes of cooling and fluids, NHS) against heatstroke (confusion, collapse, seizure, very high
  temperature); **cooling table**: ILCOR 2020 and RCUK 2025 whole-body cold-water immersion (1 to
  26°C) until below 39°C, or about 15 minutes without a thermometer, for exertional heatstroke;
  "cool first, transfer second" (gate G1); the AHA's supportive text on water temperature against
  ILCOR's colder-is-faster remark; no recommendation for older people in heat waves (classic
  heatstroke); the NHS and St John wet sheet and fanning, the CDC/NIOSH cold bath if possible
  (Part C 10); fluids for heat exhaustion (carbohydrate-electrolyte drinks best, AHA supportive
  text; "Avoid salt tablets", CDC/NIOSH); nothing by mouth if confused; **hypothermia**: move to
  warmth, remove wet clothing, blankets, insulate from the ground, cover head and neck (AHA/Red
  Cross 2024, gate G3); high-calorie food or drink if alert; no heat sources, rubbing or massage on
  the limbs (COR 3: Harm); no warm shower or bath if drowsy (COR 3: Harm); no alcohol (NHS, St
  John); handle gently (planned around); **frostbite**: rewarm in 37 to 40°C water (COR 1) only if
  it won't refreeze; don't rub; don't walk on frozen feet; loose dry dressings between digits (Part
  C 11); **stings**: remove a honeybee stinger quickly, how doesn't matter (AHA supportive text);
  cold and painkillers; watch for anaphylaxis (lesson 13); **snakebite table**: US (pit vipers): no
  ice, suction, cutting, shock or tourniquet, no pressure immobilisation; UK (the adder): keep calm,
  immobilise, no pressure dressing, ice, heat or tourniquet, never suck; Australia's pressure
  immobilisation for elapids named as a third practice and not taught (ANZCOR not read) (Part C 12).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why immersion beats everything else for heatstroke (water carries heat
  away far faster than air: a callback to Cooking Fundamentals lesson 2's heat-transfer arithmetic,
  which the reader has done); why rubbing and rapid rewarming harm (the AHA's reasons for COR 3,
  as given); why a pressure dressing suits one snake's venom and not another's (the ERC note on
  neurotoxic against cytotoxic venom, gate G1; fallback: the instruction and the "pit vipers, more
  than 95% of North American bites" reason from the AHA alone).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a runner collapses near the finish of a summer half-marathon,
  confused and hot; the medical tent has an ice bath; cool first, transfer second. (b) wrinkle: an
  80-year-old neighbour found confused in a hot flat during a heat wave, no bath big enough, no
  thermometer. ILCOR makes no recommendation for classic heatstroke; the lesson sequences the call,
  the practical cooling the UK pages teach, and why the athlete's method doesn't transfer.
- **Misconceptions:** "alcohol warms you up"; "rub frostbite with snow or your hands"; "take salt
  tablets in the heat"; "suck out snake venom" or "use a tourniquet on a snakebite"; "heatstroke
  always means dry skin" (sweating may continue); "get them to hospital first, cool them on the way".
- **Practice:** a scenario sort (a hiker shivering and mumbling; a roofer with cramps and a
  headache; a child with a numb white fingertip after sledging; a dog walker bitten by an adder in
  England; a gardener stung on the hand), each with the sequence and the source; write a heat-wave
  check plan for an older relative (who checks, how often, what to look for), with the NHS's heat
  exhaustion signs.
- **Images:** Commons candidates (G16) for an adder (*Vipera berus*) photograph, so a UK reader
  knows the one venomous native snake; checked for licence.
- **Sources:** Part C 10, 11, 12; Part B 14 (snakebite myths).
- **Connects:** Cooking Fundamentals lesson 2 (heat transfer); lessons 13 (stings and anaphylaxis)
  and 14 (confusion as a sign). Leads to lesson 16. Forward to Wilderness Survival and Emergency
  Preparedness, which own remote and disaster versions.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 10's burn cooling (cold water again, for a different reason) and
  lesson 4's grading key on "no recommendation" as a finding.
- **Video:** St John heat exhaustion, heatstroke (with G4's caption condition) and frostbite videos.

### 16. After the emergency, and staying ready (~70 min)

- **Objectives:** describe what a rescuer may feel after an emergency and where support is; sequence
  the first aid response to someone with suicidal thoughts that ERC and RCUK 2025 give, and say where
  to point them; state what the evidence shows about how fast first aid skills fade and what keeps
  them, and choose a way to train and keep training; explain how resuscitation guidelines are made
  and changed, and check whether an instruction you learned is still current.
- **Core ideas:** **the rescuer's aftermath**: ERC and RCUK 2025 note rescuers may need support; the
  BHF links support pages; most arrests don't survive even with good CPR (lesson 4), and that isn't
  the rescuer's failure (Part A 2.8; Part D 2.3); DNACPR and advance decisions: respect one if it
  becomes known (RCUK law booklet) (Part D 3.2); **mental health crisis**: ERC 2025 added suicidal
  thoughts to first aid (ILCOR and the AHA/Red Cross 2024 guideline did not); its steps (ask if
  they're all right, ask directly about suicidal thoughts and plans, summarise what they've said,
  offer hope; if there's a concrete plan, tell them you're getting help and call) via RCUK 2025
  (gate G1); lines: 988 (US), Samaritans 116 123 (UK and Ireland), worded exactly as Mental
  Fitness's callout; Mental Health First Aid training's evidence (Morgan 2018: gains in knowledge and
  confidence, small increase in help given; a Cochrane review could not show long-term effects, as
  ERC reports it) (Part C 13); pointer to Mental Fitness; **skills fade**: skills deteriorate
  within 3 months and plateau to 6 (Riggs 2019), and knowledge isn't associated with skill level;
  30-minute training can match a longer course at 6 months (Roppolo 2007); spaced learning (very low
  certainty, thin for laypeople) (Part D 4); ILCOR 2025: feedback devices in training (strong,
  moderate certainty), instructor-led or self-directed digital training for lay adults (weak, very
  low), and the caveat that simulated confidence "may not translate to real-life situations" (Part
  D 4; gate G11); **where to train**: US Red Cross and AHA classes, Stop the Bleed sessions; UK
  British Red Cross free workshops (no qualification) and St John sessions; Europe, national
  councils and World Restart a Heart on 16 October; RevivR, Lifesaver, the Red Cross apps (Part D
  5, 6); certificates as workplace instruments (OSHA, HSE L74 EFAW one day and FAW three days,
  three-year certificates, annual refresher recommended, not required) (Part D 5); CPR in schools
  (84% of US states by end of 2024; England statutory from 2020) (Part D 4.1); **how guidelines
  change**: ILCOR's systematic reviews and CoSTRs; the AHA, ERC and RCUK adapting them; public pages
  following months or years later (decision 2's list as the evidence); the 2025 changes as the case
  study (back blows first in the US, two fingers dropped, call-first in Europe, COVID modifications
  withdrawn, suction devices from "against" to no recommendation); how to check an instruction
  (find the body, the year and the grade; look for a newer one); **the course's question joined to
  the institute's claim sort**.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why a motor skill fades when knowledge doesn't (Riggs: knowledge not
  associated with skill), and why feedback during practice matters; why guidance changes in steps
  (review cycles) and why the public hears late (pages are reviewed on their own schedules).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a reader who did a class in 2019 audits what they learned
  against 2025 guidance: two fingers for a baby (changed in the US), abdominal thrusts first (US,
  changed), move from bed to floor (UK, changed), 30:2 (unchanged), and says which to relearn. (b)
  wrinkle: a friend texts late at night saying they don't see the point any more. The lesson
  sequences ERC and RCUK's steps, the lines, what to do if there is a plan, and where the first aid
  role ends and Mental Fitness's material and professional help begin.
- **Misconceptions:** "once trained, always trained"; "an online course is the same as a class"
  (ILCOR suggests either for acquisition; HSE wants practical skills assessed in person; both are
  stated); "asking about suicide puts the idea in their head" (not taught as a finding either way,
  per the planned-around note: ERC says ask directly); "if they died, I did it wrong"; "a
  certificate from years ago means I'm current".
- **Practice:** book or find a hands-on class near you and write down the date (or, if none is
  available, schedule RevivR or Lifesaver and a repeat in three months, per Riggs); audit three
  instructions you learned anywhere before this course against its lessons, with the body and year
  for each; begin the project.
- **Images:** the Commons `Steps of CPR-1964.jpg` (historical, licence to check, G16) as the one
  place a superseded technique is shown, captioned as the history it is (decision 10).
- **Sources:** Part A 2.8; Part C 13; Part D 3.2, 4, 5, 6; SOURCES "Safety-critical guidance".
- **Connects:** every lesson's label (decision 3); Mental Fitness (crisis); How to Learn Anything and
  Memory (spacing and retrieval, now for a motor skill); Habits and Self-Discipline (a practice
  routine that survives). Forward to Medical Literacy, Teaching and Mentoring.
- **Spaced review of:** every lesson, through the audit and the project.
- **Neutrality:** suicide is handled in line with Mental Fitness's approach and wording; the
  online-against-in-person question is contested, both sides stated.

## Assessments

- **Course-end test:** 24 items, at least 17 application, every item a scenario not in a lesson.
  Blueprint: lesson 1 (1: a liability or consent case, labelled general information), 2 (3:
  recognising arrest from a described breathing pattern, call-first under a named country, 999
  against 111), 3 (2: a compression number with its reason, the bed or pregnancy case), 4 (2:
  reading a recommendation line, who should give breaths), 5 (1: an AED prompt), 6 (2: a child
  sequence under a named country, an infant technique), 7 (2: recovery position or not, the
  overdose sequence), 8 (2: mild against severe, a special case), 9 (2: pressure to tourniquet, the
  shock position under a named country), 10 (2: burn cooling under a named country, a small-injury
  triage), 11 (1: head injury red flag or spinal decision), 12 (1: aspirin decision), 13 (1: second
  dose or posture), 14 (1: seizure call or hypoglycaemia), 15 (1: heatstroke or hypothermia). Lesson
  16 is tested through the project. **Every safety item names the country whose rule it asks about
  or has one correct answer under both (decision 12).** No item's correct answer depends on a
  gate that did not close. Key sequence irregular, all four positions used, option lengths within
  about 15 characters.
- **Project: "Your household's first ten minutes."** Deliverable: an emergency card, a rehearsal
  log, and five decision write-ups.
  1. **The card.** Your emergency number and how your location reaches the call handler; your
     address or landmark as you would say it; your country's poison line; the nearest public AED
     to your home and when it is accessible; for each household member with a known condition
     (allergy, asthma, epilepsy, diabetes, heart disease), where their medicine or device is and
     the one rule this course taught for it, with the guideline and year. (Nothing on the card is
     submitted anywhere; it stays in your home.)
  2. **The rehearsals**, logged with dates: your call script said aloud (never dialled); two minutes
     of compressions into a cushion at 100 to 120 a minute, three times over two weeks, with RevivR's
     feedback if you can use it or a metronome if not, noting when your depth or rhythm fell off; a
     spoken walk-through of the recovery position and of the choking sequence for the youngest
     person in your household.
  3. **Five decisions.** From a bank of ten scenarios the course supplies (drafted with the test,
     none reused from lessons or the test), choose five, including at least one child or infant
     case and at least one medical emergency. For each, write the sequence under your country's
     guidance with the body, year and grade of each step, what would change under the other
     country's guidance, and what you would say to the call handler.
  4. **The class.** Book, attend or schedule a hands-on class, or, where none is available, a
     digital practice course and a three-month repeat; write the date.
  **Rubric:** card complete and correct for the reader's country; rehearsals logged with an honest
  note of what fell off; each decision sequenced correctly, with attributions; the other-country
  comparison correct; a class or repeat scheduled. **Any of these fails the project outright,
  whatever else is right:** a wrong emergency number for the reader's country; anything by mouth
  for a person who is not fully awake; aspirin for a suspected stroke; loosening or removing a
  tourniquet; blind finger sweeps; a sequence that delays the call or CPR for naloxone, an AED
  fetch by a lone rescuer with a phone, or a pulse check.

## Neutrality notes

A sensitive domain (medicine) and a safety-critical one. **Every lesson gets the neutrality audit.**
Most of the course is established or is two bodies' weak recommendations resolved differently; the
failure to guard against is not ideology but **false certainty and silent picking**. The auditor
checks, in every lesson: that no difference between the US and UK/Europe is resolved by editorial
preference (decision 2); that "weak, very low certainty" is never presented as "doesn't matter" or
as "proven" (decision 5); that lagging public pages are named without sneering (decision 2); and
that no mechanism was added from general knowledge (decision 8).

- **Breaths or compression-only; five breaths first for children or compressions first;
  drowning's order; back blows first; tourniquets and improvised tourniquets; haemostatic
  dressings; leg raise in shock; burn cooling time; ice and NSAIDs for sprains; collars; aspirin;
  FAST or BE-FAST; anaphylaxis second dose and posture; oxygen; seizure side timing; febrile
  seizure cooling; oral dilution; classic heatstroke cooling; suction devices; online training.**
  Contested empirical (SOURCES "Contested questions" and each part's list). Each lesson gives the
  sides in their proponents' terms, says what evidence would settle it where the sources say, and
  resolves nothing that the bodies have not.
- **The duty to rescue** (lesson 1). A value question. The duty-to-assist view (the Vermont,
  Minnesota and Rhode Island statutes; Kaufman's advocacy named as advocacy, not cited for content)
  and the common-law view (no duty unless you caused the danger; the SARAH Act's critics in RCUK's
  words) each stated fairly. Described, never prescribed.
- **Who receives bystander CPR** (lessons 1, 4, 5). The sex and race disparities are established
  associations; their causes are not measured. Perman 2019's themes are the public's perceptions,
  labelled so. No insinuation about any group of bystanders.
- **Opioid overdose and naloxone** (lesson 7). Taught as a medical emergency in public-health
  language; no claim about drug policy; the legal status in each country described as law. The
  person who overdoses is not described with stigmatising terms.
- **Suicide** (lesson 16). Aligned with Mental Fitness's approach and numbers; ERC and RCUK's steps
  attributed; no claim about whether asking plants the idea (no primary study read).
- **DNACPR and resuscitation decisions** (lesson 16). One sentence from RCUK's law booklet; the
  ethics of withdrawal and end-of-life care are not taught.
- **Religious or cultural objections** to touching, undressing or resuscitation. Not in SOURCES.
  Modesty is addressed (lesson 5) from ILCOR and ERC's text only; the course makes no claim about
  any tradition's rules. If a reviewer finds this a material omission, it goes to `docs/QUEUE.md`
  as a research gap, not into a lesson from general knowledge.
- **Public bodies and charities.** The BHF, St John, the British and American Red Cross and the
  NHS are cited as the public faces of their countries' guidance; where their pages lag, the
  lesson names the page, not the organisation's competence.

## What downstream courses will need from this one

**Personal Safety** (next, this term) can assume scene safety for a rescuer, the emergency numbers
and how location reaches the call handler, and that a reader knows severe bleeding control and
tourniquets; it owns violence, threat and self-protection. **Home Repair and Maintenance** can
assume the rescuer's rule for electricity (power off before touching) and carbon monoxide's signs
and the case for alarms, and owns wiring and appliances. **Car Basics** can assume the road-scene
danger check and suspected spinal injury (keep still, airway first) and owns roadside procedure.
**Relationships and Family** can assume child and infant CPR, infant choking and the household
emergency card. **Human Anatomy and Physiology** can assume the reader has met ventricular
fibrillation, hypoxic arrest and venous return as first aid mechanisms, and should deepen rather
than repeat them. **Medical Literacy** can assume the AHA's class and level of evidence, ILCOR's
strength and certainty, and the difference between a registry association and a trial, and should
use lesson 4's compression-only case as its bridge. **Habits and Addiction** can assume the reader
can recognise an opioid overdose and knows naloxone's status in the US and UK. **Introduction to
Law** can assume the Good Samaritan and duty-to-assist examples as general information.
**Teaching and Mentoring** can assume skill decay and feedback as the reason practice is spaced.
**Emergency Preparedness** and **Wilderness Survival** can assume everything here and own remote,
prolonged and disaster care. **Travel Skills** can assume 112 across the EU.
