S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in First Aid and CPR, on the lesson called Anaphylaxis and asthma.

S2: Two conditions that both come with their own medicine.

S1: And usually the person carries it. The job is to recognise what's happening, get that medicine used without delay, make the call, and get the position right, which for these two is usually opposite.

S2: Start with recognising it. What am I looking for?

S1: Resuscitation Council UK's twenty twenty five guideline says to suspect it if someone has breathing trouble, from noisy breathing high in the throat to wheezing lower down; flushing, a rash or hives, cold or clammy skin, or feeling faint; stomach pain, vomiting or diarrhoea; and a recent exposure to a food they're known to be allergic to, or an insect sting. Then two warnings. The international body, ILCOR, says first aiders shouldn't be expected to recognise it without repeated training and encounters with people who've had it. And Resuscitation Council UK's twenty twenty one guideline, written for health professionals, says the skin changes may be absent in up to twenty per cent of cases. So no rash doesn't mean no anaphylaxis.

S2: Why adrenaline, and not an antihistamine?

S1: In anaphylaxis the blood vessels widen, fluid leaks out of them into the tissues, and the airways tighten and swell. Adrenaline narrows the vessels, reduces the swelling, opens the airways, strengthens the heart's contraction and damps down the release of histamine. Antihistamines, in the words of that twenty twenty one guideline, are "a third-line intervention". They can help the skin, but must not be given in preference to adrenaline. And the UK's medicines regulator, the MHRA, says an auto-injector should be used without delay if anaphylaxis is suspected, even if in doubt about the severity. The doubt is about how severe a suspected reaction is.

S2: Who's allowed to give it? I've heard both anyone, and only trained people.

S1: People get it wrong both ways. In the US, the American Heart Association and American Red Cross twenty twenty four guideline says the person should use their own autoinjector, and a first aider should assist if assistance is required. In the UK, Resuscitation Council UK's step names the person or trained individuals, and St John Ambulance tells a first aider to help them use it, or give it themselves straight away, following the instructions. This course's reading: in both countries the device is the person's own, it goes in at once, and you help as much as they need. Ask the call handler on speaker, and do what they say.

S2: And the call. Before the pen or after?

S1: The orders differ on paper and agree in practice. The US guideline says call emergency services for anyone with anaphylaxis. Resuscitation Council UK's list puts the nine nine nine call first; the MHRA, the NHS and the device leaflets, written for the person with the allergy, put the injection first and the call straight after. None of them delays adrenaline for the call, or skips the call because the adrenaline worked. With two of you, one helps while the other calls.

S2: Using the device itself?

S1: People get the hold wrong. EpiPen: hold firmly against the outer thigh for three seconds. Jext: ten seconds, a slow count to ten, then massage the area for ten. So the rule for every device is to follow the instructions printed on it, which the NHS says are on the side. And never a thumb or hand over the needle end.

S2: Now position. You said opposite.

S1: The US guideline's anaphylaxis section says nothing on posture. In the UK the texts are firm: lie down flat and raise the legs. If pregnant, on the left side. If breathing is hard, sit up gently, without changing position suddenly, and lie down again as soon as you can. And the MHRA: "stay lying down even if you feel better", and "do not stand up even if someone encourages you to". The reason, from the twenty twenty one guideline: with the vessels wide and leaking, less blood gets back to the heart, and getting up from lying to standing or sitting upright has been associated with collapse and death.

S2: How strong is that evidence?

S1: Thin. There's no trial, and the European guideline labels its posture advice expert opinion. So it's a firm instruction on thin evidence, and the US silence is an absence, not a finding that posture doesn't matter.

S2: The second dose.

S1: The US guideline's text estimates that seven to eighteen per cent of people need more than one. The countries differ on when. In the UK it's a clock: if symptoms persist five minutes after the first dose, give a second, ideally in the opposite leg. In the US guideline, a repeat dose may be considered if the person hasn't responded to the first and the ambulance will take more than five to ten minutes to arrive. Neither guideline gives a reason for its timing. Either way you need the time of the first dose, so the course suggests saying it aloud when it goes in. And the MHRA and EpiPen's US label both say carry two.

S2: Asthma, then. What changes?

S1: Position first. In asthma, sitting up is the rule. Then their own reliever, and here the countries agree. The US guideline says help the person use their own prescribed reliever as needed, and a spacer, or a nebuliser, is reasonable in preference to an inhaler alone. Resuscitation Council UK says help them use their own reliever inhaler, with a spacer if one's available.

S2: How many puffs?

S1: The US guideline gives no count. The NHS gives two schedules. A blue reliever: one puff every thirty to sixty seconds, up to a maximum of ten. An AIR or MART inhaler: one puff every one to three minutes, up to a maximum of six. And if the person's own asthma plan gives a different maximum, follow the plan. In the UK, call nine nine nine if they feel worse at any time, are no better after the maximum, or have no inhaler. The US asthma section gives no call rule, but its text on assessing an ill person says any abnormality in breathing should prompt a call to emergency services.

S2: And if either one stops responding?

S1: If they're not breathing normally, that's the emergency call and CPR, with the handler coaching you, and the defibrillator as soon as one arrives. Slow, noisy or gasping breathing is not normal. If they're breathing normally, quietly and regularly, it's the recovery position, watching the breathing the whole time.
