S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in First Aid and CPR, lesson seven. Breathing but not awake.

S2: So not the cardiac arrest. The person who won't wake up.

S1: The person who won't respond, and what their breathing tells you to do next. And everything hangs on one test from lesson two: are they breathing normally?

S2: Then I need to know exactly what normal means.

S1: Resuscitation Council UK's 2025 guideline is specific about what fails. Slow, laboured breathing, and other abnormal patterns like gasping or panting, must be recognised as signs of cardiac arrest. The American Heart Association lists the words bystanders use for that kind of breathing: abnormal, snoring, gasping. So if the person isn't breathing, or the breathing is slow, laboured or noisy, a snore, a gurgle, a gasp, or you can't tell, they're treated as in cardiac arrest, and they go on their back for CPR. In the UK's words: if there is any doubt, assume cardiac arrest and start CPR.

S2: So normal breathing is what's left.

S1: Breathing none of those words describe. Quiet, regular, unlaboured, at an ordinary pace. And nothing about the recovery position applies to anyone else.

S2: Okay. Say he passes the test. Quiet, regular breathing, won't wake.

S1: Then there's a second question before you move him: is he injured? A fall, a crash, a blow to the head. If the answers are breathing normally and not injured, he goes on his side. The European Resuscitation Council's 2025 first aid guideline, which the UK repeats almost word for word, says to place adults and children with a decreased level of responsiveness who do not meet the criteria for CPR into a side-lying recovery position. The American guidance, from the AHA and the American Red Cross in 2024, grades it: it is reasonable to put someone with decreased alertness, not caused by injury, who is breathing normally, on their side.

S2: And who doesn't go on their side?

S1: The European and UK line gives two exclusions: agonal breathing, and trauma. Agonal breathing is the gasping. That person is in arrest, and the AHA says flatly that effective CPR can't be performed in the recovery position. Lesson two had friends at a five-a-side game roll a man onto his side because he was breathing, when the loud, slow gasps were the arrest itself. That's the mistake this exclusion is for.

S2: And the injured person?

S1: The ERC says a person with known trauma is kept on their back. It doesn't say why in the text the course read, so the lesson gives the instruction without a reason. The American recovery position line is written only for a cause that isn't injury. So an injured person who won't respond stays lying as they are, and you watch the breathing. And if it stops being normal, at any point, onto the back and CPR.

S2: Why the side at all?

S1: The British Red Cross explains that when someone is unresponsive their muscles relax and the tongue can block the airway. The AHA and Red Cross text adds that the position may reduce the risk of breathing in saliva, vomit or blood. But the same guideline says something the leaflets leave out. The recovery position is associated with delayed recognition of respiratory arrest, and delayed chest compressions.

S2: Because once they're on their side, you stop watching.

S1: Facing away from you, they can stop breathing without anyone noticing. So it's not something you do and walk away from. The ERC says check regularly for normal breathing and don't leave unless you have to go for help. For children, the UK says continuously, or at least every minute. The ERC gives adults no number, so stay where you can see the chest and hear the breath.

S2: The lesson's second part is opioid overdose. What would I see?

S1: The UK guideline says suspect it when the breathing is slow, irregular or absent, the person is extremely drowsy or won't respond, or the pupils are very small. Notice the first sign. Slow breathing in someone who won't respond is the overdose picture as well as a sign of arrest, which is one more reason it never counts as normal. And the lesson is clear it teaches a medical emergency, not anything about drug use itself.

S2: And often I won't know it's an overdose.

S1: The AHA says telling an overdose from other causes can be difficult. It matters less than it sounds, because the first response is the same as for anyone unresponsive and not breathing normally: call, and CPR. An overdose slows and then stops the breathing, and the heart stops after that. That's the hypoxic kind of arrest from lessons four and six, which is why the AHA asks lay rescuers for CPR with breaths here. The UK says start CPR, which under its adult guideline means thirty compressions to two breaths if you're trained, and compressions if not.

S2: Where does naloxone come in? I'd have thought the spray is the treatment.

S1: Naloxone blocks what the opioid does and reverses the breathing problem. But the AHA's line is that nobody delays the call or CPR for it. It goes alongside. The AHA says trained rescuers, lay rescuers and members of the public can all give it. The UK guideline adds a condition, give it if you are trained, and gives no reason in the text read. The ERC says give the nasal spray, and use an injector if you're trained. Three wordings, and none of them puts naloxone before CPR.

S2: Why not spray first? It's quick.

S1: Here's the sentence the lesson calls the one to remember. The AHA says opioid antagonists, naloxone being one, are extremely unlikely to benefit an adult or child in cardiac arrest who is not receiving CPR, and they don't reverse ventricular fibrillation. Its reason is that the drug is unlikely to work when no blood is moving to carry it. And it says openly that its CPR-first order differs from the prescribing information. The leaflet in the box may say spray first; the AHA puts CPR first anyway.

S2: Say it works. They wake up.

S1: Then you stay. Naloxone wears off, and the opioid may not have. The AHA recommends adults who respond be observed in a health care setting, and the UK says follow the packaging on another dose and keep them under observation until help arrives. So you stay on the line, you watch the breathing, and if it goes slow or irregular again, it isn't normal, and you're back to CPR.

S2: The full lesson walks you through the recovery position step by step, with how the US and European versions differ, says how thin the evidence for it is, which side for a pregnant woman, the law on naloxone in both countries, and a whole section on fainting, including when a faint is an emergency call. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: And the roll, and telling normal breathing from the other kind, are things a hands-on class with a manikin and an instructor teaches better than any episode. Thanks for listening.
