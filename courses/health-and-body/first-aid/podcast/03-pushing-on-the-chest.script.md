S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in First Aid and CPR, lesson three. Pushing on the chest.

S2: Last time ended with your hands on the chest. And I think that's where most people's knowledge stops. Push hard and fast.

S1: It is, and it's where a frightened person's pushing tends to go wrong. Too shallow, too slow, too many pauses, hands in the wrong place. So this lesson gives every number behind the push. But it starts with stopping. Picture yourself a minute in, and you pause for ten seconds to wipe your eyes and look at their face. What happens to the pressure driving blood into the heart muscle?

S2: I'd guess it holds for a bit. You've built something up.

S1: It doesn't. The American Heart Association's twenty twenty five guidelines say pauses have been shown to cause an almost immediate drop in that pressure, and that the drop is associated with a lower chance of the heart restarting on its own. So a pause isn't a rest you can make up for later. That's why every guideline here says keep interruptions as few and as short as you can.

S2: Okay. The numbers.

S1: Good news here. On the adult numbers, the American Heart Association and Resuscitation Council UK say the same thing, and so do the European and international bodies. Hands: the heel of one hand on the lower half of the breastbone, in the middle of the chest, the other hand on top. The UK adds interlock your fingers, arms straight, shoulders over the chest. Depth: at least five centimetres, which is two inches, and no more than six centimetres, two point four inches. Rate: one hundred to one hundred and twenty a minute. The British Heart Foundation says try the beat of Stayin' Alive. And after every push, let the chest come all the way back up. Don't lean.

S2: Is it as exact as it sounds?

S1: The Heart Association is candid about that. It says there are plenty of observational studies but a paucity of trials on these targets, and that rate and depth are tangled together in the studies, so one can't be cleanly separated from the other. The numbers work as a set, and mostly on observational data.

S2: Where does the person need to be?

S1: Mostly where you find them. Kneel beside them on the floor. But this is where the two countries part ways, over the bed. Resuscitation Council UK's twenty twenty five guidelines say CPR works best on a firm surface, but rescuers should not move a person from a bed to the floor. Start on the bed, and push deeper if needed to make up for the mattress. The American Heart Association says a firm surface, on the back, is preferred when feasible and when it doesn't delay compressions. The Heart Association grades that one reasonable, rather than should.

S2: Why wouldn't you just move them?

S1: Because it costs time. The Heart Association's own supporting text reports a study of telephone-assisted CPR where delays from repositioning the patient happened in forty one percent of cases, most often because of the rescuer's physical limits. And St John Ambulance's public page, reviewed in April twenty twenty five, before the new UK guidelines, still says move them to the floor if it's safe for you.

S2: Give me the case.

S1: Two in the morning, in Norwich. A woman in her late seventies makes a strange noise in her sleep and goes still. Her husband is eighty, has arthritis in both knees, and did a first aid course at his bowls club in twenty nineteen, where he remembers being taught to move them onto the floor. She's in a soft double bed, and he's alone.

S2: So first he calls.

S1: Nine nine nine on speaker, since she's unresponsive, and he checks her breathing while it connects. Then the bed. Under the UK guidelines he starts on the bed and pushes deeper. But check it against the other two. The American Heart Association prefers the floor only when feasible and without delay, and for an eighty year old alone with bad knees, it's neither. St John says move her only if it's safe for you, and for him it isn't clearly safe. So all three roads end in the same place for this rescuer.

S2: How long can one person keep this up?

S1: This is the part people miss. With two or more of you, the Heart Association says it's reasonable to switch roughly every two minutes, because depth begins to fall after ninety to one hundred and twenty seconds while the rate doesn't fall significantly. So the part you can hear, the rhythm, holds up. The part that's hard to judge from above, the depth, is what goes. That's the course's reading of those two findings, but it's why the swap goes by the clock, not by how you feel.

S2: Now the fear lesson one promised numbers for. What am I doing to the person underneath me?

S1: Two cases. If they're in arrest, the largest review the course read, by Van Wijck and colleagues in twenty twenty four, pooled seventy four studies of sixteen thousand six hundred and twenty nine people. Some CPR injury in sixty percent, and rib fractures in fifty five percent. About one in two.

S2: That's a lot.

S1: It is, and it comes with limits. The review included CPR by machine, which carried more risk, and the abstract doesn't say how long CPR lasted. But the point stands. A crack under your hands is common, not a sign you did it wrong. The British Heart Foundation says keep going, to help save their life, and that a broken rib can be treated.

S2: And if they weren't in arrest at all?

S1: That's where the numbers drop. White and colleagues followed people in King County, Washington, given compressions on a dispatcher's instructions who turned out not to be in arrest. Of two hundred and forty seven with full follow-up, two percent had an injury likely or possibly from CPR, and none had an injury to an internal organ. A later review of five studies, rated very low certainty, found injuries in under one percent. The course's reading is that a short spell of compressions, stopped when the person wakes, is a different event from a full resuscitation. And Resuscitation Council UK says rescuers should not be concerned they'll cause serious injury if the person isn't in arrest.

S2: So when in doubt, push.

S1: Hard, and without stopping. And a page or a podcast can't show you what five centimetres feels like. A manikin in a hands-on class can, and that's where to learn it.
