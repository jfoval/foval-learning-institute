S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in First Aid and CPR, on the lesson called Heat, cold, bites and stings.

S2: What ties those together?

S1: They come from outside the body, and each comes with a move that feels right and isn't. Rub the cold limb. Suck out the venom. Cool the overheated runner in the ambulance on the way. Every one of those is ruled out.

S2: Start with heat.

S1: Two grades. Heat exhaustion is the body coping, badly. The NHS's signs include a headache, feeling or being sick, heavy sweating with pale, clammy skin, cramps and strong thirst, and it says it doesn't usually need emergency help if the person can cool down within thirty minutes. Heatstroke is the body failing to cope. The American Heart Association and American Red Cross twenty twenty four guideline defines it as heat illness with altered mental status, and Resuscitation Council UK says to suspect it with confusion, agitation, disorientation, seizures or unresponsiveness.

S2: Not a temperature?

S1: The European guideline adds a core temperature above forty degrees Celsius, but measuring one properly takes a rectal thermometer. So for you, the practical line is the brain. A hot person who is confused, oddly agitated, fitting or not responding has heatstroke until someone with better tools says otherwise. And the NHS adds a clock: still unwell after thirty minutes of rest, cooling and fluids is on its call list for heatstroke.

S2: So heatstroke. Call, and wait for the ambulance?

S1: Call, yes. But cooling isn't a comfort measure while you wait. It's the treatment. The US guideline says, for adults, it's reasonable to start immediate cooling by whole-body, neck-down, cool to cold water immersion, for fifteen minutes or until the neurological symptoms resolve, whichever comes first. Resuscitation Council UK calls neck-down cold water, between one and twenty six degrees Celsius, the gold standard. And the order: cool first, transfer second, and keep cooling on the way.

S2: Why water?

S1: ILCOR's review found water immersion cooled faster than the other methods it compared, like ice packs, cold showers and fanning, though the ranges overlapped too much to put them in a strict order. And speed matters: the American guideline's text says survival is related to how fast the temperature comes down. ILCOR's recommendation is a weak one, on very low certainty evidence. And the fifteen minutes is a rule for when nobody can measure, not a claim that fifteen is always enough. ILCOR says the right time isn't known.

S2: Say an older man in a stifling flat, who can't stand. What if he can't get into a tub?

S1: That's the other kind, classic heatstroke, which the European guideline says typically comes after long exposure to the sun, often in heat waves, in people whose temperature control is weaker, such as older people or children. ILCOR found no evidence comparing cooling methods in them, and makes no recommendation. That doesn't mean cooling doesn't matter, only that nobody has compared the methods. Resuscitation Council UK says, for any suspected heatstroke, use whatever cooling is to hand: ice sheets, ice packs, a fan, a cold shower. The NHS says wrap them in a cool, wet sheet, or fan them or sponge them down with cold water while you wait for help. The course's reading is that you don't wrestle someone who can't stand into a bath. And no drink while he's confused: nothing by mouth unless fully awake and able to swallow.

S2: Now cold.

S1: The NHS defines hypothermia as body temperature below thirty five degrees Celsius. The signs include shivering, pale, cold, dry skin, blue or grey skin and lips, slurred speech, slow breathing, and tiredness or confusion. And the course reads this from the US guideline's table of stages: a person who has stopped shivering is not necessarily getting better.

S2: What do I do?

S1: Stop the heat loss: somewhere warm or sheltered, wet clothes off, blankets, the head covered. In the US, call if they're less responsive, mumbling or confused, among other signs. In the UK, the NHS says A and E or nine nine nine for suspected hypothermia, and St John Ambulance says never leave them alone. Food or drink only if they're alert and able to swallow, and in the US for cold stress or mild hypothermia only; the NHS says fully awake. No alcohol. And the US table says handle a moderately hypothermic person gently.

S2: And the move that feels right and isn't?

S1: Rubbing their arms and legs, or putting heat on them. The American guideline grades it harmful, and the NHS says don't rub them either. The American guideline's text describes a simulation study: heating the limbs sends their cold blood back to the centre of the body, which can cool the core, and the core is what needs warming. A warm bath or shower is graded harmful too once responsiveness is reduced; the NHS says no hot bath at all.

S2: And if they stop responding?

S1: Breathing normally, quietly and regularly: the recovery position, blankets over and under, and keep watching. Not breathing normally: the call and CPR. Slow breathing is one of the NHS's signs of hypothermia, and Resuscitation Council UK counts slow, laboured breathing in an unresponsive person as a sign of cardiac arrest, so the call handler helps you decide. The European guide for the public says a person with hypothermia in cardiac arrest should get continuous CPR until warming has been re-established. And the American guidance warns that severe hypothermia can produce findings that mimic death. So cold and stiff is not a reason for you to stop. Keep going until the crew take over.

S2: Last, snakebite.

S1: Both countries rule out the folk remedies. Call. Keep them calm and at rest, the bitten limb still. Take off rings, watches and anything tight. The US guideline grades ice as no benefit, and suction, tourniquets, electric shock and pressure bandages as harmful. Resuscitation Council UK: no pressure dressing, ice, heat or tourniquet, no cutting, and never suck.

S2: I've heard a tight bandage is the right first aid for a snakebite.

S1: For some snakes it is. The European guideline says bites from elapids, a family with venom that attacks the nerves, may benefit from pressure immobilisation, but viper bites need keeping still without compression, and it names the adder as a viper. Resuscitation Council UK calls the adder the UK's only native highly venomous snake. In North America more than ninety five per cent of venomous bites are from pit vipers: rattlesnakes, copperheads and cottonmouths. Neither country's guidance uses the bandage, and the course doesn't teach it.
