S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in First Aid and CPR, lesson eight. Choking.

S2: The one most likely to happen across the dinner table.

S1: The American Heart Association's 2025 adult guideline calls choking one of the leading causes of accidental death in the United States, with over five thousand cases in 2021, and says prompt action by bystanders has been associated with better survival. And what you do turns on one question first. Does the cough still work?

S2: Why the cough?

S1: Because it tells you whether the blockage is partial or complete. The guidelines call it mild or severe. The AHA's paediatric guideline puts the test in one line: mild is coughing and making sounds; severe is can't make sounds. For adults, severe means a weak or absent cough, not being able to speak, a change in colour. The American Red Cross describes that as going pale or bluish. Resuscitation Council UK's cue is someone suddenly unable to speak or cough, particularly while eating. Its first step is to ask, are you choking?

S2: So picture a friend coughing hard on a crisp. Red face, eyes streaming, loud coughs. Somebody moves in to slap him on the back.

S1: The lesson says no. He's coughing and making sounds. That's mild, in both countries, and both say let the cough do the work. The AHA's text says coughing can produce high airway pressures. Stand beside him, tell him to keep coughing, and watch, because a mild obstruction can quickly become a severe one.

S2: Now the bad one. No sound at all.

S1: Here's what changed in 2025. The AHA's adult guideline now says: repeated cycles of five back blows followed by five abdominal thrusts, until the object comes out or the person stops responding. The AHA's own announcement says adult choking wasn't in its previous guidelines, and earlier guidance for children called for abdominal thrusts only. Abdominal thrusts are the squeeze from behind that a lot of people call the Heimlich manoeuvre.

S2: And the UK?

S1: The UK was already back blows first, and its 2025 guideline keeps it. Encourage the cough. If the cough fails, up to five back blows. If those fail, up to five abdominal thrusts. So for a conscious adult the two countries now teach one sequence. "Up to five" spells out that you stop the moment it clears.

S2: Is there any difference left?

S1: When to call. The UK's written steps put the 999 call after one round of blows and thrusts has failed. But its own flowchart shows the call as soon as the cough is ineffective, and its key points say call for help early, ideally on speakerphone, especially if alone. The AHA wants the emergency number called for any severe obstruction. The course teaches the earlier moment, the one all three share. If someone else is there, they call as you start the back blows. If you're alone, the phone goes on speaker beside you and you carry on.

S2: Why back blows first? The Heimlich is the famous one.

S1: The AHA starts its answer with what it doesn't have: there are no randomised trials comparing the different interventions for choking. Then it gives three things. A cohort study of seven hundred and nine people where back blows were associated with more relief and fewer injuries than abdominal thrusts. Case reports of fatal injuries from abdominal thrusts. And consistency with the infant and child guidelines, which use the same five and five. The AHA grades the order in its top class, but the lesson reads that grade in two halves: the panel's confidence is high, and the evidence underneath is one cohort study, case reports, and a wish for one sequence at every age. ILCOR's older grade for it is weak, on very low certainty evidence. Neither says the order is proven.

S2: And when the food flies out, they're fine?

S1: They should still be checked. Resuscitation Council UK says anyone treated for choking with abdominal thrusts or chest compressions should be evaluated by a healthcare practitioner, because complications and injuries may occur.

S2: And if nothing works?

S1: Eventually they stop responding. Then both countries say the same thing: start CPR straight away. Neither asks you to check the breathing first. A choking person who has collapsed may still snore or gasp, and neither is normal breathing. The AHA says to start compressions whether or not there's a pulse, and that compressions can provide enough airway pressure to push the object out. And make sure the emergency call has been made.

S2: Can I try to get the food out of their mouth?

S1: One rule each way. If you can see it, take it out when you open the airway for breaths. But never feel for it. A blind finger sweep, hooking a finger in without seeing anything, can push the object further in, the NHS warns. The AHA grades it as harmful, and the UK says do not.

S2: What about children?

S1: A child over one gets the adult sequence, back blows then abdominal thrusts. The UK's stop rule: stop at once at any sign it's clearing, coughing, loud breathing or crying. And the British Red Cross says reduce the force of the back blows for a smaller child.

S2: And a baby?

S1: A baby is where it changes most. Five back blows alternating with five chest thrusts, in both countries. Never abdominal thrusts on a baby. The AHA says they're not recommended because of the risk of injuring the organs in the abdomen. For the back blows, lay the baby face down along your forearm, resting on your thigh, support the head, keep the head lower than the body, and give sharp blows between the shoulder blades. Then turn the baby onto their back for the chest thrusts.

S2: With two fingers, like I learned?

S1: This is where the sources disagree three ways. The AHA's 2025 guideline now recommends the heel of one hand, because research suggests it pushes deeper than two fingers. Resuscitation Council UK uses the two-thumb encircling technique from lesson six, pushed more sharply. And some public pages, the NHS, the British and the American Red Cross, still show two fingers. Neither guideline teaches two fingers any more, and when a page and your country's guideline disagree, follow the guideline.

S2: And the old advice to hold a baby upside down?

S1: The British Red Cross answers that one: it isn't effective, and you might drop the baby. And afterwards, St John Ambulance says every baby given chest thrusts should be seen in hospital. The NHS says get medical help even if the object has come out.

It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: And how hard a back blow lands, or how to hold a baby head down, is what a hands-on class with a manikin and an instructor teaches. Thanks for listening.
