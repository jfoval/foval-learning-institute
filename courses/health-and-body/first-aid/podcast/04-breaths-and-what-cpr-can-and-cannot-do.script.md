S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in First Aid and CPR, lesson four. Breaths, and what CPR can and cannot do.

S1: It answers the two questions almost everyone brings to a CPR class. The first is, do I have to do mouth-to-mouth? Often asked by someone who'd rather not.

S2: Guilty. Do I?

S1: It depends who you are. For an adult, compressions come first in both countries. After that, the American Heart Association's twenty twenty five guidelines say all lay rescuers should give chest compressions, and for someone trained, adding breaths is reasonable. Resuscitation Council UK says if you're not trained to give rescue breaths, do continuous compressions without interruptions, and if you are, thirty compressions to two breaths. And in both, the call handler gives an untrained caller compression-only instructions for an adult.

S2: So nobody tells an untrained person to try breaths anyway.

S1: Neither country does, and neither makes breaths a condition of doing CPR. The Heart Association says compression-only is appropriate if lay rescuers are untrained or unwilling.

S2: And if I'm trained. How do I give one?

S1: The American Red Cross and St John Ambulance say nearly the same thing. Tilt the head back and lift the chin, pinch the nose, take a normal breath, seal your mouth over theirs, and blow steadily for about a second, until the chest rises. Then let it fall. Two breaths, and straight back to thirty compressions. Resuscitation Council UK says just enough air to make the chest start to rise, and avoid too much.

S2: Why does too much matter?

S1: The Heart Association says excessive ventilation can push air into the stomach, bring its contents back up to be breathed into the lungs, and lower the blood the compressions are moving. So when the British Heart Foundation's public page says blow hard for a second, the lesson flags it. The guidelines don't say hard. If you learned blow hard, replace it with until the chest rises. And if the chest doesn't rise after two attempts, you don't keep trying. Go back to compressions.

S2: Now the evidence. This is the lesson that teaches you to read the labels, isn't it?

S1: It is, and here's the one idea to keep. Most first aid recommendations are weak, on low or very low certainty evidence. That usually describes a choice between two ways of acting that the studies can't separate well, while the finding underneath is that acting beats not acting. Take the international committee, ILCOR, on breaths. It suggests that bystanders who are trained, able and willing give compressions with rescue breaths for adults. A weak recommendation, very low certainty.

S2: Trained, able, and willing. All three?

S1: All three. If you're trained but can't get a seal, or you just can't bring yourself to with a stranger, the line doesn't apply to you. And read it next to the sentence just above it, which says compressions for all adults in cardiac arrest.

S2: So who's right? Compressions only, or breaths?

S1: This is a contested question, and the lesson gives both cases. The case for compressions alone starts with randomised trials of what call handlers say. In one, thirty day survival was eight point seven percent with compression-only instructions and seven percent with standard CPR, a difference that could easily have been chance. Another was twelve and a half percent against eleven, for survival to leaving hospital. Pooled together, three trials came out at fourteen percent against twelve, in favour of compression-only instructions. Then the argument about people: mouth-to-mouth is a barrier to bystanders starting at all. When Japan spread compression-only CPR nationally, the share of people getting any bystander CPR rose from thirty four point six percent to forty seven point three. That's an association over time.

S2: And the case for breaths?

S1: The American Heart Association makes it. Numerous observational studies and one large secondary analysis of a trial found better outcomes with compressions and breaths together. And it adds a point about who arrests now. The call handler trials largely predate the rise in overdose-related arrests over the last decade, and compression-only may do less well in some kinds of arrest, opioid overdose among them.

S2: So where does that leave me?

S1: The guidelines split the answer by who the rescuer is, which resolves it without deciding it. Call handlers give untrained callers compression-only instructions. Trained, able, willing rescuers add breaths at thirty to two. And both are far better than no CPR, which is the one part that isn't contested.

S2: Now the second question. The one people ask more quietly. If I do it right, will they live?

S1: Usually not, but far more often than if you do nothing. Start with where the survival figure comes from. In Sweden, Hasselqvist-Ax and colleagues looked at thirty thousand three hundred and eighty one witnessed arrests. Thirty day survival was ten and a half percent when someone gave CPR before the ambulance came, and four percent when nobody did. After adjusting for the differences they measured, the authors put survival with CPR at more than twice as high.

S2: So CPR doubles your chances.

S1: That's the step the lesson won't take. It's a registry. Nobody decided at random who got CPR, and adjustment can't account for differences nobody measured. That's why the authors say associated. Registries in several countries all point the same way, and every guideline acts on it. What they can't give you is the exact size of the effect for any one person.

S2: And on television everyone seems to wake up.

S1: Two studies counted. In the nineteen nineties, one found sixty seven percent appeared to survive to hospital discharge. A later one, with Grey's Anatomy and House, sixty nine point six percent. In real life, in the US in twenty twenty five, ten point five percent survived to leave hospital, and in England in twenty twenty four, nine point five percent were alive at thirty days. Those two are counted differently, so don't compare them closely. Both are roughly one in ten.

S2: That's sobering.

S1: The British Red Cross says the chance of restarting the heart with compressions alone is very small. Usually a heart needs a shock from a defibrillator. What compressions do is pump a small amount of blood to keep the organs alive, most importantly the brain. CPR keeps the chance alive until a defibrillator or a paramedic can use it.

S2: And a breath is harder to learn from a page than a push.

S1: Much harder. The seal, the tilt, just enough. Those are things you feel. A hands-on class, with a manikin that shows the chest rising, is where it comes together.
