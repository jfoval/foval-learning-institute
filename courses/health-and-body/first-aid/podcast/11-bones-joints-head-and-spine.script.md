S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in First Aid and CPR, on the lesson called Bones, joints, head and spine.

S2: The wrist after a fall off a skateboard. The ankle on a kerb.

S1: Some of the commonest injuries a bystander meets, and most aren't emergencies. A few are, and the lesson's point is that the difference often doesn't show in the first minute. It's also the lesson where the evidence is thinnest. When the American Heart Association and American Red Cross wrote their twenty twenty-four first aid guideline, the writing group searched for studies of first aid for fractures and found none. So every fracture recommendation in it is expert opinion.

S2: Does that make it optional?

S1: No. The grade tells you what the advice rests on, not that it's wrong. Bleeding first: if the bone has come through the skin and it's bleeding badly, that's lesson nine's pressure, and a tourniquet if pressure fails. Then keep it still and supported, in the position you found it. Don't straighten it unless that's needed to move the person safely. The guideline's supporting text weighs it both ways: straightening might ease pain, but it risks nerves and blood vessels, can turn a closed fracture into an open one, and no study was found that tested whether first aiders can do it safely.

S2: When is a broken bone an emergency call?

S1: The American guideline: if the limb is blue, purple or pale, call immediately, because that colour may mean it isn't getting enough blood. In the UK, St John Ambulance says nine nine nine or one one two for an open fracture, a long bone, or a suspected broken back, neck or pelvis, and the NHS says nine nine nine or A and E for a numb or tingling arm, a bone through the skin, or an arm at an odd angle. The NHS adds, don't eat or drink, in case the bone needs surgery.

S2: And a sprain? Ice, surely.

S1: Every guideline and first aid page in the lesson uses cold, wrapped, never straight on the skin. Where they differ is how long. The American guideline says twenty to thirty minutes. The American Red Cross page, the NHS and St John, up to twenty. And the British Red Cross says no more than ten, and that anything over ten can cause further damage. Follow the figure your own class uses. And the American guideline's supporting text is honest about what cold does: it eases pain and swelling early, but it hasn't been shown to improve function or time to recovery. Ice is for comfort.

S2: Heads next. How do I know it's concussion?

S1: Often you can't. The European guideline says there's no agreed definition, and the American guideline says there's no validated scoring system for identifying it in first aid. So both countries skip the diagnosis and act on the suspicion. The American guideline says someone with signs of concussion is removed from play at once and doesn't go back until a health care professional has seen them. Resuscitation Council UK says remove them from physical activity and refer them on. The American line has the strongest evidence grade in the whole lesson.

S2: And when does a head injury need an ambulance?

S1: The American guideline lists signs: being knocked out, a worsening headache, vomiting, confusion, a seizure, changes in vision, swelling or a dent in the scalp. The UK lists also count how it happened. The NHS says nine nine nine for someone who was knocked out and hasn't woken, can't stay awake, has a seizure, or fell more than a metre or five stairs, among others.

S2: Is vomiting treated the same way?

S1: That's where the routes part. Vomiting is on the American emergency lists and the British Red Cross's nine nine nine list. The NHS page sends it to one one one, and the national guidance behind that page then tells one one one to send the person to an emergency department. And the old rule to keep someone awake all night? For a minor head injury looked after at home, the NHS says they don't need to stay awake if they're tired. That's for someone already judged not to need hospital. Someone who can't stay awake is on the nine nine nine list.

S2: The neck is the one that frightens me.

S1: Resuscitation Council UK says suspect a neck injury after a fall or dive from a height, a crush, or a road or sporting accident. If the person is awake, the countries agree on stillness. The American guideline says have them stay as still as possible and call. Resuscitation Council UK says minimise movement, and if they're awake and alert, encourage them to hold their own neck in a comfortable, stable position. And never force someone into a position they won't take.

S2: No collar?

S1: No collar. For decades the answer was a rigid collar and a long board. The American guideline now recommends against their routine use by first aiders and grades that as harmful. The international body, ILCOR, suggests against collars too. Its review found collars do reduce neck movement, but impair breathing and swallowing and raise the pressure inside the skull. The American guideline's background calls the old practice driven more by legal considerations and unproven theories than by evidence, which is the American guideline's judgement of its own field's history. But the lesson sets that beside ILCOR's caution: most of its studies used healthy volunteers or cadavers, and it wants a formal review before changing its recommendation. The people who fitted collars were following the guidance of their time.

S2: So I never move them?

S1: That's the other myth. You move them for danger. The airway comes before keeping the neck in line; Resuscitation Council UK says so. And anyone who needs CPR goes on their back.

S2: And if they're lying still after a crash and won't respond?

S1: The call, in the order lesson two gave for your country, on speaker. And check their breathing. Normal breathing is quiet and regular at an ordinary pace. Slow, laboured breathing, gasping, snoring or gurgling isn't normal. Not breathing normally, or you're in doubt, and it's CPR on their back, whatever the neck. If they are breathing normally, in the UK you leave them on their back and watch their breathing; the European and UK guidelines of twenty twenty-five say don't put an injured person in the recovery position. In the US there's no graded line for this, and the call handler decides.

It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.
