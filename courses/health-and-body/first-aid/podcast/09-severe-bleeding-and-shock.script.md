S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in First Aid and CPR, on the lesson called Severe bleeding and shock.

S2: Which sounds like the one where people freeze.

S1: And its first step is the simplest thing in the course. Your hands on the wound. The American Heart Association and American Red Cross, in their twenty twenty-four first aid guideline, say uncontrolled bleeding is the most important preventable cause of death in thirty-five per cent of trauma patients, and that it can happen before the ambulance arrives. The American College of Surgeons, which runs the Stop the Bleed programme, warns that a bleeding emergency can claim a life in three to five minutes.

S2: So how do I tell the kind that kills from the kind that just looks awful?

S1: The American guideline gives the signs. Blood pooling on the ground. Blood flowing fast or spurting from the wound. Bleeding that carries on despite direct pressure. Or bleeding that brings on symptoms like drowsiness, dizziness, chest pain or passing out. The American Red Cross adds a yardstick: about half of what a soda can holds may be life-threatening, and less in a small child or a baby. And St John Ambulance, in the UK, has the test I find most useful. A severe bleed is likely to slow with pressure. A life-threatening one won't.

S2: Okay. What do I actually do first?

S1: The call. Resuscitation Council UK's twenty twenty-five first aid guideline opens its bleeding section with call nine nine nine. In the US it's nine one one. Put the phone on speaker, because your hands are about to be busy. Gloves if you have them; if not, both Red Crosses suggest a plastic bag over your hand, or asking the person to press with their own. Then press. The American guideline, Resuscitation Council UK and the international body, ILCOR, all start there. A dressing or a clean pad on the wound, pressed firmly, straight down, and you keep pressing.

S2: So when has pressure failed?

S1: A soaked pad on a wound that's slowing hasn't failed; add to it or swap it, by your country's page, and keep pressing. Failure is bleeding that carries on however hard you press. Then, on an arm or a leg, a tourniquet. That's a strap round the limb above the wound, tightened until the bleeding stops. The commercial kind has a rod you twist, called a windlass. The American guideline says, for life-threatening bleeding from a limb, apply one and tighten it until the bleeding stops. Resuscitation Council UK says apply one as soon as possible when pressure hasn't controlled it.

S2: Where exactly does it go?

S1: Between the wound and the trunk. In the UK, Resuscitation Council UK says five to seven centimetres above the injury. In the US, Stop the Bleed's course says two to three inches; the guideline itself gives no distance. And never over a joint, not on the elbow or the knee. The lesson carries it as one picture, and says plainly this is the course's summary, not any guideline's wording: a hand's width or less above the wound, never on a joint.

S2: I've heard they really hurt.

S1: They do, and the sources say so. Stop the Bleed says flatly that tourniquets hurt. St John says to warn the person before you tighten it. None of them treats the pain as a reason to loosen. Then two rules. Write down the time it went on. And never loosen it or take it off. Resuscitation Council UK says only a healthcare professional releases it, and St John gives the reason: loosen it and the bleeding starts again.

S2: But the old worry was that a tourniquet costs you the limb.

S1: That's a century of teaching, and the lesson traces it through Stephen Richey's review from two thousand and seven. The tourniquet's bad name came from real harm, in armies that left the wounded for days, and from the fixes that followed: taking tourniquets off, then loosening them every thirty minutes, both of which let the bleeding start again. The newer evidence points the other way. Surgeons at a combat hospital in Baghdad reported that no amputations resulted solely from tourniquet use. But the course read only that study's abstract, every tourniquet number the lesson gives is observational, none comes from a randomised trial, and ILCOR rates the certainty very low. A consistent pattern of associations, not proof.

S2: What about a wound where a tourniquet can't go? The neck, say.

S1: The neck, an armpit, the groin. There's nowhere between the wound and the trunk. So you pack it. Stop the Bleed says pack gauze tightly into a deep wound until it stops the bleeding, then hold pressure until help arrives. Resuscitation Council UK builds packing into its first step, and says any clean material will do if you have no dressing.

S2: Then the lesson turns to shock. Which I'm guessing isn't a fright.

S1: No. In first aid it means the circulation failing, not enough oxygen reaching the brain and the vital organs. You'd see pale, cold, clammy skin, sweating, fast shallow breathing, grey-blue lips, nausea, restlessness, thirst. What you do: treat the bleeding, lay them down, keep them at the right temperature, and reassure them. And nothing to eat or drink. The American Red Cross says so, because it raises the risk of vomiting and breathing the vomit in. Thirst is a sign, not something to fix with a drink.

S2: And the legs? Everyone says raise the legs.

S1: That's the one real difference between the countries. In the US, the twenty twenty-four guideline says raising the feet may be reasonable only where there's no trauma or injury. So after a car hits someone, the feet stay down. In the UK, the St John and British Red Cross pages raise them, and St John makes an exception if a leg may be broken or the hip or pelvis is injured. It's a contested question. The American guideline says its evidence for the effect comes from outside first aid and the gains were temporary and inconsistent; Resuscitation Council UK has no section on it; and ILCOR left it out of its twenty twenty-five review. Follow your country's guidance and the call handler.

S2: And if they stop responding?

S1: Check their breathing. Normal breathing is quiet and regular. If it's slow, laboured, gasping, or you aren't sure, treat it as cardiac arrest. Onto their back, start CPR, and tell the call handler. And a tourniquet that's on stays on.

It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.
