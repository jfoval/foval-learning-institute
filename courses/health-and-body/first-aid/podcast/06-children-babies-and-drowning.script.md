S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in First Aid and CPR, lesson six. Children, babies and drowning.

S2: Everything up to now has been an adult on the floor.

S1: And a child's arrest usually starts differently. Resuscitation Council UK's 2025 guideline says a child's cardiac arrest is most often the end of a breathing or circulation problem that has been getting worse, not often a primary problem with the heart. The American Heart Association says the same: breathing problems remain the major cause in infants and children.

S2: Why does that change what I do?

S1: Remember why adults get compressions first. Most adult arrests begin with blood still full of oxygen, so pushing moves oxygen that's already there. On this course's reading of the AHA's word asphyxial, a child whose breathing failed first has used up much of that oxygen before the heart stops.

S2: Is there evidence breaths actually matter for children?

S1: The study the course leans on is a Japanese registry, Kitamura and colleagues in The Lancet in 2010, and the course read the abstract only. Among children aged one to seventeen whose arrest had a non-cardiac cause, a good neurological outcome a month later was recorded in seven point two percent of those given CPR with breaths, and one point six percent of those given compressions only. Where the cause was cardiac, the two did about equally well. And any bystander CPR beat none.

S2: So compressions alone still went with far better outcomes than standing back.

S1: Far better. And it's an association, not a trial. The children compared were given CPR by bystanders who chose what to give, so on this course's reading the difference could include who those bystanders were. But both countries land in the same place. The AHA says infants and children in cardiac arrest should get compressions with breaths, and compression-only is reasonable if a lay rescuer can't or won't give breaths. UK call handlers are told to encourage breaths and compressions in children of all ages, and to fall back to compressions only if the caller isn't willing or able.

S2: So the two countries agree children need breaths. Where do they differ?

S1: On what comes first. Picture a child or baby who doesn't respond and isn't breathing normally, with the emergency number on speaker. In the US, the AHA's 2025 guideline says it may be reasonable to start with compressions, then the airway and breaths. In the UK, under Resuscitation Council UK, you start with five rescue breaths, then compressions.

S2: And the ratio?

S1: The AHA gives a lone rescuer thirty to two, and two rescuers fifteen to two. Resuscitation Council UK gives fifteen to two only to people specifically trained in paediatric life support, and thirty to two to everyone else. A UK call handler talking an untrained caller through it coaches thirty to two with five breaths first.

S2: Two respected bodies, opposite first steps. What's each one's reason?

S1: The AHA gives two. One child study found starting with compressions delayed the first breath by less than six seconds. And compressions first keeps one approach for adults and children, which it says simplifies training. It opens that sentence with its own caveat: the supporting evidence is minimal. The UK reasons from the cause. Its guideline says the differences between adult and child sequences are based on the distinct causes of arrest. It doesn't spell out the step to five breaths; on this course's reading, the breaths answer an arrest that began short of oxygen.

S2: And the course doesn't pick.

S1: It doesn't. And if you only ever learned adult CPR, Resuscitation Council UK is blunt: it's far better to use the adult sequence on a child than to do nothing. Follow the call handler in the country you're in.

S2: How hard do you push on a child? That's the part people are frightened of.

S1: Both countries say a hundred to a hundred and twenty a minute, and at least one third of the front-to-back depth of the chest. The AHA puts round figures on it: about one and a half inches, four centimetres, for a baby, and about two inches, five centimetres, for a child. Resuscitation Council UK adds a ceiling for every age: never more than six centimetres. And the AHA cites three studies of body measurements showing a child's chest can be compressed to a third of its depth without damaging the organs inside.

S2: And for a baby, I learned two fingers.

S1: So did a lot of people, and the AHA removed it in 2025, because two fingers was ineffective at reaching the proper depth. In a registry of real infants, when two fingers was used, no stretch of compressions met the guideline. What both countries teach now is the two-thumb technique: both hands round the baby's chest, fingers on the back, two thumbs on the lower half of the breastbone. The AHA also allows the heel of one hand as an equal alternative, and says to use it if you can't get your hands round. Resuscitation Council UK gives the thumbs only. What neither teaches any more is two fingers.

S2: And drowning. That's where I'd panic.

S1: First rule: don't become the second casualty. Resuscitation Council UK says not to go into the water unless you're trained to rescue someone from it; reach from land, or get something that floats to them. Once they're out, the arrest is usually from lack of oxygen, in adults as well as children. The AHA says resuscitation after drowning must focus on restoring breathing as much as circulation, and it recommends CPR with breaths before the AED goes on. Shockable rhythms are a minority after drowning, so running for the machine first is usually the wrong trade.

S2: Breaths first or compressions first, though?

S1: That depends on who you are. ILCOR recommends compressions first for lay people with a drowned adult, for simplicity and a faster start, and says those trained and with a duty to respond, such as lifeguards, should consider breaths first. In the UK, trained first aiders and lifeguards give five rescue breaths, then CPR, and for a drowned child the call handler coaches five breaths first, as for any child. In the US, CPR with breaths should be given, compressions only is reasonable for an adult if you can't give breaths, and a trained rescuer may reasonably start with breaths. The AHA says there's no direct evidence on the order; the ERC's public guide says the evidence shows a better outlook with breaths first, evidence this course hasn't read. Neither is called wrong.
