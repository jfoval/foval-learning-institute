S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in First Aid and CPR, lesson two. Danger, the call, and recognising cardiac arrest.

S2: Last time ended in a kitchen, with someone's father on the floor, and the answer to what do I do first was: call, on speaker.

S1: And this lesson is the next sixty seconds. So let me give the minimum first, for anyone who takes nothing else away. If someone collapses and won't respond, call your emergency number with the phone on speaker. If they aren't breathing normally, or you aren't sure, push hard and fast in the centre of the chest. And don't stop until the ambulance crew are there and able to take over. Resuscitation Council UK's public version says it in three words. Check, call, CPR.

S2: Check what, first?

S1: That it's safe for you. St John Ambulance puts it in one line: before approaching the casualty, always make sure the area is safe. A rescuer who gets hit by a car turns one casualty into two and leaves nobody to call. Then you check whether they respond. Kneel, gently shake their shoulders, ask are you OK. The reaction has to be to you. Open eyes or a groan that has nothing to do with what you said doesn't count.

S2: Okay, they don't respond. Now I call?

S1: That's the one real difference in the first minute, and the lesson sets the two side by side. In the UK and Europe, since the twenty twenty five guidelines, you call nine nine nine, or one one two, for anyone unresponsive, and you check the breathing while the call connects. Resuscitation Council UK says rescuers no longer need to confirm abnormal breathing before calling.

S2: Why change that?

S1: The European Resuscitation Council's public guide says it plainly. Bystanders don't always recognise cardiac arrest. Most often it's the operator on the phone who does. On that reasoning, the breathing check you'd have done alone and frightened is one a trained person does better with you on the line.

S2: And the US?

S1: The American Heart Association's twenty twenty five guidelines keep recognition first. If an adult is unresponsive, with absent or abnormal breathing, meaning only gasping, the lay rescuer should assume cardiac arrest. Then a lone rescuer with a mobile phone calls nine one one and starts CPR at once. Both of those carry their strongest class of recommendation. And the Heart Association says openly that the order of call and CPR hasn't been shown to change survival. It recommends calling first for the practical reasons: dispatch gets moving, and someone can coach you.

S2: So which one's right?

S1: Neither is wrong. With a phone in your pocket, both put you on speaker to a trained call handler within seconds, and both start compressions the moment the person is judged not to be breathing normally. The difference is whether the breathing check comes a few seconds before you dial or a few seconds after. Follow the country you're in, because that's the order your call handler and your class will use.

S2: You keep saying speaker.

S1: Because CPR takes both hands, locked together on the chest, and a phone held to your ear takes one of them. Without the speaker you choose between talking to the one person who can coach you and doing the one thing that keeps blood moving. Put the phone by the person's head. And the call handler is a coach, not a switchboard. Resuscitation Council UK says they should give CPR instructions to all callers once arrest is recognised, and assume you don't know how. Every body recommends telephone instructions strongly, even though the international committee, ILCOR, rates the evidence behind them as very low certainty. And don't hang up until you're told to.

S2: Now the hard part. How do I know it's cardiac arrest?

S1: Two signs, in both countries. Unresponsive, and not breathing normally. The second is where people go wrong. The Heart Association defines agonal breathing as slow, irregular gasping that doesn't move useful air, and it puts it at up to forty to sixty percent of cardiac arrests outside hospital. The Heart Association's words for it include snoring and gasping. The European guide lists gasping, moaning, gurgling, groaning, and then says, this is not breathing.

S2: So not breathing normally is wider than not breathing.

S1: Much wider. Slow breathing, snoring, gasping, in someone who won't respond, you treat as arrest. And if you're unsure, the call handler helps you decide, which is the whole point of having called.

S2: Give me a case where this goes wrong.

S1: Five-a-side football, a Thursday evening. A man in his forties has been running hard for twenty minutes. He sits down, falls sideways. His arms and legs jerk for a few seconds, then stop, and he makes loud, slow gasps. His eyes are open. One friend says he's having a fit. Another says he's breathing, get him on his side. They roll him into the recovery position, and somebody goes to find his phone in his bag to see if there's anyone to ring.

S2: That sounds like what I'd do.

S1: Which is why the lesson uses it. Resuscitation Council UK says exercise is a common precipitant of cardiac arrest, and that a short burst of seizure-like movement can be how an arrest begins. So the friend who said fit described it correctly and drew the wrong conclusion. The rule is to check breathing once the movement stops. The loud, slow gasps are agonal breathing. The open eyes: the European guide says people who arrest during vigorous exercise often have their eyes open. And the recovery position is wrong here. Resuscitation Council UK's first aid guidelines say not to use it when breathing is agonal, and the Heart Association says you can't give effective CPR to someone on their side.

S2: And nobody called.

S1: The one act that brings a coach, an ambulance and maybe a defibrillator. What should have happened: someone calls on speaker, he goes onto his back, someone pushes, someone else fetches a defibrillator. And in twenty twenty five the UK guidelines added that athletes early in arrest may show near-normal or panting breathing. Fast breathing in someone who then sits up and talks to you is a different matter.

S2: And if I'm still not sure?

S1: Resuscitation Council UK: if there is any doubt, assume cardiac arrest and start CPR. The Heart Association gives the reason. The benefit for someone in arrest far outweighs any risk of compressions on someone who isn't.

S2: None of this makes you able to do CPR, though.

S1: No. An episode can give you the order. Your hands learn the rest in a class with a manikin and an instructor.
