S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in First Aid and CPR, on the lesson called Burns, cuts and small injuries.

S2: The everyday stuff, then.

S1: Mostly things that won't kill anyone. But the lesson makes the point that what you do in the first minutes still decides a lot. How deep a burn goes, whether a tooth can go back in. So, burns first. On the first step every source with burns advice agrees: cool it with running water, and start now. The American Heart Association and American Red Cross, in their twenty twenty-four first aid guideline, say cool it immediately, preferably with clean running water. In the UK, the NHS, the British Burn Association, St John Ambulance and the British Red Cross all start with cool running water, at once.

S2: For how long? I've heard ten minutes, I've heard twenty.

S1: And here's the interesting part. The international body, ILCOR, compared burns cooled for twenty minutes or more with other lengths of time, and no difference in outcomes could be demonstrated. So it says a specific duration can't be recommended. That left the two countries free to choose, and they chose differently. In the US, the guideline says it may be reasonable to cool for five to twenty minutes, and the American Red Cross page says the same. In the UK, the NHS says twenty minutes under cool running water, as soon as possible, within three hours. St John and the British Red Cross say at least twenty.

S2: So which is right?

S1: The lesson's answer is that neither side is wrong on the evidence ILCOR found, because ILCOR found nothing to settle it. What differs is whether shorter counts as enough. Follow your own country's figure, and both rule out skipping the cooling.

S2: Twenty minutes of cool water on a small child sounds like a lot.

S1: That's the catch. Water on a burn can chill the whole person, and small children chill fastest. The American guideline says to watch children who haven't yet reached adolescence being cooled, for signs of hypothermia, because they have more surface area for their size than adolescents and adults. The British Burn Association has the line to remember: cool the burn but warm the patient. Cover the parts that aren't burned. And the British Red Cross says aim the shower at the burn, not the whole body, and avoid a cold bath.

S2: And after the water?

S1: Rings, watches and anything tight come off early, before the area swells, because burned tissue swells and a ring can cut off the blood supply. Leave anything that's stuck to the burn. Then cover it, and the countries use different things. In the US, for intact skin or an intact blister while you wait for a professional, a clean cloth or a dry dressing that won't stick, laid on loosely. In the UK, cling film laid over the burn, not wrapped round it, because the burned area needs room to swell. No sticking plasters, don't burst blisters, and nothing greasy. The British Red Cross is blunt: butter, cream or toothpaste will not cool the area.

S2: How do I know if it needs a doctor?

S1: The American guideline sorts by depth. A superficial burn is red and painful, with no blistering and no broken skin. So by its own definition, a burn that has blistered isn't superficial. It sends to a health care professional promptly any full-thickness burn, and a partial-thickness one larger than the person's palm or on the face, hands, feet or genitals, and says call the emergency services for signs of smoke inhalation, like singed nose hairs or soot round the nose or mouth. In the UK, the NHS says call nine nine nine or go to A and E for a burn that's very large or deep, on the face, genitals or bottom, or from an acid, a chemical or electricity, and ring one one one if you're not sure.

S2: You mentioned chemicals.

S1: A chemical keeps burning while it's on the skin, so the job is to get it off fast without getting it on you. Gloves first. Brush off anything dry, take off contaminated clothing, then water, a lot of it. The American guideline says at least fifteen minutes, unless local or chemical-specific advice says otherwise. The NHS says about an hour, and the British Burn Association at least twenty. Some chemicals are best not washed with water at all, so tell the call handler what the chemical is. And unless you're trained, St John says, never try to neutralise it; the British Burn Association says that can give off heat and add to the damage.

S2: And electricity?

S1: The power goes off before you touch anyone. Then cool it. And don't judge it by its size. The American Red Cross says even a small electrical burn can mean significant internal injuries, and to be ready for CPR and a defibrillator. The NHS sends every electrical burn to nine nine nine or A and E.

S2: Let's do the small ones. A nosebleed. I was taught head back.

S1: That's the old habit. The American guideline, the NHS and St John all say forward instead. Sit down, lean forward, pinch the soft part of the nose. The American guideline says ten to fifteen minutes; the NHS says the same. St John's reason against tipping the head back: the blood can trickle down the throat and block the airway. In the US, get help if it hasn't stopped after fifteen minutes of continuous pressure.

S2: Last one. A tooth gets knocked out.

S1: First question: is it a baby tooth? Those don't go back. The NHS says putting one back could damage the tooth growing underneath. For an adult tooth, both countries say try to put it back, quickly. Hold it by the white part that sticks out of the gum, not the root. In the US, rinse a dirty tooth briefly, under ten seconds; the NHS says rinse it gently in milk, saline or saliva. Push it back into the socket and see a dentist now.

S2: And if it won't go back in?

S1: It has to travel in something, and the American guideline ranks the options. At the top, wrapping it in cling film, or a few special solutions. Then cow's milk or the person's own saliva. And never tap water. The guideline grades that as harmful, because tap water harmed the cells that help the tooth take when it goes back. The NHS names milk or saliva, so milk is a sound choice in both countries.
