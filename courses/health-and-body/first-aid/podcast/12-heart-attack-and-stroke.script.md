S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in First Aid and CPR, on the lesson called Heart attack and stroke.

S2: Start with the thing I always mix up. Isn't a heart attack the same as the heart stopping?

S1: No, and the NHS and the British Heart Foundation both say so plainly. A heart attack is a blocked blood supply to part of the heart muscle, usually a clot. The muscle in that area is damaged and can die, and if it isn't treated the heart can stop pumping altogether. A cardiac arrest is the heart stopping: the person collapses, doesn't respond and isn't breathing normally. So someone having a heart attack may be awake and talking. That's the hard part of this lesson. They're often embarrassed, and hoping it will pass.

S2: What would I actually see?

S1: The American Heart Association and American Red Cross, in their twenty twenty-four first aid guideline, give the common signs as chest pain or pressure, shortness of breath, nausea, sweating, or pain in the jaw, arms or back. And the American Heart Association says it can last more than a few minutes, or go away and come back.

S2: And the ones people talk themselves out of?

S1: The NHS lists one most people would never connect with the heart: feeling like you have indigestion, with a burning in the chest. And the American guideline says some people, such as women, people with diabetes and older people, may have other symptoms: breathlessness, pain in the back, neck, jaw or stomach, sweating, nausea, or lightheadedness. No chest pain, or pain like indigestion, doesn't rule a heart attack out.

S2: So what do I do?

S1: The countries agree on almost every step. Call first, on speaker. Don't drive them yourself. The American guideline's supporting text reports one study of chest-pain patients who came to the emergency department by private car, and about one in three hundred had a cardiac arrest on the way. Sit them down; the NHS and St John suggest the floor, knees bent, back supported, and the British Red Cross says that way they're less likely to hurt themselves if they collapse. If they have known angina and carry their own spray or tablets, help them take it. Stay with them and keep checking their breathing.

S2: And aspirin. That's the argument in every kitchen.

S1: And the two countries' wording differs in ways that matter. In the US, the guideline says a first aider may encourage an alert adult with chest pain that isn't from an injury to chew and swallow aspirin, one hundred and sixty-two to three hundred and twenty-five milligrams, unless they have a known aspirin allergy or a health care professional has told them not to take it. And if there's any uncertainty, it's reasonable to wait for the ambulance without it.

S2: And in the UK?

S1: Resuscitation Council UK's twenty twenty-five guideline says call nine nine nine, then encourage and assist a person with cardiac sounding chest pain to take three hundred milligrams themselves, ideally chewable or dissolvable, as soon as possible, but not if they have a known aspirin allergy. St John adds, not for anyone under sixteen. Follow the figure for the country you're in. And in every version the call comes first. Aspirin is what happens while you wait, never instead of the call.

S2: How strong is the evidence for it?

S1: Read the grade. The American grade is the class called may be considered. And the international body, ILCOR, whose review both countries build on, makes only a weak recommendation, on very low certainty evidence, and its question isn't aspirin or nothing. It's early aspirin from a first aider against aspirin given later, in hospital. Then the gap. The American guideline says no study has evaluated the risks of aspirin given in the first aid setting. Its supporting text says harm from aspirin in nontraumatic chest pain is uncommon, but nobody has measured the risk in first aid, so where there's a possible reason not to use it, it's reasonable not to encourage it before the ambulance arrives. The European guideline starts from the same facts and draws a firmer conclusion, to keep recommending it. Same evidence, read more cautiously in the US and more firmly in Europe.

S2: And for a stroke? Surely it's a clot too.

S1: This is the rule to keep. No guideline the course read recommends aspirin for a suspected stroke, in either country. Every aspirin sentence is written for chest pain, and none of the stroke sections mentions aspirin at all. And there's a reason not to stretch them. A stroke is either a blockage or a bleed in the brain's blood vessels. The NHS names the hospital tests, including scans, that show which type someone has had. The lesson's own inference is that nobody at the kitchen table has those tests, so nobody at the scene can know which kind it is, and no guideline asks you to guess. So the course's rule, drawn from those texts: chest pain and stroke signs together, the stroke signs win. Tell the call handler both, and no aspirin unless the call handler says so.

S2: How do I recognise a stroke?

S1: The UK and the American guideline both teach FAST. Face: is one side drooping, is the smile uneven? Arms: ask them to raise both, they may only manage one. Speech: ask them their name; can they answer clearly and understand you? Time: call, and say you suspect a stroke. The American guideline says call immediately, and recommends a scale such as FAST. Resuscitation Council UK says the same.

S2: What if it all goes away after a couple of minutes?

S1: Call anyway. The American Stroke Association says call nine one one even if the symptoms go away, and check the time so you'll know when they started. The NHS says nine nine nine for signs of a stroke within the last twenty-four hours, even if they've stopped. It may be what the American Stroke Association calls a warning stroke, a transient ischaemic attack, and the NHS says without immediate medical attention the person could be at risk of a full stroke. While you wait, nothing to eat or drink, because they may find it hard to swallow.

S2: And if either one collapses?

S1: A heart attack can become a cardiac arrest. If they stop responding and aren't breathing normally, and gasping is not normal breathing, it's CPR, with the call handler already on the line, and the defibrillator the moment it arrives. If they stop responding but are breathing normally, quietly and regularly, the recovery position, watching their breathing the whole time.

It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: And the moment someone collapses is what a hands-on class practises with a manikin, which no episode can do for you. Thanks for listening.
