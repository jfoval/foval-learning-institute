S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in First Aid and CPR, lesson five. The defibrillator.

S2: The box on the wall I've walked past a thousand times and never opened.

S1: Which is most people. Lesson two left us here: someone who doesn't respond and isn't breathing normally is treated as a cardiac arrest. You call the emergency number on speaker, start CPR, and send someone for the defibrillator. This lesson is what happens when they come back. For some cardiac arrests a shock is the treatment, and nothing else a bystander has will do instead. And the machine is built for someone who has never seen one.

S2: Start with what it actually does, because my picture is from television. Flat line, paddles, everyone jumps.

S1: The lesson takes that picture apart. An AED, an automated external defibrillator, is a box with two sticky pads on a cable. The first thing it does is not shock. It reads the heart rhythm, and then it decides. The European Resuscitation Council's 2025 guideline names the two rhythms a shock can treat: ventricular fibrillation, VF for short, and pulseless ventricular tachycardia. Tachycardia means a fast rhythm; pulseless means it produces no pulse. On this course's reading of the ERC's wording, what the two have in common is that the heart's electrical activity has lost its coordination, so the heart isn't pumping.

S2: And the flat line?

S1: For every other rhythm, and the ERC names asystole, the flat line, and a normal rhythm, no shock is advised. So the television version has it backwards. A flat line needs CPR.

S2: How often is it the shockable kind?

S1: About one in five. The US registry called CARES, in its 2025 report on more than a hundred and forty thousand arrests outside hospital, recorded a shockable first rhythm in eighteen point four percent. The lesson says to read that as a rough share, because the report doesn't say when in each arrest the rhythm was recorded. And survival differed sharply: twenty nine percent to hospital discharge with a shockable rhythm, six point four percent without.

S2: So for four people in five, the machine says no shock at its first check.

S1: And what keeps them going until the crew arrives is your compressions. So the AED is never a reason to stop pushing, and no shock advised is never good news on its own.

S2: Okay, the fear. I'd be scared of shocking someone who'd only fainted.

S1: The ERC's guide for the public answers that directly: the rescuer cannot deliver a shock in error, because the machine won't discharge if it doesn't detect the right conditions. Some AEDs shock on their own; on others you press the button when told. Either way the decision is the machine's. The ERC's guideline also says the pads go only on someone who is unresponsive with abnormal breathing, so a person who fainted and is breathing normally doesn't get them. And Resuscitation Council UK says the risk to rescuers from accidental shock is low.

S2: Everyone says every minute counts. So why do any CPR first? Why not shock the moment it arrives?

S1: The American Heart Association's text gives the reason. When VF goes on, the heart's reserves of oxygen and other energy substrates may run down, and a period of CPR before the shock restores them. That's why CPR comes first and carries on while the AED is fetched and set up. But no longer than that. The AHA recommends CPR until the defibrillator is applied, and the ERC reports an ILCOR review of five randomised trials comparing a shorter with a longer spell of CPR before the first shock, which found no clear benefit from the extra. Its instruction is blunt: defibrillation should not be delayed to provide additional CPR.

S2: So the trials compared some CPR with more CPR, not with none.

S1: Exactly. Push until the pads are on, then let the machine analyse and shock without waiting.

S2: Walk me through it.

S1: This is Resuscitation Council UK's order, and the ERC's is nearly word for word the same. Switch it on; some turn on when you open the lid. Follow its prompts, spoken and on screen. Pads on the bare chest, in the position the drawing on the machine or the pads shows. If there are two of you, one keeps pushing while the other places the pads. Nobody touches the person while it analyses. If a shock is advised, make sure nobody is touching, and on a semi-automatic one, press the button. The American Red Cross adds two things: wipe a wet chest dry, and say "Clear!" loudly before the analysis and before the shock. Then, shock or no shock, straight back to compressions. Leave the pads on; after a set interval it tells you to pause for the next analysis.

S2: Pushing on a heart you've just shocked. That feels wrong.

S1: The AHA's answer is that a shock can stop VF and still leave a rhythm that doesn't move blood, and you can't tell which from outside. Two trials with more than a thousand patients found no gain in survival from pausing to check after the shock. So you don't check; the machine checks on its own schedule.

S2: The lesson spends real time on bras.

S1: Because of a gap in the numbers. In CARES's 2025 figures, a bystander used an AED on eleven point seven percent of women and thirteen point four percent of men who arrested in a public place. The AHA suggests the need to bare the chest may be part of why. The ERC says the gap may stem from apprehension about exposing and touching a woman's chest, and fears of being accused of sexual assault. Both say "may", and the lesson keeps it that way. In the UK, Resuscitation Council UK's instruction is pads on bare skin in the right place. If that's quick with the bra on, leave it. If the bra gets in the way of the pad position, remove it. The ERC says the same, except that a bra in the way should be displaced. ILCOR says there isn't enough evidence to guide routine removal, and adjusting the bra may be enough. In the US, the AHA describes adjusting it as an option. Neither wording is wrong; both want pads on skin, fast.

S2: And the underwire?

S1: The AHA reports one pig study, with no burning or arcing when pads went straight over the wire, and the ERC says there's no evidence that underwire causes harm. That's reassurance, not proof, but enough that nobody should delay a shock over wire. And the ERC's line on it: put life-saving interventions before concerns for modesty.

S2: The full lesson has what each minute of delay costs, and why not to multiply it out, where the pads go in detail, how to find the AEDs near you, what the law says about using one, what changes for children and after drowning, and two worked cases. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: And none of this replaces a hands-on class, where you put training pads on a manikin with an instructor watching. Thanks for listening.
