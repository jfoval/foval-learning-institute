S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in First Aid and CPR, on the lesson called Seizures, low blood sugar and poisoning.

S2: Three very different emergencies.

S1: With one thing in common. In each, someone nearby wants to put something in the person's mouth. A spoon between the teeth, a glass of juice, a finger down the throat. The spoon and the finger are never right. The juice can be exactly right or the most dangerous thing in the room, and the difference comes down to one question: is the person fully awake and able to swallow?

S2: Start with the seizure. Someone drops and starts to jerk.

S1: One warning first, from earlier in the course. Resuscitation Council UK says a brief burst of seizure-like movement can be how cardiac arrest begins, and you assess breathing once it stops. Then the steps. Help them to the ground if they're falling, and clear the area around them. Cushion the head and loosen anything tight at the neck. Time it from start to finish, because the time decides the call. Stay with them. And two things you never do, both graded as harmful in the American Heart Association and American Red Cross twenty twenty four guideline. Don't hold them down, because restraint won't stop the seizure and may injure them or you. And nothing in the mouth. The NHS adds, including your fingers.

S2: But the old advice was, put something between the teeth so they don't swallow their tongue.

S1: The guideline answers that directly. Swallowing the tongue will not occur. The real danger is that people can breathe blood, saliva, food or other things into the lungs, during the seizure and afterwards. And people often clench their jaw, so an object risks their teeth or your finger. The same danger runs on after it stops: most people are sleepy and can't swallow normally, so nothing to eat or drink until they've fully recovered.

S2: Do I roll them on their side?

S1: Here the countries differ on when. In the US, the guideline puts the person on their side in the recovery position during the seizure, and the CDC says turn them gently, mouth pointing to the ground. In the UK, the NHS says turn them on to their side after the convulsions stop, and Epilepsy Action and St John Ambulance agree. None of them says why it waits. Neither is wrong, none of them says to pin a jerking person onto their side, and you follow the country you're in.

S2: And when the jerking stops?

S1: St John gives it in order: open their airway and check their breathing. Don't start breaths or compressions while they're still jerking. If the breathing is quiet and regular at an ordinary pace, that's normal: the recovery position, stay, and keep watching. If they don't respond to you and their breathing is absent, gasping, slow, laboured or noisy, it's not normal: onto the back, the call on speaker, and CPR.

S2: But doesn't breathing sound strange after a seizure anyway?

S1: It can. Epilepsy Action says it may sound heavy and laboured, with a deep snoring sound. And Resuscitation Council UK counts slow, laboured breathing as a sign of cardiac arrest, and says, "If there is any doubt, assume cardiac arrest and start CPR." So the course doesn't ask you to tell a noisy recovery from the start of an arrest. Tell the call handler what you hear, and if the person doesn't respond and the doubt stays, start CPR.

S2: Does every seizure need an ambulance?

S1: No, and that surprises people. The NHS says people with epilepsy don't always need one. But both countries call for a first seizure. Among the rest, the US list adds one lasting more than five minutes, one in water, pregnancy, and not being back to their usual self within five to ten minutes after it stops. The NHS adds one longer than is usual for them, or more than five minutes if you don't know their usual, and not regaining full consciousness. With a stranger and no medical card, you can't know it isn't their first. And if they don't respond to you at all once it stops, that's a call regardless.

S2: Low blood sugar next.

S1: A hypo mostly affects people with diabetes who take insulin or some other diabetes medicines. The signs include sweating, shaking, confusion and slurred speech. And now the one question matters most. Awake and able to swallow? Sugar by mouth, now. Not awake, or not able to swallow? Nothing by mouth. The American guideline grades giving it as harmful, and its text says it can lead to choking, and to breathing it into the lungs. Resuscitation Council UK says in an unresponsive person, no oral sugar in any form; call nine nine nine and consider the recovery position. If you're wondering whether they're awake enough, the course's reading is that they aren't.

S2: How much sugar, and then what?

S1: In the US, glucose tablets are preferred if they're there, and simple dietary sugars if not. The guideline's text says twenty grams or more. Call emergency services if they don't improve within ten minutes. In the UK, Resuscitation Council UK says glucose tablets, fifteen to twenty grams, or if there are none, a handful of sugary sweets or fifty to a hundred millilitres of fruit juice or a sugary drink. Repeat the sugar if they're not improving after fifteen minutes, and call nine nine nine if they are or become unresponsive, or don't improve.

S2: And poisoning. Say a toddler's found beside an open bottle of tablets, and seems fine.

S1: In the US, it's Poison Help, one eight hundred, two two two, one two two two, where a poison expert answers, with nine one one if the person collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing or can't be woken. In England, the NHS says call nine nine nine or go to A and E if you think someone may have swallowed something harmful, and NHS one one one only if you're not sure it's harmful. It warns that symptoms may take hours or days to start. So even if they seem well, it's nine nine nine or A and E.

S2: And making them sick?

S1: Never. The NHS says they could choke and stop breathing. On drinks, the countries differ. The NHS and St John say nothing to eat or drink. PoisonHelp dot org's first step for a swallowed poison is to wipe out the mouth gently and give a small amount of water. Neither page says why. Follow your country's page, and the person on the phone. And the one question sits over both: nothing by mouth, water included, for anyone who isn't fully awake and able to swallow.
