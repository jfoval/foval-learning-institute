S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're starting the course called First Aid and CPR, with its first lesson. You are the first responder.

S1: And the title is meant literally. Most cardiac arrests outside hospital happen at home. The American Heart Association puts it at seventy three point four percent in the US, and in the UK it's seventy to eighty percent. So the first person there usually isn't a paramedic. It's a partner, a son, a flatmate.

S2: Before we go further, give me the two terms the whole course runs on.

S1: Cardiac arrest is when the heart stops pumping blood, so the brain gets no oxygen. The person collapses, does not respond, and is not breathing normally. It isn't the same thing as a heart attack; lesson twelve separates those. And CPR is pushing hard and fast on the centre of the chest, sometimes with rescue breaths, to keep some blood moving until help arrives.

S2: So this lesson is about the moment before you do any of that.

S1: It's about why people hold back. Ask them, and the answers come from a short list. Someone else will do it. I'll be sued. I'll make it worse. I'm not qualified. I don't know what to do first. The lesson takes each one against the evidence.

S2: Start with someone else will do it. That's the bystander effect, and I know the story. Kitty Genovese, nineteen sixty four, thirty eight people watched and did nothing.

S1: And the story was wrong. When her killer died in twenty sixteen, the New York Times itself wrote that its portrayal of thirty eight witnesses as fully aware and unresponsive was erroneous. None saw the whole attack. Two people did call the police, and a seventy year old woman went out and held her until they came.

S2: So the bystander effect is a myth?

S1: No. The most famous evidence for it wasn't evidence. The real evidence is elsewhere. Peter Fischer and colleagues, in twenty eleven, pooled the laboratory studies, over seven thousand seven hundred people. Having others present did lower each person's chance of helping. A modest effect: people helped less, they didn't stop helping. And the effect shrank when the situation was seen as dangerous. The authors say that fits a model in which dangerous emergencies are recognised faster and more clearly as real. That's a model the findings fit, not something they showed.

S2: And outside a laboratory?

S1: Richard Philpot and colleagues watched two hundred and nineteen CCTV clips of real public conflicts in the UK, the Netherlands and South Africa. At least one bystander stepped in in ninety point nine percent of them, and each extra bystander made help a little more likely, not less. The two fit together. Each person is somewhat less likely to act, but a crowd usually produces somebody.

S2: So in a crowd, I can relax.

S1: That's the catch. Those clips were fights and arguments. Not one was a collapse. They show people step into a fight far more often than the Genovese story implies. They don't show that anyone in a crowd will kneel down and start CPR.

S2: Okay. I'll be sued. That's the one I'd actually worry about.

S1: I'll give you this the way the lesson does, as general information and not legal advice, because the law differs by state and by country. Every US state has some form of Good Samaritan law. California's protects someone who helps in good faith and without pay, unless what they did was grossly negligent. A bystander doing their honest best with CPR meets all three conditions. Then Travis Murphy and colleagues searched thirty years of US court records, nineteen eighty nine to twenty nineteen, and found one hundred and seventy cases about CPR. One hundred and sixty seven were about CPR that was inadequate or came too late. Three alleged that giving CPR did harm.

S2: So giving CPR keeps you out of court.

S1: The lesson is careful here, so I will be too. Inadequate CPR is CPR somebody gave, so some of those defendants had given it, and the abstract the course read doesn't say who they were. What it does show is that claims that the CPR itself did harm were very rare. Three in thirty years, across the whole country.

S2: And the UK?

S1: No general Good Samaritan statute; it relies on the common law. Resuscitation Council UK's booklet on the law says a few claims have been brought against people who attempted resuscitation, and no reported claim against anyone who came to help has succeeded. For a passer-by there's no general legal duty to help in the UK, though once you volunteer you owe a duty to do your honest best, not to be perfect.

S2: I'll make it worse. I'll break ribs.

S1: That can happen, and lesson three has the numbers. But in cardiac arrest the booklet says making things worse would be virtually impossible, since without intervention death is inevitable. Then the sharper worry: what if their heart was beating after all? Studies of exactly that case found injuries in about two percent or fewer. The American Heart Association's twenty twenty five guidelines say the benefit for someone in arrest far outweighs any risk to someone who isn't.

S2: And I'm not qualified.

S1: A certificate is real, and its job is employment. It doesn't decide who may help. The American Heart Association's twenty twenty five guidelines say all lay rescuers should give chest compressions for adults with presumed cardiac arrest, and that's their top class of recommendation. There's no certificate in that sentence.

S2: So put it together. A woman collapses at a bus stop, six people standing around, nobody moving.

S1: Once you've checked it's safe to reach her, which is where lesson two starts, the course's reading is that the first useful thing is to say out loud what's happening. She's collapsed and she needs help. Then don't ask the crowd whether someone could help. Point at one person. You in the grey coat, call nine nine nine and put it on speaker, or nine one one in the US. Point at another. You, find a defibrillator. The guidelines hand out tasks like that. That it beats the bystander effect is the course's own inference, and nobody it read has tested the tactic itself.

S2: One more thing a listener should hear. Can this episode teach you CPR?

S1: No, and neither can the lesson. Reading can tell you what to do and in what order. It can't teach your hands how hard to push. That takes a hands-on class with a manikin and an instructor, and practice between classes.

S2: The full lesson also has consent, for someone who can answer and someone who can't, the three US states that require you to help and the argument over whether the law should, England and Wales's twenty fifteen Heroism Act, what to do if someone tells you the person has a do not resuscitate order, how to read the guideline label beside each instruction, what the course leaves out, and two free tools to start with at home. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening.
