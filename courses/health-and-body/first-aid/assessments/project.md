Most cardiac arrests happen at home, and so do most of the other emergencies in this course: the
choking toddler, the grandparent who will not wake, the teenager with a swelling face. The person who
has to act first is usually whoever lives there. This project turns the course into four things that
stay in your home and in your hands: **an emergency card**, **a log of rehearsals**, **five
decisions written up** from scenarios the course supplies, and **a class booked**.

It takes about five hours of your own time, spread over two to three weeks: an hour for the card,
three short rehearsal sessions and two walk-throughs, about half an hour for each decision, and a
few minutes to book the class. The class itself is extra.

:::callout Before you need this
This course is education, not a certificate, and not medical advice about anyone's own situation. In an emergency, call your local emergency number first (911 in the US, 999 or 112 in the UK, 112 across the EU), put the phone on speaker, and do what the call handler tells you. Reading can teach you what to do and in what order. It can't teach your hands how hard to push or how a back blow should feel, so take a hands-on class with a manikin and an instructor, and practise between classes. Each instruction here names the guideline and year it comes from, and guidance changes.
:::

## Two rules for every part

**Never dial an emergency number to practise.** Every rehearsal of a call is spoken aloud, to
yourself or to a friend. Nothing on the card is tested by calling it.

**Never practise a technique on a living person.** Compressions go into a cushion or a firm pillow.
Back blows, thrusts and the recovery position are spoken, or placed on a cushion or a doll. If
anything hurts your wrists, back or knees, or you feel dizzy or unusually breathless, stop.

## Which country

Choose the country whose guidance you follow, the one your call handler and your class will use,
as lesson 1 said, and write it at the top. Everything below is marked against that country's
guidance. If you live somewhere else, use your national resuscitation council and say which.

## What you hand in

One plain text or Markdown file, roughly 2,000 to 3,000 words, with four parts. The card itself
stays at home; you hand in a copy with anything private blanked out.

## Part 1: the card

One side of a card or a sheet of paper that lives where your household will see it: by the phone,
on the fridge, in the kit. It holds:

1. **Your emergency number.** 911 in the US; 999 or 112 in the UK (lesson 2).
2. **Your address or a landmark, as you would say it aloud**, the way lesson 2's call handler would want
   it. In the UK, add one line: most smartphones send their location on a 999 call, and some
   ambulance services can text a what3words link if you cannot say where you are (lesson 2).
3. **Your country's poison line.** In the US, Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222 (lesson 14). In the UK,
   write what lesson 14 found: the NHS pages name no separate public poison line; 999 or A&E if you
   think someone has swallowed, touched or breathed in something harmful, NHS 111 only if you are not
   sure it is harmful.
4. **The nearest public defibrillator to your home, and when it can be reached.** Walk there, look
   at it, and note whether the cabinet is locked and whether the building closes at night (lesson
   5). In the UK the public map, defibfinder.uk, is for finding one in advance; in an emergency the
   call handler knows where the registered ones are (lessons 2 and 5).
5. **For each person at home with a known condition** (an allergy, asthma, epilepsy, diabetes, heart
   disease): where their medicine or device is kept, and the one rule this course taught for it,
   with the body and year. For example, if you are in the UK and someone carries an auto-injector:
   "Second dose at 5 minutes if no better, other leg (RCUK 2025)" (lesson 13). Ask them, when it
   suits them, what they would want the card to say; lessons 13 and 14 started this card.

Nothing on the card is sent anywhere. It stays in your home.

## Part 2: the rehearsals

Log each one with its date, what you did, and one honest line about what went wrong or fell off.

1. **The call, said aloud.** Your address or landmark, what has happened, and "unresponsive and
   not breathing normally", then the phone going on speaker beside the person's head (lesson 2).
   Once, to yourself or a friend. Never dialled.
2. **Two minutes of compressions into a cushion, three times over two weeks**, at 100 to 120 a
   minute. Use the BHF's RevivR for camera feedback on rate and depth if you can (lesson 3), or a
   metronome at 110 if you cannot; 110 is lesson 3's choice from the middle of the range, not a
   guideline figure. Each time, note when your depth or rhythm first fell off, or when your arms
   first tired, and whether that came before or after the 90 seconds at which the AHA says depth
   starts to fall (lesson 3). The AHA rates the usefulness of objects such as cushions, against a
   manikin, as not well established (AHA 2025, COR 2b, LOE C-LD; lesson 16), which is why Part 4
   exists.
3. **The recovery position, spoken or placed.** Say the steps aloud in your country's source's words
   while placing a cushion or a doll, or, as lesson 7 allowed, a willing adult for arm and knee
   placement only: no roll, no force, no hard floor.
4. **The choking sequence for the youngest person in your household**, spoken, with a doll or
   cushion for a baby's position (lesson 8). If nobody at home is under 18, do it for an adult.

## Part 3: five decisions

Choose five of the ten scenarios below. **At least one must be a child or infant case** (A, B or J)
and **at least one a medical emergency** (D, E, F, H or I). For each, write:

1. **The sequence under your country's guidance**, step by step, with the body, year and grade of
   each step where it has one ("(AHA 2025, COR 1, LOE C-LD)", "(RCUK 2025)"). Where a step comes from
   a public page or is this course's own reading, say so.
2. **What would change under the other country's guidance**, and each side's reason if the course
   gave one.
3. **What you would say to the call handler**, in the words you would use, in the order they would need it.

None of these scenarios is in a lesson or in the final test. Each has a checkpoint with what a sound
answer contains. Write yours first, then open it.

### A. The high chair

In Oldham, Rhiannon's ten-month-old son is in his high chair eating halved grapes. He goes silent:
mouth open, no cry, no cough, his face darkening. Her partner is in the next room.

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to A contains

A baby who cannot cry or cough has a severe obstruction (lesson 8). Her partner calls on speaker, or
she does if alone. Up to five back blows: face down along the forearm resting on the thigh, head
supported and lower than the body, heel of the hand between the shoulder blades (RCUK 2025). Then up
to five chest thrusts on his back: two-thumb encircling, pushed more sharply than compressions, in
the UK (RCUK 2025); the heel of one hand in the US (AHA 2025, supporting text), in cycles of 5 and 5
(AHA 2025, COR 1, LOE C-LD). Never abdominal thrusts for a baby. No blind finger sweep. If he goes
limp: CPR at once, five rescue breaths first in the UK (RCUK 2025), compressions first in the US
(AHA 2025, COR 1, LOE C-LD). If it comes out, he still goes to be checked: St John says every baby
given chest thrusts should be seen in hospital, and the NHS says get medical help even if the object
has come out. To the call handler: the address, "my baby is choking and can't breathe or cry", his
age.
:::

### B. The basketball court

In Scranton, an eleven-year-old at basketball practice sits down, then slumps sideways. He does not
respond when the coach shakes his shoulders and shouts, and he is making occasional gasping breaths.
He shows no signs of puberty. A parent is with the coach, and there is an AED by the gym door. (If
you follow UK guidance, do this scenario as if it were a school sports hall in the UK.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to B contains

Gasping is not normal breathing: this is cardiac arrest (lesson 2). In the US: recognise, then call
911 on speaker and start CPR at once; he is a child until puberty (AHA 2025), so child CPR,
compressions first (AHA 2025, COR 2b, LOE C-LD), at least a third of the chest depth, about 2 in (5
cm) (COR 2a, LOE C-LD), 100 to 120 a minute (COR 2a, LOE C-LD); 30:2 alone, 15:2 with two rescuers
(COR 2a, LOE C-EO); the parent fetches the AED and it goes on as soon as possible (AHA 2025); at 11
he is over the age for the attenuator (under 8 years, COR 1, LOE C-LD). In the UK: call 999 for any
unresponsive person and check breathing with the call handler; five rescue breaths first, then 30:2
unless specifically trained in paediatric life support (RCUK 2025); a helper brings the AED; paediatric mode if
he weighs under 25 kg and the AED has one, standard adult mode if he is heavier (RCUK 2025). Where the two differ: the first action and the
reasons lesson 6 gave (the AHA's consistency across ages, RCUK's reasoning from the usual cause). To
the call handler: the address or gym, "an eleven-year-old boy has collapsed, he is not responding and
he is gasping", and that there is an AED.
:::

### C. The lawnmower

In Kalamazoo, Wes comes round the side of the house and finds his father face down beside the
lawnmower, its engine still running. His father does not respond and is not breathing normally. Wes
is alone and has his phone. The nearest public AED he knows of is at a library ten minutes' walk
away. (UK readers: the same, in a back garden in the UK.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to C contains

Danger first: the engine off, if it can be done safely (lesson 2). Roll his father onto his back. In
the US: he is unresponsive and not breathing normally, so call 911 on speaker and start CPR at once (a
lone rescuer with a phone, AHA 2025, COR 1, LOE C-LD); compressions to at least 2 in (5 cm) but not
more than 2.4 in (6 cm) (COR 1, LOE B-NR), 100 to 120 a minute (COR 2a, LOE B-NR), full recoil;
compressions only if he is not trained or willing to give breaths (all lay rescuers should give
compressions, COR 1, LOE B-NR). In the UK: call 999 on speaker first because he is unresponsive,
check breathing with the call handler, then CPR at 5 to 6 cm and 100 to 120 a minute (RCUK 2025).
Either way, Wes does not leave to fetch the AED: CPR shouldn't be delayed to find one (ERC 2025), and
he tells the call handler he is alone, where the AED is, and follows their lead (lesson 5). To the
call handler: the address, "my dad has collapsed, he is not responding and he is not breathing
properly", that he is alone.
:::

### D. The café

In Eastbourne, a sixteen-year-old with a known nut allergy has eaten a slice of cake that turned out
to contain almonds. Within minutes her lips are swelling, her voice is hoarse, she is wheezing and
she says she feels faint. She carries two auto-injectors. Her friend is with her. (US readers: the
same, in a US diner, with the ambulance 25 minutes away.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to D contains

Anaphylaxis: adrenaline first, even if in doubt (lesson 13). In the UK: adrenaline into the outer
thigh as soon as possible, by her or by a trained person, and St John tells a first aider to help or
give it; 999, saying "anaphylaxis"; lie her down with legs raised, sitting up only if breathing is
hard, and never standing, even if she feels better (MHRA 2023; RCUK 2025); if symptoms persist five
minutes after the first dose, a second, ideally in the other leg (RCUK 2025). In the US: she uses
her own auto-injector (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1, LOE B-NR), you help if she needs it (COR 1, LOE
C-EO), call 911 (COR 1, LOE C-EO); a second dose may be considered if she does not respond and EMS is
more than 5 to 10 minutes away (COR 2b, LOE B-NR), which 25 minutes is; the US guideline's
anaphylaxis section says nothing on posture. Say the time of each dose aloud. If she stops
responding and is not breathing normally, CPR. To the call handler: "anaphylaxis", her age, the time
of the first dose.
:::

### E. The bowling alley

In Peoria, a man of 68 in your bowling team suddenly cannot get his words out. The left side of his
face droops and he cannot lift his left arm. He says he has a headache and asks if anyone has an
aspirin. (UK readers: the same, at a bowling alley in the UK.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to E contains

Face, arm and speech are all positive: suspected stroke (lesson 12). In the US, activate EMS at once
(AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1, LOE B-NR) and note the time the symptoms started; the ASA's public
message adds balance and eyes (BE-FAST), while the guideline names FAST. In the UK: FAST and 999 (RCUK
2025), telling the call handler you suspect a stroke (St John). **No aspirin**: no guideline this
course read recommends it for a suspected stroke, and nobody at the scene can know whether it is a
bleed (lesson 12). Nothing to eat or drink (St John). Keep him comfortable and supported, and watch:
if he stops responding, breathing normally means lesson 7, not normally means CPR. Tell the call
handler if he has diabetes, since low blood sugar can look like a stroke. To the call handler: the
address, "I think he's having a stroke", what you saw, and the time it started.
:::

### F. The sofa

In Dumfries, Nadia's sister, who has type 1 diabetes, is slumped on the sofa, mumbling, eyes half
closed, and cannot hold her head up. There is a glass of orange juice on the table, and their brother
wants to pour some into her mouth. (US readers: the same, in a US apartment.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to F contains

She isn't fully awake and can't swallow safely, so **nothing by mouth**: in the UK, RCUK says do not
give oral sugar in any form to an unresponsive person because of the risk of aspiration, call 999
and consider the recovery position (RCUK 2025); in the US, oral glucose should not be given to
people who are not awake or not able to swallow (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 3: Harm, LOE C-EO), and
EMS is called for someone unable to swallow (COR 1, LOE C-EO). Check her breathing: quiet and
regular, the recovery position and watching (lesson 7); not normal, CPR (lesson 2). Glucagon only if
she carries it and someone knows how to use it (lesson 14). If she comes round and can swallow
safely, then sugar. To the call handler: the address, "my sister has type 1 diabetes, she's barely
conscious and can't swallow", when she last ate or took insulin if you know.
:::

### G. The shop floor

In Olympia, a shop assistant falls through a glass display shelf and a long cut on the back of his
lower leg, below the knee, is spurting blood. Firm pressure with a folded cloth isn't stopping it.
There's a bleed kit with a tourniquet behind the counter, and a customer has 911 on speaker. (UK
readers: the same, with 999 on speaker and a PAcT kit.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to G contains

Life-threatening bleeding on a limb that pressure isn't controlling (lesson 9). In the US: a
tourniquet, tightened until the bleeding stops (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1, LOE B-NR), 2 to 3 inches
above the wound, not over the knee (Stop the Bleed). In the UK: a tourniquet as soon as possible once
pressure fails, 5 to 7 cm above the injury and not over a joint (RCUK 2025). Both: it will hurt;
write the time; **never loosen or remove it**, which only a healthcare professional does (RCUK
2025; Stop the Bleed). Then watch for shock: the US guideline says lie a person in shock on their
back, and raises the feet only where there's no trauma (AHA/Red Cross 2024), so not here; St John's
UK severe bleeding page raises the legs, and its fractures page says not if a leg may be broken
(lesson 9). If he stops responding, lesson 2. To the call handler: "severe bleeding from the leg,
pressure isn't stopping it, we have a tourniquet", and the time it went on.
:::

### H. The van

At a festival near Lowestoft, you find your friend's boyfriend in the back of a van, not responding
when you shake him and shout. His breathing is very slow, with long gaps, and his pupils are tiny.
There's a naloxone nasal spray in his bag, and you were shown how to use one by a drug service.
(US readers: the same, at a festival in the US, with an over-the-counter spray.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to H contains

Very slow breathing with long gaps isn't normal breathing (lessons 2 and 7), so this is CPR, not the
recovery position. In the UK: call 999 on speaker; if unresponsive and not breathing normally, start
CPR; give naloxone if you are trained, which you are (RCUK 2025); the ERC says start CPR and give
nasal naloxone. In the US: call 911 without delaying it for naloxone (AHA 2025, COR 1, LOE C-EO);
CPR with breaths (COR 1, LOE B-NR); anyone can give naloxone, but never at the cost of the call or
CPR, and in cardiac arrest it "may be reasonable" alongside CPR (COR 2b). **The call and CPR never
wait for the spray.** If he wakes: stay with him, follow the packaging on a second dose, and keep him
under observation until help arrives (RCUK 2025); the AHA wants adults who respond observed in a
health care setting (COR 1, LOE C-LD). To the call handler: where the van is, "he's not responding and
barely breathing, I think it's an overdose", and when the naloxone went in.
:::

### I. The meeting room

In Cheyenne, a colleague of about thirty, with no history of seizures that anyone knows of, goes
rigid in a meeting and falls from her chair, then starts jerking. (UK readers: the same, in an
office in the UK.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to I contains

Protect her: help her down if falling, clear the chairs, cushion her head (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1,
LOE C-EO; the NHS). Time it. Don't hold her down (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 3: Harm, LOE C-EO), and
nothing in her mouth (COR 3: Harm, LOE C-EO). Call: a first seizure is on both countries' lists
(AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1, LOE C-LD; the NHS). The side position: during the seizure in the US (the
guideline's injury recommendation; the CDC), after the convulsions stop in the UK (the NHS), and
neither country says to pin a jerking person on her side. When it stops, lesson 2's check: quiet
and regular breathing, the recovery position and watching; absent, gasping, slow, laboured or
snoring, CPR. Nothing to eat or drink until fully recovered. To the call handler: "a woman has had a
seizure, her first as far as we know", how long it lasted, and how she is now.
:::

### J. The straighteners

In Bradford, a two-year-old grabs a pair of hot hair straighteners left on the bedroom floor. Her
palm and fingers are red and already blistering. (US readers: the same, in a US home.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to J contains

Cool it at once under cool running water (ILCOR 2025, strong recommendation; AHA/Red Cross 2024,
COR 1, LOE B-NR). For how long: in the UK, 20 minutes (the NHS; the British Burn Association; St
John; the British Red Cross); in the US, 5 to 20 minutes (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 2b, LOE B-NR).
Keep the rest of her warm and watch a small child for over-cooling (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1, LOE
C-LD; the British Burn Association). Anything tight near the burn off before it swells (COR 1, LOE
C-EO). No butter, creams or ice. Cover it: cling film laid on, not wrapped, in the UK (the NHS); a
clean, dry, non-sticking dressing laid on loosely in the US (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 2b, LOE C-EO).
Medical help: it's blistered and on her hand, so in the US the guideline wants prompt evaluation by
a health professional (COR 1, LOE B-NR), and the American Red Cross page stars burns in the very
young and to the hands for immediate emergency treatment and a 911 call; in the UK, the British Red
Cross says always get medical advice for a baby or child, the NHS says phone 111 for advice about a
child under 5 if you're not sure, and 999 or A&E for a burn that is large or deep (lesson 10). To the
person on the phone: her age, what burned her, where, and how big.
:::

## Part 4: the class

Book, attend or schedule a hands-on class with a manikin and an instructor from lesson 16's list for
your country, and write its name and date. If none is available to you, schedule RevivR or Lifesaver
now and a repeat three months later, the point at which Riggs's review found skills had already
fallen (lesson 16). Write the date of your next refresher either way.

## Rubric

| Part | Full marks | Partly there | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| The card | All five items, correct for your country: the right number, a location you could say aloud, the poison line or the UK note, an AED you've actually visited with its hours, and a sourced rule for each condition at home | One item missing or unsourced, or an AED found on a map but not visited | A wrong emergency number, or no card |
| The rehearsals | All four logged with dates, three compression sessions over two weeks, each with an honest note of what fell off and when | Fewer sessions, or a log with no note of what went wrong | No log, or any rehearsal done on a person or by dialling |
| Five decisions | Five, including a child or infant case and a medical emergency; every step in the right order with its body, year and grade, and the course's own readings labelled as such | Right order, but attributions missing or wrong in places | A sequence out of order in a way that matters |
| The other country | Each difference named, with each side's reason where the course gave one, and neither called wrong | Differences named without reasons, or one missed | No comparison, or one side called wrong |
| The call handler | What you'd say, in order, in words you'd use, with the location first or right after breathing | Correct content, but in an order that makes the call handler dig | Missing |
| The class | A class booked or attended, or a digital course scheduled with a three-month repeat, with dates | A class named but not booked | Nothing scheduled |

**Any of these fails the project outright, whatever else is right**, because each one could cost a
life:

- a wrong emergency number for your country;
- anything by mouth for a person who isn't fully awake;
- aspirin for a suspected stroke;
- loosening or removing a tourniquet;
- a blind finger sweep;
- a sequence that delays the call or CPR for naloxone, sends a lone rescuer with a phone away to fetch
  an AED, or has a lay rescuer check for a pulse.

## A note on what this project is not

It isn't a certificate and doesn't replace one, and nothing in it shows that your hands can do what
your page says. That's Part 4's job, and an instructor's. It's the plan you'd want to have made
before the day you need it, with the numbers written down while you're calm.
