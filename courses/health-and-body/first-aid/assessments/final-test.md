  # --- Helping, and the law (L1) ---
  - q: >-
      In a department store in Wolverhampton you've been giving CPR for four minutes to a man who
      collapsed by the lifts. A woman kneels beside you, says she's an off-duty paramedic, and
      offers to take over. What does the UK law booklet this course read say about carrying on?
      (General information, not legal advice.)
    options:
      - Hand over and leave at once, since helping under her now would make you liable for her care
      - Carry on until she shows you proof she's a paramedic, since the duty of care is yours till then
      - Let her take over, and keep helping under her direction if she wants you, not on your own
      - Carry on alone, since the 2015 Act protects anyone acting heroically, whatever they do
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      RCUK's booklet on CPR and the law, in its April 2018 edition, says it wouldn't be reasonable
      for an unqualified person to carry on alone if "a professional rescuer were present or arrived
      at the scene and offered to help", though you may keep helping under their direction. The duty
      of care you take on by volunteering is to help "as far as they are able", and the booklet asks
      for no proof before you accept better help, so carrying on until she shows some is carrying on
      alone. Staying to help under her doesn't expose you either: the same booklet reports no
      successful UK claim against anyone who came to help. And the Social Action, Responsibility and
      Heroism Act 2015 tells a court in England and Wales what to have regard to; it grants no
      immunity, whatever you do. This is general information, not legal advice.

  # --- Recognising arrest, the call, 999 or 111 (L2) ---
  - q: >-
      In a lecture theatre in Coventry, a student of about twenty slumps from her seat to the floor.
      Her arms and legs jerk for about ten seconds and then go still. She doesn't respond when you
      shake her shoulders and shout, and every few seconds she gives a slow, snorting gasp. A friend
      is calling 999 on speaker. Under RCUK's 2025 adult guidelines, what now?
    options:
      - Start CPR, since jerking can come at the start of an arrest and those gasps aren't breathing
      - It was a fit, so roll her into the recovery position and watch her until the crew get there
      - Wait a minute before acting, since breathing just after a fit can look stopped or odd at first
      - Put something soft between her teeth first, in case the jerking starts again while you wait
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      RCUK 2025: "A short period of seizure-like activity may occur at the onset of cardiac arrest.
      Once the seizure stops, assess breathing." Her slow gasps are on its list: "Slow, laboured
      breathing, as well as other abnormal patterns such as agonal gasping or panting, must be
      recognised as signs of cardiac arrest." The ERC's 2025 guide for the public adds that brief,
      seizure-like movements are often seen in younger people in cardiac arrest. So calling it a fit
      and rolling her onto her side is the mistake: RCUK says not to use the recovery position with
      agonal breathing, and effective CPR can't be given on the side (AHA 2025). Waiting a minute is
      a minute with no circulation; RCUK's rule for doubt is to assume arrest and start CPR, and the
      call handler on speaker helps you judge. Nothing goes in anyone's mouth during or after a
      seizure (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 3: Harm).
  - q: >-
      Your phone's battery died an hour ago. On an allotment in Lincolnshire you find your neighbour
      collapsed between the vegetable beds, not responding and not breathing normally. You shout for
      help and nobody answers; the nearest phone is in a house across the lane. Under RCUK's 2025
      adult guidelines, what do you do?
    options:
      - Stay and do CPR until someone passes, since leaving him means minutes without compressions
      - Go and call 999 as fast as you can, then come back and start CPR if he still needs it
      - Feel for a pulse at his neck first, and go for help only if you can't find a pulse there
      - Roll him onto his side so his airway stays open, and then go across to the house to call
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      RCUK 2025 says to shout for help, and then: "If you think no one will come to help, then you
      will have to leave the person and call 999. Do this as quickly as possible." When you come
      back, if he's still unresponsive and not breathing normally, you start CPR at once. Staying
      feels kinder, but until someone calls, no ambulance, coach or defibrillator is on its way, and
      RCUK's advice is to make the hard choice fast. A pulse check isn't in RCUK's steps for the
      public at all, and the AHA's 2025 adult guideline bases lay recognition on response and
      breathing rather than a pulse. And the side position is for someone breathing normally, which
      he isn't; effective CPR can't be given on the side (AHA 2025).
  - q: >-
      In Yeovil your grandfather, who is 81, is lying on the sofa watching television when he goes
      limp, and for about twenty seconds you can't rouse him. Then he comes round, breathing
      normally, knows where he is, and says he must have nodded off. He has no chest pain and isn't
      hurt. Under the NHS fainting page, what fits?
    options:
      - Call 999, since fainting while lying down is one of the NHS page's reasons to call
      - See a GP in the next few days, since he came round fast and is fully himself again
      - Call NHS 111 for advice, since he has recovered and you aren't sure what caused it
      - Nothing more tonight, since waking inside a minute means it was a simple faint
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The NHS fainting page's reasons to call 999 include fainting while lying down, alongside not
      breathing, not waking within a minute, not fully recovering, chest pain or a pounding or
      irregular heartbeat, a serious injury, a seizure, and fainting while exercising. He fainted
      lying on the sofa, so it's 999 even though he's himself again. The page's default after a
      faint is a GP appointment, which is right only when none of those reasons applies. NHS 111 is
      for when you're "not sure if you need to call 999", and here the page has settled that for
      you. Coming round quickly is usual for a faint, since the page says people usually wake
      within 30 seconds, and it doesn't take him off the list. (In the US the AHA/Red Cross 2024 guideline
      calls for 911 after any faint.)

  # --- Pushing on the chest (L3) ---
  - q: >-
      In a dentist's waiting room in Madison, Wisconsin, a patient collapses in cardiac arrest. A man
      who did an American Red Cross course gives 30:2. Before each pair of breaths he re-tilts the
      head, checks his seal twice and watches the face, so every stop lasts about 20 seconds. Under
      the American Red Cross's advice and the AHA's 2025 reasoning, what should change?
    options:
      - Nothing, since two careful breaths are worth more than the seconds of pushing they cost
      - Push faster between stops, at about 140 a minute, to make up for what the stops lose
      - Keep each stop under 10 seconds, since the pressure feeding the heart falls at once
      - Give one breath per stop instead, at 30 to 1, which halves the time his hands are off
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The American Red Cross says to keep interruptions to chest compressions under 10 seconds, and
      the AHA's 2025 guideline gives the reason: pauses "result in an almost immediate drop in
      coronary perfusion pressure", the pressure that drives blood into the heart muscle, which it
      links with a lower chance of the heart restarting. The AHA sets the one-second breath for the
      same purpose, "to keep the pauses in chest compressions as brief as possible during 30:2 CPR",
      and two one-second breaths fit inside 10 seconds. A pause isn't something you make up for
      later, and 140 a minute is outside the 100 to 120 both countries give (AHA 2025, COR 2a, LOE
      B-NR; RCUK 2025). No guideline read here uses 30 to 1; the adult ratio is 30:2 in both.
  - q: >-
      A woman who is about eight months pregnant collapses in a cinema foyer. She doesn't respond and
      isn't breathing normally. A member of staff has the emergency number on speaker, and a second
      customer is free to help. Under the AHA's and RCUK's 2025 guidelines, as this course reads them
      for a bystander, what comes first?
    options:
      - Wedge her onto her left side on a folded coat, since a tilt is what both guidelines prefer
      - Compressions at once with her flat on her back, and tell the call handler she's pregnant
      - Hold off on compressions until the crew arrive, since pushing on her chest could harm her
      - Roll her into the recovery position on her left side, to take the weight off her big veins
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      A pregnant woman in cardiac arrest gets CPR as anyone else would, and RCUK 2025 tells rescuers
      to consider pregnancy in any collapsed woman of childbearing age. Later in pregnancy both
      guidelines add manual displacement of the womb to her left by hand while she stays flat (AHA
      2025, COR 1, LOE C-LD, once the top of the womb is at or above the navel; RCUK 2025, above 20
      weeks), and both prefer it to tilting her, because in simulation trials compressions were
      better on a body lying flat (AHA 2025). Both texts are written for resuscitation teams and give
      no bystander version, so this course's reading is that nothing delays the compressions, and
      the call handler says whether and how the second customer does the displacement. Holding off
      leaves her with no circulation at all. And effective CPR can't be given on the side (AHA 2025).

  # --- Breaths, and reading a label (L4) ---
  - q: >-
      In Omaha, a wasp stings your friend's son on the eye itself, not the lid. Your friend finds
      this line in the AHA and American Red Cross's 2024 first aid guideline: "Stings to the eye
      should be evaluated by a trained medical professional." (COR 1, LOE C-EO). She says C-EO is
      only opinion, so it's a suggestion she can skip. Using the AHA's Table 1, what does the label
      tell her?
    options:
      - It's weak, since a class that rests on expert opinion alone can't be more than a suggestion
      - It's class 1, but on opinion only, so the AHA leaves it to her own judgement whether to go
      - It rests on trials, since only randomised trials can earn a recommendation of class 1
      - Class 1, "should", on expert consensus, so strong advice that rests on opinion, not trials
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      In the AHA's Table 1, class 1 is "Strong", benefit far greater than risk, with the wording
      "should", and C-EO is "Consensus of expert opinion based on clinical experience". The table
      says the two are set independently, and "A recommendation with LOE C does not imply that the
      recommendation is weak." So this is the AHA's strongest class on its thinnest kind of evidence,
      and it tells her to have the eye seen. Nothing in the table makes a class 1 optional because of
      its level: the level describes the evidence, not how firmly the AHA recommends. And class 1
      doesn't need trials; evidence from trials would be LOE A or B-R, which this isn't.
  - q: >-
      In a hardware store in Amarillo a customer collapses and isn't breathing normally. The cashier
      did an AHA course two years ago, and a colleague has 911 on speaker. There's vomit around the man's
      mouth, she has no mask, and she isn't willing to give him breaths. Under the AHA's 2025
      guidelines, what should she do?
    options:
      - Continuous compressions, since compressions alone are appropriate when she's unwilling
      - Give breaths anyway, since the AHA grades breaths COR 1 for anyone who has been trained
      - Wait for a pocket mask to be found, and then start CPR with breaths in the first cycle
      - Wipe his mouth and give two breaths, then carry on with compressions only after that
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The AHA's 2025 adult guideline says all lay rescuers should give chest compressions (COR 1,
      LOE B-NR), that for lay rescuers trained in CPR breaths are "reasonable" (COR 2a, LOE B-R), and
      that "a chest compression–only approach is appropriate if lay rescuers are untrained or
      unwilling to provide breaths." Her training makes breaths an option, graded 2a, not a duty
      graded 1. ILCOR's 2025 line says the same from its side: breaths are suggested for bystanders
      who are "trained, able, and willing", all three. A mask is reasonable when immediately
      available (AHA 2025, COR 2a), and the AHA warns that putting on protective equipment may delay
      CPR, so waiting for one costs compressions. Two token breaths appear in no guideline read here.

  # --- The defibrillator (L5) ---
  - q: >-
      At a convention centre in Salt Lake City, two of you are using an AED on a man in cardiac
      arrest. It has just delivered a shock. The other rescuer wants to pause and watch his chest for
      a few seconds to see whether the shock worked before anyone touches him again. Under the AHA's
      2025 guidelines, what happens now?
    options:
      - Pause for about ten seconds to check, since if the shock worked, CPR could do him harm
      - Take the pads off, since the shock was the one job the machine had and it has done it
      - Check for a pulse at his neck, and restart compressions only if you can't find one
      - Restart compressions at once, since a shock can stop VF without a pumping heart after it
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The AHA's 2025 guideline says "it is reasonable to immediately resume chest compressions after
      shock administration rather than pause CPR to perform a postshock rhythm check" (COR 2a, LOE
      B-R), and RCUK 2025 says the same: "After the shock has been delivered, immediately restart
      chest compressions." The AHA's reason is that ending VF "may not result in a perfusing rhythm",
      and it reports two trials, enrolling more than 1,000 patients between them, that found no
      gain in survival from pausing to check, and observational studies linking a delay to less
      return of circulation. The pads stay on, because the AED analyses again after a set interval
      (RCUK 2025). And lay rescuers don't check a pulse: the AHA's 2025 adult guideline bases their recognition on response and
      breathing instead.

  # --- Children and babies (L6) ---
  - q: >-
      At a soft-play centre in Exeter a five-year-old is found unresponsive and not breathing. The guest
      who reaches him first is a children's nurse who is specifically trained in paediatric basic life
      support, and another guest is calling 999 on speaker. Under RCUK's 2025 guidelines, what
      sequence does the nurse follow?
    options:
      - Thirty compressions first and then two breaths, the same order as for a collapsed adult
      - Five breaths first, then 30 compressions to 2 breaths, which is what call handlers coach
      - Five breaths first, then 15 compressions to 2 breaths, the ratio her training allows
      - Continuous compressions only, since breaths would slow down the pushing a child needs
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      RCUK's 2025 paediatric guideline says "Give five initial rescue breaths", then "Immediately
      proceed with chest compressions", and "Use a compression-to-ventilation ratio of 15:2 if you
      are specifically trained in PBLS; otherwise, use a ratio of 30:2." She is, so it's 15:2. The
      30:2 with five breaths first is what RCUK's call handlers coach for untrained callers and those
      trained only in adult CPR. Compressions first is the AHA's 2025 order (COR 2b, LOE C-LD), not
      RCUK's. And RCUK wants breaths given in children of all ages, with compression-only CPR the
      fallback for a rescuer who can't or won't give them.
  - q: >-
      In a flat in Carlisle a father is about to give his three-month-old daughter the five rescue
      breaths that RCUK's 2025 guidelines start with. She's unresponsive and not breathing, and the
      call handler is on speaker. How should he give them?
    options:
      - Head level with a slight tilt, her nose pinched, his mouth over hers, until her chest rises
      - Head tipped well back, his mouth over her mouth and nose, gentle breaths till her chest rises
      - Head level, his mouth over her nose alone with her mouth held shut, until her chest rises
      - Head level with a slight tilt, his mouth over both her mouth and nose, till her chest rises
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      RCUK's 2025 paediatric guideline keeps an infant's head in the neutral position, tilted only
      slightly, with the chin lifted by two fingers on the chin bone, and says to blow steadily into
      the infant's mouth and nose together for about a second, enough to make the chest visibly
      rise. Pinching her nose and sealing over her mouth alone is the adult breath, and no guideline
      read here gives a baby a nose-only breath either: for a baby it's mouth and nose together. RCUK
      gives more head tilt to older children and full extension to adolescents, as in adults, so a
      head tipped well back is the wrong end of that scale, however gentle the breaths. And each
      breath is just enough to see the chest rise, because too much air can go into the stomach
      (AHA 2025).

  # --- Breathing but not awake (L7) ---
  - q: >-
      At a sleepover in Ludlow, a 9-year-old can't be woken. There's no sign she fell or is hurt,
      her breathing is quiet and regular, and the call handler on 999 agrees it's normal, so she's in
      the recovery position. The ambulance is 20 minutes away, and her mum wants to go downstairs to
      find her medical card. Under RCUK's 2025 paediatric guidance, how closely is her breathing
      watched?
    options:
      - Every five minutes or so, since the side position keeps her airway open between checks
      - Continuously, or at least every minute, with someone staying beside her the whole time
      - Continuously, rolling her onto her back every few minutes to check her breathing properly
      - Every half hour, turning her onto her other side each time to protect her lower arm
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      RCUK's 2025 paediatric guideline says to check a child's breathing continuously, or at least
      every minute, and the ERC's steps for the recovery position say not to leave the person unless
      you have to go for help. The AHA and American Red Cross 2024 guideline gives the reason: the
      recovery position "is associated with delayed recognition of respiratory arrest and delayed
      initiation of chest compressions." So the side position is no reason to look away between
      checks; missing a change is its known risk. Her mum can go while someone else watches.
      Rolling her onto her back to check undoes the position, and no guideline read here asks for
      it: you watch her chest and listen where she lies. No source this course read sets a half-hour
      turn. If her breathing stops being quiet and regular, she goes onto her back for CPR (RCUK
      2025).
  - q: >-
      In a motel room in Las Cruces a man is found unresponsive and not breathing at all, with tiny
      pupils. His friend sprayed naloxone into his nose two minutes ago and says to give it a few
      more minutes to work. 911 is on speaker. Under the AHA's 2025 guidelines, what should happen
      now?
    options:
      - Start CPR with breaths now, since naloxone is unlikely to help unless CPR is moving blood
      - Wait a few more minutes, since the AHA says nasal naloxone can take several minutes to work
      - Give him a second dose now and then wait again, since a repeat dose restarts a heart faster
      - Roll him onto his side while the spray takes effect, so that he can't choke on his tongue
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      He's unresponsive and not breathing, and for an opioid-associated emergency the AHA's 2025
      guideline says lay rescuers should give CPR with breaths (COR 1, LOE B-NR), and that neither
      the call nor CPR waits for naloxone (COR 1, LOE C-EO). Its reason: "Opioid antagonists are
      extremely unlikely to benefit an adult or child in cardiac arrest who is not receiving CPR and
      do not reverse VF." The AHA does say naloxone may take several minutes and repeated doses, but
      that is about reversing a stopped breath, and a bystander can't tell a stopped breath from a
      stopped heart without the pulse check lay rescuers don't do. In cardiac arrest the AHA calls
      naloxone something that "may be reasonable" if it doesn't interfere with CPR (COR 2b), and
      nothing read here says a dose restarts a heart. The side position is for someone breathing
      normally, and CPR can't be given on the side (AHA 2025).

  # --- Choking (L8) ---
  - q: >-
      At a pub quiz in Cambridge a man chokes on a piece of pork pie. At first he coughed loudly,
      but now his coughs are weak and almost silent, he can't answer when you ask if he's
      choking, and his lips are turning bluish. Under RCUK's 2025 guidelines, what do you do?
    options:
      - Keep telling him to cough, since any cough at all means the blockage is still mild
      - Wait until he stops coughing completely, since back blows only start after that
      - Get 999 called and give up to five back blows, since his cough has stopped working
      - Give abdominal thrusts at once, since a weak cough means back blows won't be enough
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      RCUK's first aid guideline starts back blows "If the person is unable to cough or the cough
      becomes ineffective", and a cough that has gone weak and nearly silent, with no words and a
      colour change, is the severe obstruction the AHA describes as "weak or absent coughing,
      inability to speak, changes in color (cyanosis)". Watching a mild cough was for exactly this
      moment, so a cough of some kind no longer means mild, and waiting for it to stop altogether
      loses time. Abdominal thrusts come after the back blows, not instead of them (RCUK 2025).
      RCUK's key points say to call for help early, on speaker if you're alone.
  - q: >-
      A man who lives alone in Grand Island, Nebraska, chokes on a piece of steak at his kitchen
      table. He can't speak or cough, and his mobile phone is beside his plate. Under the American
      Red Cross's advice, what should he do?
    options:
      - Call 911, then wait by the unlocked front door, since thrusts on himself could injure him
      - Call 911 even though he can't speak, then give himself thrusts or use a chair back
      - Give himself thrusts first, and call 911 only if the food still hasn't come out after
      - Throw his back against a door frame, and call 911 only once the food has come out
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The American Red Cross says someone choking alone should call 911 from a landline or a
      GPS-enabled mobile, because even if they can't speak "the open line will cause the
      dispatcher to send help". Then they can give themselves abdominal thrusts with their own
      fist, or press the abdomen against "the back of a chair or a railing", avoiding sharp edges.
      So the call comes first, not after the thrusts or once the food is out, and after it he acts
      rather than waiting at the door, because the page's next step is the thrusts.
      Throwing his back against a door frame is in no source read here. Neither the AHA's 2025
      guideline nor RCUK's makes a recommendation on choking alone, so this is the American Red
      Cross's advice, with no evidence cited.

  # --- Severe bleeding and shock (L9) ---
  - q: >-
      At a lumber yard in Boise, Idaho, a chainsaw kicks back and cuts deep into the middle of a
      worker's calf. Blood keeps spurting through the pad however hard you press. The yard's bleed
      kit has a commercial tourniquet, and 911 is on speaker. Under the AHA/Red Cross 2024
      guideline and the Stop the Bleed course, what now?
    options:
      - Tourniquet 2 to 3 inches above the wound, clear of the knee, tightened till the flow stops
      - Keep pressing and raise the leg on a crate above his heart, since the height slows the flow
      - Tourniquet high on the thigh, left loose enough that he can still feel and move his foot
      - Press hard into his groin crease instead, to shut off the artery that feeds the whole leg
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The AHA/Red Cross 2024 guideline: "For life-threatening extremity bleeding, a tourniquet
      should be applied and tightened until the bleeding stops" (COR 1, LOE B-NR), and blood
      spurting through firm pressure is that bleeding. The Stop the Bleed course gives the place,
      "Apply 2 to 3 inches above wound", and "Do not place over the elbow or knee". A band left
      loose can stop blood draining out of the leg without stopping it flowing in, so the wound
      bleeds more (Richey); tourniquets hurt, and no source treats that as a reason to loosen. Nothing
      in the AHA/Red Cross guideline, ILCOR's review or RCUK's tells you to raise a bleeding limb;
      that is the NHS cuts page's line for everyday cuts. Pressing in the groin is a pressure point,
      which the AHA calls of uncertain use (COR 2b, LOE C-LD) and ILCOR recommends against. In the
      UK, RCUK 2025 gives "5-7cm above the injury, but not over a joint".
  - q: >-
      In Durham a woman falls down a flight of concrete steps. Her lower leg is bent at an odd angle,
      and a deep cut on it bled heavily until pressure controlled it. She's pale, cold and clammy
      but alert, and 999 is on speaker. Under St John Ambulance's pages, how should she lie?
    options:
      - On her back with both legs raised on a bag, since signs of shock mean raising the legs
      - Propped sitting up with a sweet drink, to bring her blood pressure back up as you wait
      - Lying down with her legs left where they are and kept warm, since a leg looks broken
      - On her side in the recovery position, since anyone with signs of shock goes on their side
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      St John's shock page lies a casualty down and raises their legs, but its fractures page says
      to treat for shock without raising the legs "if either are suspected to be broken or there is
      injury to the pelvis or a hip", and a leg bent at an odd angle may well be broken. So she lies
      down with her legs as they are, kept warm and reassured. RCUK's 2025 first aid guideline has
      no section on the position for shock, which is why the UK answer comes from these pages. The
      American Red Cross says nothing to eat or drink, and nothing read in the UK says otherwise.
      The recovery position isn't St John's step for shock, and RCUK says not to move a person into
      it after trauma.

  # --- Burns, cuts and small injuries (L10) ---
  - q: >-
      In a flat in Preston, a man scalds the back of his hand with steam from a pan, holds it under
      the tap for a few seconds, and carries on cooking. Two hours later it still stings, and the
      skin is red but not blistered. He wonders whether cooling it now is any use. Under the NHS's
      advice, what should he do?
    options:
      - Nothing more, since cooling only helps in the first few minutes after the burn happens
      - Cool running water for 10 minutes, since after two hours a shorter cooling is all it needs
      - Hold a bag of ice on it for 10 minutes, since after two hours water is too gentle to help
      - Hold it under cool running water for 20 minutes now, since it's still within 3 hours
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The NHS says to hold a burn or scald under cool running water for 20 minutes, "as soon as
      possible, within 3 hours of it happening". Two hours in, he's inside that window, and the
      British Burn Association gives the same 20 minutes within 3 hours. Neither says cooling stops
      helping after the first few minutes, and neither shortens the 20 minutes for a late start; the
      American Red Cross's 5 to 20 is the US figure, and this question asks for the NHS's. The
      British Burn Association and St John say no ice.
  - q: >-
      In a backyard in Wichita, a woman is hammering a nail when something flies up into her eye.
      She rinses it with tap water for several minutes, but it still feels as if something is in
      there. Under the AHA/Red Cross 2024 guideline, what now?
    options:
      - Keep rinsing without rubbing, and see a doctor tomorrow if it still feels scratchy then
      - Get medical care now, and don't rub it, since it still feels as if something is in there
      - Lift it off her eye with the corner of a damp tissue, since rinsing hasn't cleared it out
      - Cover it with a pad and let her sleep, since tears overnight will wash out what's left
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The guideline sends a person to medical care at once (COR 1, LOE C-LD) after an eye injury at
      speed, as from nailing, and when something still feels as if it's there after rinsing. It
      also says not to rub the eye (COR 1, LOE C-EO), because rubbing may make a scrape on its
      surface, or a wound that has gone in, worse. Not rubbing is right, but waiting until tomorrow
      or overnight ignores the first rule. Lifting a speck off with a damp tissue corner is St John's
      step for dust or grit sitting on the surface, and here the nail and the lasting feeling both
      say get care now.

  # --- Bones, joints, head and spine (L11) ---

    options:
      - Care at home with paracetamol, since he didn't black out and he's chatting normally
      - Get advice from NHS 111, since he's alert and the fluid could just be from a cold
      - Call 999 only if the fluid is still running in an hour, since it may yet be a cold
      - Call 999, because clear fluid from the nose after hitting his head is on its list
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The NHS page's 999 list includes someone who has hit their head and "has clear fluid coming
      from their ears or nose", and it makes no exception for someone who seems alert or for a fluid
      that might have another cause, so waiting an hour to see is not on the page. Its 111 list is
      for being sick, dizziness, blood thinners, alcohol or drugs, and other worrying symptoms. Care
      at home with paracetamol is the page's advice for a minor head injury that doesn't need
      hospital. In the US the AHA/Red Cross 2024 list says "such as", and the American Red Cross
      page marks fluid from the nose or ears for a 911 call.

  # --- Heart attack and stroke (L12) ---
  - q: >-
      In Boulder, a 57-year-old woman has had a heavy, squeezing pain in her chest for fifteen
      minutes. She's alert, isn't allergic to aspirin, no doctor has told her not to take it, and
      911 is on speaker. The only aspirin in the house is a bottle of 81 mg low-dose tablets. Under
      the AHA/Red Cross 2024 guideline and the American Red Cross page, what about aspirin?
    options:
      - Give her one 81 mg tablet, since the low-dose kind is the one meant for the heart
      - Encourage her to chew and swallow two to four of the tablets, 162 to 324 mg in all
      - Wait for EMS, since the guideline's dose needs a regular-strength 325 mg tablet
      - Encourage her to chew eight of them, since 648 mg works more strongly than 325
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The guideline says a first aid provider may encourage an alert adult with nontraumatic chest
      pain to chew and swallow 162 to 325 mg of aspirin, unless they have a known aspirin allergy or
      a health professional has told them not to take it (COR 2b, LOE B-NR). The American Red Cross
      page spells that out as two to four low-dose (81 mg) tablets or one regular-strength (325 mg)
      tablet, so low-dose tablets are one of its two ways and there's no reason to wait. One tablet
      is below the range and eight is far above it. In the UK the figure is 300 mg, which the person
      takes herself after the 999 call (RCUK 2025).

  # --- Anaphylaxis and asthma (L13) ---
  - q: >-
      At a trailhead outside Missoula, a hiker stung by a hornet used his own auto-injector seven
      minutes ago. His face is still swelling and his breathing is no better, and 911 says the
      ambulance is about 30 minutes out. He has a second auto-injector. Under the AHA/Red Cross
      2024 guideline, what fits?
    options:
      - Hold off, since the US guideline allows one dose before EMS takes over
      - A second may be considered, but not until 15 minutes after the first
      - Use the second now, since he hasn't responded and EMS is a long way off
      - Keep it until he stops responding, then give it and start CPR as well
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The guideline says a repeat dose "may be considered" if a person doesn't respond to the first
      dose and the arrival of EMS will exceed 5 to 10 minutes (COR 2b, LOE B-NR). Seven minutes on
      he's no better and the crew is half an hour away, so both conditions are met, and a weak class
      is still the guideline's answer for him. Nothing in it limits him to one dose or sets a
      15-minute wait, and holding the device until he stops responding turns a treatment into a
      resuscitation. In the UK the rule is a clock: no better five minutes after the first dose, give
      a second, ideally in the opposite leg (RCUK 2025).

  # --- Seizures, low blood sugar and poisoning (L14) ---
  - q: >-
      In a library in Hexham, a man in his fifties falls from his chair and starts jerking. A card in
      his wallet says he has epilepsy and that his seizures usually last about a minute. You've moved
      the chairs back and cushioned his head, and he has now been jerking for three and a half
      minutes. Under the NHS seizure page, what fits?
    options:
      - Not yet, since the NHS line is 5 minutes and he's still a minute and a half short of it
      - Not at all, since he has epilepsy, and people with epilepsy don't need an ambulance
      - Hold his arms still so he doesn't hurt himself, and call 999 if it passes 5 minutes
      - Call 999 now, since it has lasted longer than is usual for him, which is on its list
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The NHS page's list for 999 includes a seizure that lasts longer than is usual for the person,
      or more than 5 minutes if you don't know how long their seizures usually last. His card gives
      you his usual, about a minute, so at three and a half minutes the first half applies, and the
      5 minutes is only for when you don't know. The NHS says people with epilepsy don't always need
      an ambulance or hospital for a seizure, which is not the same as never, and its list still
      applies to them. Holding someone down during a seizure is COR 3: Harm in the AHA/Red Cross 2024
      guideline, because restraint won't stop it and may injure them. In the US the guideline's line
      is more than 5 minutes (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1, LOE C-LD), which is why this question names
      the NHS.

  # --- Heat, cold, bites and stings (L15) ---
  - q: >-
      At rugby training in Gloucester on a hot afternoon, a 19-year-old forward starts stumbling and
      can't say where he is. He's awake and can walk with help. The club has a cold-water plunge tub
      the players use after matches, and a coach wants to drive him to A&E, 25 minutes away, straight
      away. Under RCUK's 2025 first aid guideline, what fits?
    options:
      - 999, then into the plunge tub neck down with people holding him, cooling before any transfer
      - Into the coach's car at once, since the hospital can cool him faster than anything at the club
      - A litre of cold water to drink first, since cooling from the inside is quickest, then the tub
      - Shade and a fan, and 999 only if he's still confused after resting there for half an hour
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Confusion in the heat makes this heatstroke. RCUK 2025 calls whole-body, neck-down immersion in
      cold water, 1 to 26 °C, "the gold standard", and its order is cool first, transfer second, with
      cooling kept up on the way; without a thermometer, 15 minutes or until his thinking clears. The
      NHS says 999 for heatstroke, and the ERC's text says call as cooling begins. Driving off first
      puts the cooling after a 25-minute journey, the order RCUK and the ERC rule out, and the ERC
      links survival without complications to cooling at 0.15 °C a minute or faster. Nothing by mouth
      while he's confused: that's lesson 14's rule, which lesson 15 applies to heatstroke, and no
      text read here cools from the inside. A fan is one of RCUK's alternatives when there's no
      water, but no better after 30 minutes is the line for heat exhaustion, and his confusion has
      already crossed it. In the US the AHA/Red Cross 2024 guideline also gives cold-water immersion
      (COR 2a, LOE C-LD) and the call (COR 1, LOE C-EO).

:::callout Before you need this
This course is education, not a certificate, and not medical advice about anyone's own situation. In an emergency, call your local emergency number first (911 in the US, 999 or 112 in the UK, 112 across the EU), put the phone on speaker, and do what the call handler tells you. Reading can teach you what to do and in what order. It can't teach your hands how hard to push or how a back blow should feel, so take a hands-on class with a manikin and an instructor, and practise between classes. Each instruction here names the guideline and year it comes from, and guidance changes.
:::

Every question puts you somewhere the lessons didn't: a new person, a new place, a new line from a
guideline. The people and what happens to them are invented; the rules in the explanations are the real ones the
lessons read, each with the body and year it comes from.

Where a safety step differs between the two countries, the question names the country whose rule
it's asking about, and that's the rule to answer by. Where a question names neither, or says both,
