  # --- Helping, and the law (L1) ---
  - q: >-
      In a shopping centre in Birmingham you've been giving CPR for four minutes to a man who
      collapsed by the escalators. A woman kneels beside you, says she's an off-duty paramedic, and
      offers to take over. What does the UK law booklet this course read say about carrying on?
      (General information, not legal advice.)
    options:
      - Carry on alone, since by volunteering you took on a duty to finish until the ambulance comes
      - Stop and walk away, since touching him again after she's arrived exposes you to a claim
      - Let her take over, and keep helping under her direction if she wants you, not on your own
      - Carry on alone, since the 2015 Act protects anyone acting heroically, whatever they do
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      RCUK's booklet on CPR and the law, in its April 2018 edition, says it wouldn't be reasonable
      for an unqualified person to carry on alone if "a professional rescuer were present or arrived
      at the scene and offered to help", though you may keep helping under their direction. The duty
      of care you take on by volunteering is to help "as far as they are able", and nothing in it
      means turning down better help. Handing over doesn't expose you either: the same booklet
      reports no successful UK claim against anyone who came to help. And the Social Action,
      Responsibility and Heroism Act 2015 tells a court in England and Wales what to have regard to;
      it grants no immunity, whatever you do. This is general information, not legal advice.

  # --- Recognising arrest, the call, 999 or 111 (L2) ---
  - q: >-
      In a sports hall in Coventry a badminton player in his thirties stops mid-rally, sits down and
      topples sideways. His eyes are open and he's breathing fast and heavily, but he doesn't respond
      when you shake his shoulders and shout his name. Someone is calling 999 on speaker. Under
      RCUK's 2025 adult guidelines, what does that breathing tell you?
    options:
      - It can be a sign of cardiac arrest in an athlete, so if there's any doubt, start CPR
      - He's winded from the rally, so sit him up and let his breathing settle before you act
      - He's breathing, so roll him onto his side and wait for the breathing to slow down
      - Nothing yet, since RCUK counts slow gasps as a sign of arrest but not fast breathing
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      RCUK 2025 added this for athletes: "early after the onset of cardiac arrest, athletes may
      display a near-normal or panting breathing pattern", and it lists panting beside agonal gasping
      among the patterns that "must be recognised as signs of cardiac arrest". So slow gasps aren't
      the only sign, and fast breathing doesn't rule arrest out. His open eyes don't either: the
      ERC's 2025 guide for the public says people who arrest during vigorous exercise often have
      their eyes open. A man who doesn't respond to you isn't simply winded, and rolling him onto
      his side would leave a person who may be in arrest in a position where effective CPR can't be
      given (AHA 2025). RCUK 2025's rule for doubt is to assume cardiac arrest and start CPR, and the
      call handler on speaker helps you judge the breathing.
  - q: >-
      Your phone's battery died an hour ago. On an allotment in Lincolnshire you find your neighbour
      collapsed between the vegetable beds, not responding and not breathing normally. You shout for
      help and nobody answers; the nearest phone is in a house across the lane. Under RCUK's 2025
      adult guidelines, what do you do?
    options:
      - Stay and do CPR until someone passes, since leaving him means minutes without compressions
      - Go and call 999 as fast as you can, then come back and start CPR if he still needs it
      - Feel for a pulse at his neck first, and go for help only if you can't find a pulse there
      - Roll him onto his side so his airway stays open, and then go across to the house to call
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      RCUK 2025 says to shout for help, and then: "If you think no one will come to help, then you
      will have to leave the person and call 999. Do this as quickly as possible." When you come
      back, if he's still unresponsive and not breathing normally, you start CPR at once. Staying
      feels kinder, but until someone calls, no ambulance, coach or defibrillator is on its way, and
      RCUK's advice is to make the hard choice fast. A pulse check isn't in RCUK's steps for the
      public at all, and the AHA's 2025 adult guideline bases lay recognition on response and
      breathing rather than a pulse. And the side position is for someone breathing normally, which
      he isn't; effective CPR can't be given on the side (AHA 2025).
  - q: >-
      Your father in Derby has had headaches on and off for two days, and they're worse at home,
      where an old gas fire runs all evening. Tonight he's suddenly confused and can't tell you what
      day it is, though he's awake and breathing easily. Under the NHS carbon monoxide page, what
      fits best?
    options:
      - Call NHS 111 for advice, since he's awake and breathing easily and so it isn't an emergency
      - Open a window, turn off the fire, and book him in to see a GP at some point later this week
      - Let him sleep it off in a warm room, and call for help only if his headache gets any worse
      - Get him outside, turn the fire off if you can, and call 999 because of the sudden confusion
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The NHS carbon monoxide page lists headache and confusion among the symptoms, and its advice
      for someone who thinks they may have symptoms is to stop using the appliance if they can, go
      outside, and call NHS 111. But it says to call 999 or go to A&E if someone who has been exposed
      has "suddenly become confused", along with finding it hard to breathe, losing consciousness,
      feeling weak, or chest or muscle pain. Sudden confusion is on that list, so this is a 999 call
      even though he's awake and breathing. A GP later in the week, or a night's sleep in the same
      house, leaves him breathing the gas in the meantime. Get yourself outside too: the page's
      advice to go outside applies to anyone who may have breathed it.

  # --- Pushing on the chest (L3) ---
  - q: >-
      A man in Omaha practises compressions on a cushion at about 140 a minute, because he read that
      one study found hearts restarted more often at 121 to 140 than at slower rates. Under the AHA's
      2025 guidelines, what would you tell him?
    options:
      - Keep to 140, since getting the heart to restart is the first thing CPR is there to do
      - Slow right down to about 80, so the heart has time to refill between each of the pushes
      - Slow to 100 to 120, since the AHA set that range by who survived, not by hearts restarting
      - Any rate is fine if the depth is right, since the AHA sets no range of its own for rate
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The AHA's 2025 guidelines give 100 to 120 a minute (COR 2a, LOE B-NR), and RCUK's 2025
      guidelines give the same. The AHA's supporting text reports three observational studies, of
      more than 13,700 patients, that linked 100 to 119 a minute to better survival to hospital
      discharge than slower or faster rates. It also reports the study he read, and says the writing
      group "placed higher value on survival compared with ROSC", return of spontaneous circulation.
      A heart restarting and a person leaving hospital alive are different outcomes, and the range
      was set by the second. Slower rates carried the same survival penalty in those studies; the
      chest coming back up between pushes is recoil, which both guidelines ask for separately. And
      the range is there, graded 2a.
  - q: >-
      A woman who is about eight months pregnant collapses in a cinema foyer. She doesn't respond and
      isn't breathing normally. A member of staff has the emergency number on speaker, and a second
      customer is free to help. Under the AHA's and RCUK's 2025 guidelines, as this course reads them
      for a bystander, what comes first?
    options:
      - Wedge her onto her left side on a folded coat, since a tilt is what both guidelines prefer
      - Compressions at once with her flat on her back, and tell the call handler she's pregnant
      - Hold off on compressions until the crew arrive, since pushing on her chest could harm her
      - Roll her into the recovery position on her left side, to take the weight off her big veins
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      A pregnant woman in cardiac arrest gets CPR as anyone else would, and RCUK 2025 tells rescuers
      to consider pregnancy in any collapsed woman of childbearing age. Later in pregnancy both
      guidelines add manual displacement of the womb to her left by hand while she stays flat (AHA
      2025, COR 1, LOE C-LD, once the top of the womb is at or above the navel; RCUK 2025, above 20
      weeks), and both prefer it to tilting her, because in simulation trials compressions were
      better on a body lying flat (AHA 2025). Both texts are written for resuscitation teams and give
      no bystander version, so this course's reading is that nothing delays the compressions, and
      the call handler says whether and how the second customer does the displacement. Holding off
      leaves her with no circulation at all. And effective CPR can't be given on the side (AHA 2025).

  # --- Breaths, and reading a label (L4) ---
  - q: >-
      A friend who practises compressions on a sofa cushion finds this line in the AHA's 2025
      guideline on resuscitation education: "The usefulness of alternative objects to train laypeople
      in chest compressions, when compared to a manikin, is not well established." (COR 2b, LOE
      C-LD). Using the AHA's Table 1, what does the label tell her?
    options:
      - That a cushion has been shown not to work, since class 2b marks a practice found useless
      - That she should stop at once, since any class below 1 marks a practice the AHA says to avoid
      - That a cushion equals a manikin, since the rating rests on several high-quality trials
      - A weak class on limited data, so whether a cushion trains as well as a manikin is unclear
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      In the AHA's Table 1, class 2b is "WEAK", with benefit greater than or equal to risk, and
      "Usefulness/effectiveness is unknown/unclear/uncertain or not well-established" is one of the
      phrases it gives for that class. LOE C-LD means studies "with limitations of design or
      execution". So the line says the evidence can't yet tell whether practising on an object trains
      you as well as a manikin. A practice found to do no good would be class 3: No Benefit, and a
      2b isn't that. Nothing in the table tells anyone to stop a 2b. And several high-quality trials
      would be LOE A, which this isn't. The course's own advice fits the label: a cushion is a start,
      and a class with a manikin is where you learn what the depth feels like.
  - q: >-
      In a hardware store in Tulsa a customer collapses and isn't breathing normally. The cashier did
      an AHA course two years ago, and a colleague has 911 on speaker. There's vomit around the man's
      mouth, she has no mask, and she isn't willing to give him breaths. Under the AHA's 2025
      guidelines, what should she do?
    options:
      - Continuous compressions, since compressions alone are appropriate when she's unwilling
      - Give breaths anyway, since the AHA grades breaths COR 1 for anyone who has been trained
      - Wait for a pocket mask to be found, and then start CPR with breaths in the first cycle
      - Wipe his mouth and give two breaths, then carry on with compressions only after that
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The AHA's 2025 adult guideline says all lay rescuers should give chest compressions (COR 1,
      LOE B-NR), that for lay rescuers trained in CPR breaths are "reasonable" (COR 2a, LOE B-R), and
      that "a chest compression–only approach is appropriate if lay rescuers are untrained or
      unwilling to provide breaths." Her training makes breaths an option, graded 2a, not a duty
      graded 1. ILCOR's 2025 line says the same from its side: breaths are suggested for bystanders
      who are "trained, able, and willing", all three. A mask is reasonable when immediately
      available (AHA 2025, COR 2a), and the AHA warns that putting on protective equipment may delay
      CPR, so waiting for one costs compressions. Two token breaths appear in no guideline read here.

  # --- The defibrillator (L5) ---
  - q: >-
      At a convention centre in Salt Lake City, two of you are using an AED on a man in cardiac
      arrest. It has just delivered a shock. The other rescuer wants to pause and watch his chest for
      a few seconds to see whether the shock worked before anyone touches him again. Under the AHA's
      2025 guidelines, what happens now?
    options:
      - Pause for about ten seconds to check, since if the shock worked, CPR could do him harm
      - Take the pads off, since the shock was the one job the machine had and it has done it
      - Check for a pulse at his neck, and restart compressions only if you can't find one
      - Restart compressions at once, since a shock can stop VF without a pumping heart after it
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The AHA's 2025 guideline says "it is reasonable to immediately resume chest compressions after
      shock administration rather than pause CPR to perform a postshock rhythm check" (COR 2a, LOE
      B-R), and RCUK 2025 says the same: "After the shock has been delivered, immediately restart
      chest compressions." The AHA's reason is that ending VF "may not result in a perfusing rhythm",
      and it reports two trials, enrolling more than 1,000 patients between them, that found no
      gain in survival from pausing to check, and observational studies linking a delay to less
      return of circulation. The
      pads stay on, because the AED analyses again after a set interval (RCUK 2025). And lay rescuers
      don't check a pulse: the AHA's 2025 adult guideline bases their recognition on response and
      breathing instead.

  # --- Children and babies (L6) ---
  - q: >-
      At a birthday party in Exeter a five-year-old is found unresponsive and not breathing. The guest
      who reaches him first is a children's nurse who is specifically trained in paediatric basic life
      support, and another guest is calling 999 on speaker. Under RCUK's 2025 guidelines, what
      sequence does the nurse follow?
    options:
      - Thirty compressions first and then two breaths, the same order as for a collapsed adult
      - Five breaths first, then 30 compressions to 2 breaths, which is what call handlers coach
      - Five breaths first, then 15 compressions to 2 breaths, the ratio her training allows
      - Continuous compressions only, since breaths would slow down the pushing a child needs
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      RCUK's 2025 paediatric guideline says "Give five initial rescue breaths", then "Immediately
      proceed with chest compressions", and "Use a compression-to-ventilation ratio of 15:2 if you
      are specifically trained in PBLS; otherwise, use a ratio of 30:2." She is, so it's 15:2. The
      30:2 with five breaths first is what RCUK's call handlers coach for untrained callers and those
      trained only in adult CPR. Compressions first is the AHA's 2025 order (COR 2b, LOE C-LD), not
      RCUK's. And RCUK wants breaths given in children of all ages, with compression-only CPR the
      fallback for a rescuer who can't or won't give them.
  - q: >-
      In a flat in Carlisle a father is about to give his three-month-old daughter the five rescue
      breaths that RCUK's 2025 guidelines start with. She's unresponsive and not breathing, and the
      call handler is on speaker. How should he give them?
    options:
      - Tip her head right back as for an adult, pinch her nose, and breathe into her mouth only
      - Give short, hard puffs into her mouth, so the air gets past anything that's in her throat
      - Seal his mouth over her nose, hold her mouth shut, and give a full deep breath each time
      - Head level with a slight tilt, his mouth over her mouth and nose, until her chest rises
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      RCUK's 2025 paediatric guideline keeps an infant's head in the neutral position, tilted only
      slightly, with the chin lifted by two fingers on the chin bone, and says to blow steadily into
      the infant's mouth and nose for about a second, enough to make the chest visibly rise. It
      gives more head tilt to older children and full extension to adolescents, as in adults, so the
      adult tilt is the wrong end of that scale. Hard puffs and a full deep breath break the rule for
      every breath in this course, just enough to see the chest rise, because too much air can go
      into the stomach (AHA 2025). No guideline read here gives a baby a nose-only breath.

  # --- Breathing but not awake (L7) ---
  - q: >-
      At a summer fair in Norfolk you find a woman lying on the grass behind a tent. She doesn't
      respond when you shake her shoulders and shout, and there's no sign that she fell or is hurt.
      You call 999 on speaker. Her breathing is quiet and regular, and the call handler agrees it's
      normal. Under RCUK's 2025 guidelines, what do you do while you wait?
    options:
      - Start chest compressions, since RCUK treats anyone who won't respond as being in arrest
      - Put her in the recovery position, and keep watching her breathing until help arrives
      - Leave her flat on her back, since the recovery position is really for injured people
      - Sit her up against the tent pole, so that she can't choke if she's sick while you wait
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      RCUK's 2025 first aid guideline puts adults and children with a decreased level of
      responsiveness who don't meet the criteria for CPR into a side-lying recovery position, and she
      is breathing normally with no sign of injury. The US guidance agrees: the AHA and American Red
      Cross 2024 first aid guideline calls the recovery position reasonable for decreased alertness
      of non-traumatic cause with normal breathing (COR 2a, LOE C-LD). RCUK 2025 says to suspect
      arrest in anyone unresponsive, which is why you called, but CPR is for someone not breathing
      normally, or when you're in doubt. Injury is one of the two cases in which RCUK says not to use
      the position, not the case it's for. Sitting her up is not the position either guideline gives
      here. And keep watching: the AHA and Red Cross 2024 guideline says the recovery position is
      associated with delayed recognition of respiratory arrest.
  - q: >-
      In a motel room in Albuquerque a man is found unresponsive and not breathing at all, with tiny
      pupils. His friend sprayed naloxone into his nose two minutes ago and says to give it a few
      more minutes to work. 911 is on speaker. Under the AHA's 2025 guidelines, what should happen
      now?
    options:
      - Start CPR with breaths now, since naloxone is unlikely to help unless CPR is moving blood
      - Wait a few more minutes, since the AHA says nasal naloxone can take several minutes to work
      - Give him a second dose now and then wait again, since a repeat dose restarts a heart faster
      - Roll him onto his side while the spray takes effect, so that he can't choke on his tongue
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      He's unresponsive and not breathing, and for an opioid-associated emergency the AHA's 2025
      guideline says lay rescuers should give CPR with breaths (COR 1, LOE B-NR), and that neither
      the call nor CPR waits for naloxone (COR 1, LOE C-EO). Its reason: "Opioid antagonists are
      extremely unlikely to benefit an adult or child in cardiac arrest who is not receiving CPR and
      do not reverse VF." The AHA does say naloxone may take several minutes and repeated doses, but
      that is about reversing a stopped breath, and a bystander can't tell a stopped breath from a
      stopped heart without the pulse check lay rescuers don't do. In cardiac arrest the AHA calls
      naloxone something that "may be reasonable" if it doesn't interfere with CPR (COR 2b), and
      nothing read here says a dose restarts a heart. The side position is for someone breathing
      normally, and CPR can't be given on the side (AHA 2025).
  # --- Choking (L8) ---
  - q: >-
      At a pub quiz in Cambridge a man chokes on a chip. At first he coughed loudly, but now his
      coughs are weak and almost silent, he can't answer when you ask if he's choking, and his lips
      are turning bluish. Under RCUK's 2025 guidelines, what do you do?
    options:
      - Keep telling him to cough, since any cough at all means the blockage is still mild
      - Wait until he stops coughing completely, since back blows only start after that
      - Get 999 called and give up to five back blows, since his cough has stopped working
      - Give abdominal thrusts at once, since a weak cough means back blows won't be enough
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      RCUK's first aid guideline starts back blows "If the person is unable to cough or the cough
      becomes ineffective", and a cough that has gone weak and nearly silent, with no words and a
      colour change, is the severe obstruction the AHA describes as "weak or absent coughing,
      inability to speak, changes in color (cyanosis)". Watching a mild cough was for exactly this
      moment, so a cough of some kind no longer means mild, and waiting for it to stop altogether
      loses time. Abdominal thrusts come after the back blows, not instead of them (RCUK 2025).
      RCUK's key points say to call for help early, on speaker if you're alone.
  - q: >-
      A man who lives alone in Omaha chokes on a piece of steak at his kitchen table. He can't
      speak or cough, and his mobile phone is beside his plate. Under the American Red Cross's
      advice, what should he do?
    options:
      - Lie down on the floor and wait, since without a second person nothing can clear it
      - Call 911 even though he can't speak, then give himself thrusts or use a chair back
      - Drink water in big gulps to push the food down, since thrusts need a second person
      - Throw his back against a door frame, and call 911 only once the food has come out
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The American Red Cross says someone choking alone should call 911 from a landline or a
      GPS-enabled mobile, because even if they can't speak "the open line will cause the
      dispatcher to send help". Then they can give themselves abdominal thrusts with their own
      fist, or press the abdomen against "the back of a chair or a railing", avoiding sharp edges.
      Neither the AHA's 2025 guideline nor RCUK's makes a recommendation on choking alone, so this
      is the American Red Cross's advice, with no evidence cited. No source read suggests water, putting off the call wastes
      the minutes help needs to arrive, and lying down alone leaves nobody to act.

  # --- Severe bleeding and shock (L9) ---
  - q: >-
      At a garden centre in Shrewsbury, a hedge trimmer slips and cuts deep into the middle of a
      worker's forearm. Blood keeps pumping through the pad however hard you press, a PAcT kit is on
      the wall, and 999 is on speaker. Under RCUK's 2025 first aid guideline, what now?
    options:
      - Tourniquet 5 to 7 cm above the wound, off the elbow, tightened till it slows and stops
      - Keep pressing and raise the arm above his head, since the pad alone isn't doing enough
      - Move your hands to the inside of his upper arm and press there to cut off the supply
      - Add more pads and keep pressing, since tourniquets are only for amputations and blasts
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      RCUK 2025: "Apply a tourniquet as soon as possible for life-threatening extremity bleeding
      that is not controlled by direct manual pressure", placed "5-7cm above the injury, but not
      over a joint", tightened until the bleeding "slows and stops", with the time written down.
      Blood pumping out through firm pressure is the failure both the AHA/Red Cross 2024 guideline
      and St John describe. St John's page links tourniquets with catastrophic bleeding such as an
      amputation, but it also says one may be needed once pressure has been tried and severe
      bleeding continues. Raising the arm is the NHS cuts page's line for everyday cuts, and
      pressing on the upper arm is a pressure point, which ILCOR recommends against.
  - q: >-
      In Durham a woman falls down a flight of concrete steps. Her lower leg is bent at an odd angle,
      and a deep cut on it bled heavily until pressure controlled it. She's pale, cold and clammy
      but alert, and 999 is on speaker. Under St John Ambulance's pages, how should she lie?
    options:
      - On her back with both legs raised on a bag, since signs of shock mean raising the legs
      - Propped sitting up with a sweet drink, to bring her blood pressure back up as you wait
      - Lying down with her legs left where they are and kept warm, since a leg may be broken
      - On her side in the recovery position, since anyone with signs of shock goes on their side
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      St John's shock page lies a casualty down and raises their legs, but its fractures page says
      to treat for shock without raising the legs "if either are suspected to be broken or there is
      injury to the pelvis or a hip", and a leg bent at an odd angle may well be broken. So she lies
      down with her legs as they are, kept warm and reassured. RCUK's 2025 first aid guideline has
      no section on the position for shock, which is why the UK answer comes from these pages. The
      American Red Cross says nothing to eat or drink, and nothing read in the UK says otherwise.
      The recovery position isn't St John's step for shock, and RCUK says not to move a person into
      it after trauma.

  # --- Burns, cuts and small injuries (L10) ---
  - q: >-
      In a flat in Preston, a man scalds the back of his hand with steam from a pan, holds it under
      the tap for a few seconds, and carries on cooking. Two hours later it still stings, and the
      skin is red but not blistered. He wonders whether cooling it now is any use. Under the NHS's
      advice, what should he do?
    options:
      - Nothing more, since cooling only helps in the first few minutes after the burn happens
      - Rub in some butter or a cream now, since it's too late for water to do the burn any good
      - Hold a bag of ice on it for 10 minutes, since after two hours water is too gentle to help
      - Hold it under cool running water for 20 minutes now, since it's still within 3 hours
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The NHS says to hold a burn or scald under cool running water for 20 minutes, "as soon as
      possible, within 3 hours of it happening". Two hours in, he's inside that window, and the
      British Burn Association gives the same 20 minutes within 3 hours. Neither says cooling stops
      helping after the first few minutes. The British Red Cross says butter and cream won't cool a
      burn and oils hold the heat, and the British Burn Association and St John say no ice.
  - q: >-
      In a backyard in Wichita, a woman is hammering a nail when something flies up into her eye.
      She rinses it with tap water for several minutes, but it still feels as if something is in
      there. Under the AHA/Red Cross 2024 guideline, what now?
    options:
      - Rinse it for another 15 minutes, and see a doctor next week if it still feels scratchy then
      - Get medical care now, without rubbing it, since the feeling that something's there persists
      - Lift it off her eye with the corner of a damp tissue, since rinsing hasn't cleared it out
      - Cover it with a pad and let her sleep, since tears overnight will wash out what's left
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The guideline sends a person to medical care at once (COR 1, LOE C-LD) after an eye injury at
      speed, as from nailing, and when something still feels as if it's there after rinsing. It
      also says not to rub the eye (COR 1, LOE C-EO), because rubbing may make a scrape on its
      surface, or a wound that has gone in, worse. Waiting a week or overnight ignores both reasons.
      Lifting a speck off with a damp tissue corner is St John's step for something sitting on the
      surface, and here the nail and the lasting feeling both say get care now.

:::callout Before you need this
This course is education, not a certificate, and not medical advice about anyone's own situation. In an emergency, call your local emergency number first (911 in the US, 999 or 112 in the UK, 112 across the EU), put the phone on speaker, and do what the call handler tells you. Reading can teach you what to do and in what order. It can't teach your hands how hard to push or how a back blow should feel, so take a hands-on class with a manikin and an instructor, and practise between classes. Each instruction here names the guideline and year it comes from, and guidance changes.
:::

Every question puts you somewhere the lessons didn't: a new person, a new place, a new line from a
guideline. The people and places are invented; the rules in the explanations are the real ones the
lessons read, each with the body and year it comes from.

Where a safety step differs between the two countries, the question names the country whose rule
it's asking about, and that's the rule to answer by. Where a question names neither, or says both,
its right answer is the same under the US and the UK guidance. One question is on the law, and its
answer is general information, not legal advice.

Passing this test isn't a certificate, and it doesn't show that your hands can do any of it. The
box above says where that comes from.
