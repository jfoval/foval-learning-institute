Most cardiac arrests happen at home, and so do most of the other emergencies in this course: the
choking toddler, the grandparent who will not wake, the teenager with a swelling face. The person who
has to act first is usually whoever lives there. This project turns the course into four things that
stay in your home and in your hands: **an emergency card**, **a log of rehearsals**, **five
decisions written up** from scenarios the course supplies, and **a class booked**.

It takes about six and a half hours of your own time, spread over two to three weeks: an hour for
the card, including the walk to your nearest defibrillator; three short rehearsal sessions and two
walk-throughs; about 45 minutes for each decision, because each one means looking up grades across
two or three lessons; and a few minutes to book the class. The class itself is extra.

:::callout Before you need this
This course is education, not a certificate, and not medical advice about anyone's own situation. In an emergency, call your local emergency number first (911 in the US, 999 or 112 in the UK, 112 across the EU), put the phone on speaker, and do what the call handler tells you. Reading can teach you what to do and in what order. It can't teach your hands how hard to push or how a back blow should feel, so take a hands-on class with a manikin and an instructor, and practise between classes. Each instruction here names the guideline and year it comes from, and guidance changes.
:::

## Two rules for every part

**Never dial an emergency number to practise.** Every rehearsal of a call is spoken aloud, to
yourself or to a friend. Nothing on the card is tested by calling it.

**Never practise a technique on a living person**, with one exception, the one lesson 7 allowed:
the recovery position's arm and knee placement on a willing adult, with no roll, no force and no
hard floor. Compressions go into a cushion or a firm pillow. Back blows, thrusts and everything else
are spoken, or placed on a cushion or a doll. If anything hurts your wrists, back or knees, or you
feel dizzy or unusually breathless, stop.

## Which country

Choose the country whose guidance you follow, the one your call handler and your class will use,
as lesson 1 said, and write it at the top. Everything below is marked against that country's
guidance. If you live somewhere else, use your national resuscitation council for your own
sequence, say which, and in Part 3 compare it with whichever of the US or UK guidance you did not
use.

## What you hand in

One plain text or Markdown file, roughly 2,000 to 3,000 words, with four parts. The card itself
stays at home; you hand in a copy with anything private blanked out.

## Part 1: the card

One side of a card or a sheet of paper that lives where your household will see it: by the phone,
on the fridge, in the kit. It holds:

1. **Your emergency number.** 911 in the US; 999 or 112 in the UK (lesson 2).
2. **Your address or a landmark, as you would say it aloud**, the way lesson 2's call handler would want
   it. In the UK, add one line: most smartphones send their location on a 999 call, and some
   ambulance services can text a what3words link if you cannot say where you are (lesson 2).
3. **Your country's poison line.** In the US, Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222 (lesson 14). In the UK,
   write what lesson 14 found: the NHS pages name no separate public poison line; 999 or A&E if you
   think someone has swallowed, touched or breathed in something harmful, NHS 111 only if you are not
   sure it is harmful.
4. **The nearest public defibrillator to your home, and when it can be reached.** Walk there, look
   at it, and note whether the cabinet is locked and whether the building closes at night (lesson
   5). Don't open it: read the cabinet and its sign, because some cabinets raise an alarm when
   opened. In the UK the public map, defibfinder.uk, is for finding one in advance; in an emergency
   the call handler knows where the registered ones are (lessons 2 and 5).
5. **For each person at home with a known condition** (an allergy, asthma, epilepsy, diabetes, heart
   disease): where their medicine or device is kept, and the one rule this course taught for it,
   with the body and year. For example, if you are in the UK and someone carries an auto-injector:
   "Second dose at 5 minutes if no better, other leg (RCUK 2025)" (lesson 13). Ask them, when it
   suits them, what they would want the card to say; lessons 13 and 14 started this card. Leave
   their device where it is; the card says where it is kept.

Nothing on the card is sent anywhere. It stays in your home.

## Part 2: the rehearsals

Log each one with its date, what you did, and one honest line about what went wrong or fell off.

1. **The call, said aloud.** Your address or landmark, what has happened, and "unresponsive and
   not breathing normally", then the phone going on speaker beside the person's head (lesson 2).
   Once, to yourself or a friend. Never dialled.
2. **Two minutes of compressions into a cushion, three times over two weeks**, at 100 to 120 a
   minute. Use the BHF's RevivR for camera feedback on your compressions if you can (lesson 3), or a
   metronome at 110 if you cannot; 110 is lesson 3's choice from the middle of the range, not a
   guideline figure. Each time, note when your depth or rhythm first fell off, or when your arms
   first tired, and whether that came before or after the 90 seconds at which the AHA says depth
   starts to fall (lesson 3). The AHA rates the usefulness of objects such as cushions, against a
   manikin, as not well established (AHA 2025, COR 2b, LOE C-LD; lesson 16), which is why Part 4
   exists.
3. **The recovery position, spoken or placed.** Say the steps aloud in your country's source's words
   while placing a cushion or a doll, or, as lesson 7 allowed, a willing adult for arm and knee
   placement only: no roll, no force, no hard floor.
4. **The choking sequence for the youngest person in your household**, spoken, with a doll or
   cushion for a baby's position (lesson 8). If nobody at home is under 18, do it for an adult.

## Part 3: five decisions

Choose five of the ten scenarios below. **At least one must be a child or infant case** (A, B or J)
and **at least one a medical emergency other than cardiac arrest** (D, E, F, H or I). For each,
write:

1. **The sequence under your country's guidance**, step by step, with the body, year and grade of
   each step where it has one ("(AHA 2025, COR 1, LOE C-LD)", "(RCUK 2025)"). Where a step comes from
   a public page or is this course's own reading, say so.
2. **What would change under the other country's guidance**, and each side's reason if the course
   gave one. If you chose a third country above, compare it with the US or the UK.
3. **What you would say to the call handler**, in the words you would use, in the order they would need it.

No scenario repeats a lesson's case or the final test's, though each draws on a lesson's rules.
Each has a checkpoint with what a sound answer contains, laid out the way yours should be: your
country's sequence, the other country's, why they differ, and the call. Write yours first, then
open it.

### A. The birthday party

In Oldham, at Rhiannon's son's birthday party, a four-year-old guest is eating grapes when she goes
silent: mouth open, no sound, no cough, her face darkening. Another parent pulls a suction
anti-choking device out of her bag and says, "Use this." (US readers: the same, at a party in the
US.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to A contains

A child who cannot make a sound or cough has a severe obstruction, and at four she is a child, not a
baby, so she gets the child sequence (lesson 8). The device waits.

**In the UK.** Someone calls 999 on speaker as soon as possible (RCUK 2025). Lean her forward and
give up to five back blows between the shoulder blades, more gently than for an adult (the British
Red Cross); then up to five abdominal thrusts, standing behind her, fist between the navel and the
bottom of the breastbone, pulled sharply in and up, not pressing on the lower ribs (RCUK 2025; the
NHS). Stop at once if she coughs, breathes loudly or cries (RCUK 2025). RCUK makes no recommendation
for or against the suction devices and says the established treatments should come first (RCUK,
reviewed July 2026). If she stops responding: CPR at once, starting with five rescue breaths
(RCUK 2025); take out anything clearly visible with a single sweep, and never sweep blind. If it
comes out, she still sees a doctor: the NHS says get medical help even if the object has come out,
because part of it may be left behind or she may have been hurt.

**In the US.** A helper calls 911 on speaker. Repeated cycles of 5 back blows alternating with 5
abdominal thrusts (AHA 2025, COR 1, LOE C-LD). The AHA finds too little evidence to recommend the
devices for children either way (AHA 2025, COR 2b, LOE C-LD). If she stops responding: CPR
beginning with chest compressions, with no pulse check (AHA 2025, COR 1, LOE C-LD); remove an
object only if you see it when you open the airway for breaths (COR 1, LOE C-LD); no blind finger
sweep (COR 3: Harm, LOE C-LD).

**Why they differ.** Only if she stops responding: breaths first in the UK, compressions first in
the US, for the reasons lesson 6 gave (RCUK's reasoning from the usual cause of a child's arrest,
the AHA's consistency across ages). On the device they agree: neither recommends it over back blows
and thrusts, and RCUK's stated reason is that those can be given at once with nothing but your
hands.

**To the call handler.** The address, "a four-year-old is choking, she can't breathe or make a
sound", and whether she is still responding.
:::

### B. The basketball court

In Scranton, an eleven-year-old at basketball practice sits down, then slumps sideways. He does not
respond when the coach shakes his shoulders and shouts, and he is making occasional gasping breaths.
He shows no signs of puberty. A parent is with the coach, and there is an AED by the gym door. (If
you follow UK guidance, do this scenario as if it were a school sports hall in the UK.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to B contains

He is unresponsive and gasping, and gasping is not normal breathing: this is cardiac arrest
(lesson 2).

**In the US.** Recognise, then call 911 on speaker and start CPR at once. He is a child until
puberty (AHA 2025), so child CPR: compressions first (AHA 2025, COR 2b, LOE C-LD), at least a third
of the chest depth, about 2 in (5 cm) (COR 2a, LOE C-LD), 100 to 120 a minute (COR 2a, LOE C-LD);
30:2 alone, 15:2 with two rescuers (COR 2a, LOE C-EO). The parent fetches the AED and it goes on as
soon as possible (AHA 2025); at 11 he is over the age for the attenuator (under 8 years, COR 1, LOE
C-LD).

**In the UK.** Call 999 for any unresponsive person and check his breathing with the call handler
(RCUK 2025). Five rescue breaths first, then 30:2 unless you are specifically trained in paediatric
life support (RCUK 2025). A helper brings the AED; paediatric mode if he weighs under 25 kg and the
AED has one, standard adult mode if he is heavier (RCUK 2025).

**Why they differ.** The first action and the moment of the call. Lesson 6 gave the reasons for the
first: the AHA's consistency across ages, and RCUK's reasoning from the usual cause of a child's
arrest. Lesson 2 gave them for the second: the AHA values a quick dispatch and a call handler's
help, and RCUK says the call handler recognises arrest better than a frightened bystander.

**To the call handler.** The gym and its address, "an eleven-year-old boy has collapsed, he's not
responding and he's gasping", and that there's an AED.
:::

### C. The lawnmower

In Kalamazoo, Wes and his sister come round the side of the house and find their father face down
beside the lawnmower, its engine still running. He does not respond and is not breathing normally.
There is one phone between them, Wes's. The nearest public AED they know of is at a library ten
minutes' walk away. (UK readers: the same, in a back garden in the UK.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to C contains

Danger first: the engine off, if it can be done safely, which is this course's application of
lesson 2's rule that you make the area safe before you go in. Roll him onto his back.

**In the US.** He is unresponsive and not breathing normally, so assume cardiac arrest (AHA 2025,
COR 1, LOE C-LD): one of them calls 911 on speaker while the other starts CPR at once (lesson 2).
Compressions to at least 2 in (5 cm) but not more than 2.4 in (6 cm) (COR 1, LOE B-NR), 100 to 120
a minute (COR 2a, LOE B-NR), full recoil; compressions only if nobody is trained or willing to give
breaths (all lay rescuers should give compressions, COR 1, LOE B-NR). CPR carries on until an AED
is on (AHA 2025, COR 1).

**In the UK.** Call 999 on speaker first because he is unresponsive, and check his breathing with
the call handler; then CPR at 5 to 6 cm and 100 to 120 a minute (RCUK 2025).

**Either way, the one phone.** It stays on speaker beside his head, with whoever is pushing, so the
call handler can coach the CPR; nobody takes it to the library. Whether Wes's sister goes for the
AED, twenty minutes there and back, or stays and takes over the compressions every two minutes
(lesson 3), is the call handler's decision, because they know where the registered AEDs are and
whether someone nearer can bring one (lessons 2 and 5). The ERC's line is that CPR should not be
delayed to find one, and with two of them it need not be.

**Why they differ.** Only the moment of the call, for the reasons lesson 2 gave (see B).

**To the call handler.** The address, "my dad has collapsed, he's not responding and he's not
breathing properly", that there are two of them and one phone, and where the library AED is.
:::

### D. The café

In Eastbourne, a sixteen-year-old with a known nut allergy has eaten a slice of cake that turned out
to contain almonds. Within minutes her lips are swelling and she is wheezing, and she uses one of her
two auto-injectors. Two minutes later she is still wheezing hard and wants to sit up to breathe, but
she also says she feels faint and the room is spinning. Her friend is with her. (US readers: the
same, in a US diner.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to D contains

Anaphylaxis (lesson 13). The adrenaline was right, and was right to come without delay: the MHRA
says an auto-injector should be used without delay if anaphylaxis is suspected, even if in doubt
about the severity. The question now is posture, and her two symptoms pull in opposite directions.

**In the UK.** 999, saying "anaphylaxis" (MHRA 2023; the NHS). Posture: lie down with the legs raised (MHRA
2023; St John; the NHS); if breathing is hard, sit up gently and lie down again as soon as you can
(MHRA 2023), and RCUK allows sitting up with the legs out in front (RCUK 2025). Never standing or
walking, even if she feels better (MHRA 2023; the NHS). For her, this course's reading of those
lines: let her sit up, on the floor with her legs out, while the wheeze is the worse problem, and
lay her down with her legs raised the moment the faintness wins or she can breathe lying down. If
her symptoms persist five minutes after the first dose, a second, ideally in the other leg (RCUK
2025).

**In the US.** She used her own auto-injector, which is what the guideline wants (AHA/Red Cross
2024, COR 1, LOE B-NR); you help if she needs it (COR 1, LOE C-EO); call 911 (COR 1, LOE C-EO). A
second dose may be considered if she does not respond and EMS is more than 5 to 10 minutes away
(COR 2b, LOE B-NR). The US guideline's anaphylaxis section says nothing on posture; its general text
presumes an alert person will choose the position that best helps their breathing.

**Why they differ.** The second dose: a five-minute clock in the UK, a weak "may be considered"
tied to how far away EMS is in the US (lesson 13 gave both texts, and neither gives a reason for its
timing). Posture: the UK texts have a rule and the US guideline does not.

**To the call handler.** "Anaphylaxis", her age, the time of the first dose, and that she is
wheezing and feels faint. Saying the time of each dose aloud as it goes in is this course's
suggestion (lesson 13). If she stops responding and is not breathing normally, CPR (lesson 2).
:::

### E. The bowling alley

In Peoria, a man of 68 in your bowling team suddenly cannot get his words out. The left side of his
face droops and he cannot lift his left arm. Ten minutes later it has all gone: he is talking
normally, a little embarrassed, and says he'll drive himself home and see his doctor next week.
(UK readers: the same, at a bowling alley in the UK.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to E contains

Face, arm and speech were all positive: a suspected stroke, and the fact that it has passed does not
change the call (lesson 12).

**In the US.** Activate EMS at once (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1, LOE B-NR), and note the time the
symptoms started. The American Stroke Association: "Call 911 even if the symptoms go away." It calls
symptoms that pass a transient ischaemic attack, a warning stroke. The guideline names FAST; the
ASA's public message adds balance and eyes (BE-FAST).

**In the UK.** FAST and 999 (RCUK 2025), telling the call handler you suspect a stroke (St John). The
NHS: call 999 if someone has had signs of a stroke in the last 24 hours, even if they have stopped,
and don't drive yourself to A&E; it says a TIA should be treated as urgent. Nothing to eat or drink
(St John).

**Either way.** He does not drive himself, which the NHS says in so many words, and the call brings
the ambulance. No aspirin
if he asks: no guideline this course read recommends it for a suspected stroke, and nobody at the
scene can know whether it was a bleed (lesson 12). Stay with him and watch; if the signs come back,
tell the call handler at once, and if he stops responding, breathing normally means lesson 7 and
not normally means CPR.

**Why they differ.** They do not, on the decision: both countries' texts say call even if it passes.
The only difference is the name of the check, FAST or BE-FAST.

**To the call handler.** The address, "I think he's had a stroke; his face drooped and he couldn't
speak or lift his arm, and it passed after about ten minutes", the time it started, and whether he
has diabetes, since low blood sugar can look like a stroke.
:::

### F. The football match

In Dumfries, Nadia's nine-year-old daughter, who has type 1 diabetes, comes off the pitch pale,
sweaty and cross. She's awake and answers questions, but she clamps her mouth shut and pushes away
the juice and the glucose tablets. Her uncle wants to hold her still and pour the juice in. (US
readers: the same, at a match in the US.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to F contains

Probably low blood sugar (lesson 14). She is awake, so she is not in the nothing-by-mouth group, but
she will not swallow, so the tablets and juice are not working either. Nobody pours anything into a
child who is fighting it.

**In the UK.** Consider half a teaspoon (2.5 g) of table sugar under her tongue, which RCUK gives
for a child who is uncooperative with swallowing but not unresponsive (RCUK 2025). Once she will
swallow, glucose tablets 15 to 20 g, or 50 to 100 mL of fruit juice; repeat if she's no better after
15 minutes (RCUK 2025). Call 999 if she becomes unresponsive or does not improve (RCUK 2025).

**In the US.** A slurry of granulated sugar and water under her tongue may be reasonable for a child
who is awake but unwilling or unable to swallow glucose (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 2b, LOE C-LD).
Once she'll swallow, glucose tablets if available (COR 2a, LOE B-NR). Call EMS if she can't swallow
or has a seizure (COR 1, LOE C-EO), or if she has not improved within 10 minutes of the glucose
(COR 1, LOE C-EO).

**Either way.** Sugar under the tongue is for a child who is awake, never for one who won't respond.
If she stops responding, nothing by mouth at all (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 3: Harm, LOE C-EO; RCUK
2025), a call, and lesson 7's check: quiet and regular breathing, the recovery position and
watching; if she does not respond and her breathing is not normal, CPR (lesson 2). Glucagon only if she
carries it and someone knows how to use it (lesson 14).

**Why they differ.** Little: the same idea under the tongue, a weak US grade and a UK "consider";
the US gives the call a 10-minute clock and the UK gives none. Lesson 14 found no reason given for
either timing.

**To the call handler**, if you call: the address, "my daughter has type 1 diabetes, I think her
sugar's low and she won't swallow anything", when she last ate or had insulin, if you know.
:::

### G. The shop floor

In Olympia, a shop assistant falls through a glass display shelf, and a long cut on the back of his
thigh is spurting blood. Pressure did not stop it. A colleague has already put the kit's tourniquet
on, a hand's width above the wound, and tightened it, and the bleeding has slowed but not stopped.
A customer has 911 on speaker. (UK readers: the same, with 999 on speaker and a PAcT kit.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to G contains

Life-threatening bleeding from a limb, and the tourniquet is not finished (lesson 9).

**In the US.** A tourniquet is tightened until the bleeding stops (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1, LOE
B-NR), so it goes tighter. Warn him: Stop the Bleed says tourniquets hurt. Write the time it went
on (Stop the Bleed). It is never removed: Stop the Bleed says do not remove it. Then shock: a person
with signs of shock and a normal level of alertness can reasonably be kept on their back
(AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 2a, LOE C-LD), and the feet go up only where there's no trauma or injury
(COR 2b, LOE C-LD), so his stay down.

**In the UK.** Tighten until the bleeding "slows and stops", and if one tourniquet is not enough, a
second can go on above the first (RCUK 2025). It will hurt; St John says to warn him. Write the
time (RCUK 2025). "Do not release the tourniquet. It should only be released by a healthcare
professional." (RCUK 2025); St John adds that a loosened one lets the bleeding start again. Then
shock: St John's severe bleeding page raises a bleeding casualty's legs, and its fractures page says
not to if a leg may be broken; a cut from glass with no sign of a break is the first case.

**Why they differ.** The tourniquet steps agree. The legs differ: the US guideline keeps the feet
down after any injury, and St John raises them unless a leg may be broken. Lesson 9 gave the reason
each side offers, the same aim of blood to the vital organs, and the AHA's note that the evidence
is "temporary, and not all studies show this effect".

**To the call handler.** "Severe bleeding from the back of his thigh; a tourniquet is on and it's
still bleeding", the time it went on, and, later, whether he becomes pale, cold or confused. If he
stops responding, lesson 2.
:::

### H. The van

At a festival near Lowestoft, you find your friend's boyfriend in the back of a van, not responding
when you shake him and shout. His breathing is very slow, with long gaps, and his pupils are tiny.
You call 999 on speaker, start CPR, and give the naloxone nasal spray from his bag, as a drug
service once showed you. A few minutes later he's breathing normally, groggy, and says he's fine and
wants to go back to the music. Twenty minutes on, the ambulance is not there yet, he has gone quiet
again, and his breathing is slowing. (US readers: the same, at a festival in the US, with an
over-the-counter spray.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to H contains

Very slow breathing with long gaps in someone who does not respond is not normal breathing (lessons 2
and 7), so the first part was right: the call, CPR, and the spray. The second part is the one
lesson 7 warned about: naloxone wears off, and the opioid has not.

**In the UK.** Call 999 on speaker; if unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR (RCUK
2025). Give naloxone if you are trained (RCUK 2025); being shown by a drug service counts, on this
course's reading, since RCUK gives no threshold. The ERC says start CPR and give nasal naloxone.
When he wakes, he stays: RCUK says he should remain under observation until emergency help arrives,
and to follow the packaging on when to give another dose (RCUK 2025). So he does not go back to the
music. When he goes quiet again: lesson 7's check. Unresponsive and breathing normally, the
recovery position and watching; unresponsive and not breathing normally, CPR again; a second dose
if the packaging says so.

**In the US.** Call 911 without delaying it for naloxone (AHA 2025, COR 1, LOE C-EO); CPR with
breaths (COR 1, LOE B-NR); anyone can give naloxone, but never at the cost of the call or CPR, and in
cardiac arrest it "may be reasonable" alongside CPR (COR 2b). An adult who responds should be
observed in a health care setting (AHA 2025, COR 1, LOE C-LD), because the breathing can slow again
once the naloxone fades. The relapse gets the same check and the same CPR.

**Why they differ.** Who may give naloxone: the AHA says anyone, RCUK says "if you are trained" and
gives no reason, and the ERC allows nasal naloxone for anyone. And where he's watched afterwards: a
health care setting in the US, until help arrives in RCUK's text. The AHA gives its reason, that
naloxone wears off; RCUK's text read gives none.

**To the call handler.** Where the van is, "he's not responding and barely breathing, I think it's
an overdose", and when the naloxone went in; later, "he woke up and now he's going again".
:::

### I. The meeting room

In Cheyenne, a colleague of about thirty, with no history of seizures that anyone knows of, goes
rigid in a meeting and falls from her chair, then starts jerking. (UK readers: the same, in an
office in the UK.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to I contains

A first seizure (lesson 14).

**In the US.** Help her down if she's falling and clear the area (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1, LOE
C-EO); cushion her head (the CDC). Time it. Don't hold her down (COR 3: Harm, LOE C-EO), and nothing
in her mouth (COR 3: Harm, LOE C-EO). Call: a first seizure is on the guideline's list (COR 1, LOE
C-LD). The side position during the seizure (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1, LOE C-EO; the CDC).

**In the UK.** Protect her from injury and cushion her head (the NHS; St John). Time it. Nothing in
her mouth, "including your fingers" (the NHS). Call 999: a first seizure is on the NHS's list. The
side position after the convulsions stop (the NHS).

**Either way, when it stops.** Lesson 2's check. If she does not respond and her breathing is
absent, gasping, slow, laboured or noisy, it is not normal: onto her back, the call on speaker, and
CPR. If it's quiet and regular, the recovery position and watching. Heavy, snoring breathing as
she comes round goes to the call handler, as lesson 14 said, and if she does not respond and the
doubt stays, start CPR. Nothing to eat or drink until she has fully recovered. None of the pages
read in lesson 14 says to pin a jerking person on her side.

**Why they differ.** Only the timing of the side position: during the seizure in the US texts,
after it in the NHS's. The US texts give reasons for turning her (the AHA, less risk of breathing in
vomit; the CDC, a clear airway); none of the UK pages lesson 14 read says why it waits.

**To the call handler.** "A woman has had a seizure, her first as far as we know", how long it
lasted, and how she is now: responding or not, and how she's breathing.
:::

### J. The straighteners

In Bradford, a two-year-old grabs a pair of hot hair straighteners left on the bedroom floor. Her
palm and fingers are red and already blistering. (US readers: the same, in a US home.)

:::checkpoint What a sound answer to J contains

A burn to a small child's hand (lesson 10). Cool it at once under cool running water: on that the
countries agree (ILCOR 2025, strong recommendation; AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1, LOE B-NR).

**In the UK.** Cool for 20 minutes (the NHS; the British Burn Association; St John; the British Red
Cross), keeping the rest of her warm and watching a small child for over-cooling (the British Burn
Association). Anything tight near the burn off before it swells. No butter, creams or ice. Cover it
with cling film laid on, not wrapped (the NHS). Medical help: the British Red Cross says always get
medical advice for a baby or child; the NHS says phone 111 for advice about a child under 5 if
you're not sure, and 999 or A&E for a burn that is large or deep.

**In the US.** Cool for 5 to 20 minutes (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 2b, LOE B-NR), watching a young
child for hypothermia (COR 1, LOE C-LD). Anything tight near the burn off (COR 1, LOE C-EO). No
butter, creams or ice. A clean, dry, non-sticking dressing laid on loosely (COR 2b, LOE C-EO). It's
blistered and on her hand, so the guideline wants prompt evaluation by a health professional (COR
1, LOE B-NR), and the American Red Cross page marks burns in the very young and to the hands for
immediate emergency treatment and a 911 call.

**Why they differ.** How long: ILCOR found no duration it could recommend, so the AHA/Red Cross
guideline gives a range and the UK bodies a single figure; the British Red Cross's reason is that
sooner and longer cooling lessens the burn's impact (lesson 10). The cover differs too, cling film
against a dry dressing.

**To the person on the phone.** Her age, what burned her, where, and how big.
:::

## Part 4: the class

Book, attend or schedule a hands-on class with a manikin and an instructor from lesson 16's list for
your country, and write its name and date. If none is available to you, schedule RevivR or Lifesaver
now and a repeat three months later, the point at which Riggs's review found skills had already
fallen (lesson 16). Write the date of your next refresher either way.

## If something doesn't fit your household

- **No public defibrillator within reach.** Write that on the card, with the nearest one you found
  and how far it is, and ask the call handler about AEDs if you ever need one.
- **Nobody at home has a known condition.** Item 5 says "none known", and you write one rule you'd
  want for a visitor who does: an auto-injector, an inhaler or a seizure.
- **You live alone.** The call rehearsal is said to yourself, and the card goes where a visitor or
  a neighbour would find it.

## Rubric

Each part is marked at one of three levels.

- **The card.** Full marks: all five items, correct for your country: the right number, a location
  you could say aloud, the poison line or the UK note, an AED you've visited with its hours, and a
  sourced rule for each condition at home. Partly there: one item missing or unsourced, or an AED
  found on a map but not visited. Not yet: a wrong emergency number, or no card.
- **The rehearsals.** Full marks: all four logged with dates, three compression sessions over two
  weeks, each with an honest note of what fell off and when. Partly there: fewer sessions, or a log
  with no note of what went wrong. Not yet: no log, any technique done on a person beyond the
  recovery position's arm and knee placement, or any call dialled.
- **Five decisions.** Full marks: five, including a child or infant case and a medical emergency;
  every step in the right order with its body and year, the grade where it has one, and the
  course's own readings labelled. Partly there: the right order, but attributions missing or wrong
  in places. Not yet: a sequence out of order in a way that matters.
- **The other country.** Full marks: each difference named, with each side's reason where the course
  gave one, and neither called wrong. Partly there: differences named without reasons, or one
  missed. Not yet: no comparison, or one side called wrong.
- **The call handler.** Full marks: what you'd say, in words you'd use, with where you are first.
  Partly there: the right content, in an order that makes the call handler dig. Not yet: missing.
- **The class.** Full marks: a class booked or attended, or a digital course scheduled with a
  three-month repeat, with dates. Partly there: a class named but not booked. Not yet: nothing
  scheduled.

The project passes when no part is at "Not yet" and nothing on the list below appears.

**Any of these fails the project outright, whatever else is right**, because each one could cost a
life:

- a wrong emergency number for your country;
- anything by mouth for a person who is not fully awake;
- aspirin for a suspected stroke;
- loosening or removing a tourniquet;
- blind finger sweeps;
- a sequence that delays the call or CPR for naloxone, an AED fetch by a lone rescuer with a phone,
  or a pulse check.

## A note on what this project is not

It isn't a certificate and doesn't replace one, and nothing in it shows that your hands can do what
your page says. That's Part 4's job, and an instructor's. It's the plan you'd want to have made
before the day you need it, with the numbers written down while you're calm.
