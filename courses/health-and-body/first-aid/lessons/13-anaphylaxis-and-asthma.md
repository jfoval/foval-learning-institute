If you took Cooking Fundamentals, you met allergy from the kitchen side. Lesson 4 told you that
heat doesn't remove an allergen, that a trace can be enough, and that taking it off the plate isn't
good enough. That lesson was about stopping the reaction. This one is about the minutes after it
starts anyway: a colleague whose lips swell after a pastry, a boy stung by a wasp at a football
match, a friend whose asthma closes in.

Both conditions come with their own medicine, and usually the person carries it. Your job is to
recognise what's happening, get that medicine used without delay, make the call, and get the
position right, which for these two is opposite. And in anaphylaxis there is a clock, because a
second dose may be due.

devices.[6][7][11] Adrenaline is called epinephrine in the US; it is the same drug.

## Recognising anaphylaxis

The NHS calls anaphylaxis a life-threatening allergic reaction that happens very quickly, set off
by food, medicine or insect stings, usually within minutes of contact.[7] RCUK's 2025 guideline
says to suspect it if someone has:[2]

- **Breathing trouble**, from noisy breathing high in the throat (stridor, from swelling in the
  upper airway) to wheezing lower down.
- Flushing, a rash or hives, cold or clammy skin, or feeling faint.
- Stomach pain, vomiting or diarrhoea.
- **A recent exposure** to a food they're known to be allergic to, or an insect sting.

The NHS's call-999 list adds what to watch for as it gets worse: sudden swelling of the lips, mouth,
throat or tongue; struggling to breathe or to swallow; skin, tongue or lips turning blue, grey or
pale (on brown or black skin, often easier to see on the palms or soles); sudden confusion,
drowsiness or dizziness; a child who is limp or floppy.[7]

Two warnings. ILCOR's good practice statement, unchanged since 2010, is candid: "First aid
providers should not be expected to recognize the signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis without
repeated episodes of training and encounters with persons with anaphylaxis (good practice
statement)."[4] And RCUK's 2021 anaphylaxis guideline, written for health professionals, says the
skin changes may be absent in up to 20% of cases, so no rash does not mean no anaphylaxis.[5] The
same guideline says breathing trouble in anaphylaxis can look like an asthma attack with nothing
else to suggest an allergy, and that anaphylaxis should be considered in anyone with sudden
breathing difficulty, especially after a food or sting they are known to react to.[5] Hold on to
that for the asthma half of this lesson.

:::predict A register has tracked the UK's deaths from anaphylaxis since 1992. For a reaction to food, what do you guess was the median time from the start of the reaction to the breathing or the heart stopping?

Thirty minutes. For insect venom it was 15, and for reactions caused by medical treatment it was
5.[15] That is from Pumphrey's 2000 study of the register, of which this course read the abstract.
It also says every fatal reaction thought to be due to food caused difficulty breathing, and that
adrenaline had been used in 62% of the fatal cases but before the arrest in only 14%.[15]

Read it with its limits. It is a register of deaths, about 20 a year, traced from death
certificates, which its author says undercount the real number. It is not a comparison of
treatments, and it says a few reactions will be fatal whatever is given. Its conclusion is that
immediate recognition and early adrenaline are crucial.[15]
:::

## Adrenaline first

The AHA/Red Cross guideline's supporting text says strong international consensus, based mainly on
observational data, endorses early epinephrine injected into the muscle of the outer thigh as the
primary treatment of anaphylaxis, whatever triggered it.[1] RCUK's 2021 guideline says adrenaline
works best when given early, and that delay is associated with longer reactions, low blood pressure
and deaths.[5]

**Why adrenaline.** In anaphylaxis, RCUK's 2021 guideline explains, the blood vessels widen, fluid
leaks out of them into the tissues, and the airways tighten and swell.[5] Adrenaline narrows the
vessels, reduces the swelling, opens the airways, strengthens the heart's contraction and damps
down the release of histamine, one of the chemicals driving the reaction. The EpiPen leaflet says
much the same in plain words.[5][11] One drug works on the airway, the breathing and the
circulation at once.

**Why not an antihistamine.** RCUK's 2021 guideline: "Antihistamines are considered a third-line
intervention and should not be used to treat Airway/Breathing/Circulation problems during initial
emergency treatment."[5] Its main text says they can help itchy skin once the person is stable,
and that most guidelines worry their use delays adrenaline.[5] The US guideline doesn't discuss
them for anaphylaxis; the FDA, in 2024, called epinephrine the only life-saving treatment for
it.[1][13]

**Don't wait to be sure.** The MHRA says an auto-injector "should be used without delay if
anaphylaxis is suspected, even if in doubt about the severity of the event".[6] Keep its scope: the
doubt is about how severe a suspected reaction is. Its reason is that the risks of delay outweigh
the possible risks of adrenaline that turns out not to have been needed.[6] The device leaflets
say: if in doubt, use it.[11] In the US, the guideline's first anaphylaxis recommendation is that a
person with an autoinjector available should use it (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1, LOE B-NR), and its
background says they're prescribed to be used at the first sign of a reaction.[1]

## Who gives it

People get this wrong in both directions: some think anyone may inject anyone, others that only a
trained first aider may touch the device. Read who each sentence is for.

| | In the US | In the UK |
|---|---|---|
| **The guideline** | The person should use their own autoinjector (COR 1, LOE B-NR). "A first aid provider should assist a person experiencing anaphylaxis to use their autoinjector if assistance is required." (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1, LOE C-EO)[1] | RCUK: adrenaline is given either by the person themselves or by trained individuals. Its general principle: "only use equipment and medications you have been trained to use", except that the emergency services may direct you to use equipment you haven't had in-depth training on (RCUK 2025)[2] |
| **The public page** | The guideline's supporting text: breathing trouble, low blood pressure or confusion may stop someone reaching or working their device, and a first aider may assist[1] | St John Ambulance: help them to use it, or give the injection yourself straight away, following the instructions[9] |
| **The device's paperwork** | EpiPen's US label: for use by the person or their caregiver, and anyone who may give it should understand how[12] | The MHRA: for use by the patient or the patient's carer. EpiPen's UK leaflet: designed to be used easily by people without medical training[6][11] |

This course's reading of those rows: in both countries the device is the person's own, it goes in
at once, and you help as much as they need, from finding it to pulling the cap. If they cannot use
it at all, the UK texts give you two routes: St John tells a first aider to do it, following the
printed instructions, and RCUK's exception covers direction from the emergency services, who will
be on the phone by then. In the US the widest line read is the AHA's "assist", and this course read
no US text that deals directly with a stranger giving the injection when the person can't. In
either country the call handler is on speaker. Ask, and do what they say.

## The call

- **In the US**: the guideline's third recommendation is to activate the emergency response system
  for anyone with anaphylaxis (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1, LOE C-EO).[1] EpiPen's US label says to
  get emergency help right away, even after using it.[12]
- **In the UK**: RCUK's list puts the 999 call first and says adrenaline should be given as soon as
  possible (RCUK 2025).[2] The MHRA, the NHS and the leaflets, which speak to the person with the
  allergy, put the injection first and the call straight after, saying "anaphylaxis".[6][7][11]

The orders differ on paper and agree in practice: nobody read here delays adrenaline for the call,
and nobody skips the call because the adrenaline worked. The leaflets say to call even if the
symptoms are improving, because the reaction may come back and the person needs hospital
observation.[11] With the phone on speaker, the injection and the call can happen in the same
minute; with two of you, lesson 1's tactic applies, and one helps while the other calls.

## The devices

An adrenaline auto-injector is a spring-loaded pen that pushes a hidden needle into the thigh
muscle when it is pressed firmly against it. The MHRA names two in the UK, EpiPen and Jext; in the US
the EpiPen label was read.[6][11][12]

| | EpiPen | Jext |
|---|---|---|
| **Look** | Blue safety cap, orange tip (the needle end)[11][12] | Yellow cap, black tip (the needle end)[11] |
| **Where** | Outer thigh. "Place the orange needle end against the outer thigh, through clothing if needed." (US label)[12] | Outer thigh; "designed to inject through the clothes"[11] |
| **How long to hold** | "Hold firmly against thigh for 3 seconds." (UK leaflet, revised July 2026). "Hold the EpiPen or EpiPen Jr auto-injector in place for a full 3 seconds following activation (count slowly 1, 2, 3)." (US label)[11][12] | "Hold the injector firmly in place against the thigh for 10 seconds (a slow count to 10) then remove." Then massage the area for 10 seconds (leaflet revised September 2023)[11] |

Three seconds against ten is the detail people get wrong, and EpiPen's US label warns that if it
isn't held long enough it might not deliver the right dose.[12] The rule for every device,
including ones that change after this lesson: **follow the instructions printed on the device**,
which the NHS says are on the side of the injector.[7] Two more lines from the paperwork: each
device is one dose, and EpiPen's US label warns that needles have bent or cut the skin when a young
child kicked, so it tells caregivers to hold the child's leg firmly before and during the
injection.[12]

**Nasal sprays.** The FDA approved neffy, an epinephrine nasal spray, on 9 August 2024 for people
of at least 30 kg (66 lb); its label now adds a lower dose for 15 to under 30 kg, and puts any
second dose in the same nostril with a new spray, "starting 5 minutes after the first dose".[13][12]
The FDA based the approval on four studies in 175 healthy adults without anaphylaxis, measuring
adrenaline in the blood and its effect on blood pressure and heart rate, not on a trial in people
having a reaction.[13] In the UK, the MHRA approved EURneffy, a 2 mg nasal spray, for adults and
children of 30 kg or more (reported by Anaphylaxis UK on 24 July 2025), and a 1 mg version for
children aged 4 and over weighing 15 to 30 kg (reported 30 June 2026, expected on prescription
towards the end of 2026).[14] The ERC's 2025 guideline says that without an auto-injector, "use a
device for intranasal administration."[3]

:::predict Someone with anaphylaxis has used their auto-injector and feels a little better. They want to stand up and get some air. What do you guess the UK sources say, and why?

Stay lying down, even if you feel better; do not stand or walk. The reason: in anaphylaxis, getting
up has been followed by collapse and death. The next section has the texts.
:::

## Which way up

| | In the US | In the UK |
|---|---|---|
| **Position** | The AHA/Red Cross 2024 guideline's anaphylaxis section gives no posture recommendation, and this course read no US source that does[1] | Lie down flat with the legs raised (MHRA 2023; the NHS; St John). RCUK 2025: lying down "unless there are breathing difficulties, in which case they may sit up with their legs extended."[6][7][9][2] |
| **Pregnant** | Not addressed | On the left side (MHRA 2023; the NHS)[6][7] |
| **Breathing hard** | Not addressed | Sit up gently, without changing position suddenly, and lie down again as soon as you can (MHRA 2023); sit up slowly (the NHS)[6][7] |
| **Feeling better** | Not addressed | "stay lying down even if you feel better" and "do not stand up even if someone encourages you to" (MHRA 2023). "Do not stand or walk at any time, even if you feel better." (the NHS)[6][7] |

The ERC's European wording is broader: "Ensure that the person remains in a seated or lying
position."[3] RCUK's UK version narrows it to lying down, with sitting kept for breathing trouble.[2]

**Why.** This is the mechanism to take away. With the vessels wide and leaking, less blood flows
back to the heart and it fills poorly (RCUK 2021). Getting up makes that worse: "Changes in posture
from supine to standing or sitting upright have been associated with cardiovascular collapse and
death during anaphylaxis."[5] Supine means lying on your back. Keeping the person flat, legs raised
or not, "will maximise venous return", the blood coming back to the heart through the veins, and so
what the heart can pump out.[5] The MHRA tells patients the same in one line: lying down helps blood
reach the heart and vital organs.[6]

**How strong is it?** The ERC labels its posture advice "(expert opinion)", and RCUK's 2021
guideline rests its posture sentence on two published papers about deaths, which this course
hasn't read.[3][5] There is no trial. So it's a firm instruction on thin evidence, like much of
first aid, and the US guideline's silence is an absence, not a finding that posture does not matter.
No text on either side tells you to stand the person up. Lesson 9 raised the legs for shock; here
the UK raises them for a reason it spells out.

:::checkpoint A man with anaphylaxis is lying flat after his auto-injector, but he's wheezing hard and says he cannot breathe lying down. You're in the UK. What happens to his position?

He sits up, gently, with his legs out in front of him (RCUK 2025; the MHRA; the NHS), and lies down
again once his breathing allows it (the MHRA).[2][6][7] He doesn't stand and doesn't walk, not
even to a car or a quieter room. Sitting for breathing is the UK exception; standing never is.
:::

## The second dose

The US guideline's supporting text estimates that 7% to 18% of people with anaphylaxis need more
than one dose, and that most who had a second improved after it.[1] ILCOR, whose review both
countries build on, keeps its 2015 recommendation: "We suggest a second dose of epinephrine be
administered by autoinjector to adults and children with severe anaphylaxis whose symptoms are not
relieved by an initial dose (weak recommendation, very low–certainty evidence)."[4] The countries
differ on when.

| | In the US | In the UK |
|---|---|---|
| **The guideline** | A repeat dose "may be considered" if the person doesn't respond to the first and the "arrival of EMS will exceed 5 to 10 minutes" (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 2b, LOE B-NR)[1] | If symptoms persist five minutes after the first dose, give a second, "ideally in the opposite leg." (RCUK 2025)[2] |
| **Public advice** | EpiPen's US label: a second can be used if symptoms continue or come back[12] | "use your second AAI if you haven't improved after 5 minutes" (MHRA 2023). "If your symptoms have not improved after 5 minutes, use a 2nd adrenaline auto-injector." (the NHS). St John: repeat doses at five-minute intervals if there is no improvement or symptoms return[6][7][9] |
| **Carry two** | The guideline's list of optional items for an American Red Cross first aid kit gives an epinephrine autoinjector as "Minimum 1, recommended 2 doses"[1] | "prescribers should prescribe 2 AAIs" and "you should always carry 2 AAIs at all times" (MHRA 2023)[6] |

The UK rule is a clock: five minutes, no better, second dose, and the ERC says the same.[3] The US
guideline's rule has two conditions, no response and help still more than 5 to 10 minutes away,
graded 2b, "may be considered", on nonrandomised evidence. Neither text read gives a reason for its
timing, so this course won't supply one. Follow the country you're in. Either way you need the time
of the first dose, so say it aloud when it goes in, or tell the call handler. The UK leaflets say
that if there is no improvement, or it is worse, within 5 minutes, either the person or the person
with them gives the second.[11]

## If they stop responding

Pumphrey's register shows how anaphylaxis can end: with the breathing or the heart stopping.[15]
If the person stops responding and is not breathing normally, it is lesson 2: CPR, with the call
handler coaching you, as St John's anaphylaxis page says, and the AED as soon as one arrives
(lessons 3 to 5).[9] Slow, noisy or gasping breathing isn't normal. If they stop responding but are
breathing normally, quietly and regularly, it's lesson 7's recovery position, which is what EpiPen's
UK leaflet says for an unconscious person, watching the breathing the whole time.[11]

## The office kitchen in Des Moines

A box of pastries arrives at an office in Des Moines. A colleague with a peanut allergy eats half of
one, then stops. Within minutes her lips are swelling, she's wheezing, and she says her throat feels
tight. Her auto-injector is in her bag under the desk, and her hands are shaking too much to get it
out.

**Recognise.** A known allergy, a recent exposure, lip swelling and wheeze: RCUK's list fits, and no
rash is needed to decide.[2][5]

**Call and fetch.** One colleague calls 911 on speaker and says "anaphylaxis" (AHA/Red Cross 2024,
COR 1).[1] You get the bag.

**The device.** You find it, check it is hers, and help her use it in her outer thigh, through her
trousers, following the steps printed on its side. The guideline's first recommendation is written
for her, and its second for you: assist if assistance is required (COR 1).[1] It's an EpiPen, so
it's held for 3 seconds.[12] Someone says the time aloud: 10:14.

**Position.** She's wheezing and wants to sit on the floor against the wall. The US guideline has no
posture instruction for anaphylaxis; its general positioning text presumes an alert person will
choose the position that best helps their breathing.[1] Nothing in the US texts read says to stand
her up, so nobody walks her to a chair by the window.

**Watch.** The call handler says the ambulance is about 12 minutes out. At 10:19 she is no better:
the swelling is the same and the wheeze is worse. She has a second device.

:::checkpoint Your turn for the next step. Under the AHA/Red Cross 2024 guideline, does she get a second dose at 10:19? Say which words decide it.

Yes, one may be considered, because both conditions are met: she has not responded to the first dose,
and an ambulance 12 minutes away will take more than 5 to 10 minutes (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 2b,
LOE B-NR).[1] Her label also allows a second if symptoms continue.[12] Tell the call handler, note
the time, and keep watching. If she stops responding and is not breathing normally, it's CPR.

If you said no because the ambulance is coming, you read the first condition and skipped the second.
If you said she should have had it at 10:15, you ran the UK's five-minute clock in the US.
:::

## The football tournament in Harrogate

A 14-year-old at a youth tournament in Harrogate is stung by a wasp on the neck. Within minutes he's
blotchy, his face is swelling and he's short of breath. He uses his own EpiPen at 10:02 with his
coach's help while a parent calls 999, and he's laid down. By 10:08 he feels much better and wants
to get up and rejoin his team. A visiting coach from the US says, "He's fine now. Let him walk it
off."

**Whose rules.** They are in England, so the UK's, which are the ones the call handler will use.

**Position.** He stays lying down. The MHRA's sentence fits this moment exactly: "do not stand up
even if someone encourages you to".[6] The visiting coach isn't quoting a US rule; the US guideline
has no posture rule for him to quote. He is filling a silence with a guess.

**Second dose.** He has improved, so the five-minute rule does not call for one (RCUK 2025; MHRA
2023).[2][6] But the second device stays in someone's hand: St John says repeat doses can be given
at five-minute intervals if symptoms return.[9]

**"Fine" at six minutes.** The leaflets say to call even if symptoms improve, and that the person
needs hospital observation because the reaction may happen again later.[11] So the ambulance comes,
and he goes with it, lying down. RCUK also says to remove the trigger if you can, and the NHS says to
try to remove a sting if it's still in the skin; neither should delay anything else.[2][7]

## Asthma

An asthma attack is the other emergency where the person usually carries their own medicine. The
NHS says asthma symptoms happen when the airways become swollen and narrower, and describes an
attack as severe symptoms that make it hard to breathe and can be life-threatening; St John says
that in an attack the muscles of the air passages go into spasm.[8][9] St John's signs include a
tight chest, speaking in short sentences or a whisper, a grey-blue tinge to the lips, earlobes and
nailbeds, and, in a severe attack, exhaustion.[9]

**Sit them up.** The NHS tells someone having an attack to "sit up straight" and try to stay calm;
Asthma + Lung UK says to sit up, and St John to sit them down comfortably.[8][10][9] It is the opposite
of anaphylaxis.

**Help with their own reliever.** Here the countries agree.

- **In the US**: first aid providers should help a person with asthma who's having difficulty
  breathing to use their own prescribed bronchodilators, the guideline's word for relievers, as
  needed (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1, LOE B-R). An inhaler with a spacer, or a nebuliser, is
  reasonable in preference to an inhaler alone (COR 2a, LOE B-R), and an improvised spacer if
  there is no commercial one (COR 2a, LOE B-R).[1]
- **In the UK**: a first aider should help a person with asthma who is having breathing trouble to
  use their own reliever inhaler, with a spacer if one's available (RCUK 2025).[2]

ILCOR's recommendation is narrower in who it speaks to: it suggests that trained first aid
providers assist, a weak recommendation on very low certainty evidence (ILCOR 2025, unchanged since
2015; paraphrased from the accepted manuscript).[4] RCUK's asthma step carries no training condition
of its own.[2]

**Why a spacer.** The AHA's supporting text says a spacer, used with a metered dose inhaler, improves
the delivery of the medicine to the lungs; St John says the inhaler is more effective with one,
especially for young children.[1][9] Without one: "Improvised spacers made with a 500-mL (≈16 oz)
plastic cold drink bottle or a 150-mL (≈5 oz) disposable paper cup appear to provide drug delivery
similar to that achieved with commercial spacer devices".[1]

**Is it safe to help?** The AHA's supporting text says salbutamol (albuterol in the US) causes no
clinically significant change in heart rate, blood pressure or potassium.[1] ILCOR adds what is not
known: whether first aiders can correctly identify an asthma attack, and whether relievers could
harm someone whose breathing trouble has another cause.[4] That is one reason the person's own
diagnosis and inhaler matter.

### How many puffs

The US guideline says "as needed" and gives no count, and this course read no US source that
does.[1] UK public advice gives two schedules, depending on the inhaler.

| The person's reliever | The UK schedule (the NHS; Asthma + Lung UK) |
|---|---|
| **A blue reliever inhaler** | "take 1 puff every 30 to 60 seconds until you feel better, up to a maximum of 10 puffs." Shake it between puffs, and use a spacer if there is one[8][10] |
| **An AIR or MART inhaler** | "take 1 puff every 1 to 3 minutes until you feel better, up to a maximum of 6 puffs."[8][10] |

AIR is an anti-inflammatory reliever, used only when symptoms come; MART, maintenance and reliever
therapy, is one inhaler used every day and also when symptoms come.[8] St John's page says its own
10-puff advice "is not for patients on a Maintenance and Reliever Therapy (MART) plan."[9] Neither
the NHS nor Asthma + Lung UK says why the AIR and MART schedule is slower and stops at six, so this
course will not either.

So "ten puffs" is not a rule for everyone. The NHS adds that if the person's asthma action plan
gives a different maximum for an attack, follow the plan, and St John says a person on MART will know
which inhaler to use and may carry a written plan.[8][9] Ask them, and look for the plan, before you
count.

### When to call

- **In the UK**: 999 if they feel worse at any time, are no better after the maximum dose, or have no
  inhaler (the NHS; Asthma + Lung UK). If they're no better after 10 minutes and no ambulance has
  come, they take the inhaler again up to the same maximum, and Asthma + Lung UK says to call 999
  again if that doesn't help.[8][10] St John also calls for a first attack and for exhaustion.[9]
  The NHS: don't drive them to A&E yourself.[8]
- **In the US**: the guideline's asthma section gives no call rule, and this course read no US
  public page with one. Its text on assessing an ill person says any abnormality in breathing should
  be treated as an emergency that prompts a call to EMS.[1]

After an attack that settles without an ambulance, the NHS says to see a GP within 2 days, and
Asthma + Lung UK to ask for an urgent same-day appointment.[8][10] If they stop responding, it is as
for anaphylaxis: breathing normally, lesson 7; not breathing normally, lesson 2.

:::checkpoint A friend with asthma and a known peanut allergy starts wheezing hard a few minutes after eating a satay skewer. She has a blue inhaler and an auto-injector. You're in the UK. What comes first?

The auto-injector. A sudden breathing problem after a known food trigger is on RCUK's list, and
RCUK's 2021 guideline says anaphylaxis can look like asthma and should be considered in exactly this
case.[2][5] The MHRA says to use the device without delay if anaphylaxis is suspected, even if in
doubt about how severe it is.[6] Then 999, saying anaphylaxis, and she sits up to breathe, which the
UK allows for breathing difficulty.

No source read tells you to withhold her inhaler, and Pumphrey's abstract lists inhaled beta
agonists among the measures that matter.[15] But none lets it stand in for adrenaline. The order,
device first and inhaler after, is this course's reading of those texts; no source read sets it out
in one sentence.
:::

## At school

A child's own device or inhaler can be at home in the wrong bag. Both countries have made room for a
spare.

:::callout General information, not legal advice
This is general information, not legal advice. The law differs by state and by country.
:::

- **In the UK**: since 1 October 2014 UK schools may buy a salbutamol inhaler without a
  prescription, for emergencies when a child with asthma can't reach their own; since 1 October 2017
  schools in England may buy adrenaline auto-injectors without a prescription, for children at risk
  of anaphylaxis whose own device isn't available or is not working. Both are permissions, and the
  guidance helps schools that choose to keep one write a policy. This course read the summary of
  each, not the guidance documents.[16]
- **In the US**: the School Access to Emergency Epinephrine Act of 2013 gives states extra
  preference for certain federal asthma grants if they require schools to let trained staff give
  epinephrine to a student reasonably believed to be having an anaphylactic reaction, keep a supply,
  and have trained staff on site during school hours, and if the state attorney general certifies
  that state law gives those staff adequate civil liability protection.[17] The details are state
  law, which this course didn't read.

## What people get wrong

**"Try an antihistamine first, and use the pen if it gets worse."** Adrenaline first, even if in
doubt about severity (MHRA 2023); antihistamines are third line (RCUK 2021).[6][5]

**"He's better now, so he can get up."** Stay lying down (MHRA 2023; the NHS); getting up has
preceded collapse and death (RCUK 2021).[6][7][5]

**"One auto-injector is enough."** Carry two (MHRA 2023); 7% to 18% need more than one dose (AHA/Red
Cross 2024).[6][1]

**"All auto-injectors are held for ten seconds."** EpiPen 3, Jext 10. Follow the device.[11][12]

**"Either anyone can inject it, or only the trained may."** The person uses their own, a first aider
assists (AHA/Red Cross 2024), RCUK sets its training principle with the emergency-services
exception, and St John tells a UK first aider to do it if the person cannot.[1][2][9]

**"It's settled, so cancel the ambulance."** The reaction may come back; the person needs hospital
observation.[11]

**"Ten puffs of any inhaler."** Ten is for a blue reliever; AIR and MART are up to 6, and the
person's plan comes first (the NHS).[8]

## Practice

:::exercise Your household's devices

Take 10 minutes, with a clock or your phone's stopwatch and a firm cushion or rolled towel. **Never
practise with a real device**, and never press anything against a person, including yourself. If
anything hurts your hand or wrist, stop.

1. If someone in your home has an auto-injector, pick it up without removing any cap and read the
   printed instructions. Write its name, hold time and where it goes on a card, with where it is
   kept, your country's second-dose rule and position rule, and the body and year for each. For an
   inhaler, write the schedule from their own plan if they have one, or the NHS schedule for its type.
2. Using the trainer pen that came with it (no needle, no medicine) or an ordinary pen, press it
   against the cushion and count the hold time aloud: 3 or 10.
3. Start the stopwatch, say "first dose", and say aloud, as if to someone beside you, the call and
   the word to use, the position, and what you'll check at five minutes. Do not dial anything. Show
   the card to the person it is about and ask what you got wrong.
:::

:::exercise Four decisions

Take 10 minutes. For each, write what you'd do first, the call decision, and the source.

| | The situation |
|---|---|
| A | In Scarborough, a man with no known allergies gets itchy hives all over after a new antibiotic. His breathing, voice and colour are normal and he feels well otherwise |
| B | In Chattanooga, a 9-year-old with asthma is wheezing and speaking only in short bursts at a park. Her mother has her inhaler but no spacer; the café across the path has paper cups |
| C | On a flight from London, a passenger with a nut allergy has a swelling tongue and a hoarse voice. She has two auto-injectors, and no ambulance can reach her for hours |
| D | In a garden in England, a woman stung by a bee faints, comes round within a minute, and is now pale, clammy and wheezy |
:::

:::checkpoint Compare your four with these.

**A.** Hives are on RCUK's list, but alone they are only the skin half. RCUK's 2021 guideline, for
professionals, recognises anaphylaxis by airway, breathing or circulation problems, usually with
skin changes, and says that when those problems are absent but there are other allergic signs, the
person needs careful observation.[2][5] So stay and watch. If his lips or tongue swell, his breathing
changes or he feels faint, he is on the NHS's call-999 list.[7] Whether he takes the next dose of the
antibiotic is a question for whoever prescribed it.

**B.** Sit her up and help her mother help her use her own inhaler (AHA/Red Cross 2024, COR 1); a
paper cup can be an improvised spacer (COR 2a), and the AHA's text names a 150 mL cup.[1] Speaking
in short bursts is on St John's list of signs.[9] If she is worse at any point, or you are unsure,
call 911: the US guideline treats any abnormality in breathing as an emergency.[1]

**C.** Her device now, and tell the cabin crew at once. Lying down, sitting up with her legs out if
her breathing needs it (the UK texts).[2][6] If she's no better, both countries' rules allow a second
dose: the UK's five minutes applies wherever help is, and the US guideline's two conditions are met,
so a repeat dose may be considered.[1][2] No source read gives advice for a flight; this applies the
ordinary rules.

**D.** A sting, then feeling faint, clammy skin and a wheeze: RCUK's list fits, so treat it as
anaphylaxis.[2] Her device if she has one, 999, and lying flat with her legs raised, sitting up
only if the wheeze makes lying impossible, and not on her feet even though she's come round (MHRA
2023; the NHS).[6][7]
:::
