S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Nutrition: The Evidence, on the lesson called Processed food and meat.

S2: The box first, as always.

S1: This course is education, not advice about your own diet. If you're in one of the groups the box at the top of the lesson names, such as being pregnant or having diabetes or kidney disease, talk to a doctor or a registered dietitian first. If food, eating or your weight has started to feel out of your control, tell a doctor. The helpline numbers are in that box.

S2: So how does it open?

S1: With two headlines. In October twenty fifteen the World Health Organization's cancer agency, IARC, classified processed meat as carcinogenic to humans, Group one. In twenty nineteen a panel of fourteen people from seven countries, called NutriRECS, working from five reviews done for the purpose, published a recommendation that sounds like the opposite: the panel suggests adults continue current processed meat consumption. A weak recommendation, on low-certainty evidence. And its own reviews had found an association running the same way IARC's evidence did.

S2: So one of them misread the data?

S1: The lesson says you needn't decide that. It asks how much is evidence, how much is argument about the evidence, and how much is a judgement about what matters.

S2: Start with ultra-processed. What does it even mean?

S1: The term comes from a classification called NOVA, built by Monteiro's group, which groups foods according to the extent and purpose of industrial processing. Processed and ultra-processed are different groups in it. Monteiro's group says the processes and ingredients are designed to create highly profitable, convenient, hyper-palatable products liable to displace all other NOVA food groups.

S2: And the evidence that it matters?

S1: The trial everyone cites is Kevin Hall's, at the US National Institutes of Health in twenty nineteen. Twenty adults lived on a ward and ate an ultra-processed diet for two weeks and an unprocessed one for two weeks, in random order. The meals were matched for presented calories, energy density, sugar, salt, fibre and the main nutrients, and people could eat as much or as little as they liked. On the ultra-processed diet they ate about five hundred kilocalories a day more and gained nine tenths of a kilo. On the unprocessed diet they lost about as much.

S2: So processing itself makes you overeat?

S1: The authors say their study was not designed to identify the cause. And the matching was less complete than it sounds. The share of added sugar, of saturated fat and of insoluble fibre differed. And energy density was matched only as served: the ultra-processed diet's solid food was about eighty five percent more energy dense, which the authors say likely contributed to the extra eating. And it was two weeks a diet, twenty people, on a ward.

S2: How do the two sides read it?

S1: Hall's team ends: limiting consumption of ultra-processed foods may be an effective strategy for obesity prevention and treatment. On the other side, Astrup's point was that the trial's effect on intake faded quickly. In his words, it can be entirely explained by more conventional and quantifiable dietary factors, including energy density, intrinsic fibre, glycemic load and added sugar.

S2: Where do they agree?

S1: Both name energy density. The dispute is whether ultra-processed is a useful name for a bundle of properties that includes it, or a detour around properties you can measure directly. Neither side claims a mechanism specific to processing is established. Monteiro, arguing for the category, writes that although more research should be done to identify these mechanisms, existing evidence is sufficient to recommend the avoidance of ultra-processed foods. Astrup writes that little evidence supports effects beyond those that can be accounted for by nutrient composition, energy density, and food matrices.

S2: Now meat. What does nobody dispute?

S1: That the association is there. IARC put processed meat in Group one on sufficient evidence in humans that it causes colorectal cancer. Its figure: every fifty gram portion of processed meat eaten daily increases the risk of colorectal cancer by about eighteen percent. NutriRECS's own reviews found the same direction.

S2: Then how do they end up with opposite advice?

S1: Partly because they asked different questions. IARC asked whether it can cause cancer, and its groups grade how strong that evidence is, not how big the risk. The World Cancer Research Fund asked what people should eat to prevent cancer, and advises little, if any, processed meat. NutriRECS asked what adults should do about the meat they eat, and weighed deaths, heart disease and diabetes as well as cancer.

S2: Walk me through NutriRECS.

S1: Its guideline says recommendations require consideration of the certainty in the evidence, the magnitude of potential benefits and harms, and explicit consideration of people's values and preferences. On certainty, its reviews rated the cohort evidence low or very low, one of them citing inadequate adjustment for known confounders, residual confounding and recall bias. On magnitude, measured per three servings a week, it called the associations very small. And on values, a review found low-certainty evidence that omnivores are attached to meat and are unwilling to change this behaviour when faced with potentially undesirable health effects. Put together, on the lesson's reading: a very small possible benefit, at low certainty, against something people value. Under its grading system that gives a weak recommendation, and this one pointed to leaving intake where it was.

S2: And the critics?

S1: Harvard's School of Public Health, in its words: the new guidelines are not justified as they contradict the evidence generated from their own meta-analyses. It says the effects may seem small because the unit, three servings a week, is small. And it reports three panel members voted against the recommendations. Qian, Hu and colleagues argued the evidence should have been upgraded, on grounds including dose-response and consistency across studies, and that inadequate attention was paid to what might be replacing red meat.

S2: Does the panel get an answer?

S1: The course hasn't read a reply from the panel, so it gives the panel's case from its method, in its own summary. Its reviews reported an association, in the expected direction, and called it very small and uncertain. And the panel put the values step in the open, ran a review of it and printed the result. Harvard's own page says a long trial of meat reduction has never been conducted, for practical and ethical reasons. So the argument is over how much weight cohorts can bear without one.

S2: So how does the lesson sort it?

S1: Established, by every source: more processed meat goes with more bowel cancer. Contested, and empirical: how certain that evidence is, what replaces the meat, and how big the effect is. Among the values: how to weigh a very small, uncertain reduction in risk against what people want to eat. The course writes out the premise each side needed, and says these are its own reconstructions. For NutriRECS: when the evidence of benefit is uncertain and the possible benefit to each person is very small, advice should not ask people to give up something they value. For the World Cancer Research Fund: when a food is a cause of a serious disease and no level of intake has been shown to be free of risk, advice should say eat little or none of it, even if the benefit to each person is small, because a small risk across a whole population adds up to many cases. The course describes those and doesn't decide them.

S2: We've left out how well specialists agree on what's ultra-processed, a review that graded one finding convincing and very low at once, a longer trial, the one large trial in the panel's reviews, a co-author's dissent from them, who paid on each side, the World Health Organization's page dated September twenty twenty six, and what the new US guidelines say. The full lesson is free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening.
