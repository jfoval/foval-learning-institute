S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Nutrition: The Evidence, on the lesson called Fat and the heart.

S2: The one people argue about over dinner. The box at the top of the lesson first, though.

S1: This course is education, not advice about your own diet. If you're in one of the groups it names, such as being pregnant or having diabetes or kidney disease, talk to a doctor or a registered dietitian first. If food, eating or your weight has started to feel out of your control, tell a doctor; the helplines are in that box. And this lesson adds one line. If you take a medicine to lower your cholesterol, nothing in it is a reason to change that without your doctor.

S2: So where does it start?

S1: In Minnesota, between nineteen sixty eight and nineteen seventy three. In state mental hospitals and a nursing home, nine thousand, four hundred and twenty three people were randomly assigned to one of two diets. One replaced saturated fat with corn oil and a corn-oil margarine. The other kept the animal fats, margarines and shortenings the hospitals already served. It was double blind. The corn-oil diet cut blood cholesterol by thirteen point eight percent, against one percent in the controls. It didn't cut deaths. Much of it went unpublished until Christopher Ramsden's team recovered it in twenty sixteen.

S2: That sounds like the end of the argument.

S1: Supporters of the limit have serious answers to it. But first the lesson pulls apart three claims that usually travel together. One: industrial trans fat harms the heart. Established; every side accepts it. Two: LDL cholesterol causes the fatty build-up in artery walls, and saturated fat raises LDL. Also established, with a small dissent. Three: eating less saturated fat cuts heart attacks, strokes and deaths at the intakes people actually eat. Contested, by serious people on both sides.

S2: And the mistake is blurring them.

S1: Treating the second as if it were as shaky as the third, or the third as if it were as solid as the first. Trans fat is what agreement looks like. The cohorts, the US regulator and WHO line up against it, and nobody in the saturated fat fight disputes it. The course didn't read the feeding trials on it.

S2: Then the third claim, the fight itself.

S1: The best-known synthesis is the twenty twenty Cochrane review: fifteen trials of at least two years, fifty six thousand, six hundred and seventy five people. Pooling twelve of those trials, fifty three thousand, seven hundred and fifty eight people, the trials suggested that cutting saturated fat reduced combined cardiovascular events by seventeen percent, a risk ratio of nought point eight three, on moderate-quality evidence. The authors call it a potentially important reduction, and found no evidence of harm.

S2: Combined events. What about deaths?

S1: Little or no effect, on death from any cause or from cardiovascular disease, also moderate quality. And in the trials at low risk of bias, the events effect was smaller and not statistically significant. The review counts that against its own headline; it's part of why the seventeen percent is moderate, not high.

S2: And the case for the limit?

S1: It's a chain. Saturated fat raises LDL, LDL causes the disease, and the trials are the last link. The American Heart Association, in twenty seventeen, concluded strongly that lowering intake of saturated fat and replacing it with unsaturated fats, especially polyunsaturated fats, will lower the incidence of cardiovascular disease. It says trials that swapped in polyunsaturated vegetable oil cut cardiovascular disease by about thirty percent, though its abstract doesn't name them. WHO's ten percent limit is a strong recommendation on moderate certainty, because evidence of different kinds points to lower risk and it found nothing against a lower intake.

S2: And the case against?

S1: It opens with a concession. Astrup and eleven colleagues, in twenty twenty, accept that saturated fats increase LDL cholesterol. Their case is that the recommendation to limit dietary saturated fatty acid intake has persisted despite mounting evidence to the contrary. On LDL, they write that in most individuals the rise comes in larger particles, which are much less strongly related to risk. And the newest statement of the dissent, the Scientific Foundation behind the twenty twenty six US guidelines, compiled by Ramsden, says that none of the individual trials demonstrated the anticipated benefit, and that within typical intake ranges, saturated fat appears neither uniquely harmful nor protective. It calls that a neutral stance: foods containing saturated fat can be part of healthy dietary patterns when consumed in reasonable amounts and within minimally processed contexts.

S2: Back to Minnesota. What are the serious answers?

S1: One comes from the paper itself. People were followed only while in hospital, and only about a quarter stayed in the study for a year or longer. Heart disease takes years; most people ate the diet for months.

S2: Does the dissent get to object back?

S1: In the other direction, yes. The Scientific Foundation sets aside the Oslo Diet-Heart Study, which also cut hydrogenated margarines, added sardines and cod liver oil, and improved the diet overall. It says that makes it impossible to isolate the effects of the oils, and with trials like that excluded, there is no indication of benefit. Reports say the Heart Association's thirty percent includes Oslo and leaves out Minnesota, though the course couldn't read the full text to confirm it.

S2: So each side drops a trial the other counts.

S1: If those reports are right, and each has a reason. Minnesota was short and leaky; Oslo changed several things at once. The course's own reading, and it labels it as its own, is that the gap between that thirty percent and the null result in Ramsden's own meta-analysis, which admitted only unconfounded trials, is largely a gap in which trials count.

S2: Is there anything newer?

S1: Steen and colleagues, December twenty twenty five, pooling seventeen trials. For people at low cardiovascular risk, they found reducing or modifying saturated fat has little or no benefit over five years. For people at high risk, low to moderate certainty evidence of important reductions in deaths and major events, particularly heart attacks, when it's replaced with polyunsaturated fat.

S2: Same change, different answer depending on who you are?

S1: Here's the course's own arithmetic, on round figures, taking the seventeen percent as fixed. Healthy younger adults, two in a hundred of whom would have an event over a few years: about three fewer events per thousand people. Heart patients, twenty five in a hundred: about forty three fewer per thousand. Same relative effect, twelve and a half times the absolute gain.

S2: And where's the line for worth it?

S1: Steen set thresholds of importance over five years, five per thousand for fatal outcomes and ten for nonfatal ones. Treat these as nonfatal and three falls below, forty three well above. But those thresholds are the authors' judgement of how many fewer events are worth a change of diet, and another panel could set them elsewhere. That's a value laid on the evidence. Both camps can cite Steen.

S2: We've left out the evidence that LDL causes the disease and the small dissent from it, the cohort studies on which fat replaces saturated fat, the evidence on cheese and eggs, the Sydney trial in heart patients, Ancel Keys and the sugar industry papers, and who paid for the research on every side. The full lesson is free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening.
