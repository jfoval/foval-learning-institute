S2: The box at the top first, and today one line in it really matters.

S1: This course is education, not advice about your own diet. If you're pregnant or trying to be, have diabetes or kidney disease, take a medicine such as warfarin, or are deciding what a child should eat, talk to a doctor or a registered dietitian first. If food, eating or your weight has started to feel out of your control, tell a doctor, and the helpline numbers are in that box. Kidney disease is the one to hold on to today, because this lesson is mostly about protein.

S2: So. How much protein?

S1: Ask three expert bodies and you get three answers. The official US allowance is point eight grams per kilogram of body weight a day. A geriatric medicine group says at least one point oh to one point two for people over sixty five. A sports nutrition society says one point four to two point oh for people who exercise. It looks like a quarrel, but mostly it isn't. They're different kinds of number, built for different jobs.

S2: Start with the point eight, then. That's the RDA?

S1: The Recommended Dietary Allowance. People differ in how much of a nutrient they need, so a requirement is a spread. The middle of it is the Estimated Average Requirement, the EAR, which meets the needs of half of healthy people. The RDA is the EAR plus two standard deviations, which the official definition says covers nearly all, ninety seven point five percent. So, in the course's gloss, only about one person in forty needs the RDA or more.

S2: So most people need less than the RDA?

S1: On the official figures, yes. For one person eating the EAR, the National Academies put the risk of falling short at fifty percent. At the RDA, only two to three percent. It's a planning number with a margin built in, because nobody knows their own requirement. And the official table says the RDA is not used for groups. By the course's reasoning, count everyone below it as short and you'd count lots of people whose own need is lower than what they eat.

S2: But the new American guidelines went higher, didn't they?

S1: As of September twenty twenty six, the twenty twenty five to twenty thirty Dietary Guidelines for Americans set protein serving goals of one point two to one point six grams per kilogram a day. By the course's arithmetic that's fifty to a hundred percent above an RDA that hasn't been revised. The evidence for that goal, and the dispute over it, are lesson nine's. The older case for more than point eight comes from two places this course read, plus the sports evidence.

S2: Go on.

S1: The first is a different way of measuring. Courtney-Martin and colleagues, who developed the method they use, read protein need from the breath, and they estimate an EAR of point nine and an RDA of one point two in young adults and in older men and women. In their words, the estimates are about forty percent higher than the current protein recommendations on a body weight basis. But the lesson flags two things. Their one point two is the top of a confidence interval, not the EAR plus two standard deviations of how people differ, so the two numbers aren't built the same way. And the older-adult estimates rest on small studies, twelve women and six men. The research file also has no primary statement from the other side answering them, so it can't give one.

S2: And the second?

S1: A position on older people. The PROT-AGE Study Group said in twenty thirteen that older adults need more dietary protein than younger adults, and recommended at least one point oh to one point two for people over sixty five. Their reason is age-related changes in how the body handles protein. It's an expert position paper, not a systematic review with graded evidence, and that's the weight to give it.

S2: And then there's one point six. It's everywhere.

S1: Mostly from one paper. Morton and colleagues pooled forty nine randomised trials, one thousand, eight hundred and sixty three people, all combining protein supplements with resistance training. First the size. The supplements added about a third of a kilogram of lean mass, on top of what training alone produced. Then the line everyone quotes: beyond total intakes of about one point six grams per kilogram, supplementation gave no further gains.

S2: So one point six is the target.

S1: It's a ceiling on one benefit, in people who were training, not a recommended amount. And it says nothing directly about someone who isn't training, because nobody in the trials was. The full paper puts a confidence interval on that one point six running from one point oh three to two point two. They present the two-part line even though it wasn't statistically significant. And they suggest about two point two for people trying to get every last gram of muscle.

S2: Doesn't all this protein strain your kidneys?

S1: The lesson answers in two parts, about different people. For healthy kidneys, Devries and colleagues pooled twenty eight trials, and the change in kidney filtering did not differ between higher-protein diets and normal or lower ones. They conclude that high protein intakes don't adversely influence kidney function, as measured by filtering rate, in healthy adults. The lesson notes that two of the authors also wrote the Morton review, so the reassurance comes from researchers on the higher-protein side. That isn't a conflict of interest, and a kidney specialist would still want to know it. They name an unclear risk of selection bias in the trials as the main limitation.

S2: And for people whose kidneys aren't healthy?

S1: The advice runs the other way. KDIGO, the kidney guideline group, suggests keeping protein at point eight in adults with chronic kidney disease in its categories three to five, and says avoid high protein intake, above one point three, in adults with chronic kidney disease at risk of progression. Which category a person is in is for their doctor. And put the numbers side by side. That avoid line of one point three sits inside the new US goal of one point two to one point six. The research file notes those guidelines tell people with a chronic disease to talk to their clinician. A figure that's reasonable for a healthy lifter can be one a kidney specialist would tell a patient to avoid.

S2: And the official ranges for carbohydrate, fat and protein?

S1: The Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range. For adults, for instance, twenty to thirty five percent of energy from fat. In twenty twenty four a National Academies committee wrote that these were predicated on a narrative literature review that yielded estimates based on subjective interpretation of the data. It concluded the approach is not consistent with current evidence-based standards, and recommended removing the range from the framework altogether.

S2: So I should ignore it?

S1: Not quite what they said. Some users may wish to keep applying it, but they should realise its limitations. So when an app treats thirty five percent fat as if it were an upper limit, it's treating a range set by judgement as though it were a threshold set by measurement.
