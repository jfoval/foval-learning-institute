S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Nutrition: The Evidence, on the lesson called Supplements and alcohol.

S2: Two lines before we begin. This course is education, not advice about your own diet.

S1: So if you're pregnant or trying to be, have diabetes or kidney disease, take a medicine such as warfarin, or are deciding what a child should eat, talk to a doctor or a registered dietitian first. If food, eating or your weight has started to feel out of your control, tell a doctor. The helplines are in the box at the top of the lesson. And this lesson adds its own line: nothing here says what to take or whether to drink. Doses belong with a doctor or pharmacist.

S2: Supplements first. Which ones pass their own test?

S1: In twenty twenty two the US Preventive Services Task Force graded vitamins, minerals and multivitamins for preventing heart disease and cancer. Two got a firm no, a D grade: beta-carotene and vitamin E. For beta-carotene the harms outweigh the benefits, and the harm is more lung cancer in people who smoke or have been exposed to asbestos at work. For vitamin E, no net benefit.

S2: And multivitamins?

S1: Something different. For multivitamins, and every other single or paired nutrient, "the current evidence is insufficient to assess the balance of benefits and harms". That's called an I statement. It doesn't mean doesn't work. It means can't tell. And the grades don't apply to children, anyone pregnant or who may become pregnant, people who are chronically ill or in hospital, or anyone with a known nutritional deficiency. Most of the cases where a supplement clearly helps sit in that group.

S2: So where does a supplement actually work?

S1: The clean case is folic acid. In nineteen ninety one a Medical Research Council trial randomised one thousand, eight hundred and seventeen women who'd already had a pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect, a group of serious birth defects that includes spina bifida. Twenty seven of the completed pregnancies with a known outcome had a defect: six in the two folic acid groups, twenty one in the two without. The authors called it a seventy two percent protective effect.

S2: Why did that work when the others didn't?

S1: On the course's reading, the outcome is tied directly to the vitamin. The Task Force says many of these defects are caused by low folate levels, so the pill supplied what was missing. In twenty twenty three it reaffirmed its highest grade, A, for folic acid, for all persons planning to or who could become pregnant.

S2: And they can do harm.

S1: They can, and the lesson has a box listing the harms its sources record. Two are for anyone on warfarin. Vitamin K can reduce warfarin's ability to prevent clotting, and the NHS says not to take St John's wort or vitamin K supplements while you're on it. If that's you, read the box, and ask your warfarin clinic or doctor before anything else.

S2: Now alcohol. Two US federal reviews, weeks apart, pointing different ways.

S1: Start with the common ground, on cancer. In January twenty twenty five the Surgeon General, in a separate advisory, found "a causal relationship between alcohol use and increased risk for at least seven different types of cancer", contributing to nearly one hundred thousand cancer cases and about twenty thousand cancer deaths a year in the US. Of the two reviews, the one more favourable to moderate drinking, from the National Academies, agrees on breast cancer: moderate drinking was associated with a higher risk than never drinking. The other, from a federal committee on underage drinking, lists the same seven cancers as the Surgeon General. And heavy drinking is harmful on every reading.

S2: And the heart?

S1: That's the J-curve. In many cohorts light drinkers die less often than people who don't drink, and heavier drinkers more often. The strongest recent statement of the case that it's real is the National Academies' review, from December twenty twenty four. It took on the best-known objection directly, by requiring a comparison group that separated never drinkers from former drinkers. Pooling eight studies, it found "a 16 percent lower risk of all-cause mortality" among moderate drinkers than never drinkers, and associations with lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, both at moderate certainty. The UK's Chief Medical Officers in twenty sixteen found net benefits smaller than previously thought, and significant only in women over fifty five, and the Global Burden of Disease group in twenty twenty two a J-shape for people forty and over.

S2: That sounds like a yes.

S1: The review states its limits. No conclusion in it was issued with high certainty, the comparison mainly reflected drinking toward the lower end of moderate, and it "does not offer dietary recommendations or advice."

S2: And the case that the curve is an artefact?

S1: First, the comparison group. People who don't drink include people who stopped, some because they were ill: the sick quitter problem. Stockwell and colleagues pooled eighty seven cohorts. Before adjusting, light drinkers' relative risk of death was point eight six. Adjusted for this and for study quality, point nine seven, an interval that includes one.

S2: But the Academies had already separated out former drinkers.

S1: So this side has two more arguments. Light drinkers differ in other ways: in the UK Biobank, adjusting for their healthier lifestyles weakened the apparent heart benefit. And the genes. In East Asia, common variants change how the body handles alcohol and strongly affect how much people drink. In half a million Chinese adults, Millwood and colleagues found the ordinary analysis showed the dip for stroke and heart attack in men, and the genetic analysis showed none. Stroke risk rose steadily with genetically predicted drinking. Their conclusion: the apparently protective effects of moderate alcohol intake against stroke "are largely non-causal". For heart attacks they hedge, finding in this one study little net effect.

S2: So who's right?

S1: The other federal review, a draft from January twenty twenty five, modelled the observational evidence and concluded that the risk of dying from alcohol "begins at low levels of average use", though its modelling found some protective associations too. On the lesson's reading, each answers the other's strongest objection only partly. The never-drinker rule answers the sick quitter, but not the healthy-drinker difference, and the Academies themselves rated the studies in one analysis as having some concerns about bias, mainly from confounding. The genetic studies answer confounding, but the methods most used assume a straight line when the question is a dip, as the Biobank team points out.

S2: Hasn't anyone just run a trial?

S1: One was planned. MACH would have randomised people to about one drink a day or none. In twenty eighteen the NIH ended its funding, citing concerns about the study design. Researchers who study the alcohol industry later wrote that "Alcohol companies agreed to fund the MACH trial to advance their commercial interests rather than to help answer a major scientific question." No randomised trial has settled it.

S2: And what do the guides say now?

S1: The twenty twenty five to twenty thirty US guidelines, published in January twenty twenty six, dropped the old numeric limit for "Consume less alcohol for better overall health." On who shouldn't drink at all the guides agree, pregnant women among them. And one safety point from the UK guidance: very heavy drinkers, who are likely to experience alcohol withdrawal, are advised to seek medical advice before they stop drinking.

S2: We've left out the big trial of vitamin D and fish oil, the evidence on vitamin D in older people, B12, iron and winter vitamin D, what the US regulator does and doesn't check on a label, tainted products and liver injury, the lifetime cancer risks in numbers, where the phrase no safe level comes from, and who paid for the rest of the research. The full lesson is free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening.
