S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Nutrition: The Evidence, on the lesson called Patterns, and the guidelines that bundle them.

S2: The course's standing line first. It's education, not advice about your own diet.

S1: So if you're pregnant or trying to be, have diabetes or kidney disease, take a medicine such as warfarin, or are deciding what a child should eat, talk to a doctor or a registered dietitian first. If food, eating or your weight has started to feel out of your control, tell a doctor. The helplines are in the box at the top of the lesson. And one more thing. Guidelines and the arguments about them change, so everything here about the new US guidelines is as of September twenty twenty six.

S2: Where does it begin?

S1: With a retraction. On the thirteenth of June twenty eighteen, the New England Journal of Medicine retracted the best-known diet trial of the decade and, the same day, published it again. PREDIMED had assigned seven thousand, four hundred and forty seven people in Spain, at high cardiovascular risk, to a Mediterranean diet with extra olive oil, the same diet with extra nuts, or advice to cut fat. Part of it turned out not to have been randomised. At one of its eleven sites, for instance, some participants were assigned to a group without it.

S2: And the answer changed?

S1: Barely. The hazard ratios, a measure of relative risk over time, went from point seven zero to point six nine for olive oil, and stayed at point seven two for nuts. Every interval is still below one. And results were similar after leaving out the people whose assignments were known or suspected to have broken protocol.

S2: So nothing really happened.

S1: The number survived. The kind of evidence changed. For up to about a fifth of the people, chance may not have decided who ate what, so for them the result rests on adjustment, and adjustment can only balance what somebody measured. John Ioannidis granted that the reanalysis showed similar results, and added, "however, the study should no longer be considered a randomized trial". So the two readings agree on the number, and differ on what kind of evidence it now is. The course found no allegation of fraud.

S2: How big was it, in people?

S1: Crude event rates over a median of four point eight years were three point eight percent with olive oil, three point four with nuts, and four point four in the control arm. By the course's subtraction, one fewer person in a hundred for nuts and point six fewer for olive oil, against advice to cut fat, not against a typical diet. The reduction was mostly in strokes, and a Cochrane review found low-quality evidence of little or no effect on deaths, from cardiovascular causes or from any cause.

S2: Now the guidelines everyone's arguing about.

S1: The Dietary Guidelines for Americans, twenty twenty five to twenty thirty, released on the seventh of January twenty twenty six. Ten pages, against the last edition's one hundred and sixty four, with a ninety page companion called the Scientific Foundation.

S2: And a headline might tell you health bodies condemned it.

S1: So the lesson starts where the headlines didn't, with what both sides accept. The American Heart Association, on the day of release, welcomed the guidelines and commended "the emphasis on increasing intake of vegetables, fruits and whole grains while limiting consumption of added sugars, refined grains, highly processed foods, saturated fats and sugary drinks." And whatever you may have read, the saturated fat cap was kept, at ten percent of daily calories.

S2: Then where do they part?

S1: Protein first. For: the guidelines' heading is Prioritize Protein Foods at Every Meal, and the launch site says, "We are ending the war on protein." The Scientific Foundation objected that the advisory committee "consistently advocated plant-based dietary patterns, deprioritized animal-sourced proteins, and favored high linoleic acid vegetable oils", and called its plan to list beans, peas and lentils ahead of meat "a symbolic reordering lacking scientific justification".

S2: And against?

S1: Frank Hu, who chairs Harvard's nutrition department, warns that "Substantially raising overall protein intake without distinguishing between different protein sources may have unintended long-term health implications." The Heart Association urges people "to prioritize plant-based proteins, seafood and lean meats". The new figure is one point two to one point six grams per kilogram, and a critical commentary by Pasiakos and colleagues calls its basis "insufficiently comprehensive", while granting that recognising protein as part of a healthy diet is a strength. The course didn't read the Scientific Foundation's own protein chapter.

S2: Then fats.

S1: For: the Scientific Foundation argues that the emphasis on restricting saturated fat "may have inadvertently encouraged selection of processed products, such as low- and nonfat yogurts with added sugars, artificial sweeteners, emulsifiers, thickeners, and other chemical additives." Against: an expert analysis for the American College of Cardiology says evidence from feeding trials and cohort studies "does not support promotion of butter or beef tallow", and that with the new servings of animal protein foods and those fats, saturated fat intake will exceed ten percent of daily calories. But the same analysis concedes that dairy food consumption hasn't been associated with increased cardiovascular risk. Its objection is to butter and tallow, and to where the total lands.

S2: And the fight over how the document was made?

S1: That's the sharpest disagreement. A panel of scientists, the twenty twenty five advisory committee, wrote a report first. The Scientific Foundation says the administration "implemented an independent evidence review process to address and correct deficiencies" in that report. Its reviewers were chosen through a federal contracting process, each review had two independent peer reviewers, and it names its nine authors and prints their disclosures. Nina Teicholz, a science journalist who founded the Nutrition Coalition, had campaigned against the old process for years. And Zoë Harcombe, a researcher who favours lower carbohydrate eating, wrote in January twenty twenty six that the new guidelines "bore almost no resemblance to the Scientific Report which preceded them", and called it a "monumental change".

S2: And the other side?

S1: The American Society for Nutrition, which supported the broad eating pattern, said "departing from the established scientific review process undermines confidence in the DGAs and nutrition science", and pointed to a lack of transparency about the new reviews. Deirdre Tobias of Harvard, a member of the twenty twenty five committee: "As of today, there has not been transparency in who wrote the new DGAs."

S2: So Harcombe and the Society agree on the facts.

S1: They agree the guidelines departed from the committee's report, and part over whether that was a repair or a breach. The Society also said it supports its members who served as review authors.

S2: So who's right?

S1: As of September twenty twenty six the course calls the process question open, and doesn't answer it. Whether an administration should set aside its advisory committee's report is partly about evidence and mostly about who should decide, by what procedure, with what accountability, and those are value questions informed people answer differently. And it names its own imbalance: of the journal commentaries that followed, it read three, all critical, and none from the guidelines' side.
