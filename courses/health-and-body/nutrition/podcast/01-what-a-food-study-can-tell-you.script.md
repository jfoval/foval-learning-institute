If you're pregnant or trying to be, have diabetes or kidney disease, take a medicine such as warfarin, or are deciding what a child should eat, talk to a doctor or a registered dietitian first. And if food, eating or your weight has started to feel out of your control, tell a doctor. The helpline numbers for the UK and the US are in that box, at the top of every lesson.

S2: Okay. So where does the course start?

S1: With a cookbook. In a study published in twenty thirteen, two researchers picked fifty common ingredients from random recipes and searched the medical literature for studies linking each one to cancer. Forty of the fifty had them. The research to frighten you about your kitchen, or reassure you, mostly exists already. The course is about which of it can bear the weight.

S2: Why is that hard? We test drugs all the time.

S1: A drug trial can give half its volunteers a dummy pill, and nobody knows which they got. Food breaks almost every part of that. The clearest statement of why comes from the field's defenders, a group at Harvard who wrote a long answer to the critics in twenty fifteen. You can't hide a diet, so a diet trial usually can't be blinded. People don't stay on diets: the group reports dropout rates of thirty to forty percent in weight-loss trials, even after just one year. And the outcomes, heart attacks and deaths, are slow.

S2: And under all of that?

S1: You cannot change one thing. If you eat less of anything, you eat more of something else, or less in total.

S2: Give me a case where it mattered.

S1: Beta-carotene, the orange pigment in carrots. The cohort studies, which record what people eat and follow them for years, linked it to less lung cancer. The field's best-known critic, John Ioannidis, gives the figure on blood levels: people at the top against people at the bottom had a relative risk of death of point six nine, with an interval from point five nine to point eight. About a third lower.

S2: Measured in blood. That sounds solid.

S1: Then three big randomised trials gave beta-carotene pills or a placebo. In ATBC, twenty nine thousand, one hundred and thirty three Finnish male smokers took twenty milligrams a day for five to eight years. Lung cancer rose by eighteen percent on beta-carotene, and total mortality was eight percent higher. In CARET, eighteen thousand, three hundred and fourteen smokers, former smokers and asbestos workers took thirty milligrams with vitamin A. The relative risk of death from any cause was one point one seven. The third, in US doctors over about twelve years, found neither benefit nor harm.

S2: So the cohorts said lower and two of the trials said higher. Who got it wrong?

S1: There are two explanations, and the course thinks both are true. The critic's reading is that the cohorts were confounded. A high blood level marks a diet rich in fruit and vegetables, and plausibly not smoking and other healthy habits. That's the research file's reading, not something the cohorts measured. And you can't adjust away what you didn't measure. The defender's reading is that the trials asked a different question. The cohorts saw beta-carotene inside food, as part of a whole diet. The trials gave big doses of one isolated compound, often to smokers. In the Harvard group's words, an equally, and sometimes more likely, possibility is that the R C T and observational studies are answering very different questions.

S2: So both sides agree the pills failed.

S1: They do. They part over what that says about cohort research as a whole, and that can be counted. In twenty twenty one Schwingshackl and colleagues did. They lined up ninety seven diet and disease pairs, trial evidence matched with cohort evidence. For the seventy one pairs with a yes-or-no outcome, like a death, they divided the trial result by the cohort result. If they agreed perfectly, that ratio would be one. The pooled ratio was one point oh nine.

S2: So trials find smaller effects?

S1: The authors warn against reading it that way. A ratio above one can mean the trial found a little less benefit, or a little more harm. It means the two differ a little on average. That's a partial vindication for the defence. But the prediction interval, where the ratio for a single new pair might fall, runs from point eight one to one point four six. On average they agree. For any one question, you can't assume it. And most of the disagreement came from pairs that didn't ask the same question, especially supplements in trials against nutrient status in cohorts.

S2: Which is beta-carotene exactly.

S1: Exactly. By the course's own rough arithmetic, one point oh eight over point six nine is about one point five seven, above the top of that interval. Where the exposure was identical, the authors found the estimates were similar.

S2: So what does the course actually hand me to carry around?

S1: A question. Every Core course before this one added one to the institute's way of reading a claim, and Mental Fitness's was, what did the comparison group get? This course adds: instead of what? Because you can't change one thing, a claim about a food is a claim about a swap. Take cut saturated fat. Li and colleagues followed eighty four thousand, six hundred and twenty eight women and forty two thousand, nine hundred and eight men for twenty four to thirty years, and modelled moving five percent of energy from saturated fat to something else. Here are three of the lesson's four rows. Replaced by polyunsaturated fat, coronary heart disease risk was twenty five percent lower. By carbohydrate from whole grains, nine percent lower. By refined starches and added sugars, not significantly associated with risk.

S2: Same thing taken out, different answers.

S1: And those are associations in cohorts, not trials. But there's a real objection. The scientific document published alongside the US Dietary Guidelines for twenty twenty five to twenty thirty says those substitution models infer hypothetical nutrient exchanges that did not actually occur in a person's diet. The lesson calls that a fair methodological point. Nobody in those cohorts was told to swap anything.

S2: So it's not quite Mental Fitness's question.

S1: No. That one's about the arm of a trial. This one's about the plate, and it applies to a cohort as much as to a trial. The mainstream says the replacement decides the answer. The dissent says the swaps are hypothetical. Either way, you can't judge cut saturated fat until somebody says what's replacing it.
