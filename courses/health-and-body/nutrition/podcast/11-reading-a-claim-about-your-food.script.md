S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Nutrition: The Evidence, on its last lesson, Reading a claim about your food.

S2: This lesson leans hardest on the box at the top, so here it is in full.

S1: This course is education, not advice about your own diet. If you're pregnant or trying to be, have diabetes or kidney disease, take a medicine such as warfarin, or are deciding what a child should eat, talk to a doctor or a registered dietitian first. And if food, eating or your weight has started to feel out of your control, tell a doctor, or call Beat in the UK, or ANAD's peer-support helpline in the US. Both numbers are in that box.

S2: So. A claim about food. Where do you start?

S1: With the question this course has asked all along. Instead of what? You can't change one thing. So the course puts that question in front of the institute's sort, the three bins. Measurable, meaning some result could count against the claim. Unmeasurable as stated, where nothing in it could come out the other way. And false, meaning it was checked and didn't hold.

S2: Give me a claim.

S1: Drink at least eight glasses of water a day, and tea and coffee don't count. Instead of what? Two swaps hide in it: water in place of other drinks, and water on top of whatever you already get, food included. So it's two claims.

S2: And is either true?

S1: In two thousand and two H. Valtin, in a review for a physiology journal, went looking for the evidence. He reported: "No scientific studies were found in support of 8 x 8." And surveys of thousands of presumably healthy adults strongly suggested such large amounts aren't needed.

S2: For everyone?

S1: He fences it himself. The conclusion is limited to healthy adults in a temperate climate leading a largely sedentary life. And the other way, he says large intakes are advisable for treating or preventing some diseases, and certainly called for under special circumstances, such as vigorous work and exercise, especially in hot climates. On the second half, he cites studies showing caffeinated drinks may indeed be counted toward the daily total. The course read his abstract, not those studies.

S2: But there's an official figure, isn't there? Three point seven litres for men?

S1: The US National Academies set an Adequate Intake of total water of three point seven litres a day for men aged nineteen to thirty and two point seven for women. It's total water, from drinks and food. And the Academies say that for a healthy person, intake below it may not confer additional risk, and it should not be interpreted as a specific requirement.

S2: So the verdict?

S1: Half by half, in the course's reading. At least eight glasses is measurable and unsupported for healthy, mostly sedentary adults in a mild climate. Not shown false for everyone, because, as Valtin says, it's difficult or impossible to prove a negative. Tea and coffee don't count goes against both sources, the nearest thing to a false claim in the lesson, on the strength of an abstract and a summary chapter. And if a doctor has given you a fluid amount, more or less than usual, that number is about you, and this isn't.

S2: What about caffeine in pregnancy? I've heard two hundred milligrams.

S1: Three bodies use that number, in three framings. The NHS says you should not have more than two hundred milligrams a day, because too much caffeine has been linked to a higher chance of miscarriage and stillbirth. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists says moderate consumption, less than two hundred a day, does not appear to be a major contributing factor in miscarriage or preterm birth. And Europe's food safety authority says intakes up to two hundred a day do not raise safety concerns for the foetus.

S2: So they agree.

S1: On the number, not the framing, and none says harm begins at two hundred. The obstetricians' evidence is from twenty ten, and the course didn't read the newer studies that have argued for a lower figure. So, on the lesson's grades, three bodies use two hundred milligrams is established. Harm begins above it is contested.

S2: Can the course tell me whether my own diet is fine?

S1: No. The evidence is about groups, and no average says where you'd fall, so the course won't bless the way you eat, and it won't condemn it. Someone who can look at your diet, health and medicines together can. In the UK, Dietitian is a protected title: to use it you must be registered with the Health and Care Professions Council. The British Dietetic Association, which has an interest in the distinction, notes that nutritionist, nutritional therapist and diet expert are not protected. A nutritionist may well be trained and on the UK voluntary register; the title alone won't tell you. In the US, registered dietitian and registered dietitian nutritionist, R D and R D N, are national credentials. Whether nutritionist is regulated depends on your state, though the course saw that only in a search result and read no state's rules, so check with your state's licensing board. Neither title guarantees good advice. And for anything on the box's list, a doctor is the other person to ask, often first.

S2: When does it stop being a question for a course at all?

S1: The lesson gives reasons behind some of the box's list, and a few it doesn't name. One it doesn't name is losing weight without trying: the NHS says it can be a sign of stress or a serious illness, see a GP just in case. A medicine that meets food: foods high in vitamin K, like broccoli and spinach, can affect how warfarin works. You can still eat them, but talk to your warfarin clinic, and talk to your doctor before any big change to your diet. So eat more leafy greens, ordinary advice for most people, is one to talk over first if you're on warfarin.

S2: And eating disorders.

S1: The NHS describes an eating disorder as a mental health condition where you use the control of food to cope with feelings and other situations. Among its signs are spending a lot of time worrying about your weight and body shape, and having very strict habits or routines around food. Its advice: if you think you may have an eating disorder, see a GP as soon as you can. And with treatment, most people can recover. If you're worried about someone else, let them know you're worried about them, encourage them to see a GP, and you could offer to go with them.

S2: That feels close to what this course trains, doesn't it? Checking everything.

S1: The lesson says so. Reading claims closely can turn into checking every label and every meal. If rules about food have started to take up more of your day than you'd like, that's close to the NHS's very strict habits or routines around food, and it's the box's question, not this course's. Beat's number there is its England line, and its website lists the numbers for Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. ANAD's line is peer support, not treatment.

S2: And the decision the course ends on?

S1: The course project ends in one of three decisions about one claim: change something, keep something deliberately, or stop acting on the claim. On the course's reading, an established finding about a swap you'd actually make can carry a change, weighed against what it costs you. A contested one can't settle it, so the decision rests on a premise of yours, and you write it down. A value question is yours. A swap that's not stated means the claim can't bear a change by itself, and doesn't show your present eating is fine either. But if the box's list applies to you and touches the claim, the decision is ask first, and the project scores that in full.
