S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Nutrition: The Evidence, on the lesson called Energy in, energy out.

S2: This one's about weight, so the box at the top matters even more than usual.

S1: It does. The course is education, not advice about your own diet. If you're pregnant or trying to be, have diabetes or kidney disease, take a medicine such as warfarin, or are deciding what a child should eat, talk to a doctor or a registered dietitian first. If food, eating or your weight has started to feel out of your control, tell a doctor, and the helpline numbers are in that box. And the lesson says of itself that it describes what studies found about weight. It says nothing about what your weight, or anyone's, should be.

S2: So start with the thing everybody's heard. Cut five hundred calories a day, lose a pound a week.

S1: The sum behind it is that a pound of body fat holds about three thousand five hundred calories, and five hundred times seven is three thousand five hundred. In twenty thirteen Thomas and colleagues tested it against seven weight-loss experiments where intake was supervised or objectively measured, and concluded it grossly overestimates actual weight loss.

S2: Is the three thousand five hundred wrong, then?

S1: Not really, and that's the interesting part. Hall and Chow wrote a one-page letter warning against the erroneous conclusion that what's wrong with the rule is the number itself. The figure is appropriate for modest weight changes in overweight and obese people, they say, but an overestimate in others. The most serious error is that the rule doesn't account for dynamic changes in energy balance during an intervention. The five hundred was measured against what the body burned at the start. As weight falls, a smaller body costs less to run, in the research file's words, with some adaptation on top, so the same intake becomes a smaller deficit each week, and the loss slows towards a new steady weight.

S2: How slow?

S1: Hall's model in twenty eleven put it at half-times of about one year. In the course's gloss, roughly half the eventual change has happened by the end of the first year, and the rest comes ever more slowly. And people carrying more body fat lose more from the same change, and take longer to get there.

S2: So where's the fight?

S1: Everyone agrees on the account: if you gained weight, intake exceeded expenditure. Hall and eleven colleagues call energy balance a law of physics that is agnostic as to obesity mechanisms. The dispute is which way the causes run. In twenty twenty one Ludwig and sixteen co-authors, Willett among them, set out the carbohydrate-insulin model. In their words, it proposes a reversal of causal direction: increasing fat deposition in the body, resulting from the hormonal responses to a high glycemic load diet, drives positive energy balance.

S2: In English?

S1: The research file's summary of their full text is this. A meal of rapidly digested carbohydrate raises insulin, which pushes fuel into storage. A few hours later the fuel in the blood falls, the brain reads a shortage, hunger rises, and energy use may fall. The authors themselves say studies of cellular metabolism and energy sensing are needed to test it. So, in the course's own gloss, on this view overeating is the result of fat storage, not its cause.

S2: And the other side?

S1: Hall and colleagues replied in twenty twenty two with the energy balance model. It says the brain is the primary organ responsible for body weight regulation, working mainly below conscious awareness, controlling intake in response to the body's energy needs and to environmental influences. And they call the carbohydrate-insulin model a special case of theirs, with a narrower focus on high glycemic load diets. Each side says the other describes it unfairly. But neither says calories don't count. They disagree about what makes people take in more of them.

S2: Someone must have tested it.

S1: Each side has its trial. Hall's twenty twenty one inpatient trial had twenty adults eat as much as they liked of a plant-based, low-fat, high glycemic diet for two weeks and an animal-based ketogenic diet for two weeks. The authors took the insulin model to predict more eating on the high glycemic diet. On the low-fat diet people ate less: six hundred and eighty nine calories a day less than on the low-carbohydrate diet over the two weeks, and five hundred and forty four less in the final week. The authors say the model's predictions were inconsistent with their observations. The research file adds two limits of its own: the diets differed in many ways at once, and two weeks is exactly the length the insulin model's authors call too short.

S2: And the other trial?

S1: Ebbeling and colleagues in twenty eighteen, Ludwig among the authors. A hundred and sixty four adults who had already lost weight were fed diets of sixty, forty or twenty percent carbohydrate for twenty weeks, with calories set to hold weight steady, and energy use measured by doubly labelled water. The low-carbohydrate group's energy use rose by two hundred and nine calories a day more than the high-carbohydrate group's. Their conclusion: consistent with the carbohydrate-insulin model.

S2: That sounds like a win.

S1: Then Hall, Guo and Speakman reanalysed the public data. The analysis plan was changed after the trial finished, and the original preregistered plan didn't find significant diet differences. Reported intake ran well below reported expenditure, which suggests eating outside the study. And doubly labelled water measures carbon dioxide, not calories. They found no significant difference in carbon dioxide, so the difference in calories came from assuming each diet burned a different fuel mix. But they also reported what cuts against them: their own exploratory analysis still found expenditure two hundred and fifty five calories higher on low carbohydrate at the midpoint, and two hundred and twenty four at the end.

S2: And Ebbeling answered?

S1: In twenty twenty, with a measure that doesn't use doubly labelled water at all: how much each group had to be fed to hold its weight. It was higher on low carbohydrate, by a hundred and eighty one to two hundred and forty six calories a day across their models. Every interval stays above zero, but one reaches down to eight, so the size is far less certain than the direction. In this institute's terms, it's a contested empirical question, not settled either way.

S2: And for somebody listening who's thinking about all this in terms of their own body?

S1: The lesson closes its teaching with two cautions. NICE's eating-disorder guideline says not to use single measures such as body mass index to decide whether to offer treatment, and the research file draws the consequence: a person in a larger body can have an eating disorder, and a number on a scale doesn't rule one in or out. And two teenage cohorts linked dieting to later eating-disorder outcomes. They're observational, dieting may be an early sign as well as a cause, and what they support is caution about dieting in adolescence. If any of this is close to home, the numbers are in the box at the top.

S2: We left out whether your metabolism really slows in middle age, exercise and the evidence for a ceiling on what it adds, The Biggest Loser six years on, two pooled analyses that point opposite ways, and the practice problems. The full lesson is free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening.
