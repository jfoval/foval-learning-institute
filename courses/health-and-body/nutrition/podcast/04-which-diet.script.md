S2: The box at the top first. This course is education, not advice about your own diet.

S1: If you're pregnant or trying to be, have diabetes or kidney disease, take a medicine such as warfarin, or are deciding what a child should eat, talk to a doctor or a registered dietitian first. If food, eating or your weight has started to feel out of your control, tell a doctor, and the helpline numbers are in that box. And like the last lesson, this one describes what studies found about weight, and says nothing about what yours should be.

S2: So. Every diet book says its plan is the one. How do you find out?

S1: Put a few hundred people on different diets at random and weigh them a year later. The cleanest test of the two most argued-over diets is DIETFITS, published in twenty eighteen. Six hundred and nine adults were randomised to a healthy low-fat or a healthy low-carbohydrate diet for twelve months, with twenty two small-group sessions that emphasised diet quality. And notice the word healthy in both names. Both groups were steered towards better food. The planned difference was which of fat or carbohydrate they cut.

S2: And who won?

S1: Nobody, detectably. The low-fat group lost five point three kilos on average and the low-carbohydrate group six point oh. A difference of point seven, with an interval from minus point two to one point six. It crosses zero, so the trial can't say which way the small difference runs.

S2: But the whole idea now is that the right diet depends on you. Your genes, your insulin.

S1: That was the trial's second question, and it's what made it famous. They measured a genetic pattern built from three gene variants, and how much insulin each person released thirty minutes after a dose of glucose. In the course's reading, the insulin measure tests a prediction of lesson three's carbohydrate-insulin model. Neither predictor worked. In the authors' words, neither of the two hypothesised predisposing factors was helpful in identifying which diet was better for whom.

S2: So that settles it?

S1: No. Two details keep it from proving more than it does. First, at twelve months the low-carbohydrate group was eating thirty percent of its energy from carbohydrate. That's a long way from a ketogenic diet, so DIETFITS tells you nothing directly about eating very little carbohydrate. Second, four hundred and eighty one of the six hundred and nine finished, and a year is a year, not five. And in the course's reading, not the authors', both arms shared a change, towards less refined food, and that shared change may be most of why both groups lost weight. On the predictors, it's two candidates in one trial of two fairly moderate diets. It doesn't rule out one nobody's found yet.

S2: Then what does predict it?

S1: Thirteen years earlier, Dansinger and colleagues in Boston put four famous diets side by side: Atkins, Zone, Weight Watchers and Ornish, forty people each. At a year, counting anyone who dropped out as losing nothing, the average losses ran from two point one to three point three kilos. And the central finding is one sentence. Amount of weight loss was associated with self-reported dietary adherence level, a correlation of point six oh, but not with diet type, point oh seven. How well people stuck to it went with how much they lost. Which diet they'd been given barely did. And the authors say overall adherence rates were low.

S2: So just stick with whatever you pick.

S1: The lesson wants two problems seen first. Adherence was measured by asking, and lesson two showed how far self-report can drift. Someone losing weight may well feel, and report, that they're sticking to the plan. And nobody was randomised to stick or not. After two months people chose their own level of effort, so adherence is observational inside a randomised trial. People who stick may differ in health, time, money or stress. In the course's reading of the design, the diet-type result is the randomised part, and it stands on firmer ground.

S2: There's something else in that trial, isn't there? The spread.

S1: The biggest gap between any two diets' averages was one point two kilos. The standard deviations, how far individuals typically sit from the average, ran from four point eight to seven point three, by the course's arithmetic four to six times that gap. Within the same diet some people lost a lot, some lost nothing and some gained.

S2: Which proves everybody has a diet that suits them.

S1: That's the idea people most often get wrong. A wide spread proves people differ in how much they lose. That could be how well they stuck to it, their circumstances, illness, measurement, chance, or a real match between person and diet. A trial where each person tried one diet can't separate those. And DIETFITS went looking for the match with the two best candidates it had, and didn't find it.

S2: And after the first year? Keeping it off is a different question.

S1: Before that, the lesson repeats something from lesson three. An eating disorder can occur at any body size. If reading about weight loss stirs up worries about your own eating, the helplines in the box are there for that. Then Look AHEAD, one of the largest and longest lifestyle trials. Five thousand, one hundred and forty five adults with type two diabetes and overweight or obesity, randomised to an intensive programme or to diabetes support and education. At eight years, half the intervention group were at least five percent below their starting weight, against thirty six percent of the others.

S2: And the heart attacks and strokes it was designed to prevent?

S1: They didn't fall detectably. The trial was stopped early for futility, meaning the monitors judged it very unlikely to show a benefit, at a median of nine point six years, with a hazard ratio of point nine five and an interval from point eight three to one point oh nine. The intervention did bring greater reductions in average blood sugar, and greater initial improvements in fitness and in every cardiovascular risk factor except LDL cholesterol. But the events didn't fall. And the comparison group lost weight too, three point five percent by the end against six point oh. So it compared a larger loss with a smaller one, over that time. That's one outcome, in people with type two diabetes, and it doesn't tell you about other outcomes or other people.

S2: And for anyone listening who lost weight and then regained it?

S1: England's guideline, from NICE, tells clinicians to say that weight regain is likely to happen, and if it does it is not because they or their healthcare professional have failed. And to encourage people to improve their diet even if they don't lose weight, because there can be other health benefits. The trials describe how hard sustained change is for most people, not a flaw in the people who regain.
