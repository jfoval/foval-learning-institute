S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Nutrition: The Evidence, on the lesson called Salt and sugar.

S2: Two things everyone's told to cut. But the box at the top of the lesson comes first.

S1: This course is education, not advice about your own diet. If you're in one of the groups that box names, which include anyone with diabetes or kidney disease, or taking a medicine such as warfarin, talk to a doctor or a registered dietitian first. If food, eating or your weight has started to feel out of your control, tell a doctor. The helpline numbers are in the box.

S2: So what does everyone agree on about salt?

S1: That less sodium lowers blood pressure. A Cochrane review of thirty four trials, each at least four weeks long, found that cutting salt by four point four grams a day lowered systolic pressure, the higher number, by about four millimetres of mercury. The fall was bigger in people with high blood pressure, and smaller but real in people without. Both sides of the dispute accept that. The argument is about how low to go.

S2: So how low?

S1: WHO's target is under two thousand milligrams of sodium a day, just under a teaspoon of salt, and the global average in twenty twenty one was more than twice that. The US National Academies drew their line higher: reduce intakes if above two thousand three hundred. And they said why they stopped there. Below it, cutting sodium lowers blood pressure, but the effect on chronic disease risk could not be characterized. Across the trials no deficiency symptoms were reported, and they found insufficient evidence of other harms from low intakes.

S2: And who says low might be worse?

S1: Mainly one research group in the course's sources, with a cohort called PURE. In a twenty fourteen paper, a hundred and one thousand, nine hundred and forty five people in seventeen countries had each given one morning urine sample, and the team estimated their sodium from it. Against a middle range of four to six grams of sodium a day, seven grams or more went with more risk of death or a major cardiovascular event. So did less than three grams.

S2: What's their case against the guidelines, in their own words?

S1: A twenty twenty review signed by twenty four authors says the low-sodium guidelines were developed without effective interventions to achieve sustained low sodium intake in free-living individuals, without a feasible method to estimate sodium intake reliably in individuals, and without high-quality evidence that low sodium intake reduces cardiovascular events, compared with moderate intake. They contend that current evidence, despite methodological limitations, suggests most of the world eats a moderate range, about two point three to four point six grams of sodium a day, that isn't associated with increased risk, and that risk increases above five grams. Then the concession, whole. While current evidence has limitations, and there are differences of opinion in interpretation of existing evidence, it is reasonable, based upon observational studies, to suggest a population-level mean target of under five grams a day in populations with mean sodium intake of over five grams a day, while awaiting the results of large randomised controlled trials of sodium reduction on incidence of cardiovascular events and mortality.

S2: One urine sample, by formula. Is that a good ruler?

S1: That's where the lesson opens. In twenty nineteen a team went back to two thousand, nine hundred and seventy four American adults whose intake had been measured properly, by full-day urine collections, three to seven times each, and followed for a median of twenty four years. Measured that way, sodium and death rose together in a straight line. Run through three published spot-sample formulas instead, every formula appeared to draw a J or a U. Same people, same deaths, different ruler. And when the team held the sodium reading constant, the estimates still appeared to be inversely related to mortality at the low end. Their conclusion: the formulas per se seem to be related to mortality independent of sodium. But that paper doesn't name PURE, and the course didn't read a reply from the PURE group, so the link between the two is the course's. And PURE's group treated its estimate as a group-level measure, a claim about averages, while the twenty nineteen paper tested each person's estimate. Whether that answers the critique is part of the dispute.

S2: So is the low end settled now?

S1: No. Showing a method can draw a J doesn't show low intakes are safe or beneficial. It weakens one line of evidence for harm. A big randomised trial in rural China did count strokes, and a salt substitute with a quarter of its sodium chloride swapped for potassium chloride cut them by fourteen percent. That's the relative figure; by the course's arithmetic, it's about four and a half fewer strokes per thousand person-years. But its people were older and at high risk, it lowered sodium and raised potassium at once, and no trial in the course's sources compares low sodium with moderate.

S2: Should people switch to those substitutes?

S1: WHO now suggests them. But a summary of its guideline, from the institute that ran that trial, notes a rationale for risk from high potassium if they're consumed in excess by people with chronic kidney disease, or on potassium supplements or potassium-sparing diuretics. Whether a medicine you take rules the swap out is a question for a doctor or pharmacist.

S2: And sugar. Same shape?

S1: Same shape, different ground. The agreed part is weight. A review of trials found that when adults ate as they liked, cutting sugar went with about point eight of a kilo less body weight. But when sugar was swapped for other carbohydrate with calories held equal, the change was point nought four of a kilo. Almost nothing. So of any sugar trial, ask: instead of what?

S2: Then who says sugar is special?

S1: Most directly, in the course's sources, Lustig. Table sugar is made of two molecules, glucose and fructose, and his case is that fructose is unlike glucose. In the course's own plain translation of his words: when someone is already eating more than they burn and their liver's sugar stores are full, fructose overloads the liver's energy machinery, the liver makes new fat, and it stops responding well to insulin. He concludes that fructose can exert detrimental health effects beyond its calories and in ways that mimic those of ethanol, its metabolic cousin. So his claim is about the liver and metabolic disease, not mainly weight, and it carries its own condition: people eating more than they need.

S2: And the reply?

S1: Kahn and Sievenpiper, in a twenty fourteen debate. In their words, there is no clear or convincing evidence that any dietary or added sugar has a unique or detrimental impact relative to any other source of calories on the development of obesity or diabetes. And: for those who wish to reduce energy consumption, ingesting less sugar is a good place to start. However, doing so does not automatically portend any clinical benefit. Their trial evidence is Sievenpiper's review of fructose feeding trials, which found no overall effect on body weight when calories were held equal.

S2: So who's right?

S1: The lesson leaves that open. The trials that would test Lustig's claim, long ones at equal calories measuring liver fat or new diabetes, are what its research names as missing. Until they exist, Kahn and Sievenpiper can say there's no clear or convincing evidence of a unique harm, and Lustig can say the evidence they cite tests a different claim.

S2: We've left out the feeding trial that tested three salt levels, WHO's two sugar limits and what each rests on, Lustig's own short trial, the evidence on sugary drinks, sweeteners and the aspartame cancer label, and who paid for the research on each side. The full lesson is free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening.
