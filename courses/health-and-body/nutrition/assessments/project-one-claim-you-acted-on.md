Every lesson in this course took a claim about food and asked what stands behind it. This project
does that once more, from start to finish, on a single claim you have actually lived by: the reason
you drink oat milk, skip breakfast, avoid seed oils, buy the wholemeal loaf, or have never trusted
eggs. It ends with a decision, and the decision is allowed to be "change nothing".

:::callout Before you change anything
This course is education, not advice about your own diet. If you're pregnant or trying to be, have diabetes or kidney disease, take a medicine such as warfarin, or are deciding what a child should eat, talk to a doctor or a registered dietitian first. If food, eating or your weight has started to feel out of your control, tell a doctor, or call Beat on 0808 801 0677 in the UK or ANAD's peer-support helpline on 1-888-375-7767 in the US.
:::

It takes about four hours, spread over a week or two: half an hour to choose the claim and write
down where you met it, two hours or so tracing it and reading the guides, and an hour and a half
to write it up.

## What this project is not

**It is not a diet experiment, and nothing in it asks you to weigh yourself or record what you eat.**
The object of study is the claim, and the only part that is about you is the last step, where you
decide what to do.

That is deliberate. Lesson 2 showed how far a food record drifts from what was eaten, so a week of
logging would teach you little about the claim. And for some readers, counting and weighing is not a
neutral act. If tracking food or weight has ever made eating feel more anxious or harder to control,
leave it out of this project entirely, including step 7. Every step can be done with nothing but the
claim, a search engine and the guides, and the rubric doesn't reward a single number about your body.

## Choosing the claim

Pick one claim about food that you have acted on, or that someone has told you recently and you were
tempted to act on. It can come from a friend, a doctor's waiting room poster, a podcast, a label, a
news story or this course. Write it down in the words you met it in, with where and roughly when.

Three things make a better choice.

1. **It has changed, or could change, what goes on your plate.** "Heating olive oil destroys what's
   good in it" is a good project if you've stopped cooking with it because of it.
2. **It points at evidence**, even vaguely: "studies show", a number, a named expert.
3. **It is not one of this course's own worked cases.** Beta-carotene, the saturated-fat swap table,
   the salt substitute trial, eight glasses of water and the rest have been traced for you already. A claim near one of them
   is fine, as long as the trail you follow is your own.

Don't choose a claim about a medicine, a supplement you take for a diagnosed condition, or what a
child should eat. Those belong to the callout's list, and step 6 would stop you there anyway. And if
the claims that come to mind are all about eating less or about your weight, and rules about food
have started to take up more of your day than you'd like, take that to the callout first. That's
the same check lesson 11's exercise asked for, and the project will wait.

## What you hand in

**One plain text or Markdown file, about two pages: roughly 900 to 1,300 words, in seven short
sections, one per step.** Put the claim, word for word, at the top, with where you met it and the
date you traced it. Websites change, and a date makes your quotations checkable.

## The seven steps

### Step 1: the claim, as you met it

Copy it exactly, including hedges such as "may" and "linked to". Then say in one or two sentences
what you did, or nearly did, because of it. A claim tidied on the way into your file is no longer
the claim.

If the claim is two claims joined by "so" or "which means" (a finding welded to a piece of advice),
split it now and follow the finding. The advice comes back in steps 5 and 7.

### Step 2: trace it, and name the design and the read level

Follow the claim back as far as it goes. A claim counts as **traced** when you have opened the thing
it rests on and either found the finding there or found that it isn't there. Europe PMC and PubMed
will usually give you a study's abstract free if you have a title, an author or a phrase.

Write down four things.

1. **What it rests on**: a study, a review, a guideline, a press release, a person, or nothing you
   could find.
2. **Whether the finding is there**, and in what words. Copy the sentence that matters exactly, in
   quotation marks.
3. **The design**, from lesson 1's table: cohort, case-control, randomised trial, controlled
   feeding, Mendelian randomisation, a review of one of those, or something else, named.
4. **How far you read**: a headline or a post, a press release, the abstract, or the whole paper.
   Say which, every time, in the form the lessons use ("read at abstract level").

Where the trail ends, say where. "Untraceable" is a result, and it comes in kinds: nothing cited, a
dead link, a paywall, or a source that does not contain the claim.

### Step 3: sort it, and say what would settle it

First put it in one of lesson 11's three bins: measurable, unmeasurable as stated, or false. A
measurable finding then gets the grade lessons 6 to 8 used: **established**, **contested** (an
empirical question serious people argue over), or **a value question** (one the evidence can inform
and not decide). Many claims are more than one: lesson 8 found the meat dispute was all three at once. Give
each part its sort and one piece of evidence for it.

Then say, for the empirical parts, what evidence would settle them: what design, in whom, for how
long, comparing what with what. Lesson 1 is where to look for what each design can and cannot reach.
If the honest answer is "a trial nobody could run", say that, and say what the next best thing is.

### Step 4: instead of what?

**What replaces the food?** Eating less of anything means eating more of something else, or less in
total, and lesson 1 showed that the same cut can have different answers depending on the swap.

Answer it twice: once for the evidence (in the study you traced, what was eaten instead, or what was
the comparison?) and once for you (if you acted on the claim, what did you eat instead?). Those two
answers often don't match, and when they don't, the study says less about your swap than the claim
suggested. If the source doesn't say what replaced the food, write "not stated". That is a finding.

### Step 5: what two guides say

Lesson 9 compared five guides, four in its table and Canada's in its exercise: the US *Dietary
Guidelines for Americans* [2020-2025](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/sites/default/files/2020-12/Dietary_Guidelines_for_Americans_2020-2025.pdf)
and [2025-2030](https://cdn.realfood.gov/DGA.pdf), the UK's [Eatwell
Guide](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-eatwell-guide), [Canada's Food
Guide](https://food-guide.canada.ca/) and the World Health Organization's [healthy diet fact
sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet). **Pick two, and find what each says about
your claim**, quoted, with the date of the version you read. The 2020-2025 US edition has been
replaced by the 2025-2030 one; if you pick it, say so, and say what the newer edition changed.

A guide saying nothing is a result too. So is two guides disagreeing, and if they do, say whether
they disagree about the evidence or about what to do with it, which is lesson 8's distinction
between the empirical part and the value part.

### Step 6: is this a self-help question for you?

Read the callout at the top of this page again, slowly, and check your claim against its list:
pregnancy or trying to be, diabetes, kidney disease, a medicine such as warfarin, a decision about
what a child eats, and food, eating or weight that has started to feel out of your control.

**If any of those is true for you, and it touches your claim, this stops being a self-help question.
Stop here, and take the claim and your step 2 to a doctor or a registered dietitian before you
change anything.** Your project is still complete: write in this section that the claim is one for a
professional, and make step 7's decision "ask first". That scores in full, because it's the right
answer.

**You don't have to say which item applies, or anything about your health.** "Checked; this one is
for my doctor" is enough. Nobody marking this needs your medical history, and you shouldn't hand it
over to pass a course.

If none of the list applies, say so in one line and carry on.

### Step 7: decide, and name the cost

Make one of three decisions, specifically enough that you could tell in a month whether you had
kept it.

1. **Change something.** Name it as a thing you do, not a number you reach: "lentils or beans in
   two dinners a week instead of the processed meat", not a target on the scale.
2. **Keep something deliberately.** You'll carry on as you are, and here is why.
3. **Stop acting on the claim.** The evidence didn't bear the weight you had put on it.

Then name **what it rests on** (your steps 2 to 5, and where they fall short of your decision), **what
it costs** (money, time, a food you like, an argument at the dinner table), and **one thing that would
tell you it was the wrong call**.

If you want a way to check in a month, make it about the behaviour and not your body: a yes or no, at
the end of each week, on whether you did the one thing. **Don't weigh yourself or log your food for
this project.** If even a weekly yes or no feels like the start of counting, skip the check; the
decision stands without it.

## A worked model: step 2, on one claim

This is how step 2 might look. Lesson 9 names the study and points here, and the words the claim
arrives in are invented for this model. Everything quoted from the study is copied from its abstract.

**The claim, as met:** a friend says, "A study of two thousand people on the carnivore diet found
almost no side effects."

**What it rests on.** A search for "carnivore diet" survey, with the friend's "two thousand people"
as the clue, finds a title naming "2029 Adults": [Lennerz and colleagues, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/cdn/nzab133), in *Current
Developments in Nutrition*, free at PubMed Central. Read at abstract level.

**Whether the finding is there.** Yes, and fairly quoted. The abstract says "A total of 2029
respondents" and "Prevalence of adverse symptoms was low (<1% to 5.5%)." Its conclusion: "Contrary to
common expectations, adults consuming a carnivore diet experienced few adverse effects and instead
reported health benefits and high satisfaction."

**The design.** None of lesson 1's five. The methods sentence says: "A social media survey was
conducted 30 March-24 June, 2020 among adults self-identifying as consuming a carnivore diet for ≥6
mo." So it is a survey at one point in time, of people who chose to answer, with no comparison group
and nobody followed forward. Every figure in it was reported by the people themselves, which lesson 2
spent a whole lesson on.

**What that design can show, and what it cannot.** It can show what people who were still on the
diet after six months, and who saw the post and answered it, said about themselves. It cannot show
what the diet does to people in general. Memory's question, who is in the sample, does most of the
work here: someone who tried the diet, felt unwell and stopped was unlikely to be answering a survey
for people currently on it. That is this model's reasoning, not something the abstract measured.

**What the claim left out.** The same abstract reports: "Among a subset reporting current lipids,
LDL-cholesterol was markedly elevated (172 mg/dL), whereas HDL-cholesterol (68 mg/dL) and
triglycerides (68 mg/dL) were optimal." The authors call the LDL figure "markedly elevated"
themselves, and lesson 6 is where LDL's link to heart disease was sorted as established. They also
end with their own limit: "The generalizability of these findings and the long-term effects of this
dietary pattern require further study."

**Where the trail ends.** At the abstract. The full paper is free and would say how the lipid figures
were collected, which is the next thing to read before leaning on them in either direction.

**So the traced claim, in a form the source supports:** of 2,029 adults who answered an online survey
after at least six months on the diet, few reported adverse symptoms, and among those who reported
lipid results, LDL was high. That is narrower than "almost no side effects", and both halves of it
come from the same abstract.

Notice what step 2 didn't do. It didn't decide whether the diet is good or bad. Step 3 would sort
the parts, step 4 would ask what the meat replaced, and step 5 would find that the guides have
something to say about a diet with almost no vegetables, fruit or grains. Your own step 2 should
stop where this one does: at what the source is, what it says and how far you read.

## Rubric

Five criteria, each marked 0 to 3. **The pass mark is 10 of 15.**

**Tracing** (steps 1 and 2)
- **3:** The claim is quoted exactly. It is followed to its source, or marked untraceable with where
  the trail ended. The key sentence is quoted exactly, and the design and the read level are both
  named correctly. For an untraceable claim, naming the kind of dead end and how far you got takes
  the place of the key sentence and the design.
- **2:** Traced, with the design and read level named, but one of them is wrong or the key sentence
  is paraphrased.
- **1:** A source is named but not opened, or opened without the design or read level stated.
- **0:** No attempt to trace, or a source cited that does not contain the claim, presented as though
  it did.

**Classification** (step 3)
- **3:** Each part of the claim is sorted as established, contested or value, with one piece of
  evidence for each sort, and the empirical parts say what design, in whom and for how long would
  settle them.
- **2:** Sorted with evidence, but what would settle it is vague ("more research").
- **1:** Sorted without evidence, or a claim with more than one part given a single sort.
- **0:** Not sorted, or a contested question called settled in either direction.

**The swap** (step 4)
- **3:** "Instead of what?" answered for the evidence and for the reader separately, with "not
  stated" used where the source is silent, and a sentence on whether the two swaps match.
- **2:** Answered for both, without comparing them.
- **1:** Answered for one only.
- **0:** Not answered, or the comparison group given where the swap was asked for.

**Guidance** (step 5)
- **3:** Two guides found and quoted, or recorded as silent on the claim, each dated, and any
  disagreement described as about evidence, about values, or both.
- **2:** Two guides quoted, with dates missing or the disagreement left undescribed.
- **1:** One guide, or two described from memory rather than quoted.
- **0:** No guide consulted.

**The decision** (steps 6 and 7)
- **3:** Step 6 is done, and the decision is specific, rests on steps 2 to 5 with any gap named,
  names its cost and names what would show it was wrong. A reader for whom step 6 said "ask a
  professional", and who wrote that as the decision, scores 3.
- **2:** Specific and grounded, but the cost or the sign of being wrong is missing.
- **1:** A decision stated without what it rests on, or phrased as a target for the body.
- **0:** No decision, or step 6 skipped.

**Two of these reward saying less, not more.** "Untraceable" and "not stated" score as well as a
tidy answer, because a gap filled from memory is the error this course has been showing you in other
people's claims. And the best decision may be the dullest: keep eating as you do, for reasons you can
now name.

## If the trail runs out

It often does. A claim that came from a friend who heard it on a podcast may end at the podcast, and
a podcast that cites "a Harvard study" may never say which. **That is a finished step 2**, and steps
3 to 7 still work on the claim as it stands: sort it as unsupported, ask what it would replace, see
what the guides say, and decide.

If you get a few hours in and find the claim was never about evidence at all ("my grandmother lived
to ninety on it"), you can finish it as it stands or start again with another claim. Say at the top
which you did.

## A note on what this project decides

It decides one thing, about one claim, for you. It doesn't make you a judge of the claim for anyone
else, and a claim you decide not to act on may still be right for somebody whose plate, health or
budget is different. Lesson 11 is where the course hands you the whole sort, and the callout above is
where it says when a question about food stops being yours to answer alone.
