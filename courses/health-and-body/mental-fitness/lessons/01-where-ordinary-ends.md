Most people who open a course called Mental Fitness are having a hard time, or know someone who is.
That is worth saying first, because it changes what this lesson has to do before it teaches
anything.

:::callout If you're struggling right now
This course is education, not care. If you're thinking about suicide or self-harm, or don't feel able to keep yourself safe, contact emergency services (911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 112 across the EU, 000 in Australia) or a crisis line: call or text 988 in the US and Canada, call Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK and Ireland, or Lifeline on 13 11 14 in Australia. Elsewhere, findahelpline.com lists free, confidential lines by country.
:::

That box is on every lesson in this course, word for word, and it is not a formality. The [NHS page on
urgent help](https://www.nhs.uk/nhs-services/mental-health-services/where-to-get-urgent-help-for-mental-health/) puts it better than this course could: "A mental health emergency should be taken as
seriously as a physical one. You will not be wasting anyone's time."[4] And the US line is not only
for people thinking about suicide. Its own page says it covers "mental health struggles, emotional
distress, alcohol or drug use concerns, or just need someone to talk to".[4]

This lesson is about a line: where ordinary stress, worry and low mood end, and something that needs
more than a course begins. It will show you how the guidelines draw that line. It will not help you
place yourself on one side of it, and it says why.

## Everyone, and some people

The World Health Organization's [fact sheet on anxiety](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/anxiety-disorders) opens like this: "Everyone can feel anxious
sometimes, but people with anxiety disorders often experience fear and worry that is both intense and
excessive." And then: "They are difficult to control, cause significant distress and can last a long
time if untreated."[3]

Read those two sentences slowly, because the whole of this lesson is in them. The first half says
anxiety is ordinary. The second says what makes it a disorder: intensity, excess, difficulty
controlling it, distress, and how long it lasts. Not the presence of the feeling. The feeling is
everyone's.

The depression fact sheet does the same thing. Depression "is different from regular mood changes and
feelings about everyday life", and a depressive episode lasts "most of the day, nearly every day, for
at least two weeks."[3] Again the line is drawn by duration and pervasiveness, not by sadness itself.

WHO's figures show why the line matters at the scale of a population. It estimates that "5.8% of the
global population currently experience an anxiety disorder", and that in 2023 depression affected
"5.2% of adults".[3] Those are modelled estimates, which is why WHO says "an estimated", and this
course repeats it. So most people who feel anxious or low this week do not have a disorder, and a
substantial minority of people do.

## How a clinician is told to draw the line

The two guidelines this course leans on are from the UK's National Institute for Health and Care
Excellence: CG113 for generalised anxiety disorder and panic, and NG222 for depression. **This course
read their recommendation chapters and the parts named in each lesson**, not the evidence reviews
behind them.[1][2]

A word about how NICE grades what it recommends, because you'll need it all course. It does not print
letters. It grades by verb: "Where there is clear and strong evidence of benefit, we will use the word
'offer'. Where the benefit is less certain we use the word 'consider'."[1] So when this course
quotes NICE, it keeps the verb. **Sleep** lesson 6 taught you to read a recommendation's strength
separately from its evidence; this is the same idea in NICE's form.

For anxiety, CG113's appendix says the key symptoms are "excessive anxiety and worry about a number of
events or activities" and "difficulty controlling the worry", and that "The worry should occur on a
majority of days for at least 6 months."[1]

And then the sentence that matters most in this lesson. A clinician is told to "conduct a
comprehensive assessment that does not rely solely on the number, severity and duration of symptoms,
but also considers the degree of distress and functional impairment."[1]

NG222 says the same thing about depression. Its severity "exists along a continuum and is
essentially composed of 3 elements: symptoms (which may vary in frequency and intensity); duration of
the disorder; the impact on personal and social functioning."[2] Symptoms, duration, impact. Three
things, weighed together, by somebody trained to weigh them.

:::predict Before you read on. Both guidelines tell clinicians not to rely on a count of symptoms. Why would a guideline refuse the one thing that is easiest to measure?

Because the same count can mean very different things. Six months of worry that a person can put down when they need to, and that does not stop them working, is a different thing from six months of worry that has taken over their days, even if both would tick the same boxes.

That is why the guidelines add distress and function. It is also why this course will not give you a checklist. A checklist would let you score yourself, and the guidelines' own point is that the score is not the answer. That reading of why is this course's, from the guidelines' wording.[5]
:::

## Two people, one symptom

Here's a worked case. Both people are invented for this lesson.[5]

Priya cannot stop worrying. She lies awake running through what could go wrong, her shoulders ache,
and she's short with people. Her final exams are in nine days.

Tom has the same worry, the same aching shoulders, the same short temper. He's had them on most days
since last winter, eight months ago, about work, money, his parents and whatever else comes up. He's
stopped going to the gym and has been called in about missed deadlines.

Put them against what the guidelines weigh.

- **Duration.** Priya's worry has a date on it and nine days to run. Tom's has lasted beyond CG113's
  six months and has no event it is attached to.
- **Distress and control.** Both are distressed. Only Tom's worry is about "a number of events or
  activities", in CG113's phrase, and seems to jump from one to the next.
- **Function.** Priya is still doing what the exams require. Tom's work and life have narrowed.

:::checkpoint Does this mean Priya is fine and Tom has generalised anxiety disorder?

No, and this is the most important thing in the lesson.

It means the two cases look very different against what the guidelines weigh, and that Tom's is the kind a guideline says a clinician should assess. Whether it is a disorder is that clinician's judgement, made with a conversation this lesson cannot have. Priya might also be having a harder time than a course can see.

What the case shows is the shape of the line, not which side anybody is on. If Tom were real, the useful sentence is not "you have GAD". It is "that's worth talking to a doctor about, and here's how".
:::

## One attack is not a disorder

Panic is where people most often decide they have something. A sudden rush of fear, a pounding
heart, trouble breathing, a sense that something terrible is happening. It is frightening, and it is
common.

CG113's advice for emergency departments contains the sentence this section rests on: "It is important
to remember that a panic attack does not necessarily constitute a panic disorder".[1]

There's a second sentence in the same advice, and it is the reason this lesson will not reassure you
about any physical symptom. People who arrive with a panic attack should "undergo the minimum
investigations necessary to exclude acute physical problems."[1] The guideline checks the body first.
**So if you have chest pain, difficulty breathing or anything that feels physical, that is a medical
question before it is a psychological one**, and this course will not tell you it is probably anxiety.

## Stepped care

Both guidelines organise treatment the same way. CG113's first recommendation on the subject: "Follow
the stepped-care model, offering the least intrusive, most effective intervention first."[1]

For anxiety, the steps run like this, in the guideline's words, shortened:

| Step | Who it is for | What's offered |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "All known and suspected presentations of GAD" | "Identification and assessment; education about GAD and treatment options; active monitoring" |
| 2 | GAD "that has not improved after education and active monitoring" | "individual non-facilitated self-help, individual guided self-help and psychoeducational groups" |
| 3 | "an inadequate response to step 2 interventions or marked functional impairment" | CBT or applied relaxation, "or a drug treatment" |
| 4 | "Complex treatment-refractory" GAD and "very marked functional impairment" | Specialist care |

Why begin with education and waiting to see? The guideline explains: "education and active monitoring
may improve less severe presentations and avoid the need for further interventions."[1]

NG222 does the same for depression in two tables, one for less severe and one for more severe.[2] For
less severe depression, guided self-help is listed first. For more severe, it is near the bottom, with
a note: "In more severe depression, the potential advantages of providing other treatment choices
with more therapist contact should be carefully considered first."[2] Read the heading of the first
table before treating its order as a league table: it is "in order of the committee's interpretation
of their clinical and cost effectiveness and consideration of implementation factors". Cost and
practicality are in the order, so it is not a ranking by effect.

### Where this course sits

A course is a kind of self-help, and it is worth being exact about where that puts it.

For most readers, who are stressed or low in the ordinary way, this course sits before step 1: it is
education, the thing step 1 includes, without the assessment and monitoring that make step 1 care.
For a reader with a disorder, it is at most something alongside step 2, and it is not what NICE means
by self-help at step 2, which lesson 3 will show you is more specific than a course. **This course is
not treatment, at any step.** That is this course's placement of itself, from the guidelines'
tables.[5]

## What this course leaves out

This course covers stress, worry and anxiety, low mood, and resilience, in adults: where they become
clinical, and what the evidence says about the methods people are offered. It leaves a lot out, on
purpose, and names it so you know where not to look here.

- **Mental illness as a subject.** Post-traumatic stress, obsessive-compulsive disorder, bipolar
  disorder, eating disorders, psychosis, substance problems, self-harm and suicide prevention. This
  course has not researched any of them and will not improvise. NG222 itself flags one reason this
  matters: a history of elevated mood can point to bipolar disorder, where, in WHO's words, "Different
  medicines and treatments are used".[2][3]
- **Children and teenagers.** Everything here is from adult guidelines.
- **Insomnia.** **Sleep** lesson 6 covered what's offered for chronic insomnia. This course points
  there.
- **Medication advice of any kind.** Lesson 8 describes a dispute about antidepressants. No lesson
  tells anyone to start, stop or change a medicine.
- **A map of your health system.** In the UK, NHS Talking Therapies takes self-referrals: "you can
  refer yourself to NHS talking therapies without speaking to a GP", and "You do not need to have a
  diagnosed mental health condition."[4] Elsewhere, this course's honest answer is that the route is
  a doctor, and it cannot map a country's services for you.

## When to stop reading and get help

These are the reasons, drawn from the NHS, the crisis lines and the guidelines, to contact someone
rather than read on.[1][2][4] They're not a checklist to score yourself against. They're things to
act on.

- Thoughts of suicide or self-harm, or not feeling able to keep yourself or someone else safe: the
  callout at the top of this lesson, now.
- Somebody's life at risk, for example a serious injury or an overdose: emergency services, now.
- Symptoms that feel physical, such as chest pain: a medical question first.
- Low mood or worry that has gone on for weeks or months and is getting in the way of work, sleep,
  eating or seeing people: a GP, or in the UK a self-referral to NHS Talking Therapies.

## Three things people get wrong

**"Anxiety is bad, and the goal is not to feel it."** WHO's first sentence: "Everyone can feel anxious
sometimes".[3] Anxiety before an exam or an interview is the ordinary kind. The disorder is anxiety
that is intense, excessive and hard to control, over time.

**"I had a panic attack, so I have panic disorder."** CG113: "a panic attack does not necessarily
constitute a panic disorder".[1]

**"If I tick enough symptoms, I have depression."** NG222 tells clinicians to make an assessment "that
does not rely simply on a symptom count".[2] The count is one of three things weighed.

## Practice

:::exercise What you'd say to a doctor

Take 20 minutes. Nobody sees this but you.

Think of a hard stretch in your own life, now or in the past. Don't score it. Instead, write three
short paragraphs, one for each thing the guidelines weigh:

1. **How long**, and whether it was tied to an event or spread across many things.
2. **How distressing**, in your own words.
3. **What it got in the way of**: work, study, sleep, eating, people.

That is roughly the shape of what a GP would want to hear, and having written it makes the
conversation easier if you ever need it. If writing it brings up something that feels unsafe, stop
and use the callout at the top of this lesson.
:::

:::exercise Find your route before you need it

Take 15 minutes.

Write down, somewhere you'll find it:

1. The crisis line for where you live, from the callout or from findahelpline.com.
2. Your emergency number.
3. The first route to non-urgent help where you live: your GP's number, or in the UK the NHS Talking
   Therapies self-referral for your area.

It takes a quarter of an hour now and saves searching at the worst possible moment.
:::
