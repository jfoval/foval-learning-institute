  - q: >-
      A stress-management course reports an effect of g = 0.6 on anxiety, "compared with a waiting
      list". A rival course reports g = 0.3, "compared with a relaxation class". Which is better?
    options:
      - The first, since 0.6 is twice 0.3 and the effect sizes are in the same units
      - The second, since a relaxation class is a harder comparison than a waiting list
      - Neither can be said from this, since the two were compared with different things
      - Both are equal, since effect sizes are standardised and so are always comparable
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Standardising puts both in the same units, but each number is a difference from its own
      comparison group, and a waiting list and an active class are different baselines. The second
      beat something harder, which is a reason for caution about ranking them, not a verdict that it
      is better.
  - q: >-
      A self-help programme's effect is g = 0.3 against usual care. Using this course's normal-curve
      conversion, what does that roughly mean?
    options:
      - The average participant improved by 30 percent more than a person on usual care
      - The average participant ended better off than about 62 percent of the usual-care group
      - About 30 percent of participants recovered, compared with none in the usual-care group
      - The programme worked for 3 out of every 10 people who tried it and not for the rest
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      On the course's conversion, which assumes bell-shaped scores, 0.3 standard deviations puts the
      average participant ahead of about 62 percent of the comparison group. An effect size isn't a
      percentage improvement, a recovery rate or a count of people it worked for.
  - q: >-
      In a network of psychotherapy trials, people on a waiting list did worse than people given no
      treatment at all. What does that do to an effect size measured against a waiting list?
    options:
      - It makes the effect look smaller than it really is, since the waiting list helped a bit
      - It has no effect, since a waiting list and no treatment are the same in practice
      - It proves the therapy being tested doesn't work, since only the comparison mattered
      - It may make the effect look larger, since the comparison group did unusually badly
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      If the comparison group does worse than it would have with no treatment, the gap between it and
      the treated group widens, so the effect can look bigger. The authors hedge this ("may"), and it
      doesn't show the therapy fails; it shows the number depends on the comparison.
  - q: >-
      An app's advert says "clinically proven, as effective as therapy". Its study compared the app
      with a waiting list. What's the most useful thing to say?
    options:
      - That "as effective as therapy" needs a comparison with therapy, which this wasn't
      - That the claim is false, since an app can never match a trained therapist's results
      - That the claim is fine, since beating a waiting list is exactly what therapy does too
      - That the app is harmful, since the study used a comparison known to flatter results
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      A claim about matching therapy needs therapy in the comparison. The study may show the app
      beats waiting, which is something, but it can't show it matches therapy. That's a gap in the
      evidence, not proof the claim is false, and nothing here suggests harm.
  - q: >-
      The largest meta-analysis of CBT for depression reports g = 0.79 against control conditions
      and no significant difference from medication in the short term. What does that pair show?
    options:
      - That CBT is more than twice as effective as medication, since 0.79 is far above 0.3
      - That CBT only works when it's compared with doing nothing, and not in real practice
      - That the size of the number changes with the comparison, and the head-to-head is smaller
      - That medication is ineffective, since CBT beat control conditions by a large margin

The next seven lessons are about methods people are offered for stress, worry and low mood. Every one
of them comes with a number. This lesson is about how to read that number, because in this subject
the number means very little until you know one more thing about it.

The one more thing is a question: **compared with what?**

## The band

Here are some of the effects this course's research file found for methods offered for depression
and anxiety, each with its comparison. They're standardised effect sizes, the Hedges' g that
**Note-Taking** lesson 5 taught, so a bigger number means a bigger difference between two groups'
averages, measured in standard deviations.

| Method | Effect | Compared with | Read here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unguided self-help CBT, depression | g = 0.45 | control conditions | abstract[1] |
| Self-guided internet CBT, depression | g = 0.27 | usual care, waiting list or attention control | abstract[2] |
| Smartphone apps, depression | g = 0.28 | control conditions | abstract[3] |
| Smartphone apps, generalised anxiety | g = 0.30 | control conditions | abstract[3] |
| CBT, depression | g = 0.79 | control conditions such as care as usual and waitlist | abstract[1] |

Four of the five sit between about 0.25 and 0.45. **That band, roughly 0.2 to 0.6, is where almost
every method in this course lands**, and the one number above it has a reason, which is the subject of
this lesson.[5]

## What 0.3 looks like

**Sleep** lesson 5 used a conversion you can use here too. If scores in both groups are spread in the
bell shape statisticians call the normal curve, an effect of g puts the average person in the treated
group ahead of a certain share of the comparison group.

| Effect size | Average treated person is ahead of about |
|---|---|
| 0.2 | 58 percent of the comparison group |
| 0.3 | 62 percent |
| 0.5 | 69 percent |
| 0.8 | 79 percent |

**That conversion is this course's arithmetic on the normal curve, not a figure from any study**, and
it only holds if the bell-shape assumption does.[5] With no effect at all, the figure would be 50
percent.

One study in the table gives a second way to feel the size. For self-guided internet CBT, the authors
report a "number needed to treat" of 8.[2] That is their figure, and it means roughly that for every
eight people given the programme instead of the comparison, one more person responded than would
have otherwise.

:::predict Before you read on. An effect of 0.3 puts the average treated person ahead of about 62 percent of the comparison group. Does that mean the method works for 62 percent of people?

No. It is a statement about two groups' averages, not about how many people it worked for. Some people in the treated group will have done worse than some in the comparison group, and some in the comparison group will have got better with nothing at all.

It also says nothing about you. Whether a method helps one person is a question an average can't answer, which is why lesson 9 ends with a refusal.
:::

## Compared with what?

Every effect size is a difference between a treated group and a comparison group. So its size depends
on two things: how much the treated group improved, **and how much the comparison group did**.

In this subject there are three common comparisons, and they do not behave the same way.

- **A waiting list.** People are told they'll get the treatment later.
- **No treatment**, or **usual care**: people carry on as they would have.
- **Another active treatment**: a different therapy, a relaxation class, a medicine.

The third is the hardest test, because the comparison group is getting something too. The first two
look similar. They turn out not to be.

A 2014 [network meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/acps.12275) by Furukawa and colleagues put 49 trials of therapy for depression
together, with 2,730 participants, and compared the comparison conditions with each other. Their
finding: "the effect size estimates for CBT were substantively different depending on the control
condition. The odds ratio of response for NT over WL was statistically significant at 2.9".[4] NT is
no treatment, and WL is waiting list. **People given no treatment at all were more likely to respond
than people told to wait.**

:::checkpoint Before you read on. If people on a waiting list do worse than people given nothing, what happens to a treatment's effect size when it is measured against a waiting list?

It can look bigger. The effect size is the gap between the groups, and a comparison group that does unusually badly widens the gap without the treatment doing anything more.

The paper's title offers a reason, that a waiting list "may be a nocebo condition", meaning that being told to wait may itself hold improvement back. That is their proposal, with a "may" in it, and this course read the abstract, not the analysis behind it.[4]
:::

The authors are careful about their own finding, and the care travels with it: "the quality of
evidence, including publication bias, was less than ideal and none of the preplanned sensitivity
analyses limiting to high-quality studies could be conducted, while findings of significant
differences did not persist in post hoc sensitivity analyses trying to adjust for publication
bias."[4] Their conclusion is correspondingly modest: "There may be important differences in control
conditions currently used in psychotherapy trials."[4]

So this is not a proven law. It is a warning worth carrying: **a number measured against a waiting list
might be flattered, and you should find out before you compare it with anything else.**

## One therapy, three numbers

Here's the worked case. It is the largest meta-analysis of any single kind of psychotherapy, in its
authors' words, "the largest meta-analysis ever of a specific type of psychotherapy for a mental
disorder": Cuijpers and colleagues, 2023, 409 trials and 52,702 patients.[1] This course read its
abstract.

Against control conditions: "CBT had moderate to large effects compared to control conditions such as
care as usual and waitlist (g=0.79; 95% CI: 0.70-0.89)". That is the high number in the table.

Against medication: "The effects of CBT did not differ significantly from those of pharmacotherapies
at the short term".

Against other therapies: "CBT was significantly more effective than other psychotherapies, but the
difference was small (g=0.06; 95% CI: 0-0.12) and became non-significant in most sensitivity analyses."[1]

Same therapy, same meta-analysis. Against controls that include waiting lists, 0.79. Against a
medicine, no significant short-term difference. Against other talking therapies, 0.06 and not robust.

**The trap is setting 0.79 beside a medicine's effect against a placebo pill**, which lesson 8 will
show is about 0.3. That would compare a therapy against a waiting list with a pill against a pill, and
it would make the therapy look more than twice as good. The fair comparison is the one in the same
abstract where the two meet head to head, and there the difference was not significant in the short
term.[1][5]

## The app that is "as effective as therapy"

Now one with a wrinkle. The app and the advert are invented for this lesson.[5]

An app advertises itself as "clinically proven, as effective as therapy". You find the study: the app
against a waiting list, an effect of 0.3.

Run the question. Compared with what? A waiting list. "As effective as therapy" would need therapy in
the comparison, and there was not any. So the study can support "better than waiting, in that trial".
It can't support "as good as therapy", and its comparison may flatter even the first claim.

The real evidence on apps, for scale: a 2019 meta-analysis found that "Smartphone interventions
significantly outperformed control conditions in improving depressive (g=0.28, n=54) and generalized
anxiety (g=0.30, n=39) symptoms".[3] Notice that "n" counts trials there, not people, and that the same
abstract found "no significant benefit over control conditions on panic symptoms (g=-0.05, n=3)".[3]
**Apps have real, modest evidence against control conditions. None of that is evidence of matching a
therapist.**

## The course's question

Each Core course before this one added a question to the institute's way of reading a claim.
**Memory** added the sample: how many people, and who. **Focus and Deep Work** added the instrument.
**Sleep** added the clock: how much, or when.

This course adds **the comparator question: compared with what?** Every method lesson from here on
will give you its number and its comparison together, and lesson 9 joins the question to the rest.

## Three things people get wrong

**"A bigger effect size means a better treatment."** Only if the two were compared with the same
thing. CBT's 0.79 against controls and its no-significant-difference against medication are the same
therapy.

**"An effect of 0.3 means 30 percent better."** It is a difference in standard deviations. On this
course's conversion, it means the average treated person is ahead of about 62 percent of the
comparison group.

**"If it beat the control group, it'll work for me."** An average over a group says nothing certain
about one person, and the comparison group sets how big the average looks.

## Practice

:::exercise Rank them twice

Take 20 minutes.

Take the five effects in this lesson's first table. Rank them once by size. Then rank them again by
how hard their comparison was, from waiting list (easiest to beat) to another active treatment
(hardest), marking any where the comparison is a mix.

Write one sentence on what changed between the two rankings, and one on which number you'd now trust
least, and why.
:::

:::exercise One app, one question

Take 20 minutes.

Find one app, programme or course that says it helps with stress, anxiety or mood and claims evidence.
This one counts.

1. Write down its claim, word for word.
2. Find what the evidence was compared with. A waiting list, usual care, another treatment, or nothing
   stated.
3. Write one sentence: what the claim would need its comparison to be, and whether it was.

If you can't find what it was compared with, that is the finding.
:::
