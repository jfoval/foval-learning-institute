Worry is the part of anxiety that most people recognise in themselves: the thought that keeps coming
back, the what-ifs at two in the morning. It is also where the advice people are given runs furthest
ahead of the evidence. This lesson is about both.

## When worry is generalised anxiety disorder

Lesson 1 drew the line in general. Here it is for worry specifically, in CG113's words, and with
lesson 1's warning attached: these are what a clinician weighs, not a checklist to score yourself
against.

The key symptoms of generalised anxiety disorder, or GAD, are "excessive anxiety and worry about a
number of events or activities" and "difficulty controlling the worry", and "The worry should occur
on a majority of days for at least 6 months." If both are present, a clinician is told to "ask about
the following associated symptoms: restlessness, being easily fatigued, difficulty concentrating,
irritability, muscle tension, disturbed sleep."[1]

And the sentence that governs all of it: a clinician should "conduct a comprehensive assessment that
does not rely solely on the number, severity and duration of symptoms, but also considers the degree
of distress and functional impairment."[1]

So worry is not the disorder. Worry that spreads across many things, that the person can't control,
that goes on most days for months, and that costs them their distress and their functioning, is what
a clinician looks at.

## What NICE offers, and what it does not name

At step 2, for GAD that "has not improved after education and active monitoring in step 1", NICE says
to "offer 1 or more of the following as a first-line intervention, guided by the person's preference:
individual non-facilitated self-help; individual guided self-help; psychoeducational groups."[1] The
verb is "offer", NICE's strong grade, and lesson 3 showed what each of those means.

At step 3, if a person chooses a high-intensity psychological treatment: "offer either CBT or applied
relaxation." Applied relaxation is not being told to relax. NICE specifies that it should "be based on
the treatment manuals used in the clinical trials of applied relaxation for GAD", "be delivered by
trained and competent practitioners", and "usually consist of 12 to 15 weekly sessions ... each
lasting 1 hour."[1]

Now what the page does not say. This course searched the whole of CG113's recommendations page.
"Mindfulness", "breathing", "yoga" and "physical activity" do not appear on it. "Exercise" appears
once as a treatment, and that is a 2004 recommendation for panic disorder: "The benefits of exercise
as part of good general health should be discussed with all people with panic disorder as
appropriate."[1] **No recommendation for GAD names exercise, mindfulness or any single technique.**
NICE recommends exercise and mindfulness for less severe depression, which is a different guideline
and a different condition; lessons 5 and 6 come to them.[2]

:::predict Before you read on. NICE names no single worry technique for GAD, only structured packages and therapy. What might that tell you about the evidence for single techniques?

Probably that the guideline developers did not find enough of it to recommend any on its own. That is a guess about their reasons, which this course did not read, but the rest of this lesson shows what the evidence for one popular technique actually looks like.[6]
:::

## What the evidence shows for treating GAD

The evidence on psychological treatment of GAD is fairly consistent, and every study below was read
at abstract level.

[The Cochrane review of psychological therapies for GAD](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001848.pub4), by Hunot and colleagues, is old: its searches
ended in 2006, and no newer version was found. It reported that "Based on thirteen studies,
psychological therapies, all using a CBT approach, were more effective than TAU/WL in achieving
clinical response at post-treatment (RR 0.63, 95%CI 0.55 to 0.73)".[3] TAU/WL is treatment as usual or
waiting list.

That RR needs reading carefully. It is a risk ratio of not responding. So 0.63 means the risk of **not**
responding was lower with CBT than in the comparison group: a figure below 1 favours CBT. Read the
other way round, it would say exactly the opposite of what the review found. That gloss is this
course's.[6]

The same review adds a comparison that matters: "No significant difference in clinical response was
indicated between CBT and supportive therapy at post-treatment (RR 0.86, 95%CI 0.70 to 1.06), however
significant heterogeneity was indicated".[3] Against supportive therapy, a harder comparison, the
difference was not significant.

A 2014 meta-analysis by Cuijpers and colleagues pooled 41 studies of GAD and found a large effect,
g = 0.84, against control groups, adding that "The majority of studies used waiting lists as control
condition."[4] Lesson 2's question has its answer right there. An internet-treatment meta-analysis by
Eilert and colleagues found g = 0.79 for anxiety, and its abstract does not name the control
conditions, so this course can't say what that figure was measured against.[4]

On CBT against applied relaxation, two reviews lean differently. Cuijpers 2014 reported "some
indications that CBT was ... more effective than applied relaxation in the longer term". A 2022
review by Flückiger and colleagues found "negligible relative efficacy differences" between applied
relaxation and CBT without applied relaxation "at each assessment time".[4] NICE offers both at step
3. **Contested, and small either way.**

## A technique, tested alone

Now the worked case: the single technique a reader is most often handed for worry. It goes by
several names, worry postponement or stimulus control for worry, and in the trials below it meant
setting aside a fixed daily period for worrying and putting worry off until then. **That is a
description of what the studies tested, not an instruction.** It traces back to a 1983 paper by
Borkovec and colleagues, which this course has not read.[5]

Here are four trials of it, each with what its comparison group got.

| Trial | Who | Compared with | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| McGowan and Behar, 2013 | 53 people with high worry, 2 weeks | "focused worry" | better on worry, anxiety, negative mood and insomnia; not on depression |
| Versluis and colleagues, 2016 | online, 361 of 996 finished, 6 days | recording worry each day | no difference in worry frequency or duration |
| Krzikalla and colleagues, 2024 | 47 with GAD, 2 sessions with a clinician | waiting list | lower worry scores, "the only significant effect" |
| McCarrick and colleagues, 2025 | 186 online, 14 days | four arms | an augmented version beat standard postponement on worry duration |

All four were read at abstract level.[5]

:::predict Before you read on. Look at the "Compared with" column. Which result would you trust least as evidence that postponement works on its own, and why?

Krzikalla's, as evidence for doing it alone. Its comparison group got nothing (a waiting list, which lesson 2 showed may flatter), its sample had two sessions with a clinician, and the authors report lower worry as "the only significant effect".

McCarrick's result is about something else again: the significant finding is an augmented version against standard postponement, not postponement against a control. And Versluis, the largest, found nothing against a comparison that was itself doing something.
:::

Read the trials against each other and the picture is honest and unexciting. McGowan's authors,
whose trial was positive, frame the method as belonging "in larger treatment packages for individuals
who experience high levels of worry".[5] Versluis's authors found that "Contrary to our expectation, the
registration group ... did not differ from the intervention group", and add that "participants had
high trait worry levels and reported difficulty with postponing worrying".[5] And Krzikalla's team
summarise the field before their own trial: "Worry postponement (WP) with a stimulus control rationale
appears to be effective in non-clinical samples. However, preliminary research in participants with
generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) does not support its efficacy in reducing negative metacognitions or
worry."[5]

**Small trials, pointing different ways, and no meta-analysis this course found.** That is the
evidence for the tip. Its classification, under the standards this course follows, is contested at
the level of single small trials.[6]

### A package, and a part of it

Here's the wrinkle, and it is the same one **Sleep** lesson 6 met with sleep hygiene.

CBT for worry has been tested as a whole and helps, against waiting lists and usual care, at the sizes
above. Worry postponement is one component that CBT packages can include. A package that works does not
show that every part of it works alone, and a part that has not been shown to work alone is not shown not
to work either.[6]

:::checkpoint So is worry postponement useless?

No, and "useless" is exactly the overreach this lesson warns against. What the trials show is that on its own, and especially done alone at home, the evidence is thin and mixed. Inside a structured CBT programme, with or without a guide, it is one part of something that has been tested as a whole.

That is why NICE's step 2 names structured CBT-based self-help rather than a technique. If worry is getting in the way of your life, the evidence points at the package, and lesson 3 showed what the package looks like.
:::

## Panic, briefly

Lesson 1 covered the most important thing about panic: one attack is not a disorder, and chest pain is
a medical question first. For panic disorder itself, CG113 says: "For people with mild to moderate
panic disorder, offer or refer for 1 of the following low-intensity interventions: individual
non-facilitated self-help; individual facilitated self-help."[1] The same step-2 shape as GAD. And the
2004 line on exercise, quoted above, belongs here: it is advice to discuss exercise "as part of good
general health", not a treatment.

## Four things people get wrong

**"Postponing worry is proven."** It is popular and it sounds sensible, which is why it is easy to take
as established. Its trials are few, small and disagree, and its own researchers place it inside
larger packages.

**"Applied relaxation means being told to relax."** The name suggests it. NICE specifies a manualised
treatment of 12 to 15 weekly hour-long sessions with a trained practitioner.

**"NICE recommends exercise and mindfulness for anxiety."** It recommends them for less severe
depression. For GAD, neither appears as a treatment.

**"If self-help didn't fix my worry, nothing will."** Self-help is step 2. Step 3 is therapy, and CG113
says it is for "an inadequate response to step 2 interventions or marked functional impairment".[1]
Self-help not working is the reason step 3 exists.

## Practice

:::exercise Find the trials behind a tip

Take 25 minutes.

Pick one piece of advice about worry you've been given or have read: a technique, not a general
reassurance.

1. Write it down, word for word, with where it came from.
2. Search for a randomised trial of that technique on its own. A search engine for "[technique]
   randomised trial" or Europe PMC will do.
3. For whatever you find, write what the comparison group got, how many people took part, and whether
   they had a clinician.

End with one line: is this a tested package, a component tested alone, or untested? If you found
nothing in 25 minutes, that is the answer.
:::

:::exercise What step 2 looks like where you live

Take 15 minutes.

Find out what's available where you live at NICE's step 2: self-help programmes based on CBT, guided
self-help, or psychoeducational groups. In England, NHS Talking Therapies offers them; elsewhere,
write down who you'd ask. You do not need to use it.
:::
