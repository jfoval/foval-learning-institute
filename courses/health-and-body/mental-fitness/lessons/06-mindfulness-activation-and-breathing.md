      - Nothing, since meditation is meditation whether it comes from an app or a teacher
      - The review found no effect at all, so the app is citing a negative result
      - The review tested something quite different, teacher-led groups of volunteers against passive controls
      - Group courses are known to work worse than apps, so the app would do better than that
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Galante and colleagues' confident finding is about "Group-based teacher-led" programmes among
      "community adults who volunteer", against passive controls. An app used alone is none of those,
      so the finding doesn't transfer by default. The review did find an effect, and nothing here
      compares apps with groups.
  - q: >-
      A trial finds a mindfulness course was "non-inferior" to a commonly prescribed antidepressant
      for anxiety disorders, with no placebo group. What can it tell you?
    options:
      - That both treatments work, since non-inferior means each beat doing nothing
      - That mindfulness is better, since it had far fewer adverse events than the drug
      - That neither works, since without a placebo group nothing can be concluded at all
      - That the two did about as well as each other, not how much each helped by itself
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Non-inferiority means the course wasn't worse than the drug by more than a set margin. Without
      a placebo or no-treatment arm, the trial can't say how much of either group's improvement came
      from its treatment. Fewer side effects is a real difference, but it's a different question from
      which worked better.
  - q: >-
      Someone with a history of severe depression and recent thoughts of self-harm wants to join an
      eight-week mindfulness course. What do the sources this lesson read say about that?
    options:
      - That's fine, since mindfulness is gentle and suitable for almost everyone
      - That the course's own standards exclude suicidality, so a clinician first
      - That it's best avoided, since meditation reliably makes depression worse
      - That it's a good substitute for treatment, since MBCT prevents relapse

    explain: >-
      Van Dam and colleagues report that MBSR's own standards "exclude suicidality and the presence
      of any psychiatric disorder", and NIH says meditation "could cause or worsen certain psychiatric

      for relapse prevention in remission, which isn't this person's situation.
  - q: >-
      A friend says behavioural activation is "basically just keeping busy". What's the most accurate
      reply from this lesson?
    options:
      - That's about right, since it works by filling time so there's less room to worry
      - It focuses on the link between activities and mood, and is a structured therapy
      - It's the opposite, since it asks people to rest until they feel like doing things
      - It's a form of medication management, which is why it's compared with antidepressants

    explain: >-
      NG222 describes it as focusing on "the link between an individual's activities and their mood",

      and usually delivered with a practitioner. The other options misdescribe it.
  - q: >-
      A breathing app claims its technique "lowers your stress hormones". The best meta-analysis of
      breathwork this course read measured what?
    options:
      - Cortisol in saliva, which fell by a medium amount across twelve trials
      - Heart rate variability, which improved in most of the trials it included
      - Self-reported stress, which fell by a small-to-medium amount against controls
      - Blood pressure, which fell only in people who breathed slowly for weeks

    explain: >-

      A Cochrane review finds behavioural activation beat treatment as usual on one analysis, but the
      difference "was no longer evident" when drop-outs were counted as failures. What's the fairest

## Mindfulness: the effect, sorted by comparison

**Focus and Deep Work** lesson 6 asked whether meditation trains attention. This lesson asks about a
different outcome, mood and anxiety, and the evidence is different too.

Start with the review that used the hardest comparison. [Goyal and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2013.13018), in 2014, "identified
randomized clinical trials with active controls for placebo effects", meaning comparison groups given
something that looked like a treatment.[1] Across 47 trials with 3,515 participants: "Mindfulness
meditation programs had moderate evidence of improved anxiety (effect size, 0.38 [95% CI, 0.12-0.64]
at 8 weeks and 0.22 [0.02-0.43] at 3-6 months), depression (0.30 [0.00-0.59] at 8 weeks and 0.23
[0.05-0.42] at 3-6 months)". And: "We found no evidence that meditation programs were better than any
active treatment (ie, drugs, exercise, and other behavioral therapies)."[1] Their conclusion for
clinicians: "meditation programs can result in small to moderate reductions of multiple negative
dimensions of psychological stress."[1]

Against a passive comparison the picture is firmer. Galante and colleagues, in 2023, pooled individual
participants' data from 13 trials of mindfulness-based programmes, or MBPs, in 2,371 adults, and found
that "In comparison with passive control groups, MBPs reduced average distress between one- and
six-months post-intervention with a small to moderate effect size (standardised mean difference (SMD)
-0.32; 95% confidence interval (CI) -0.41 to -0.24". And: "Confidence in the primary outcome result is
high."[2]

:::predict Before you read on. That "high" confidence is about a particular kind of programme and a particular kind of person. Read the conclusion's own words and list every condition attached: "Group-based teacher-led MBPs generally reduce psychological distress among community adults who volunteer to receive this type of intervention."

Group-based. Teacher-led. Community adults, not patients. Who volunteered. And, from the result, against passive controls.

An app used alone on a phone is none of the first four. So that confidence doesn't carry over to it by default, and this course won't carry it over. That reading is this course's.[7]
:::

An earlier review by the same lead author, in 2021, reached a similar place from a different angle:
"Compared with specific active control conditions, there is no statistically significant evidence of
MBPs' superiority", and "the findings do not support generalisation of MBP effects across every
setting." And: "Other preventative interventions may be equally effective."[2]

### Where mindfulness has a specific result

One use has its own evidence. Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, MBCT, was tested for preventing
relapse in people with recurrent depression who were in full or partial remission. Kuyken and
colleagues pooled individual data from 1,258 patients: "patients receiving MBCT had a reduced risk of
depressive relapse within a 60-week follow-up period compared with those who did not receive MBCT
(hazard ratio, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.58-0.82). Furthermore, comparisons with active treatments suggest a
reduced risk of depressive relapse within a 60-week follow-up period (hazard ratio, 0.79; 95% CI,
0.64-0.97)."[3] A hazard ratio below 1 means relapse happened less often over time.

That's a real finding for a specific group: people who've had depression before and are currently
well. It isn't evidence for mindfulness as a general stress tool, and the authors don't claim it is.[3]

## "Comparable to antidepressants"

In 2018, a group of mindfulness researchers wrote a critique of their own field, "Mind the Hype". Its
abstract: "Misinformation and poor methodology associated with past studies of mindfulness may lead
public consumers to be harmed, misled, and disappointed." And on one claim in particular: "MBIs are
sometimes misleadingly described as "comparable" to antidepressant medications (ADMs)(Goyal et al.,
2014)." MBIs are mindfulness-based interventions. They continued: "Given the absence of scientific
rigor in much clinical mindfulness research (Davidson & Kaszniak, 2015; Goyal et al., 2014), evidence
for use of MBIs in clinical contexts should be considered preliminary."[4]

These authors aren't opponents of mindfulness; they study it, which is what gives the critique its
weight. And a mindfulness teacher would add, fairly, that serious programmes were never sold as a
treatment for psychiatric disorders, which the critique itself supports, as the next section shows.[4]

Five years later, a trial was designed to test that very comparison. Hoge and colleagues randomised
adults with diagnosed anxiety disorders to an eight-week mindfulness-based stress reduction course,
MBSR, or a commonly prescribed antidepressant, with "a noninferiority design with a prespecified
noninferiority margin" and "outcome assessments ... performed by blinded clinical interviewer".[5]
Of 276 recruited, 208 completed. "The difference between groups was -0.07 (0.16; 95% CI, -0.38 to
0.23; P = .65), where the lower bound of the interval fell within the predefined noninferiority margin
of -0.495, indicating noninferiority of MBSR compared with escitalopram."[5] Escitalopram is the
antidepressant.

On side effects the two differed a lot: "At least 1 study-related adverse event occurred for 110
participants randomized to escitalopram (78.6%) and 21 participants randomized to MBSR (15.4%)."[5]

:::checkpoint So is mindfulness now "comparable to antidepressants"?

For anxiety disorders, one well-designed trial found an eight-week MBSR course not worse than a common antidepressant by more than a margin set in advance. That's a real result, and it's more than existed in 2018.

But it's one trial, with no placebo or no-treatment group, so it can't say how much either group improved because of its treatment. The trial's authors would say their design answers the 2018 objection directly; a critic in the 2018 mould would say one non-inferiority trial is where a claim of comparability starts, not where it ends. Both readings are fair, and this course doesn't choose. That setting-side-by-side is this course's.[7]
:::

This lesson touches medication, so the course's standing sentence applies: nothing here is a reason to
start, stop or switch a treatment, and NICE advises anyone who wants to stop an antidepressant to
"talk with the person who prescribed their medication", because "it is usually necessary to reduce the
dose in stages over time".[6]

## Who mindfulness isn't for

The same 2018 critique carries two sentences that matter more than any effect size for some readers:
"The official standards of practice for MBSR exclude suicidality and the presence of any psychiatric
disorder (Santorelli, 2014)." And: "The NIH states that "meditation could cause or worsen certain
psychiatric problems" but does not provide any practice guidelines beyond a boilerplate disclaimer to
"check with your doctor" before trying meditation (NCCIH, 2016)."[4]

NICE's depression guideline says the same in its description of group mindfulness: it "May be
difficult for people experiencing intense or highly distressing thoughts, or who find focusing on the
body difficult."[6]

So the programme with the strongest evidence was designed for people without a psychiatric disorder,
and its own standards leave out anyone who is suicidal. If that's you, a mindfulness course isn't the
first step; a clinician is. That reading is this course's, from those sentences.[7]

## Behavioural activation

Behavioural activation, or BA, is the least known of the three and has the most clinical backing. The
Cochrane review defines it as "a brief psychotherapeutic approach that seeks to change the way a person
interacts with their environment."[6] NICE's depression guideline describes it as focusing on
"identifying the link between an individual's activities and their mood", and says it "Does not
directly target thoughts and feelings".[6] In plain terms, it works on what a person does, on the idea
that doing can come before feeling like doing, which is this course's gloss.[7] **Habits and
Self-Discipline** looked at behaviour change from the other side.

The Cochrane review, by Uphoff and colleagues, included 53 studies with 5,495 participants. This course
read its abstract.[6]

- **Against treatment as usual:** "moderate-certainty evidence that behavioural activation had greater
  short-term efficacy than treatment as usual (risk ratio (RR) 1.40, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.10
  to 1.78; 7 RCTs, 1533 participants), although this difference was no longer evident in sensitivity
  analyses using a worst-case or intention-to-treat scenario." Here the risk ratio counts a good
  outcome, so above 1 favours BA, and "worst-case" means counting people who dropped out as not
  improved.
- **Against CBT:** "moderate-certainty evidence suggesting no evidence of a difference in short-term
  treatment efficacy between behavioural activation and CBT (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.07; 5 RCTs, 601
  participants)."
- **Against medication:** BA "was more effective than humanistic therapy ... and medication (RR 1.77,
  95% CI 1.14 to 2.76; 1 RCT; 141 participants; moderate certainty), but both of these results were
  based on a small number of trials and participants."
- **Against placebo:** "No evidence on treatment efficacy was available".

And their conclusion, in its own terms: "behavioural activation may be more effective than humanistic
therapy, medication, and treatment as usual, and that it may be no less effective than CBT,
psychodynamic therapy, or being placed on a waiting list. However, our confidence in these findings is
limited due to concerns about the certainty of the evidence."[6]

:::predict Before you read on. The authors say BA "may be no less effective than ... being placed on a waiting list". Read that phrase again. Is it good news or bad news for BA?

It's an honest oddity, and it cuts against BA. "No less effective than a waiting list" is a strange thing to report about a therapy, and it comes from the one waiting-list trial the review found: 26 participants, where BA "may be more effective" but the interval (0.90 to 5.09) ran from slightly worse to much better. So the review couldn't show BA beat waiting, on that trial.

The lesson from lesson 2 applies: the comparison with the fewest data gives the least certain answer, and here it's the one that would usually look best.
:::

BA is the same kind of CBT-related method that NICE's guided self-help can use for depression, in the
table lesson 3 read, which means NICE places its self-help form with a practitioner.[6]

## Breathing

Breathing exercises are the easiest of the three to try and have the smallest evidence base. Fincham
and colleagues, in 2023, pooled randomised trials of breathwork: "The primary outcome
self-reported/subjective stress included 12 randomised-controlled trials (k = 12) with a total of 785
adult participants. Most studies were deemed as being at moderate risk of bias. The random-effects
analysis yielded a significant small-to-medium mean effect size, g = - 0.35 [95% CI - 0.55, - 0.14]".
Effects on anxiety and depression were "similar". Their last sentence: "However, we urge caution and
advocate for nuanced research approaches with low risk-of-bias study designs to avoid a miscalibration
between hype and evidence."[8]

Two things to keep. The outcome is **self-reported** stress; nothing in this review's main result
measures a change in the body. And this course has no source on any specific breathing technique, so it
doesn't describe one as evidence-based. Lesson 4's applied relaxation, twelve to fifteen sessions with
a trained practitioner, is a different thing from a breathing exercise.

## Four things people get wrong

**"Mindfulness works as well as antidepressants."** In 2018 its own researchers called that a
misleading summary. One trial since, for anxiety disorders, found an MBSR course not worse than a
common antidepressant, with no placebo arm. That's a start, not a settled comparison.

**"Meditation is harmless."** The programme with the strongest evidence excludes people who are
suicidal or have a psychiatric disorder, and NIH says meditation "could cause or worsen certain
psychiatric problems".

**"Behavioural activation is just keeping busy."** It's a structured therapy focused on the link between
activities and mood, which did about as well as CBT in the trials that compared them.

**"Breathing exercises lower your stress hormones."** The main meta-analysis measured self-reported
stress, in 785 people, mostly at moderate risk of bias.

Take 20 minutes.

Find three claims about mindfulness or meditation: an app store page, a workplace wellbeing email, an
article. For each, write down:

1. What it claims, word for word.
2. What it implies the comparison was: nothing, a passive control, an active treatment, or unstated.
3. Who the evidence it rests on was measured in, if you can tell: patients, volunteers, workers,
   students.

Then one sentence on which of the three claims goes furthest beyond the evidence this lesson read.
