Every lesson in this course took a claim about the mind and followed it back to where it came from.
This project does that once more, from start to finish, on a single programme somebody is offering
people for stress, worry or low mood. It is the course's methods used together, on something real,
with nobody's answer to copy.

:::callout If you're struggling right now
This course is education, not care. If you're thinking about suicide or self-harm, or don't feel able to keep yourself safe, contact emergency services (911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 112 across the EU, 000 in Australia) or a crisis line: call or text 988 in the US and Canada, call Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK and Ireland, or Lifeline on 13 11 14 in Australia. Elsewhere, findahelpline.com lists free, confidential lines by country.
:::

It takes about three hours, spread over a week: an hour to choose the programme and copy out what it
claims, an hour or so tracing its evidence across a few evenings, and an hour to write it up.

## What this project is not

It isn't a mood log, and it is not about you. You will not record how you feel, score yourself, or test
whether the programme works on you. That is deliberate. A course cannot safely ask someone who may be
struggling to track their symptoms for a week, and nobody here would read what they wrote. The
object of study is the programme and what it says about itself.

If reading a programme's material closely brings up something hard, stop. That is a reasonable thing
to do and it doesn't spoil the project; pick it up another week, or choose a programme further from
home. If it brings up anything that feels unsafe, use the lines in the box above now. And if you are
using a programme and it isn't helping, lesson 1's list of reasons to talk to someone applies, and
this project can wait.

## Choosing the programme

Pick one programme, app, book or course aimed at stress, worry or low mood. It can be one you have
used, one you've been recommended, one your employer offers, or one you have seen advertised. **This
course counts**, and it makes a hard case, because it claims very little.

Two things make a better choice. It should say something about evidence: "clinically proven",
"backed by research", a figure, a named study. And it should be something a person could actually
be offered, rather than a news story about one.

Don't choose a medicine. Lesson 8 is the only place this course discusses drugs at length, and it did so
without advising anyone. Whether a medicine suits someone is a conversation with a prescriber, not
an audit.

## What you hand in

One plain text or Markdown file, roughly 1,200 to 2,000 words, in four parts and a closing
paragraph. Put the programme's name, where you found it, and the date you read it at the top.
Websites change, and a date makes your quotations checkable.

## Part 1: what it claims

Copy out every claim the programme makes about what it does, word for word, with where each one is:
the store page, the "science" page, the back cover, an email. Keep the hedges ("may", "can help")
and the grammar exactly as printed. A claim fixed up on the way into your file is no longer the
claim.

Then sort each one, as lesson 9 did. Is it measurable, unmeasurable as stated, or already shown
false? Watch for welds, where "so" or "which means" joins a measurable half to a piece of advice,
and sort each half on its own.

If yours makes more than ten, take the ten that promise most.

## Part 2: what evidence it cites, traced

For every figure the programme uses, and every "studies show", follow the trail as far as it goes.
A figure counts as **traced** when you've opened what it cites and either found the number there or
found that it isn't there. Anything short of that is untraceable, and you say where it stopped.

1. **What it cites**: a study, a review, a press release, a named researcher, or nothing.
2. **Whether the figure is there.** Open what it cites and find the number, or something like it.
   Lesson 5's press release and lesson 7's headlines showed how the two can differ.
3. **Whether it is the same programme.** A trial of a different app, or of a group course with a
   teacher when you are looking at an app used alone, is evidence about something else. Lesson 6
   showed how far a finding tied to one kind of delivery can travel.
4. **Who was studied**: patients, volunteers, students, workers, and how many.
5. **How far you read**: the press release, the abstract, or the whole paper. Say which, every time.

Where a trail ends, say where. "Untraceable" is a finding, and it comes in several kinds: nothing
cited, a dead link, a paywall you could not get past, or a study that does not contain the figure.
Europe PMC and PubMed will usually find a study's abstract free if you have its title or authors.

## Part 3: the comparator behind each figure

For each figure you traced, ask the course's question: **what did the comparison group get?** Use
lesson 2's list, from easiest to beat to hardest: a waiting list, no treatment, usual care, an
attention or placebo control, another active treatment. Add the two the course met later: a placebo
pill, as in lesson 8's drug trials, which belongs in the placebo-control band just short of another treatment, and nobody assigned at all, as in lesson 7's survey, where people
were compared as they came. If the study doesn't say, write "not stated", which lessons 4 and 7 each
met in an abstract.

Then set each comparator against the claim's wording. "As effective as therapy" needs therapy in
the comparison. "Reduces anxiety" against a waiting list says the programme beat being told to wait,
and lesson 2 showed that comparison may flatter. Say, for each claim, whether its comparison group
could support its words.

## Part 4: where it sits against NICE's step 2

Lesson 3 read NICE's specification of self-help for generalised anxiety disorder, and it is free.
Open [CG113's recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg113/chapter/Recommendations) (or
open [NICE CG113](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg113) and choose "Recommendations"), then search
the page for "1.2.12". Recommendations 1.2.12 to 1.2.15 set out step 2: 1.2.13 specifies
non-facilitated self-help, 1.2.14 guided self-help, and 1.2.15 psychoeducational groups. If your
programme is aimed at low mood rather than worry, lesson 3 also read
[NG222](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng222)'s definition of guided self-help for depression,
which is close to CG113's.

Pick the specification that matches what the programme is. A book, app or course you work through
alone takes 1.2.13. One with a person who checks in takes 1.2.14. A class takes 1.2.15. Then go
through it a line at a time. For non-facilitated self-help, that's four lines: readable materials;
based on CBT principles; instructions to work through them systematically over at least six weeks;
and minimal therapist contact. For guided self-help, add 1.2.14's lines on who the person is and
how often they meet you. For each line, write yes, no or partly, with one sentence of evidence from
the programme itself.

One thing to keep straight while you do it. NICE's specification describes what a clinician should
offer a person diagnosed with GAD at step 2. A programme sold to anyone is not obliged to meet it,
and missing a line is not a verdict that the programme is bad. It is a statement of what the programme
is, which is exactly what a reader deciding whether to use it needs to know.

## The closing paragraph

**One paragraph: what the programme could fairly say about itself.** Write it as the programme's own
description, if the people who made it had read this course and wanted every sentence to hold up.

Keep what the evidence supports, at the size and against the comparison it was measured at. Cut or
reword what it does not. If the programme has no traceable evidence, the fair paragraph says what it
is and what it contains, and claims nothing about what it does. If the evidence is good, the fair
paragraph says so plainly; lesson 3's reviews found self-help beating its controls for anxiety, and
leaving that out would be as wrong as inflating it.

## Rubric

| Criterion | What full marks look like |
|---|---|
| Claims quoted exactly | Every claim is copied word for word, hedges included, with where it appeared and the date it was read |
| Every cited figure traced or marked untraceable | Each figure is followed to its source with the read level stated, or marked untraceable with where the trail ended |
| Comparator named for each | Each traced figure has what the comparison group got, or "not stated", and each claim's wording is checked against it |
| The NICE comparison made line by line | Every line of the relevant specification has yes, no or partly, with evidence from the programme |
| The closing paragraph neither harsher nor kinder than the evidence | The fair description keeps what was shown, at its size and against its comparison, and drops only what wasn't |

**Two of the five, the first and the last, are about honesty rather than analysis**, as in the
projects of the courses before this one. A tidied quotation makes the rest of the audit an audit of
something you wrote. And the closing paragraph is where a reader who has spent a week finding
problems is most tempted to be harsher than the evidence, or, with a programme they like, kinder.

## If the trail runs out

It often does, and that is not a failure of the project. A programme whose claims cite nothing gives
you short parts 2 and 3 and a long closing paragraph, and that is a real result. If you get a week in
and find the programme makes no claims about evidence at all, you can either finish it as it stands
or start again with a programme that does. Say at the top which you did.

The one thing not to do is fill a gap from memory. If you can't find what a study's comparison group
got, "not stated" is the answer, and a guess written as though it were found is the error this
whole course has been teaching you to spot in other people's work.

## A note on what this project decides

It decides nothing about whether you should use the programme. An audit can find that a programme
claims more than its evidence shows and still leave it useful to someone, and it can find a
programme's claims modest and accurate and still leave it wrong for someone. Lesson 9 is where
the course asks you to decide something about your own life, and lesson 1's list
is where it says when that stops being a self-help question.
