  # --- Ordinary and clinical, stepped care (L1) ---
  - q: >-
      An article says: "If you've worried every day for two weeks, you have generalised anxiety
      disorder." How does that sentence stand against lesson 1's reading of the guidelines?
    options:
      - Only its length is wrong, since six months of daily worry would settle the question alone
      - It's sound, since two weeks is the length WHO's fact sheet gives for an anxiety disorder
      - Its length falls short of CG113's six months, and even six months wouldn't settle it alone
      - Its certainty is the flaw, since two weeks of worry may suggest GAD but can't prove it
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      CG113's appendix asks for worry "on a majority of days for at least 6 months", and tells a
      clinician to make an assessment that doesn't "rely solely on the number, severity and duration
      of symptoms" but weighs distress and functional impairment too. So the length is wrong, and six
      months of worry wouldn't settle it on its own either. Softening "you have" to "may suggest"
      keeps the wrong length, so the hedged version is still wrong about what the guideline asks for.
      And WHO's two weeks is its description of a depressive episode, not of an anxiety disorder.

    options:
      - It overreaches, since step 1 adds assessment and a clinician checking back to the education
      - It's fair, since step 1 is education about anxiety, and education is what a podcast provides

      - It may suit some listeners, though stepped care only applies to people already diagnosed

  # --- Effect sizes and comparators (L2) ---
  - q: >-
      A stress course reports g = 0.2 against usual care. Using this course's normal-curve
      conversion, what's the fairest plain reading?
    options:
      - About 20 percent of participants improved who wouldn't have improved on usual care
      - Probably no real effect, since an effect this small usually can't be told apart from zero
      - The course helped about 58 percent of those who took it, and did little for the others
      - The average participant ended ahead of about 58 percent of the usual-care group
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      On the course's table, which assumes bell-shaped scores, 0.2 puts the average treated person
      ahead of about 58 percent of the comparison group, where no effect would be 50. The 20 percent
      option reads the effect size as a share of people, which it isn't, and the option saying 58
      percent were helped turns a comparison of two groups' averages into a count of people helped.
      Small isn't the same as zero: whether 0.2 can be told apart from nothing depends on its
      interval, which the question doesn't give.
  - q: >-
      A review reports that a self-help programme for low mood has a number needed to treat of 6
      against usual care. What does that mean?
    options:
      - For every six given it rather than usual care, about one more responded than otherwise
      - About one in six who used it responded, and the other five probably didn't respond at all
      - Six weeks of the programme are probably needed before most people can expect a response
      - It made a response roughly six times as likely as usual care, give or take a little
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      A number needed to treat of 6 means roughly one extra responder for every six people given
      the programme rather than the comparison, the reading lesson 2 took from Logic and Argument.
      The one-in-six option forgets that people in both groups responded, so the number isn't the
      share who did. It isn't a length of time, and it isn't a ratio of the two groups' chances,
      which is what the six-times option makes of it.
  - q: >-
      In a trial of 48 people, a new stress course shows no significant difference from an
      established one. Its advert says it's "proven as effective as the market leader". How does the
      advert stand?
    options:
      - It's roughly fair, since no significant difference is the usual sign of two equal effects
      - It overclaims, since a small trial that finds no difference hasn't shown the two are equal
      - It undersells, since matching the market leader suggests the new one is probably better
      - It can't be judged, since a trial of under a hundred people can't show anything either way
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      "No significant difference" isn't "the same", which is how lesson 2 read the few head-to-head
      app trials: a small trial can miss a gap that's there. Showing "as good as" takes a
      non-inferiority trial with a margin set in advance, which lesson 6 read. So "roughly fair"
      grants the advert what the trial didn't show. Matching isn't beating, so nothing is undersold,
      and a small trial does tell you something, only imprecisely.

  # --- Self-help CBT (L3) ---
  - q: >-
      A worry app's trial found g = 0.7 against people told they'd get the app in eight weeks. A
      blog says that makes it "as good as the self-help in the Cochrane review", which found 0.67
      against "no intervention". How does that comparison stand?
    options:
      - Probably little, since 0.7 and 0.67 are close and both are standardised effects in one unit
      - The app probably did better, since a waiting list is the harder of the two comparisons
      - Different comparisons, since one faced a waiting list and one a mix with attention controls
      - The review's figure is likely the weaker, since pooling 72 studies dilutes an effect's size
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Standardising puts both numbers in the same units, not against the same comparison, so being
      close in size says little. The app was compared with a waiting list, which lesson 2 showed may
      flatter; the review's "no intervention" included attention and relaxation controls, so lesson
      3 reasoned that against a waiting list alone it might have come out larger. A waiting list is
      the easiest comparison, not the harder one, and pooling studies doesn't dilute an effect.
  - q: >-
      Two people start the same internet CBT course for depression, one with a PHQ-9 score of 7 and
      one with 16. The service says a guide "makes no difference", so neither is offered one. On the
      individual-data analysis in lesson 3, what's the fairest reading?
    options:
      - The claim holds for both, since guided and unguided did about equally well in the long run
      - A guide probably matters more for the person on 7, since milder symptoms respond to support
      - It may hold for both, with the end-of-treatment gap small at every level of severity
      - Guidance went with better results at treatment's end, and more so for scores like the 16
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Karyotaki and colleagues found guided iCBT "associated with more effectiveness than unguided
      iCBT" after treatment, "but we found no evidence of a difference at 6 or 12 months following
      randomization". By severity, the differences in subthreshold depression (PHQ-9 scores 5 to 9)
      "were small, while guided iCBT was associated with overall better outcomes in patients with
      baseline PHQ-9 greater than 9". So "no difference" fits the long run only, which is where the
      both-equal option stops; the gap was small only at the milder level, not at every level; and
      the pattern runs the other way from the option favouring the person on 7. None of it promises
      either person anything, since it describes groups.

      - Y, since it had diagnosed patients, a manual, a therapist and an active comparison
      - X probably comes closer, since it beat its comparison while Y only matched relaxation
      - Neither, since a single technique can only be shown to work inside a full CBT package
      - Both about equally, since each is a single trial and one trial can't settle a technique
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Y has the shape of Hoyer's trial in lesson 4: diagnosed patients, a manual, a trained

  # --- Exercise (L5) ---
  - q: >-
      A newspaper reports a new review in which exercise reduced depression by 0.85, "far more than
      the 2026 Cochrane review's 0.67". Before comparing the two, what's the first thing to find out?
    options:
      - Whether it pooled more trials, since the review with more trials is probably nearer the truth
      - Whether its figure had a minus sign, since only a negative figure shows that mood improved
      - What its comparison groups got, since Cochrane's 57 trials were against no treatment or a control
      - Whether it's the newer review, since a later review usually replaces an earlier one
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Lesson 2's question comes first. Cochrane's figure, printed as -0.67, was for 57 trials against
      no treatment or a control intervention, and a review leaning on waiting lists could come out
      larger without exercise doing any more. More trials can share the same biases, which is why
      Cochrane's seven best-run trials gave a smaller figure than all 57. The sign only says which
      way a source reports improvement: Cochrane's minus means depression went down, while lesson 3's
      self-help review printed its improvement as a positive 0.67. And being newer doesn't make two
      figures comparable.
  - q: >-
      A fitness app says exercise helps low mood "with no side effects, unlike medication". What

    options:
      - That side effects were rare and mild, though the review didn't say which groups had them
      - That adverse events weren't common, though exercisers' included injuries and depression
      - That its harms are probably unknown, since exercise trials seldom record harms at all
      - That it may be less safe than sertraline, since exercisers reported more adverse events
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The abstract: "Adverse events were not common in any comparison, but included musculoskeletal

  # --- Mindfulness, behavioural activation and breathing (L6) ---
  - q: >-
      A company offers every employee an eight-week MBCT course "to prevent burnout", citing the
      trials in which MBCT reduced depressive relapse. How do those trials bear on the offer?
    options:
      - They support it, since MBCT beat active treatments too, and antidepressants were among them
      - They probably support it loosely, since relapse and burnout are both measured as low mood
      - They count against it, since MBCT did worse than the active treatments it was set against
      - They don't reach it, since they studied recurrent depression in remission, not staff stress
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Kuyken's pooled data came from people with recurrent depression in full or partial remission,
      and the authors claim efficacy for relapse prevention in that group; lesson 6 said it isn't
      evidence for mindfulness as a general stress tool. So even loose support stretches it: burnout
      isn't depressive relapse, whatever scale it's measured on. The active treatments included
      antidepressants, but the abstract doesn't report the drug comparisons on their own, and against
      active treatments the hazard ratio of 0.79 favoured MBCT rather than counting against it.
  - q: >-
      A trial sets a non-inferiority margin of -0.40 for a breathing course against a relaxation
      class. The difference is -0.10, with an interval from -0.52 to 0.32. What does it show?
    options:
      - Non-inferiority was shown, since the estimate of -0.10 sits comfortably inside the margin
      - Probably equal, since the interval includes zero and no significant gap was found between them
      - Non-inferiority wasn't shown, since the interval's lower end, -0.52, passes the margin
      - The breathing course is worse, since part of its interval runs out beyond the margin
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      As in the MBSR trial in lesson 6, the test is whether the interval's lower bound sits inside
      the margin. Here -0.52 is further from zero than -0.40, so a shortfall bigger than the margin
      is still possible and non-inferiority isn't shown. The estimate alone isn't the test. The
      interval also reaches 0.32, so being worse isn't shown either. And an interval that includes
      zero means no detectable difference, not equal.

  # --- Antidepressants and serotonin (L8) ---
  - q: >-
      Kirsch and colleagues found the drug-placebo difference grew with how depressed people were at
      the start. What did they say explained that?
    options:
      - The drug probably working better in severe cases, as sicker people had more room to improve
      - Placebo groups improving less in very severe depression, not drug groups improving more
      - Severely depressed people leaving placebo arms early, which may have flattered the drug arms
      - A change of scale, since the most severe cases were scored on a longer version of the scale
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Their conclusion: the relationship "is attributable to decreased responsiveness to placebo
      among very severely depressed patients, rather than to increased responsiveness to
      medication." So the gap widened because the placebo groups improved less, which rules out the
      drug working better. Drop-out appears in lesson 8 as Cipriani's argument that trials may
      understate the drugs, a different point running the other way, and nothing in Kirsch's account
      turns on which version of the scale was used.
  - q: >-
      An invented analysis of trials of a drug for panic suggests that about 12 percent of
      participants had a large benefit from the drug beyond placebo, and that most improved about
      equally on drug or dummy pill. A headline reads: "Panic pill works for patients, study proves:
      they do far better on the drug." What happened on the way?
    options:
      - Little of note, since a large benefit in some people is a benefit the drug really gave
      - An attribution moved, with the headline crediting a study rather than the authors' analysis
      - Probably nothing, since beating the dummy pill in any share of people means the pill works
      - A suggestion became proof, and the part where most improved on either pill dropped out
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      "Suggests" became "proves" and "participants" became "patients", the second of Sleep lesson
      7's shapes: a finding hardened and its scope widened. And "far better on the drug" is true only
      of the 12 percent: most improved on drug and dummy pill alike, and that's the part that dropped
      out, the third shape as this course's lesson 7 widened it. The benefit to some is real, which is
      why "little of note" and "the pill works" miss what the headline did to everyone else, and a
      study is where the claim came from, so no credit moved. The same finding could be hardened the
      other way, into "useless for 88%", and it would be just as wrong.

  # --- Sorting a claim (L9) ---
  - q: >-
      A podcast says: "People who journal every day are less anxious, which means journaling reduces
      anxiety." Using lesson 9's sort, how does the sentence come apart?
    options:
      - Two claims, since "which means" welds an association to a cause it can't show alone
      - Measurable as a whole, since a finding about daily journalers already tests if journaling works
      - Unmeasurable as stated, since "less anxious" has no agreed measure that research could use
      - Two claims, and the second is false, since journalers likely differed from others to begin with
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The weld is "which means". The first half is measurable, and as stated it compares people
      nobody assigned to journal, like the stress survey in lesson 7. The second half is a claim
      about a cause, measurable too, by a trial that assigns people to journal or not, and the first
      half can't settle it: people who journal may differ in other ways, the worry lesson 7 raised
      about that survey. That leaves the cause not shown, which isn't the same as false, since nobody
      has tested it and found it failing. And the trials in this course measured anxiety on rating
      scales.
