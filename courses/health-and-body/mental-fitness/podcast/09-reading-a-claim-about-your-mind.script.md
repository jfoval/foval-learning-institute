S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Mental Fitness, on its last lesson, Reading a claim about your mind.

S2: Before anything else. This course is education, not care. The crisis numbers are at the top of every lesson. In the US and Canada you can call or text nine eight eight.

S1: And this lesson is for the moment you meet a claim about your own mind that you'd like to act on. It uses two habits and makes one refusal.

S2: Habits first.

S1: The first is the institute's sort, three bins. Measurable, meaning a study could check it. Unmeasurable as stated, meaning a word in it has no agreed measure. And false, meaning it's been checked and didn't hold. The lesson wrote its own specimens, and each verdict is the course's reading.

S2: Give me one.

S1: Stress is bad for you.

S2: That feels obviously true.

S1: The course sorts it as unmeasurable as stated. Which stress, how much, bad for what, compared with whom? Lesson seven's survey had to split it into two questions, how much stress people reported and whether they believed it affected their health. Neither on its own independently predicted premature death, only the combination did, and the authors say the study can't establish cause. So the specimen isn't false. It's the outline of a claim.

S2: Next.

S1: Most people who go through something traumatic never really get over it. False, as a statement of what lesson seven's review found. Across fifty four studies, the resilient course was the commonest, at an average of sixty five point seven percent. So the specimen describes real people, only not most of them. And no average can say which course one person is on.

S2: And the hard one?

S1: Meditation is as good as medication, so try an app before you bother a doctor. That's two claims welded together by so, and they don't get the same verdict. The first half is measurable, and lesson six read a trial close to it. For adults with diagnosed anxiety disorders, an eight week course called MBSR was not worse than a common antidepressant by more than a margin the trial set in advance. But that's one trial, read at the level of its abstract, with no placebo arm, of a course rather than an app, for anxiety disorders rather than medication in general. What's been measured is much narrower than the sentence.

S2: And the second half?

S1: Advice to one person, which no trial of an average can settle. The guidelines do put self-help early for many people, after an assessment, and the assessment is the step the specimen skips. And bother is the wrong word. The NHS, writing about mental health emergencies, says you will not be wasting anyone's time. That page is about emergencies. Carrying the sentence over to an ordinary appointment is the course's step, and one it thinks holds.

S2: Habit two.

S1: This course's question. What did the comparison group get? For a claim about a method, works always means does better than something. Take a sentence you might meet on a phone. Clinically proven to reduce anxiety. Against a waiting list, it says the app beat being told to wait. Against an attention control, it beat time and contact with no active ingredient. Against therapy, it matched a therapist. Three different findings in one set of words, and a waiting list is the comparison lesson two ordered as the easiest to beat.

S2: Now the refusal. This is the question a lot of people bring. Is what I'm living with ordinary stress, or something more?

S1: And the lesson answers it in one sentence. This course can't tell you whether what you have is ordinary or clinical, and here's who can.

S2: Why can't it?

S1: The reason is in the guidelines the course has read all along. For generalised anxiety, NICE tells a clinician to conduct a comprehensive assessment that doesn't rely solely on the number, severity and duration of symptoms, but also considers the degree of distress and functional impairment. For depression, one that doesn't rely simply on a symptom count, but takes in severity, previous history, and the duration and course of the illness. The course draws four things from that wording that a clinician has and a reader alone doesn't.

S2: Go on.

S1: History. The depression guideline asks about any past mood elevation, to see whether it might be bipolar disorder, and someone looking only at their low spells might never raise the high ones. The body. For someone who arrives at an emergency department with a panic attack, the anxiety guideline asks for the minimum investigations needed to exclude acute physical problems. No course can examine anyone. Risk. The depression guideline says, always ask people with depression directly about suicidal ideation and intent. A clinician can act on the answer. A page can only print the numbers. And time. The anxiety guideline's first step includes active monitoring, which lesson one glossed as a clinician checking back at a planned point. A course can't notice whether you're getting better or worse.

S2: So it's just being cautious?

S1: The refusal runs both ways. It won't tell you it's just stress, and it won't tell you you're ill, because neither is something a reader of averages can know about one person.

S2: Then who can?

S1: If you're not safe right now, the lines at the top of every lesson. In the UK, urgent but not an emergency, the NHS says to call one one one and select the mental health option. In England, your GP, or NHS Talking Therapies, where you can refer yourself without speaking to a GP. You need to be registered with a GP and aged eighteen or over, or sixteen or over in some areas. Elsewhere, a doctor, usually the one you'd see first for anything. And going isn't a sign that self-help, or you, failed. In the anxiety guideline, assessment is where care begins.

S2: And the decision the lesson ends on?

S1: Four endings, each legitimate. Talk to someone. Change one thing. Keep something deliberately. Or stop deciding. But first read lesson one's list of reasons to contact someone. If anything on it sounds like your life, talking to someone comes first, and the others can wait or run alongside it. And if the one thing you want to change is starting, stopping or changing a medicine, that belongs to the first ending too. Talk to the prescriber, or a doctor if you don't have one. On stopping, NICE tells prescribers to explain that it's usually necessary to reduce the dose in stages, and that most people stop successfully. It also notes that for some people withdrawal can sometimes be more difficult, which is one more reason that conversation comes first.

S2: And whichever you choose?

S1: One more line. What would you notice that should send you to the first ending? The course doesn't choose for you, beyond putting that ending first when the list applies.
