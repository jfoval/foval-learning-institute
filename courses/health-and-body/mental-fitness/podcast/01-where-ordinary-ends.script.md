S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're starting the course called Mental Fitness, with its first lesson. Where ordinary ends.

S2: And the lesson says something before it teaches anything, so let's do the same.

S1: Let's. Some people who open a course called Mental Fitness are having a hard time right now, or are worried about someone who is. This course is education, not care. If you're thinking about suicide or self-harm, or don't feel able to keep yourself safe, there's a box at the top of every lesson with the emergency numbers and crisis lines. Contact emergency services, like nine one one in the US and Canada or nine nine nine in the UK, or a crisis line. In the US and Canada you can call or text nine eight eight. In the UK and Ireland, Samaritans are on one one six, one two three. The box has Australia's numbers too, and a site that lists free lines by country. And the lesson quotes the NHS on this. A mental health emergency should be taken as seriously as a physical one. You will not be wasting anyone's time.

S2: So what is the lesson actually about?

S1: A line. Where ordinary stress, worry and low mood end, and something that needs more than a course begins. It shows you how the guidelines draw that line. It won't help you place yourself on one side of it, and it says why. It starts with the World Health Organization's fact sheet on anxiety. It opens, everyone can feel anxious sometimes, but people with anxiety disorders often experience fear and worry that is both intense and excessive. And then, they are difficult to control, cause significant distress and can last a long time if untreated.

S2: So the feeling itself isn't the problem.

S1: Right. The first half says anxiety is ordinary. The second says what makes it a disorder. Intensity, excess, how hard it is to control, the distress, and how long it lasts. Not whether the feeling is there. The depression fact sheet does the same. A depressive episode lasts most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks. The line is drawn by duration and pervasiveness, not by sadness itself.

S2: How is a doctor told to draw it?

S1: The course leans on two guidelines from the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, NICE, one for anxiety and panic, one for depression. For anxiety, the key symptoms are excessive anxiety and worry about a number of events or activities, and difficulty controlling it, on a majority of days for at least six months. And then the sentence the whole lesson rests on. A clinician is told to conduct a comprehensive assessment that does not rely solely on the number, severity and duration of symptoms, but also considers the degree of distress and functional impairment.

S2: Functional impairment meaning what?

S1: How far it gets in the way of work, study, people and ordinary life. The depression guideline says the same kind of thing. Don't rely simply on a symptom count. And it calls severity a continuum. So both guidelines draw a line across something that shades by degree. Where exactly it should sit is argued over, and the course says plainly it hasn't read that argument.

S2: Give me a case.

S1: Two people, both invented for the lesson. Priya can't stop worrying. She lies awake running through what could go wrong, her shoulders ache, and she's short with people. Her final exams are in nine days. Tom has the same worry, the same aching shoulders, the same short temper. He's had them on most days since last winter, eight months ago, about work, money, his parents, whatever comes up. He's stopped going to the gym and been called in about missed deadlines.

S2: Okay, Tom's the one. Past six months, it's about everything, and his life's shrinking. Priya's worry has a date on it.

S1: That's the right reading of the shape. What separates them is how long, how far it reaches, and what it gets in the way of. Tom's work and life have narrowed. Priya is still doing what the exams require.

S2: So Priya's fine, and Tom has generalised anxiety disorder.

S1: No, and the lesson calls that the most important thing in it. It means Tom's is the kind of case a guideline says a clinician should assess. Whether it's a disorder is that clinician's judgement, made in a conversation a lesson can't have. And Priya might be having a harder time than a course can see. If Tom were real, the useful sentence isn't, you have GAD. It's, that's worth talking to a doctor about, and here's how.

S2: What about panic? One bad attack feels like it has to mean something.

S1: The anxiety guideline is careful here. A panic attack does not necessarily constitute a panic disorder, and appropriate treatment of a panic attack may limit the development of panic disorder. So one attack isn't a diagnosis, and it isn't nothing either. And in the same advice, for emergency departments, the guideline checks the body first. So chest pain is a medical question before it's a psychological one. The NHS says to call nine nine nine if you get sudden pain or discomfort in your chest that does not go away, or if you have chest pain and you feel sweaty, sick, light headed or short of breath. This course won't tell you chest pain is probably anxiety.

S2: Say someone does need more than a course. What happens?

S1: Steps. The anxiety guideline says follow the stepped care model, offering the least intrusive, most effective intervention first. Step one is assessment, education, and active monitoring, which means a clinician checking back with you at a planned point. Step two is self-help, on your own or guided by a trained practitioner, and classes about anxiety. Step three is cognitive behavioural therapy or applied relaxation, or a drug treatment.

S2: So I have to fail at self-help before anyone offers me therapy.

S1: No. Step three is for, in the guideline's words, an inadequate response to step two interventions or marked functional impairment. That or matters. Someone whose life has narrowed a long way doesn't have to fail at self-help first. And it isn't therapy on demand either.

S2: And where does a course like this one sit?

S1: The placement is the course's own, from the guidelines' tables. For someone stressed or low in the ordinary way, it sits before step one. It's education, one of the things step one includes, without the assessment and monitoring that make step one care. For someone with a disorder, it's at most something alongside step two, and it isn't what NICE means by self-help, which lesson three shows is more specific than a course. This course is not treatment, at any step. And going to a doctor with any of the reasons the lesson lists isn't overreacting, or a sign that self-help has failed. It's what the first step is for.
