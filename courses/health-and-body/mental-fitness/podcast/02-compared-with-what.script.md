NICE's advice to anyone on an antidepressant who wants to stop is to talk with the person who prescribed their medication, because it is usually necessary to reduce the dose in stages over time.

S2: So what's this lesson for?

S1: The rest of the course. The next lessons are about methods people are offered for stress, worry and low mood, and every one comes with a number. This lesson is how to read it. And the question it adds is this. What did the comparison group get?

S2: Start with the number, then. How big are these effects?

S1: They're standardised effect sizes, a gap between two groups' averages measured in standard deviations. For self-help and apps, the research the course read found effects between about nought point two five and nought point four five. And the course's own summary is that almost every method in it lands in a band of roughly nought point two to nought point six.

S2: I have no feel for what nought point three means.

S1: The lesson converts it. If scores in both groups are spread in the bell shape statisticians call the normal curve, an effect of nought point three puts the average treated person ahead of about sixty two percent of the comparison group. With no effect at all, it would be fifty. That conversion is the course's own arithmetic, not a figure from any study, and it only holds if the bell-shape assumption does.

S2: So it works for sixty two percent of people.

S1: No. It's a statement about two groups' averages, not about how many people it worked for. Some people in the treated group did worse than some in the comparison group, and some in the comparison group got better with nothing at all. And it says nothing about you. Whether a method helps one person is a question an average can't answer.

S2: So where does the comparison group come in?

S1: Every effect size is a gap. So its size depends on how much the treated group improved, and on how much the comparison group did. And trials here use different kinds of comparison. The course orders them from easiest to beat to hardest, and the ordering is its own. A waiting list, where you're told you'll get the treatment later and get nothing meanwhile. No treatment, where you carry on without being told you're waiting. Usual care. A placebo therapy, which is time with a professional with the active ingredients left out. And another active treatment.

S2: Wait. A waiting list is easier to beat than no treatment? Neither group gets anything.

S1: That's most people's guess. A twenty fourteen network meta-analysis by Furukawa and colleagues took forty nine trials of CBT for depression, with two thousand seven hundred and thirty participants, and compared the comparison conditions with each other. In that network, the odds of responding were about three times higher with no treatment than on a waiting list. The paper gives an odds ratio of two point nine, with an interval running from one point three to five point seven.

S2: So three times as many people got better just by not being told to wait?

S1: No. Odds aren't the same as chances, so it isn't three times as many people, and the interval is wide. That plain reading is the course's. The paper's title offers a reason, that a waiting list may be a nocebo condition. The course reads that as, being told to wait may itself hold improvement back. That's the authors' proposal, with its may, in the course's words, and the course read the abstract, not the analysis behind it.

S2: And if the waiting list does badly, whatever's compared with it looks better.

S1: Right. A comparison group that does unusually badly widens the gap without the treatment doing anything more. But the authors are careful about their own finding, and the care travels with it. In the lesson's plain version, they meant to rerun the analysis on only the best-run trials and had too few to do it. And when they tried to correct for unpublished studies, the difference between waiting list and no treatment went away. So it's a warning, not a law. A number measured against a waiting list might be flattered, and you'd want to find out before you compare it with anything else.

S2: Show me the warning doing some work.

S1: Cuijpers and colleagues, twenty twenty three. Four hundred and nine trials, fifty two thousand seven hundred and two patients, and the course read the abstract. One therapy, CBT for depression, three comparisons. Against control conditions such as care as usual and waitlist, nought point seven nine. Against medication, no significant difference in the short term, and a difference in CBT's favour at six to twelve months, though the number of trials was small and the difference wasn't significant in all the re-checks. Against other talking therapies, nought point nought six, which didn't survive most of the authors' re-checks.

S2: So the same therapy looks big, level, or barely different, depending on what it's up against.

S1: That's the lesson. And here's the trap. Setting nought point seven nine beside a medicine's effect against a placebo pill, which lesson eight will show is about nought point three. That compares a therapy against waiting lists and usual care with a pill against a dummy pill, and it makes the therapy look far stronger. The fair comparison in that abstract is the one where the two meet head to head.

S2: Last one. I see apps advertised as clinically proven, as effective as therapy.

S1: The lesson invents exactly that advert. You find its study: the app against a waiting list, an effect of nought point three. So run the question. What would as effective as therapy need the comparison group to have got?

S2: Therapy. And it got a waiting list.

S1: So the study can support better than waiting, in that trial, and its comparison may flatter how much better. It can't support as good as therapy. And that's the question for every method lesson from here. What did the comparison group get?
