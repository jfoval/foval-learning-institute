S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Mental Fitness, on the lesson called Resilience, a ratio and a mindset.

S2: One thing before we start. This course is education, not care, and the crisis numbers are at the top of every lesson. In the US and Canada you can call or text nine eight eight.

S1: Yes. And the lesson takes three ideas you've probably met. Resilience is rare and can be trained. You need about three positive emotions for every negative one. And believing stress is bad for you is what makes it bad for you. Each has a real paper behind it. The skill is finding the step where the popular claim stopped matching its evidence, without sneering at the people who made it or the people who believed it.

S2: Resilience first. How rare is it?

S1: Not rare. A review pooled fifty four studies of people after potentially traumatic events and sorted the courses people took. The resilient course, which the course glosses as staying roughly steady, was the commonest, at an average of sixty five point seven percent. Recovery came next, at twenty point eight, in the course's gloss a real drop and then a climb back. That still leaves about one in three on another course. And the course adds a sentence of its own, worth keeping after a hard year: being on any of these courses says nothing about anyone's character.

S2: Now three to one.

S1: In two thousand and five, Fredrickson and Losada predicted that a ratio of positive to negative feeling at or above two point nine would mark people in flourishing mental health. The critics who later examined the paper give the two group averages as three point two for the flourishing group and two point three for the rest.

S2: And two point nine sits between them. Doesn't that show the threshold?

S1: No. It shows the flourishing group had a higher average. You'd see that if more positive feeling were a bit better all the way along, with no threshold anywhere. Two averages either side of a value can't show that something changes sharply at it.

S2: Who were the critics?

S1: Brown, Sokal and Friedman, in twenty thirteen. The course read their abstract and parts of their own preprint, whose wording may differ from the published version. Their conclusion is that the claim to have shown a critical minimum ratio of two point nine oh one three is entirely unfounded. Where did the number come from? The formula rested on settings that could have been anything within wide limits. One of them had been chosen as ten, in their words, for illustrative purposes and purely for convenience. Change it to sixteen and the same formula gives four point one six five five four zero five.

S2: So the precise figure rested on choices nobody made for reasons to do with emotions.

S1: That's the lesson's summary. But the critics drew their own boundary. They say they don't call into question that a higher positivity ratio is ordinarily more desirable than a lower one. They say they cannot prove no tipping point exists. And they granted that the study gives some empirical evidence that a higher ratio typically goes with better outcomes.

S2: And Fredrickson?

S1: Her reply, read at abstract level, calls Losada's modelling now-questioned, and she no longer rests her case on it. What she keeps is this. Within bounds, higher positivity ratios are predictive of flourishing mental health and other beneficial outcomes. A direction, with a limit, and no tipping point. She still argues that nonlinear effects need modelling. The course hasn't read the evidence she cites, so it doesn't rule on how strong it is.

S2: I've heard the paper was retracted.

S1: It was corrected. PubMed lists an erratum and no retraction. The course read the notice as Retraction Watch reprinted it, because the journal's own page refused its request. It formally withdraws the modelling as invalid, and with it the predicted ratios of two point nine and eleven point six. And it says the other elements remain valid, including the finding that ratios were higher in the flourishing group.

S2: So what's left standing?

S1: The tipping point fell. The weaker claim, that within bounds more positive feeling predicts flourishing, is one Fredrickson holds and the critics said they weren't questioning, and the correction keeps the paper's finding that ratios were higher in the flourishing group. A version that still says three to one has dropped the examination and the correction. A version that says positivity was debunked has dropped the half both sides agree on. In the course's reading, both drops run toward a simpler story.

S2: Last one. Believing stress is bad for you.

S1: Many people meet it through a talk and a book, which the course hasn't read or watched, so nothing here is a claim about them. What it read is the study, a survey. Keller and colleagues linked a nineteen ninety eight American health survey, of twenty eight thousand, seven hundred and fifty three people, to deaths through two thousand and six. Those who reported a lot of stress, and that it affected their health a lot, had a forty three percent increased risk of premature death, a hazard ratio of one point four three, against people who reported almost no stress and hardly any effect of it, after the authors' adjustments.

S2: So the belief is what kills you.

S1: That's the step the lesson is about. Neither the amount of stress nor the belief on its own independently predicted death. Only the combination did. And the authors were careful. They estimated twenty thousand, two hundred and thirty one deaths a year, but under a condition, if this were in fact a causal relationship. And straight after, they say the study is unable to establish one.

S2: What else could explain it?

S1: They named it themselves. Reverse causality. People already in poor health may say stress is hurting their health because of that poor health. When they added how healthy people said they were, the ratio fell to one point one eight, borderline. In the course's reading, that fits illness driving the answers and fits stress acting through health, and a survey can't tell them apart.

S2: And if you change the belief and see what happens? Has anyone tested that?

S1: In experiments, and they're real, and small. A twenty twenty four meta-analysis pooled randomised trials of reappraising stress and of stress-is-enhancing mindset training. The outcome was performance on a task. The effect was small, d of point two three. Correcting for studies estimated to be missing, it fell to point one four, and still held. Reappraisal alone was significant. The mindset idea alone wasn't, on six effect sizes, which the course reads as possibly too few to tell.

S2: So both headlines are wrong. The belief kills you, and the mindset research found nothing.

S1: Yes. The survey is about death and can't separate belief from illness. The experiments are about task performance and can separate cause from association. In the course's reading, neither stands in for the other.
