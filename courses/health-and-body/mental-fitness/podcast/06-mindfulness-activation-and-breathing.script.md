S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Mental Fitness, on the lesson called Mindfulness, behavioural activation and breathing.

S2: And first, the line at the top of every lesson in this course.

S1: This course is education, not care. If you're thinking about suicide or self-harm, or don't feel able to keep yourself safe, contact emergency services or a crisis line. In the US and Canada you can call or text nine eight eight. Numbers for other countries are at the top of every lesson.

S2: So three methods. A mindfulness app, get active, and a breathing exercise. Where do we start?

S1: With mindfulness, sorted by two things. Who was studied, and what they were compared with. Goyal and colleagues, in twenty fourteen, reviewed forty seven trials in adult clinical populations, where the comparison groups were given something that looked like a treatment. Mindfulness programmes had moderate evidence of improved anxiety and depression, with an effect size of nought point three eight for anxiety at eight weeks. And the same abstract says they found no evidence that meditation programmes were better than any active treatment, drugs, exercise, or other behavioural therapies. Then the other end. Galante and colleagues, in twenty twenty three, pooled thirteen trials of in-person programmes in over two thousand adults who weren't patients, compared mostly with a waiting list or nothing. They found a small to moderate reduction in distress, and rated their confidence in that result high.

S2: High confidence. So mindfulness works.

S1: Listen to the conditions in their own conclusion. Group-based, teacher-led programmes generally reduce psychological distress among community adults who volunteer to receive this type of intervention. Community adults, not patients, who volunteered, in person, against passive controls. And the lesson's own reading is that an app used alone is neither group-based nor teacher-led, so that confidence doesn't carry over to an app by default.

S2: I've heard mindfulness is as good as antidepressants.

S1: And in twenty eighteen a group of mindfulness researchers wrote a critique of their own field called Mind the Hype. And they said mindfulness is sometimes misleadingly described as comparable to antidepressants, citing the review we just heard. That review's discussion does say its small effects are comparable with what would be expected from an antidepressant in primary care, without the associated toxicities.

S2: Why would the field's own researchers call that misleading?

S1: The lesson gives its own two-step reading. First, the comparison runs across studies. The meditation effects came from trials against active controls. The antidepressant figures came from other trials, of other people, against placebo. Second, no evidence that meditation was better than active treatment is easy to hear as just as good. It isn't the same claim. No evidence of a difference isn't evidence of no difference. To show as good as, you need a trial that sets in advance how big a shortfall it will tolerate, and then shows the real shortfall is smaller. That's called a non-inferiority trial.

S2: Has anyone run one?

S1: Five years later. Hoge and colleagues randomised adults with diagnosed anxiety disorders to an eight-week mindfulness-based stress reduction course, MBSR, or escitalopram, a commonly prescribed antidepressant. A clinician's rating of anxiety severity fell by one point three five points on average with the course and one point four three with the drug, and the gap between them fell inside the margin the trial had set in advance. So the course was shown not to be worse by more than that margin.

S2: And side effects?

S1: They differed a lot. Of patients who started treatment, eight percent dropped out of the drug group because of adverse events, and nobody from the course. At least one study-related adverse event hit seventy eight point six percent of those randomised to the drug, and fifteen point four percent of those randomised to the course.

S2: So now it is comparable.

S1: The lesson doesn't settle that, on purpose. It's one trial, with no placebo or no-treatment group, so it can't say how much either group improved because of its treatment. The trial's authors would say their design answers the twenty eighteen objection directly. A critic in the twenty eighteen mould would say one non-inferiority trial is where a claim of comparability starts, not where it ends. The course sets the two side by side and doesn't choose between them.

S2: And notice fifteen percent on the course had an adverse event too.

S1: Far fewer than on the drug, but not none. And the same twenty eighteen critique notes that the official standards of practice for MBSR exclude suicidality and the presence of any psychiatric disorder. The lesson notes it has since been trialled in anxiety disorders, as we just heard. NICE's group mindfulness programme is built for depression, and cautions that it may be difficult for people experiencing intense or highly distressing thoughts. The lesson's reading of those together is that if you're having thoughts of suicide, a mindfulness course isn't the first step. A clinician is.

S2: Okay. Get active. That's behavioural activation?

S1: Yes. It's a brief talking therapy that works on what a person does rather than on what they think, focusing on the link between activities and mood. The course is honest that it has no manual among its sources, so it can't tell you what a session contains.

S2: And the evidence?

S1: A Cochrane review of fifty three trials, all in adults with diagnosed or subthreshold depression. Against CBT, moderate certainty evidence suggesting no evidence of a difference in the short term. Against treatment as usual, it did better in the short term, on moderate certainty, but that difference was no longer evident when the numbers were rerun under a worst-case or intention-to-treat scenario. The authors conclude it may be more effective than treatment as usual and no less effective than CBT, but that their confidence in these findings is limited by concerns about the certainty of the evidence.

S2: Last one. Breathing. Surely that's the easy win.

S1: The evidence base is small. Fincham and colleagues pooled twelve trials, seven hundred and eighty five adults, most at moderate risk of bias, and found a small to medium effect on stress. The authors say breathwork may be effective for improving stress and mental health. Then, in the next breath, they urge caution, to avoid a miscalibration between hype and evidence. And the outcome was self-reported stress. Nothing in the main result measured a change in the body. The course has no source on any specific technique, so it describes none as evidence-based.

S2: This lesson put mindfulness beside an antidepressant. Someone might take that as permission to switch.

S1: The lesson says none of it is a reason to start, stop or switch a treatment. If you're on an antidepressant and want to come off it, NICE says to talk with the person who prescribed it, because it's usually necessary to reduce the dose in stages over time. It says most people stop successfully, and that for some, withdrawal can sometimes be more difficult, with symptoms that can last weeks or occasionally months.

S2: We've left out mindfulness-based cognitive therapy and its results on preventing relapse in depression, the earlier, wider review of mindfulness in non-clinical settings, the rest of the behavioural activation comparisons, and where NICE places behavioural activation. The full lesson is free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.
