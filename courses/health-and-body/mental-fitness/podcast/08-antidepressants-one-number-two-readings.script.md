S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Mental Fitness, on the lesson called Antidepressants: one number, two readings, and a hypothesis.

S2: Two things first. This course is education, not care, and the crisis numbers are at the top of every lesson. And some listeners take an antidepressant.

S1: Nothing here is advice about yours, in either direction. The lesson describes a dispute among researchers. Whether a medicine is right for a particular person is a conversation with the person who prescribed it.

S2: So what's the dispute?

S1: Two headlines that seem to contradict each other. The pills barely beat a dummy pill. And they're an established treatment that works. Start with the best-known critique, Kirsch and colleagues in two thousand and eight. They used trials submitted to the US Food and Drug Administration for licensing four drugs, published and unpublished, thirty five trials with five thousand, one hundred and thirty three patients.

S2: And they found?

S1: On the Hamilton depression scale, the drug groups improved by nine point six points and the placebo groups by seven point eight. A difference of one point eight. Kirsch writes that the difference easily attained statistical significance, but that it does not meet the three point criterion for clinical significance used by NICE. The standardised figure they give is point three two, below the point five NICE suggested.

S2: So he's not saying the drugs failed to beat placebo.

S1: No. They beat it, easily, and the margin falls short of a line, which he attributes to NICE's two thousand and four guideline. The course hasn't checked whether the current guideline uses any such threshold.

S2: And the reply?

S1: The review most often quoted in reply came ten years later: Cipriani and colleagues pooled five hundred and twenty two trials, a hundred and sixteen thousand, four hundred and seventy seven participants, twenty one drugs. Every one of the twenty one beat placebo. The summary effect was point three zero.

S2: Point three two and point three zero. That's the same number.

S1: Roughly, because the two papers standardised differently. But neither is arguing about whether the average is small. It is. And with the placebo groups at seven point eight against the drugs' nine point six, most of the improvement happened in both arms. The point three is the part the trials credit to the drug, over everything else that comes with being in a trial.

S2: So where do they part?

S1: On what a small average means. Kirsch's team asked whether it depended on how depressed people were at the start. It did, but the gap widened in the most severe cases because the placebo group improved less, not because the drug group improved more. And they drew a conclusion about prescribing: there seems little evidence to support the prescription of antidepressant medication to any but the most severely depressed patients, unless alternative treatments have failed to provide benefit.

S2: That sounds sweeping.

S1: It keeps a place for the drugs in the most severe depression, and where other treatments haven't helped. And it's about prescribing policy across patients, not what any one person should do.

S2: And Cipriani's side?

S1: They report the same size as a finding that holds. All the antidepressants were more efficacious than placebo, and the summary effect sizes were mostly modest. Modest is their word. They argue these trials may understate the drugs, because people on the drug may drop out early, suspecting they have placebo, and their early scores are carried to the end. They grade their own evidence, too: certainty moderate to very low. In the parts the course read, they don't set their figure against a clinical threshold in either direction. The course hasn't read a statement of why a modest, consistent effect like this one matters clinically, and it won't write one for them.

S2: So who's right about whether point three matters?

S1: On the course's reading, the three point line isn't something a trial measures. It's a judgement about how big a difference has to be before it matters to a person, and a different committee could draw it somewhere else without moving the point three. Kirsch's severity finding doesn't depend on where the line sits. So the size of the average is established, and what it means clinically is contested.

S2: Then the third headline. Depression isn't caused by low serotonin.

S1: In twenty twenty two, Moncrieff and colleagues reviewed the reviews. Their finding: no consistent evidence of an association between serotonin and depression, and no support for the hypothesis that depression is caused by lowered serotonin. Two things rejected, a cause and an association. Most of their own sources were rated low quality, though another approach suggested some findings were reasonably robust.

S2: And the reply?

S1: Jauhar and thirty four co-authors, titled A leaky umbrella has little value. They call the conclusion overstated, including for selective reporting of data. One example is lowering serotonin on purpose, by depleting tryptophan, which the body makes it from. They say the review left out the figure for depressed people not taking antidepressants, where the effect on mood was large, an effect size of minus one point nine, the minus meaning mood went down. And then their own concession. Admittedly, a number of those studies came from the same group, and the intervals were wide. Removing a potential outlier brought it to minus one point oh six. On the course's reading, not theirs, that's still large, though its interval reaches down into small.

S2: So which is it?

S1: Look at the verbs. The response defends a role for serotonin, and a system that is perturbed. On the causal claim, in the course's reading, both can be true: a system can be involved in an illness without a shortage in it being the cause. On the association, they flatly disagree. The response's evidence is offered as exactly the link the review says isn't consistently there, and the review reads those studies as weak, small, or confounded by past drug use. The course calls it contested and reaches no verdict.

S2: But if the serotonin story is wrong, the pills don't work.

S1: Different questions. Whether a drug helps is tested against placebo. That's the point three. How it helps is separate, and on the course's reading a treatment can work through a route that has nothing to do with the illness's cause. The response objects to discussing efficacy in a review that presented no such data. The review, from the other direction, calls it an assumption that the drugs' effects show serotonin is implicated, while the response's conclusion counts the drugs' efficacy as lending credibility to a role. So they don't draw the link the same way. But neither treats a review of causes as a test of whether the drugs help, and neither paper is evidence for starting or stopping a medicine.

S2: And if someone listening wants to stop?

S1: Nothing in the lesson tells anyone to start, stop, reduce or switch a medicine. NICE advises talking with the person who prescribed it first. It's usually necessary to reduce the dose in stages, and most people stop successfully. But NICE also notes that withdrawal can sometimes be more difficult, with symptoms lasting longer, in some cases several weeks, and occasionally several months.

S2: We've left out a twenty twenty two analysis of individual patients' data, which asks whether the average hides a group who benefit a lot, what NICE's guideline recommends at each severity, and the interests both serotonin papers declare. The full lesson is free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening.
