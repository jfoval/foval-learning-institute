S2: So. Worry. The what-ifs at two in the morning. What's the lesson's angle?

S1: That worry is where advice runs furthest ahead of the evidence. So it starts with what NICE's clinical guideline for generalised anxiety disorder actually offers. At its second step, for people whose symptoms haven't improved after education and active monitoring, it offers self-help, on your own or with a guide, or psychoeducational groups, guided by the person's preference.

S2: And if that isn't enough?

S1: Step three is a choice between a high-intensity psychological treatment and a drug, and NICE doesn't rank them. It says to base the choice on the person's preference, because there is no evidence that either is better. The drug side is a conversation with a prescriber, not a course.

S2: And the psychological side is what?

S1: NICE says offer either CBT or applied relaxation.

S2: Applied relaxation. That sounds like somebody telling me to calm down.

S1: That's the popular idea, and it's wrong. NICE specifies that it should be based on the treatment manuals used in the clinical trials of applied relaxation for generalised anxiety disorder, be delivered by trained and competent practitioners, and usually consist of twelve to fifteen weekly sessions, each lasting an hour. Manualised means delivered from a written protocol. The course is honest that it didn't read a description of the technique itself. But it's a treatment, not a tip.

S2: Here's what I'd have guessed, though. NICE must say exercise and mindfulness help with anxiety.

S1: The lesson read the recommendations page in full. Mindfulness, breathing, yoga and physical activity don't appear on it. Exercise appears twice. Once as a verb, exercise caution. And once in a recommendation from two thousand and four about panic disorder, which is health advice, not treatment: the benefits of exercise as part of good general health should be discussed with all people with panic disorder as appropriate. No recommendation for generalised anxiety names exercise, mindfulness, or any single worry technique.

S2: So where does the idea come from?

S1: Partly from a different guideline, on depression, which lists group exercise and group mindfulness among its options for less severe depression. Different guideline, different condition. The next two lessons come to those.

S2: Okay. Then the technique everybody gets handed. Worry time.

S1: It goes by several names. Worry postponement, or stimulus control for worry. In the trials the lesson read, it meant setting aside a fixed time, and in one trial a fixed place, for worrying, and putting worry off until then. And the lesson is firm that that's a description of what the studies tested, not an instruction.

S2: So does it work?

S1: Four trials, all read at abstract level, and the lesson reads each one by what the comparison group got. The first, McGowan and Behar, took fifty three people with high worry for two weeks. Their comparison group was told not to avoid worry, which is an active instruction. Postponement did better on worry, anxiety, negative mood and insomnia, though not on depression or positive mood. And high worry there means a standing tendency to worry, not a diagnosis.

S2: That sounds like a yes.

S1: It's probably the strongest of the four, on the course's reading, and its own authors call it preliminary support for using the technique in larger treatment packages. The second, Versluis and colleagues, ran it online in the general population, against people who simply recorded their worry each day. No difference. And only three hundred and sixty one of nine hundred and ninety six finished, so the result describes the people who stayed.

S2: But recording your worry isn't nothing.

S1: Right. A comparison group that writes its worries down every day is doing something, so matching it isn't the same as doing nothing. The authors themselves contrast their result with earlier pen and paper studies. The third, Krzikalla and colleagues in twenty twenty four, found lower worry in forty seven people with generalised anxiety, held at four weeks. But against a waiting list, which lesson two showed may flatter a treatment. And they used a different rationale under the same name. The fourth, McCarrick and colleagues, found an augmented version beat standard postponement on how long people worried. Two versions of the technique against each other. No sleep outcome improved against control.

S2: So if a website tells me worry time is proven?

S1: The lesson's summary is small trials, mostly positive, one null online, no meta-analysis the course found, and two different rationales under one name. Contested, at the level of single small trials. That classification is the course's own.

S2: Then can a single technique ever be shown to work on its own? Or is it always the whole package?

S1: It can, and the lesson has the case. Worry exposure is another part of CBT for generalised anxiety. In two thousand and nine Hoyer and colleagues set out to test it alone, because, as they put it, its efficacy as a stand-alone treatment had never been tested. Seventy three outpatients with a diagnosis were allocated to worry exposure, applied relaxation, or a waiting list, in manualised treatment of fifteen sessions. Forty eight percent reached high end state functioning with worry exposure, fifty six percent with applied relaxation, and the two didn't differ on dropout or treatment effects. Their conclusion was that this was the first study to show a stand-alone exposure technique is efficacious in treating the disorder.

S2: So what makes that different from a worry time tip? It's one technique too.

S1: On the course's reading, not the number of techniques. How it was tested and delivered. A diagnosed sample, a manual, a trained therapist, an active comparison. That's the gap between a component shown to work and a tip. And, again as the course's reading, a package that works doesn't show every part works alone, while a part not yet shown to work alone isn't shown to fail either.

S2: And if somebody listening has tried a tip and it did nothing?

S1: Then a tip done alone at home isn't the treatment the trials test. Step three is therapy or medication, the person's choice with a clinician, and it's for an inadequate response to step two or marked functional impairment. And the lesson's own example, worry about many things, most days, for over a year, affecting work, is what the guideline says a clinician should assess. A doctor is the first step there, not a technique from a podcast.
