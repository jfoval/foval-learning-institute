S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Mental Fitness, on the lesson called Exercise, asked three ways.

S2: First, the line every lesson in this course opens with.

S1: This course is education, not care. If you're thinking about suicide or self-harm, or don't feel able to keep yourself safe, contact emergency services or a crisis line. In the US and Canada you can call or text nine eight eight. The numbers for other countries are at the top of every lesson.

S2: Now, exercise helps. Everyone's heard it. What's left to ask?

S1: How much, compared with what, and how sure anyone can be. It starts with the guideline, which is more specific than the advice. For less severe depression, NICE's first-line options include group exercise, which it defines as a physical activity programme specifically designed for people with depression, usually more than one session a week for ten weeks, with moderate intensity aerobic exercise.

S2: So not a walk round the block.

S1: That's a treatment. NICE also says, more generally, that any regular physical activity, walking, jogging, swimming, dance, gardening, could help enhance a person's sense of wellbeing. But that's a consensus recommendation, the committee's agreed view rather than a finding graded from trials, and the verb is could.

S2: Okay. The three answers.

S1: They come from the current Cochrane review of exercise for depression, with seventy three trials. The course read its abstract. The first answer is exercise against no treatment or a control, fifty seven trials. The standardised mean difference is minus nought point six seven, and the minus sign means depression went down. The review says exercise may result in a reduction in symptoms, and grades the evidence low certainty. That grade is about the evidence, not about exercise.

S2: And the second?

S1: The same comparison, but only the seven trials that were run best. They hid which group people would be in until they enrolled, analysed everyone they randomised, and used assessors blind to who had exercised. The answer shrinks to minus nought point four six. And the interval runs from minus nought point nought four, barely above nothing, to minus nought point eight eight, which is large. Seven trials can't pin the size down.

S2: And the third answer?

S1: Head to head. Ten trials set exercise against psychological therapy, and the review says there's probably little to no difference, on moderate certainty evidence. Five set it against medication, and there may be little to no difference, on low certainty. So the review's conclusion is that exercise may be moderately more effective than a control, and appears to be no more or less effective than therapy or drugs, though that rests on a few small trials.

S2: But people know they're exercising. Doesn't that mess up the trial?

S1: It does, and the review says so about every one of the seventy three. It judged all of them at high risk of performance bias, because blinding the people receiving and delivering exercise is inherently difficult. And many trials used people's own ratings of their mood, which have the potential to bias findings too.

S2: So is it all placebo?

S1: The lesson calls that the opposite overreach. Even the seven best trials found a benefit. What hasn't been separated out is how much of the effect is expectation.

S2: I've read much stronger claims than may be moderately effective.

S1: So has the lesson, and it gives them in their own words. Heissel and colleagues found large effects against non-exercising controls, and moderate effects when they kept only the low risk of bias studies. Their conclusion is that exercise is efficacious and should be offered as an evidence-based treatment, focusing on supervised and group exercise, at moderate intensity and aerobic. But in the same abstract they say the small samples of many trials and high heterogeneity in methods should be considered when interpreting the results.

S2: And the other one?

S1: Noetel and colleagues, two hundred and eighteen studies. They found moderate reductions in depression for walking or jogging, yoga, and strength training, against active controls. They call exercise an effective treatment for depression. And they say these forms of exercise, meaning walking or jogging, yoga and strength training, could be considered alongside psychotherapy and antidepressants as core treatments. And in the same abstract, results appeared robust to publication bias, but only one study met Cochrane's criteria for low risk of bias, so their confidence was low for walking or jogging and very low for other treatments. The course's summary of all three reviews is that they agree on the direction. They differ on how big, how sure, and what to call it.

S2: Then where does exercise is one and a half times more effective than therapy come from?

S1: The lesson traces it. A twenty twenty three overview by Singh and colleagues found physical activity had medium effects on depression, a median of minus nought point four three, against usual care. In its discussion it says that's comparable to or slightly greater than the effects seen for psychotherapy and medication, a range of minus nought point two two to minus nought point three seven. And the next sentence says research on the relative effectiveness of physical activity is needed to confirm these findings.

S2: And the press release?

S1: The university's release said physical activity is one and a half times more effective than counselling or the leading medications. The paper doesn't print one and a half in the parts the course read. But the middle of that range is about nought point two nine five, and nought point four three divided by that is about one point four six. So the ratio looks like arithmetic on the paper's own figures, and that arithmetic is the course's.

S2: So comparable became more effective.

S1: And the call to confirm didn't make it into the release. On the lesson's reading, the problem is the comparison group. The minus nought point four three was against usual care. The other range came from other reviews, of other trials, against their own controls. Dividing one by the other compares two baselines as much as two treatments. The trials that did test exercise against therapy and medication directly are in the Cochrane review, and they found little to no difference. The lesson reports the wording at each step and makes no judgement about anyone's intentions.

S2: Last thing. Someone on an antidepressant hears all this and thinks, I'll swap it for running.

S1: Nothing in the lesson is a reason to start, stop or swap a treatment. For more severe depression, group exercise comes last in NICE's table, which says the potential advantages of other treatment choices with more therapist contact should be carefully considered first. And if you take an antidepressant and are thinking of stopping it, NICE's advice is to talk with the person who prescribed it first, because it's usually necessary to reduce the dose in stages over time. Most people stop successfully, it says, but for some people withdrawal can sometimes be more difficult, with symptoms lasting weeks or occasionally months.

S2: We've left out the much thinner evidence on exercise for anxiety, the review's findings on harms and long-term follow-up, and how its best-trials answer changed since the earlier version. The full lesson is free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening.
