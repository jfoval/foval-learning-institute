## The first thing Stage 1 found, and it decides the course

**The two camps in the loudest argument in this subject agree on the number. They disagree about
what it means.**

Kirsch and colleagues (source 16a), the best-known critique of antidepressants, report from the FDA
trial data a drug-placebo difference of **d = 0.32** and call it below "the 0.50 standardized mean
difference criterion that NICE suggested". Cipriani and colleagues (source 16b), the network
meta-analysis usually quoted as the answer to Kirsch, report across 522 trials a summary
standardised mean difference of **0.30** for all antidepressants against placebo, and describe the
effects as "mostly modest". The measured size is almost the same. **One side reads 0.3 as too small to
matter clinically; the other reads it as a real, reliable effect of a size that is ordinary in
medicine.** A lesson that shows a reader those two numbers side by side has taught more about how
to read this field than any amount of summary.

**And almost every self-help method in this file lands in the same band.** Guided and unguided
internet CBT, smartphone apps, breathwork, mindfulness programmes against passive controls, resilience
training, stress-mindset interventions: the pooled effects run from about **g = 0.2 to about 0.6**
(sources 5 to 15). Exercise sits at the upper end on the whole literature and much lower in its
best-controlled trials (source 8). Expressive writing sits below the band (source 12). **So the
course's real subject is not "what works" but how to read a small-to-moderate effect**: against what
comparator, in whom, measured how, and graded how certain. The waiting-list finding (source 7) is
the key to that: the same therapy looks bigger or smaller depending on what it was compared with.

**The clinical guidelines are built on a stepped-care idea that is itself the course's second
lesson** (sources 1 and 2): the least intrusive effective option first, and a clear account of when
to step up. NICE's GAD guideline puts education and "active monitoring" at step 1 and self-help at
step 2; its depression guideline lists guided self-help first for less severe depression and says
antidepressants should not be routinely offered first-line there. That structure gives the course
its honest shape: **a reader with ordinary stress is at step 0, a reader with a disorder is at step 1
or beyond, and the difference is a clinician's call, not a course's.**

**The course is a sensitive domain under standards 3.4** (psychology and medicine), and
`sensitive_domain: true` is already set. The neutrality audit is mandatory. The specific risks are
three: that a lesson reads as a diagnosis ("if you have these five symptoms you have GAD"); that a
lesson reads as medication advice in either direction (the antidepressant and serotonin disputes are
exactly where a reader will be tempted to stop a drug); and that a self-help lesson reads as a
substitute for care to someone who needs care. **The refusal that ends the course must be that it
cannot tell a reader whether what they have is ordinary or clinical**, and the evidence for that
refusal is in the guidelines themselves, which require "a comprehensive assessment that does not
rely solely on the number, severity and duration of symptoms" (source 1).

## 1. NICE CG113: generalised anxiety disorder and panic disorder in adults

**National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, *Generalised anxiety disorder and panic disorder
in adults: management*, clinical guideline CG113, published 26 January 2011, last updated 15 June
2020.** https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg113 . **Read on 2026-09-23: the Recommendations chapter
in full from 1.1.1 to 1.3.41 (and the page, which ends at 1.3.45, searched in full for the terms §19a
lists; corrected at lesson 4's review), and the Appendix "Assessing generalised anxiety disorder" in full.**
Not read: the remaining panic-disorder monitoring recommendations after 1.3.41, the Context chapter,
the Recommendations for research, and the full guideline's evidence chapters. Each recommendation on
the page carries the year it was written or amended in square brackets, and that tag must travel
with any quotation (for example "[2011]", "[2004]").

**How NICE grades a recommendation.** NICE does not print GRADE letters on these recommendations. It
grades by verb. From the NICE page "Making decisions using NICE guidelines", read in full on the same
day: "Some recommendations are made with more certainty than others. We word our recommendations to
reflect this. Where there is clear and strong evidence of benefit, we will use the word 'offer'.
Where the benefit is less certain we use the word 'consider'." **A lesson that reports a NICE
recommendation must keep its verb**, because the verb is the grade.

**The definition of GAD NICE uses, verbatim from the Appendix:**
- "The assessment of generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) is based on the criteria in DSM‑IV."
- "The key symptoms of GAD are: excessive anxiety and worry about a number of events or activities;
  difficulty controlling the worry." (The page sets these as a two-item list; the semicolon is the
  list break.)
- "The worry should occur on a majority of days for at least 6 months."
- "If the 2 key symptoms are present, ask about the following associated symptoms: restlessness,
  being easily fatigued, difficulty concentrating, irritability, muscle tension, disturbed sleep."
  (Again a list on the page.)
- Factors favouring step 1 only include "few symptoms of GAD or symptoms that are intermittent or of
  less than 6 months' duration (hence subclinical)" and "only mild distress and no or limited
  functional impairment".
- Factors favouring specialist referral (step 4) include "very severe functional impairment (such as
  self-neglect)" and "persistent suicidal thoughts".

**The stepped-care model, verbatim from 1.2.1 and its table:**
- 1.2.1: "Follow the stepped-care model, offering the least intrusive, most effective intervention
  first. [2011]"
- Step 1: "All known and suspected presentations of GAD", with "Identification and assessment;
  education about GAD and treatment options; active monitoring".
- Step 2: "Diagnosed GAD that has not improved after education and active monitoring in primary care",
  with "Low-intensity psychological interventions: individual non-facilitated self-help, individual
  guided self-help and psychoeducational groups".
- Step 3: "GAD with an inadequate response to step 2 interventions or marked functional impairment",
  with "Choice of a high-intensity psychological intervention (cognitive behavioural therapy
  [CBT]/applied relaxation) or a drug treatment".
- Step 4: "Complex treatment-refractory generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) and very marked functional
  impairment, such as self-neglect or a high risk of self-harm".

**Recommendations the course will lean on, verbatim:**
- 1.2.2 (identification): "Consider asking the person about their feelings of anxiety and their
  ability to stop or control worry, using the 2-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-2).
  [2011]"
- 1.2.6: "For people who may have GAD, conduct a comprehensive assessment that does not rely solely on
  the number, severity and duration of symptoms, but also considers the degree of distress and
  functional impairment. [2011]"
- 1.2.10, on why step 1 exists: "This is because education and active monitoring may improve less
  severe presentations and avoid the need for further interventions. [2011]"
- 1.2.13, what non-facilitated self-help must be: "be based on the treatment principles of cognitive
  behavioural therapy (CBT)"; "include instructions for the person to work systematically through the
  materials over a period of at least 6 weeks". **That is the guideline's own specification of a
  self-help book, and it is a good yardstick for a reader holding one.**
- 1.2.17, the choice at step 3: "Base the choice of treatment on the person's preference as there is
  no evidence that either mode of treatment (individual high-intensity psychological intervention or
  drug treatment) is better. [2011]"
- 1.2.26: "Do not offer a benzodiazepine for the treatment of GAD in primary or secondary care except
  as a short-term measure during crises."
- 1.3.9, panic disorder: "For people with mild to moderate panic disorder, offer or refer for 1 of the
  following low-intensity interventions: individual non-facilitated self-help; individual facilitated
  self-help. [2011]" (a list on the page).
- 1.3.11: "The benefits of exercise as part of good general health should be discussed with all
  people with panic disorder as appropriate. [2004]" **Note the weakness of that sentence**: it is a
  2004 recommendation to discuss exercise as general health, not a recommendation of exercise as a
  treatment for panic.
- On panic attacks in A&E, **the full sentence** (corrected 2026-09-23 at lesson 1's review; the
  file had cut it before its second half): "It is important to remember that a panic attack does not
  necessarily constitute a panic disorder and appropriate treatment of a panic attack may limit the
  development of panic disorder." **That sentence is the whole of lesson 1's distinction in one line,
  from the guideline itself.**

**What a lesson must not do with this source.** It must not reproduce the drug recommendations
(1.2.23 to 1.2.36, 1.3.20 to 1.3.34) beyond saying that drug treatment exists at step 3 and that the
choice is a matter for the person and a clinician. No drug names in a lesson beyond what the
contested-questions lesson needs, and no doses anywhere.

## 2. NICE NG222: depression in adults

**National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, *Depression in adults: treatment and
management*, NICE guideline NG222, published 29 June 2022.** https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng222 .
**Read on 2026-09-23: the "Definitions of depression and severity" preamble in full; sections 1.1
and 1.2 in full; 1.3.1 in part; 1.4.1 to 1.4.21 (delivery of treatments, starting and stopping
antidepressants) in full; 1.4.38 to 1.4.41 (St John's Wort, light therapy, activities to help
wellbeing) in full; 1.5 in full including Table 1; 1.6.1 and Table 2's row order and the guided
self-help and group exercise rows.** Not read: 1.4.22 onward except as named, 1.6 beyond Table 2,
1.7 to 1.16 (section headings only), the rationale-and-impact sections, the visual summaries, and
the evidence reviews.

**The definition and the severity split, verbatim:**
- "Depression refers to a wide range of mental health problems characterised by the absence of a
  positive affect (a loss of interest and enjoyment in ordinary things and experiences), low mood and
  a range of associated emotional, cognitive, physical and behavioural symptoms."
- "Depression severity exists along a continuum and is essentially composed of 3 elements: symptoms
  (which may vary in frequency and intensity); duration of the disorder; the impact on personal and
  social functioning." (A list on the page.)
- "Less severe depression encompasses subthreshold and mild depression, and more severe depression
  encompasses moderate and severe depression. Thresholds on validated scales were used in this
  guideline as an indicator of severity. For example, a score 16 on the PHQ-9 scale was used, with
  scores less than 16 defined as less severe depression, and scores of 16 or more defined as more
  severe depression." (The page prints "a score 16", without "of"; keep it or mark the change.)

**The two identification questions, verbatim from 1.2.1:** "During the last month, have they often
been bothered by feeling down, depressed or hopeless? During the last month, have they often been
bothered by having little interest or pleasure in doing things?" **A lesson may show these as what a
clinician is told to ask. It must not turn them into a self-test with a verdict**; 1.2.2 and 1.2.3 say
a "yes" leads to a mental health assessment by someone competent to do it, which is the course's
exact point.

**Assessment, verbatim (1.2.6 and 1.2.7, added at lesson 1's review):** "Conduct a comprehensive
assessment that does not rely simply on a symptom count when assessing a person who may have
depression, but also takes into account severity of symptoms, previous history, duration and course of
illness." And, among the factors 1.2.7 lists: "any history of mood elevation (to determine if the
depression may be part of bipolar disorder)". **And 1.16.1**, read on the same day: "Commissioners and
providers of mental health services should consider using models such as stepped care or matched care
for organising the delivery of care and treatment of people with depression." So NG222 frames stepped
care as one model alongside matched care; CG113 is the guideline that is built on steps.

**Risk, verbatim:**
- 1.2.8: "Always ask people with depression directly about suicidal ideation and intent. [2009]"
- 1.2.9: "If a person with depression presents considerable immediate risk to themselves or others,
  refer them urgently to specialist mental health services. [2009]"

**First-line treatment for less severe depression, verbatim:**
- 1.5.2: "take into account that all treatments in table 1 can be used as first-line treatments, but
  consider the least intrusive and least resource intensive treatment first (guided self-help)".
- 1.5.3: "Do not routinely offer antidepressant medication as first-line treatment for less severe
  depression. Only offer it if that is the person's informed preference. [2022]"
- Table 1 is headed "Treatment options for less severe depression in order of the committee's
  interpretation of their clinical and cost effectiveness and consideration of implementation
  factors", and its rows run: guided self-help; group CBT; group behavioural activation; individual
  CBT; individual BA; group exercise; group mindfulness and meditation; interpersonal psychotherapy;
  SSRIs; counselling; short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy. **Read the heading: the order folds in
  cost and implementation, so it is not a ranking by effect size**, and a lesson must not present it
  as one.
- Guided self-help, as the table defines it: "Printed or digital materials that follow the principles
  of guided self-help including structured cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), structured
  behavioural activation (BA), problem-solving or psychoeducation materials." With "Support from a
  trained practitioner who facilitates the self-help intervention, encourages completion and reviews
  progress and outcomes." and "Usually consists of 6 to 8 structured regular sessions."
- Group exercise: "Uses a physical activity programme specifically designed for people with
  depression." "Usually consists of more than 1 session per week for 10 weeks." "Includes moderate
  intensity aerobic exercise."
- Group mindfulness: "Uses a programme such as mindfulness-based cognitive therapy specifically
  designed for people with depression." and, in the same row, "May be difficult for people
  experiencing intense or highly distressing thoughts, or who find focusing on the body difficult."

**For more severe depression**, Table 2's first row is "Combination of individual cognitive
behavioural therapy (CBT) and an antidepressant", followed by individual CBT, individual BA,
antidepressant medication, individual problem-solving, counselling, STPP, IPT, guided self-help and
group exercise. The guided self-help and group exercise rows both add: "In more severe depression,
the potential advantages of providing other treatment choices with more therapist contact should be
carefully considered first." **Self-help moves from first to near last as severity rises. That is the
stepped-care logic in one table and a lesson should show it.**

**The general wellbeing advice, and its grade.** 1.4.40: "Advise people that doing any form of
physical activity on a regular basis (for example, walking, jogging, swimming, dance, gardening) could
help enhance their sense of wellbeing. The benefits can be greater if this activity is outdoors.
[2022]" 1.4.41: "Advise people that maintaining a healthy lifestyle (for example, eating a healthy
diet, not over-using alcohol, getting enough sleep) may help improve their sense of wellbeing." The
page says of both: "For a short explanation of why the committee made these consensus
recommendations". **They are consensus recommendations, not evidence-graded ones**, and the verbs
are "could" and "may". A lesson that quotes them must say so.

**Stopping antidepressants (1.4.12 to 1.4.21).** Quoted here so the course knows what it is not
entitled to say. 1.4.12: "Advise people taking antidepressant medication to talk with the person who
prescribed their medication (for example, their primary healthcare or mental health professional) if
they want to stop taking it. Explain that it is usually necessary to reduce the dose in stages over
time (called 'tapering') but that most people stop antidepressants successfully. [2022]" 1.4.14 (in
part): "withdrawal can sometimes be more difficult, with symptoms lasting longer (in some cases several
weeks, and occasionally several months)". **The course may quote 1.4.12 as the single sentence on this
subject, and nothing from 1.4.16 (the tapering method).**

**St John's Wort (1.4.38), verbatim:** "Although there is evidence that St John's Wort may be of
benefit in less severe depression, healthcare professionals should: ... not prescribe or advise its
use by people with depression because of uncertainty about appropriate doses, persistence of effect,
variation in the nature of preparations and potential serious interactions with other drugs
(including hormonal contraceptives, anticoagulants and anticonvulsants). [2009]" (The ellipsis stands
for a first bullet about advising people of potencies and interactions.) Useful if a lesson meets a
reader's "natural" remedy; it is the guideline declining to recommend something it concedes may help.

**A note on a withdrawn guideline.** NICE CG123, *Common mental health problems: identification and
pathways to care* (2011), is the document older sources cite for the stepped-care model across all
common disorders. Its page, read on 2026-09-23, says: "We withdrew this guideline in May 2024, because
all of the recommendations are now covered in other NICE guidelines, or are out of date and no longer
relevant to clinical practice." **Do not cite CG123 for anything current.**

## 3. WHO fact sheets: depression and anxiety disorders

**World Health Organization, "Depressive disorder (depression)" fact sheet, and "Anxiety disorders"
fact sheet**, https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/depression and
https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/anxiety-disorders . **Both read in full on
2026-09-23.** The page metadata gives 11 September 2026 (depression) and 15 September 2026 (anxiety)
as publication dates; both cite the 2023 Global Burden of Disease database for prevalence. WHO pages
are revised without notice, so **a lesson should give the date read**.

**Depression, verbatim:**
- "It is different from regular mood changes and feelings about everyday life It involves a depressed
  mood or loss of pleasure or interest in activities for long periods of time." (The missing full
  stop after "life" is WHO's.)
- "A depressive episode is different from regular mood fluctuations. They last most of the day, nearly
  every day, for at least two weeks."
- "In 2023, an estimated 4% of the population experienced depression, including 5.2% of adults (4.1%
  among men and 6.2% among women), and 5.4% of adults aged 70 years and older."
- "In high-income countries, only about one third of people with depression receive mental health
  treatment"
- "Psychological treatments are the first treatments for depression. They can be combined with
  antidepressant medicines in moderate and severe depression. Antidepressant medicines are not needed
  for mild depression."
- "Psychological treatments may be accessed through self-help manuals, websites and apps."
- "If you think you are in immediate danger of harming yourself, contact any available emergency
  services or a crisis line."

**Anxiety, verbatim:**
- "Everyone can feel anxious sometimes, but people with anxiety disorders often experience fear and
  worry that is both intense and excessive."
- "They are difficult to control, cause significant distress and can last a long time if untreated."
- "An estimated 5.8% of the global population currently experience an anxiety disorder (1). In 2023,
  470 million people in the world had an anxiety disorder, making anxiety disorders the most common of
  all mental disorders (1)."
- "only about 1 in 4 people in need (27.6%) receive any treatment"
- "The psychological interventions with the most evidence for treating a range of anxiety disorders
  are those based on principles of cognitive-behavioural therapy. These include exposure therapy,
  during which people learn to face their fears."
- "Benzodiazepines, which have historically been prescribed for anxiety disorders, are generally not
  recommended for anxiety disorders because of their high potential for dependence as well as their
  limited long-term effectiveness."

**What these are good for.** The two opening sentences are the clearest plain-language statements of
the ordinary-versus-clinical line anywhere in this file, and they come from the body with the widest
international standing. **The prevalence figures are modelled estimates from GBD**, not survey counts,
and a lesson should say "an estimated" as WHO does. The fact sheets' "Self-care" lists (exercise,
regular sleep, cutting alcohol, relaxation, mindfulness) are **advice without an evidence grade**, and
the course should treat them as what WHO recommends, then show the reader what the trials behind each
item actually found.

## 5. What CBT does for depression, at its largest

**Cuijpers P, Miguel C, Harrer M, Plessen CY, Ciharova M, Ebert D, Karyotaki E, "Cognitive behavior
therapy vs. control conditions, other psychotherapies, pharmacotherapies and combined treatment for
depression: a comprehensive meta-analysis including 409 trials with 52,702 patients", *World
Psychiatry* 22(1), 2023, pp. 105 to 115, doi 10.1002/wps.21069, PMC9840507.** **Read on 2026-09-23:
the abstract, from Europe PMC. The full text was not opened** (the Europe PMC full-text service
returned an error). One sentence of the abstract was damaged in retrieval (a "<" sign in it) and is
not quoted.

- Scale, verbatim: "We included 409 trials (518 comparisons) with 52,702 patients, thus conducting the
  largest meta-analysis ever of a specific type of psychotherapy for a mental disorder."
- Against control conditions, verbatim: "CBT had moderate to large effects compared to control
  conditions such as care as usual and waitlist (g=0.79; 95% CI: 0.70-0.89), which remained similar in
  sensitivity analyses and were still significant at 6-12 month follow-up."
- Against drugs, verbatim: "The effects of CBT did not differ significantly from those of
  pharmacotherapies at the short term, but were significantly larger at 6-12 month follow-up (g=0.34;
  95% CI: 0.09-0.58), although the number of trials was small, and the difference was not significant
  in all sensitivity analyses."
- As self-help, verbatim: "CBT was also effective as unguided self-help intervention (g=0.45; 95% CI:
  0.31-0.60)".
- Against other therapies, verbatim: "CBT was significantly more effective than other psychotherapies,
  but the difference was small (g=0.06; 95% CI: 0-0.12) and became non-significant in most sensitivity
  analyses."

**The trap in this source.** The headline 0.79 is against control conditions that include waitlist,
and source 7 shows that waitlist flatters whatever it is compared with. **A lesson that sets CBT's
0.79 beside an antidepressant's 0.30 has compared a therapy against a waiting list with a drug against
a placebo pill**, and must not do it. The fair comparison in this abstract is the head-to-head one:

  95% CI -0.35 to 0.23; 5 studies, 231 participants; very-low certainty evidence)".
- Verbatim, conclusions: "The findings suggest positive effects of resilience training for healthcare
  professionals, but the evidence is very uncertain."

**Why these three together.** Bonanno and Galatzer-Levy say resilience is the **common** outcome after
adversity, about two in three in the pooled trajectory studies. Kunzler says training it has been
tested, in one occupational group, with post-test effects of **very low certainty**. **A reader who
has been sold "resilience" as a rare trait to be built by a programme has been sold two things the
evidence does not support**: that it is rare, and that the programmes are known to build it. That is
the lesson. Standards 3.1: the base rate is an established finding with a stated method; trainability
is contested and the best review says "very uncertain". Two further Cochrane resilience reviews exist
(healthcare students, CD013684; military and emergency personnel, CD013242) and were not opened.

## 14. The positivity ratio: a finding that was taken apart in public

**14a. Fredrickson BL, Losada MF, "Positive affect and the complex dynamics of human flourishing",
*American Psychologist* 60(7), 2005, pp. 678 to 686, doi 10.1037/0003-066X.60.7.678, PMC3126111.**
**Read: abstract, Europe PMC, 2026-09-23.** Verbatim: "the authors predict that a ratio of positive to
negative affect at or above 2.9 will characterize individuals in flourishing mental health.
Participants (N=188) completed an initial survey to identify flourishing mental health and then
provided daily reports of experienced positive and negative emotions over 28 days." And: "these
findings suggest that a set of general mathematical principles may describe the relations between
positive affect and human flourishing."

**14b. Brown NJ, Sokal AD, Friedman HL, "The complex dynamics of wishful thinking: the critical
positivity ratio", *American Psychologist* 68(9), 2013, pp. 801 to 813, doi 10.1037/a0032850.**
**Read: abstract, Europe PMC, 2026-09-23.** Verbatim: "We find no theoretical or empirical
justification for the use of differential equations drawn from fluid dynamics, a subfield of physics,
to describe changes in human emotions over time; furthermore, we demonstrate that the purported
application of these equations contains numerous fundamental conceptual and mathematical errors."
And: "Fredrickson and Losada's claim to have demonstrated the existence of a critical minimum
positivity ratio of 2.9013 is entirely unfounded."

**14c. Fredrickson BL, "Updated thinking on positivity ratios", *American Psychologist* 68(9), 2013,
pp. 814 to 822, doi 10.1037/a0033584.** **Read: abstract, Europe PMC, 2026-09-23.** Verbatim: "This
article presents my response to the article by Brown, Sokal, and Friedman (2013), which critically
examined Losada's conceptual and mathematical work". And, her position in her own words: "Even when
scrubbed of Losada's now-questioned mathematical modeling, ample evidence continues to support the
conclusion that, within bounds, higher positivity ratios are predictive of flourishing mental health
and other beneficial outcomes."

**14d. The same Brown, Sokal and Friedman paper, as the authors' preprint: arXiv 1307.7006v1, posted
26 July 2013, https://arxiv.org/abs/1307.7006.** **Read on 2026-09-24, for lesson 07 under defect 26:
the introduction's first two paragraphs, the section on how 2.9013 was derived (its closing
paragraphs), "Fredrickson and Losada’s empirical study", "An upper critical positivity ratio?", the
Conclusion and the Concluding remarks.** The rest (the tutorial on differential equations and the
examination of Losada 1999 and Losada and Heaphy 2004) was not read closely. **This is the preprint;
the published text in *American Psychologist* was not opened and may differ in wording**, so a lesson
quoting these passages says it is quoting the preprint.
- On the construct itself: "On its own, the positivity ratio as propounded by Fredrickson and Losada
  (2005) is not a particularly controversial construct; indeed, there is a long history of looking at
  ratios (e.g., Bales, 1950) and non-ratio indices (e.g., Bradburn, 1969) relating positive to negative
  emotions."
- The empirical result as they report it: "among a group of college students, those who were
  “languishing” had an average positivity ratio of 2.3, while those who were “flourishing” had an
  average positivity ratio of 3.2."
- On where the number came from: "the values of σ, b, and (especially) i plugged into Equation 6 are
  totally arbitrary, at least within wide limits; so the predicted critical positivity ratio is
  totally arbitrary as well". And: "Recall that Saltzman (1962) chose σ = 10 for illustrative purposes
  and purely for convenience; then Lorenz (1963) and Losada (1999) followed him." With σ = 16 they
  report "the predicted critical positivity ratio (with i = 16) is (P/N)crit = 1233/296 = 4.1655405."
- On the empirical study: "there is nothing inherently implausible about the idea that people with a
  higher ratio of positive to negative emotions might experience better outcomes than those with a
  lower ratio. But the suggestion that people with a positivity ratio of 2.91 are in some discontinuous
  way significantly better off than those with a ratio of 2.90, simply because this number has crossed
  some magic line, is not supported by any evidence." And: "Fredrickson and Losada’s (2005) study of
  “flourishing” versus “languishing” college students does at least provide some empirical evidence
  that a higher positivity ratio typically corresponds to better outcomes than a lower one."
- **Their own statement of scope, which matters for fairness:** "We do not here call into question the
  idea that positive emotions are more likely to build resilience than negative emotions, or that a
  higher positivity ratio is ordinarily more desirable than a lower one. But to suggest that some form
  of discontinuity sets in at some special value of the positivity ratio — especially one that is
  independent of all demographic and cultural factors — seems far-fetched. We cannot, of course, prove
  that no such “tipping point” exists".
- On motive: "Let us stress that our concern here is with the objective properties of published texts,
  not the subjective states of mind of the authors".

**What 14d changes.** The critics and Fredrickson's reply are closer than 14b and 14c alone suggest:
**the critics say in terms that they do not dispute the weaker claim Fredrickson still holds.** The
dispute is about the tipping point and the mathematics, not about whether positive emotion goes with
flourishing. A lesson should say so, in the critics' words, which also keeps it from reading as a
debunking of positive psychology. Note that the curly quotation marks and the em dash above are the
preprint's own.

**What the course may say.** The mathematical claim for a critical tipping point of 2.9013 was shown to
be unfounded, and the first author accepted that the modelling was "now-questioned" and withdrew from
it. **Her remaining claim, that more positive emotion relative to negative predicts flourishing
"within bounds", is a separate and much weaker claim, and she still holds it.** Both must be stated,
each in its author's words, and the lesson must not imply the whole field of positive emotion research
fell with the ratio. **The journal's correction notice was not found** (Europe PMC returned nothing and
the APA site blocks automated access), so the course must not use the word "retracted" or describe the
correction's wording; "partially withdrawn" is also not verified here. Say what 14b and 14c say.

**15c. Bosshard M, Gomez P, "Effectiveness of stress arousal reappraisal and stress-is-enhancing

## 19. Anxiety specifically: the second Stage 1 pass

*Added 2026-09-23 at the start of Stage 2, because the open concern below said a lesson on worry had
CG113 and little else. Every abstract here was fetched from the Europe PMC REST service on
2026-09-23 and every quoted passage was copied from it. **All read at abstract level only unless the

**What the pass found, in one paragraph.** The anxiety literature is not thin; the file was. Self-help
for anxiety disorders has a Cochrane review, three further meta-analyses and a Cochrane review of
therapist-supported internet CBT, and they agree on a shape: **moderate-to-large effects against
waiting lists and no treatment, a small deficit or none against face-to-face therapy, and
low-to-moderate certainty, very low on guided against unguided**. *(Corrected at lesson 3's review:
this said "a small but real deficit"; Pauley, Olthuis and Mayo-Wilson's own response result found no

techniques a reader is most often handed for worry**: worry postponement has small individual trials
that disagree with each other and no meta-analysis this pass found, and exercise for anxiety
disorders rests on small pooled samples with no adult Cochrane review found. And CG113 itself makes
**no recommendation of exercise, mindfulness or any single technique for GAD**; its step 2 is
structured CBT-based self-help or a psychoeducational group, and nothing else.

### 19f. Worry postponement and stimulus control: a technique, tested alone

**This is the part of the anxiety literature that stays thin after this pass**, and it is the part a
reader is likeliest to be handed as a tip: set aside a fixed time of day for worrying. The pass found individual
trials and no meta-analysis. They are listed here so that a lesson can show a technique being tested
and not always passing.

**Borkovec TD, Wilkinson L, Folensbee R, Lerman C, "Stimulus control applications to the treatment of

10.1016/0005-7967(83)90206-1.** **Not read: Europe PMC holds no abstract.** It is the paper later

**McGowan SK, Behar E, "A preliminary investigation of stimulus control training for worry: effects on

- Verbatim, the authors' own framing: "Results provide preliminary support for the use of SC training

  **In larger treatment packages**: the authors do not propose it as a stand-alone.

### 19h. Mindfulness against a drug, for anxiety disorders

**Hoge EA, Bui E, Mete M, Dutton MA, Baker AW, Simon NM, "Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction vs

abstract in full.**
- Design, verbatim: "This randomized clinical trial (Treatments for Anxiety: Meditation and Escitalopram
  [TAME]) included a noninferiority design with a prespecified noninferiority margin." And: "The outcome
  assessments were performed by blinded clinical interviewer".
- Sample, verbatim: "276 adults with a diagnosed anxiety disorder from 3 urban academic medical centers
  in the US were recruited for the trial, and 208 completed the trial."
- Result, verbatim: "The difference between groups was -0.07 (0.16; 95% CI, -0.38 to 0.23; P = .65),

- Harms, verbatim: "At least 1 study-related adverse event occurred for 110 participants randomized to

lesson can set them side by side, and the Turing test runs both ways: the trial's authors would say
their design answered the objection, and a Van Dam-style critic would say one noninferiority trial
without a placebo arm is where a claim of comparability starts, not where it ends. **The drug's name
appears in the trial's title; the course's decision on naming drugs is in the outline.** No dose from
this abstract may appear anywhere in the course.

## What the field considers essential

1. **Ordinary stress, worry and low mood are not disorders, and the line is drawn by duration,
   distress and function, not by the presence of a symptom.** WHO's two opening sentences, CG113's
   6-month worry criterion and "does not rely solely on the number, severity and duration of symptoms",
   NG222's three elements of severity (sources 1 to 3). A panic attack is not panic disorder (source 1).
2. **Care is stepped: least intrusive first, and a clear rule for when to step up.** CG113's four steps;
   NG222's two tables, where self-help leads for less severe depression and falls down the list for more
   severe (sources 1 and 2).
3. **The first-line psychological methods are CBT-based, and self-help versions of them work modestly**,
   better with a guide, and least well relative to guidance in people with more severe symptoms
   (sources 5 and 6).
4. **How big an effect looks depends on what it was compared with.** Waitlist inflates; active controls
   shrink; the best-controlled trials of exercise show a fraction of the headline (sources 7 and 8).
5. **Exercise, mindfulness programmes, behavioural activation and breathwork each have randomised
   evidence of small-to-moderate effects, at low to moderate certainty**, and none has been shown better
   than other active treatments in head-to-head trials (sources 8 to 11).
6. **Resilience is the common response to adversity, not the rare one**, and whether programmes build it
   is very uncertain (source 13).
7. **Antidepressants beat placebo by a modest average margin (about SMD 0.3) that both critics and
   defenders accept**, and what that margin means is contested (source 16). **The serotonin
   hypothesis in its simple form is disputed; a role for serotonin is defended**, and neither is a
   reason for any reader to change a medication (source 17).
8. **When to stop reading and get help** is part of the content, not a disclaimer: the crisis lines, the
   emergency numbers, and the self-referral route (source 4).

## Common misconceptions

- **"Anxiety is bad; the goal is not to feel it."** WHO: "Everyone can feel anxious sometimes". The
  disorder is anxiety that is "intense and excessive", "difficult to control" and causes "significant
  distress" (source 3).
- **"I had a panic attack, so I have panic disorder."** CG113: "a panic attack does not necessarily
  constitute a panic disorder" (source 1).
- **"If I tick enough symptoms on a checklist, I have depression."** NG222 1.2.6 tells clinicians to
  make "a comprehensive assessment that does not rely simply on a symptom count" (source 2).
- **"Exercise is 1.5 times more effective than therapy or medication."** A press release's ratio from a
  paper that said "comparable to or slightly greater than" and asked for head-to-head research. The
  head-to-head Cochrane comparison with therapy: "probably little to no difference" (sources 8b, 8e).
- **"Exercise is proven to treat depression."** The whole-literature effect is moderate; in the
  best-controlled seven trials it is smaller, and the certainty is low (source 8b).
- **"Mindfulness works as well as antidepressants."** Van Dam: "misleadingly described as
  'comparable'"; Goyal: no evidence meditation programmes beat any active treatment (sources 9a, 9e).
  The real comparison, MBCT for relapse prevention, is narrower (source 9d).
- **"Meditation is harmless."** NCCIH, quoted in Van Dam: it "could cause or worsen certain psychiatric
  problems"; MBSR's own standards exclude suicidality and psychiatric disorder (source 9e).
- **"Resilient people are rare, and resilience is a skill you can train."** About two in three follow the
  resilience trajectory (source 13b); the training evidence is "very uncertain" (source 13c).
- **"You need three positive emotions for every negative one."** The 2.9013 tipping point was shown to be
  unfounded and its author set the modelling aside (source 14).
- **"Believing stress is harmful is what kills you."** The source is an observational association, and
  the mindset-only experiments on performance were not significant (source 15).
- **"Antidepressants are no better than placebo."** They are better on average, by a modest amount both
  sides measure (sources 16a, 16b). **"Antidepressants fix a chemical imbalance."** The simple serotonin
  story is not supported by the umbrella review, and the response defends a weaker claim (source 17).
- **"Guided self-help and a self-help book are the same thing."** NICE defines guided self-help with a
  trained practitioner and 6 to 8 sessions; the IPD evidence shows the guide matters more as symptoms
  rise (sources 2, 6b).

## Contested questions and the positions on each

**How much do antidepressants help, and for whom?**
*The effect is below clinical significance except in the most severe:* Kirsch and colleagues, d = 0.32
against a 0.50 criterion, "little evidence to support the prescription of antidepressant medication to
any but the most severely depressed patients, unless alternative treatments have failed" (16a). *The
effect is real and modest, and the drugs differ:* Cipriani and colleagues, "all antidepressants were
more efficacious than placebo", SMD 0.30, certainty "moderate to very low" (16b). *The average hides a
responder group:* Stone and colleagues, including Kirsch, about 15% with "a substantial antidepressant
effect beyond a placebo effect" (16c). **What would settle it:** a reliable way to predict who responds,
which 16c names as the need. **Classification under 3.1: the size of the average effect is established;
its clinical meaning is contested.** The guidelines' position (NG222 1.5.3, not first-line for less
severe depression; first in Table 2 in combination with CBT for more severe) should be reported as the
guidelines' position, not as the course's verdict.

**Is depression caused by low serotonin?**
*No consistent evidence; the theory is not empirically substantiated:* Moncrieff and colleagues (17a).
*The review is methodologically flawed and the serotonin system is implicated:* Jauhar and 34 co-authors
(17b). **The two are arguing at different strengths of claim**, which the lesson should show. **What
would settle it:** a conventional umbrella review extracting and re-meta-analysing primary studies, which
17b says would have "legitimately answered" the SERT question. **Classification: contested**, with the
simple low-serotonin story unsupported by the evidence both sides cite. **Neither side's position bears
on whether a given reader should take a drug**, and the lesson must say so.

**Is exercise as good as therapy or medication for depression?**
*Yes, possibly better:* Singh and colleagues' comparison of effect sizes across reviews, "comparable to
or slightly greater than" (8e); Heissel and colleagues, "should be offered as an evidence-based treatment
option" (8c); Noetel and colleagues, "could be considered alongside psychotherapy and antidepressants as
core treatments" (8d). *It is probably about the same, on small trials, and the effect over no treatment
is smaller in the best trials:* Cochrane 2026 (8b). **What would settle it:** larger head-to-head trials
with blinded outcome assessment, which Cochrane, Noetel and Singh ask for (Heissel does not). **Classification: contested
on size and comparison; the direction (exercise helps on average) is well supported.** NICE lists group
exercise as a first-line option for less severe depression (source 2), which is the establishment view.

**Is mindfulness overstated?**
*The effects are real and reliable in the setting studied:* Galante 2023, "Confidence in the primary
outcome result is high" for group-based teacher-led programmes against passive controls (9c); Kuyken on
relapse (9d). *The public claims outrun the evidence:* Van Dam and colleagues (9e), Goyal on active
controls (9a), Galante 2021's "do not support generalisation" (9b). **These are not really opposed
positions**, and the lesson should say so: both sides of the field agree on the effect and disagree
with the marketing.

**Can resilience be trained?**
*Possibly, in the short term:* Kunzler, positive post-test effects on resilience, depression and stress
(13c). *Unknown, at very low certainty, with no long-term data and no effect on anxiety:* the same review.
*The premise is wrong, because resilience is the common outcome:* Bonanno (13a, 13b). **Classification:
contested, with the base rate established.**

**Does changing your stress mindset change outcomes?**
*Yes, it improves performance modestly:* Bosshard and Gomez, d = 0.23 overall (15c); Crum and
colleagues on cortisol and feedback-seeking (15b). *The effect shrinks after correction for bias, and
mindset-only interventions were not significant:* the same meta-analysis, d = 0.14 after trim and fill,
SIE-only p = 0.22. *The famous mortality claim is an association:* Keller (15a). **Classification:
contested and small; the mortality version is an overreach of an observational finding.**

**Does the positivity ratio exist?**
*The tipping point does not:* Brown, Sokal and Friedman (14b), accepted in substance by Fredrickson
(14c). *Higher ratios still predict flourishing within bounds:* Fredrickson (14c). **Classification: the
2.9013 claim is refuted; the weaker claim is contested and this course has read nothing testing it.**

## Safety-critical guidance

**This course gives no clinical advice, and it says so at the start and wherever a lesson could be read as
advice.** Standards 2.5's "education, not personalised advice" note appears once per course; in this
course it should be in the first lesson and **the crisis resources should be on every lesson**, which is a
site decision the outline should raise (see Open concerns).

- **Red flags that mean a clinician or an emergency service, not a course**, each with its source:
  thoughts of suicide or self-harm, or not feeling able to keep yourself safe (NHS urgent page; NG222
  1.2.8 and 1.2.9; WHO); "persistent suicidal thoughts" and "very severe functional impairment (such as
  self-neglect)" (CG113 Appendix); symptoms that do not improve after self-help, or "marked functional
  impairment" (CG113 steps 2 to 3); a history of elevated mood, which NG222 1.2.7 flags as possibly bipolar
  disorder, where the treatment is different (WHO: "Different medicines and treatments are used for
  bipolar disorder."). **The course must not present any of these as a checklist a reader scores
  themselves on.** It names them as reasons to talk to someone, and gives the numbers.
- **Chest pain is a medical question first.** CG113's A&E section says people presenting with a panic
  attack should "undergo the minimum investigations necessary to exclude acute physical problems". A
  lesson on panic must not tell a reader that chest pain is probably anxiety.
- **Medication.** No doses, no drug-by-drug comparisons beyond what the contested-questions lesson needs
  to explain Cipriani's design, no advice to start, stop, reduce or switch anything. The one sentence the
  course may give on stopping is NG222 1.4.12: talk to the prescriber, it is "usually necessary to reduce
  the dose in stages over time". Any lesson on the antidepressant or serotonin disputes ends with that
  sentence.
- **Supplements.** NG222 declines to recommend St John's Wort because of interactions "including hormonal
  contraceptives, anticoagulants and anticonvulsants" (source 2); a lesson that mentions it must carry that.
- **Meditation.** Van Dam's contraindications paragraph (9e) and NG222's Table 1 note on intensely
  distressing thoughts (source 2): a lesson that encourages trying mindfulness must say it is not
  for everyone and that some people get worse.
- **Crisis numbers verified 2026-09-23.** 988 (US and Canada), 116 123 (UK and Ireland), 13 11 14
  (Australia), 999 / 911 / 000 for emergencies. A number that changes is a safety defect; the outline
  should put them in one place that a single edit can update.

> For individuals with generalized anxiety disorder, worry becomes associated with numerous aspects

> Objective: Various control conditions have been employed in psychotherapy trials, but there is
> growing suspicion that they may lead to different effect size estimates. The present study aims to

> Background: Worry exposure (WE) is a core element of cognitive-behavioral treatment for
