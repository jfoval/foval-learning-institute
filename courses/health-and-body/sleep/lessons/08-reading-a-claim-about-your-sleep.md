This is the last lesson, and it has two jobs and one refusal.

The first job is to give you two habits for the next claim about sleep you meet: the institute's
standing sort, which you've used before, and one question this course adds to it. The second job is
a decision about your own sleep, which you make and this course does not. And the refusal is the
reason the lesson exists at all, so it gets a section of its own.

## Habit one: sort the claim

The sort is three categories, and **Memory** lesson 7, **Note-Taking** lesson 8 and the other closing
lessons on the Core path all use it. Measurable: a study could check it. Unmeasurable as stated: it
uses a term nobody has pinned down. False: it has been checked and did not hold.

It is used here rather than re-taught. **The four claims below are specimens written for this lesson,
not quotations from anywhere**, and the sorting of each is this course's reasoning.[8]

**One.** "Adults need seven or more hours a night." Measurable, but notice what kind of sentence it
is before you sort it. It is a recommendation, and lesson 3 read [the statement it comes from](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.4716) with its verb: "Adults should
sleep 7 or more hours per night on a regular basis to promote optimal health."[2] A recommendation is
a panel's decision about a literature, and the literature under it is of associations. So the sort
is "measurable", and what was measured is not the sentence as the specimen states it, which drops
the panel's "should" and turns advice into a fact about your body.

**Two.** "Your body repairs itself while you sleep." Unmeasurable as stated. Repairs what, measured
how, compared with what? Nothing in the sentence says what would count against it. That does not
make it wrong. It makes it a framing, and a framing can't be checked until somebody turns it into a
claim with a quantity in it.

**Three.** "Everybody needs eight hours." False, as a statement of what the field says. The
consensus statement says seven or more, sets no upper bound for healthy adults, and says that
"Individual variability in sleep need is influenced by genetic, behavioral, medical, and
environmental factors."[2] Eight is a number people remember, not the one the panel printed.

**Four, and the commonest shape.** "Night owls die younger, so get to bed by ten." Two claims welded.
The first is measurable, and this course has read nothing on it: lesson 4 had chronotype and the
shift-work classification, and neither is a mortality figure for late sleepers. The second is advice
about your life, and it is also a claim about when, which the first half would have to have been
measured on. **Each half needs its own verdict**, and the first gets "not something this course can
tell you".

:::predict Before you read on. Take the fourth specimen and say which of the four instruments from lesson 1, or the fifth from lesson 4, would have had to measure "night owl" for the first half to mean anything.

Probably a questionnaire, the proxy lesson 4 described, since a hormone assay on the scale of a mortality study is unlikely. That is this course's guess, not something it read.

The point is that "night owl" is a timing word, and a timing word needs a timing instrument. If the study behind it used sleep duration, or asked people whether they felt like morning people, the claim changed quantity somewhere between the study and the sentence. That is the next habit.
:::

## Habit two: the clock question

Every Core course that ends like this one has added a question to the sort. **Memory** added the
sample: how many people, and who. **Focus and Deep Work** added the instrument. **Note-Taking** added
the setting. This course adds the clock question.

**Is this claim about how much sleep, or about when?**

Lesson 4 introduced it, and it earns its place because almost every claim you meet is about
duration, and a sentence about one licenses nothing about the other. They're different quantities,
measured with different instruments, with different gold standards: electrodes in a laboratory for
how much, a hormone assay under dim light for when.[1][5]

Then, once you know which quantity, the question that comes with it: **which instrument produced the
number?**

Here's the clock question applied to a sentence you might actually say.

"My watch says I've been getting six and a half hours, so I'm below the recommendation." It is about
how much, so far so good. Now the instrument. The watch is a consumer device, and lesson 1's
comparison found such devices had high sensitivity and low specificity: "epoch-by-epoch sensitivity
was high (all ≥0.93), specificity was low-to-medium (0.18–0.54)".[1] So they tend to count quiet
wakefulness as sleep, and the true figure is more likely lower than the watch says than higher, at
least on the healthy young adults tested.

And the recommendation's number rests on whatever instruments the cohorts behind lesson 3's
associations used, which this course did not read.[3] Nothing says they were watches. **Comparing a
watch's figure to that recommendation may be comparing two instruments**, and nobody has told you the
exchange rate between them. That reasoning is this course's.[8]

:::checkpoint A friend says: "Get bright light first thing in the morning and you'll sleep better." Run both habits on it.

Sort first. Measurable, in principle: a study could expose people to morning light and measure their sleep. This course read no such study.

Then the clock question. It is a claim about when, in its first half: light at a time. Lesson 4's light experiment showed light early in the biological night delays the clock and late in it advances it, "Light exposure occurring in the early biological night induces a phase delay shift of the circadian pacemaker, whereas light exposure occurring in the late biological night induces a phase advance shift."[5] So for somebody whose waking falls in their late biological night, morning light would move the clock earlier. Whether that is "better" depends on which way they want to move, and whether their morning light is bright enough to matter is something nothing here measured.

The second half, "you'll sleep better", has switched quantity. It could mean earlier, longer or more pleasantly, and it does not say. That is the sort telling you the second half is unmeasurable as stated.
:::

## The sources side by side

Every source this course built a figure on, with what it measured, on whom, how far this course read
it, which quantity it is about, and what it licenses. The first four columns are the sources'. The
last is this course's reading.[8]

| Source | On whom | Read here | How much or when | What it licenses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Device comparison[1] | 34 healthy young adults, 3 nights | Abstract in full | How much | That consumer devices find sleep well and wake poorly, worse on disrupted nights |
| Restriction experiment[4] | 48 healthy adults, 21 to 38, 14 nights | Abstract in full | How much | That 4 or 6 hours in bed cost performance cumulatively, largely unnoticed, over a fortnight |
| Consensus statement[2] | A panel, about a literature | Recommendation and four statements | How much | A population recommendation, stated in associational language |
| Mortality meta-analysis[3] | 1,382,999 people in 27 cohort samples | Abstract fragments | How much | Small associations for short sleep, larger for long, not causes |
| Light experiment[5] | 36 participants in a laboratory | Abstract in full | When | That one bright hour moves the clock, in a direction set by when it lands |
| Chronotype paper[6] | A validation study | Abstract and the measure | When | That a questionnaire approximates circadian timing, and a hormone assay measures it |
| Sleep and memory review[7] | 271 samples of lab learners | Accepted manuscript, in part | How much, and design | A moderate benefit to later recall, larger or smaller by design |
| Short-sleep gene release[9] | Families with rare mutations | A university news release | How much | That natural short sleep exists, is genetic in some cases, and is rare |

The guidelines in lesson 6 and the documents in lesson 7 are not in the table, because they are not
measurements. The guidelines are judgements about trials, and lesson 7's documents are arguments
about sentences.

Read the fourth column. **Six of the eight are about how much.** Two are about when. That is the
shape of the subject, not of this course's reading: duration is what people count.

And read the last column against the reputation of each source. In every row the licence is
narrower than the claims people make with it, and in every row something real survives.

## The refusal

Here's the question most readers came to this course hoping it would answer: **how much sleep do I
need?**

This course can't tell you. That is not modesty, and it is not a way of avoiding the subject. It is the
honest reading of three facts that sit side by side.

**The population recommendation is seven or more hours.** "Adults should sleep 7 or more hours per
night on a regular basis to promote optimal health."[2] It is a recommendation for a population, built
on associations, from the field's peak bodies.

**A real minority need less, and it is rare.** A university's release on work by Ying-Hui Fu, Louis
Ptáček and colleagues reports that "people who had inherited a particular mutation in a gene called
*DEC2* averaged only 6.25 hours of sleep per night; study participants lacking the mutation averaged
8.06 hours", and that "this mutation is rare, so while it helped explain some natural short
sleepers, it couldn't account for all of them."[9] **This course read the release, not the papers.**

**And feeling fine does not tell you which group you're in.** The people held to six hours in lesson 2
were, in the authors' words, "largely unaware of these increasing cognitive deficits".[4] "I feel
fine on six" is what somebody who needs less would say, and it is also what the restricted
participants in that experiment would have said while their performance fell for a fortnight.

:::checkpoint Put those three together. What personal number of hours do they give you?

None, and it is worth seeing exactly why.

The recommendation is a number for a population, and it says in the same statement that need varies between people. The short sleepers show that some of that variation is real, and large, and rare. And the one thing a person could use to place themselves, how they feel, is the thing the experiment showed does not track what sleep loss costs.

So the three facts together rule out two easy answers. They rule out "everybody needs seven or more", because some people demonstrably don't. And they rule out "I feel fine so I'm one of them", because feeling fine is what both groups report. What's left is not a number. This course's view is that it is more honest to leave you there than to give you one, in either direction.[8]
:::

That refusal cuts both ways, and both matter. It will not give you the reassuring number, six is fine
for you. And it will not give you the alarming one either, that you're damaging yourself below seven.
Neither is something this course can know about one reader.

## Three things people get wrong

**"Somebody must have checked."** Somebody checked the sentences in lesson 7's book, and this course
read none of its sources in full. The read-level column above is the
honest answer to "has anybody checked?", and for most sleep claims you meet, nobody has told you.

**"The science says eight hours."** The consensus statement says seven or more, sets no upper limit
for healthy adults, and says need varies. Eight is folklore with a round number.

**"If I feel fine, I am fine."** The restricted sleepers in lesson 2 felt close to fine while their
measured performance fell for two weeks. How you feel is worth knowing and it is a different
instrument from how you perform.

## Practice

:::exercise Trace one
Take 20 minutes, and stop at twenty whether or not the trail has ended.

Find one claim about sleep with a number in it: an app notification, a headline, a mattress
advertisement, something a colleague told you.

Write it down word for word, with where you found it. Then follow it back.

1. **What does this source cite?** If nothing, write "nothing", and you're finished.
2. **Open what it cites. Is the number there?** Not something like it.
3. **How much, or when?** And has the claim kept the same quantity as its source?
4. **Which instrument?** Electrodes, a wrist device, a questionnaire, a diary, a hormone assay.

Write your finding in one sentence. **Five endings, and all of them are results**, in the form
**Memory** lesson 7 used: the number is there with its instrument and sample; the number is there
stripped of them; the trail ends in nothing; the trail leads somewhere you can't get to; or twenty
minutes ran out.
:::

:::exercise The decision
Take 30 minutes. This is the last thing the course asks, and if you did the project, it is section 4.

Decide what, if anything, to do about your own sleep. **Three endings, and all three are
legitimate.** Write whichever you choose in four lines.

**One: change one thing.** Name it, and name which quantity it is about: how much, or when. Say what
it rests on, from this course, at its read level. And say what it costs you, in time, in evenings,
in something else you'll stop doing.

**Two: keep something deliberately.** Not by default. Say what you're keeping, why, and what you're
accepting by keeping it. Somebody who keeps a six-hour night deliberately, having read lesson 2, is
in a different position from somebody who never thought about it.

**Three: stop deciding about it.** A legitimate ending, and for some readers the right one: the
evidence covers populations and does not reach your particular nights, and worrying about sleep is not
free. Say what would make you pick the question up again.

Then one line, whichever you chose: **what would tell you it was the wrong call?**

This course does not recommend one of the three. And if what you've noticed about your own sleep
matches any of the reasons lesson 1 gave for seeing a doctor, that is a fourth ending, and it comes
before the other three.
:::
