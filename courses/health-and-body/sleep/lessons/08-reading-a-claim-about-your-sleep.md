This is the last lesson, and it has two jobs and one refusal.

The first job is to give you two habits for the next claim about sleep you meet: the institute's
standing sort, which you've used before, and one question this course adds to it. The second job is
a decision about your own sleep, which you make and this course does not. And the refusal is the
reason the lesson exists at all, so it gets a section of its own.

## Habit one: sort the claim

The sort is three categories, and **Memory** lesson 7, **Note-Taking** lesson 8 and the other closing
lessons on the Core path all use it. Measurable: a study could check it. Unmeasurable as stated: it
uses a term nobody has pinned down. False: it has been checked and did not hold.

It is used here, not re-taught. The four claims below are specimens written for this lesson, not
quotations from anywhere, and the sorting of each is this course's reasoning.[8]

**One.** "Adults need seven or more hours a night." Measurable, but notice what kind of sentence it
is before you sort it. It is a recommendation, and lesson 3 read [the statement it comes
from](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.4716) with its verb: "Adults should sleep 7 or more hours per
night on a regular basis to promote optimal health."[2] A recommendation is a panel's decision about
a literature, and the outcomes the statement lists are introduced with "is associated with". So the
sort is "measurable", and what was measured is not the sentence as the specimen states it, which
drops the panel's "should" and turns advice into a fact about your body.

**Two.** "Your body repairs itself while you sleep." Unmeasurable as stated. Repairs what, measured
how, compared with what? Nothing in the sentence says what would count against it. That does not make
it wrong. It makes it a framing, and a framing can't be checked until somebody turns it into a claim
with a quantity in it.

**Three.** "Everybody needs eight hours." False, as a statement of what the field says. The
consensus statement says seven or more, sets no upper bound for healthy adults, and says that
"Individual variability in sleep need is influenced by genetic, behavioral, medical, and
environmental factors."[2] Eight is a number people remember; the panel printed seven or more.

**Four.** "Night owls die younger, so get to bed by ten." Two claims welded. The first is measurable,
and this course has read nothing on it: lesson 4 had chronotype and the shift-work classification,
and neither is a mortality figure for late sleepers. The second is advice about your life, and it is
also a claim about when, which the first half would have to have been measured on. Each half needs
its own verdict, and the first gets "not something this course can tell you".

:::predict Before you read on. Take the fourth specimen and say which instrument, of the four in lesson 1 or the two lesson 4 added (the hormone assay and the questionnaire that approximates it), would have had to measure "night owl" for the first half to mean anything.

Probably the questionnaire, since a hormone assay on the scale of a mortality study seems unlikely. That is this course's guess, not something it read.

The point is that "night owl" is a timing word, and a timing word needs a timing instrument. If the study behind it measured sleep duration, the claim changed quantity somewhere between the study and the sentence. If it asked people whether they felt like morning people, the quantity is right and the instrument changed. That is the next habit.
:::

## Habit two: the clock question

Three earlier courses on the Core added a question to the sort. **Memory** added the sample: how
many people, and who. **Focus and Deep Work** added the instrument. **Note-Taking** added the
setting. This course adds the clock question.

**Is this claim about how much sleep, or about when?**

Lesson 4 introduced it. In this course's reading most of the claims people meet are about duration,
and a sentence about one licenses nothing about the other.[8] They're different quantities, measured
with different instruments and with different gold standards: electrodes in a laboratory for how
much, a hormone assay under dim light for when.[1][6]

Then, once you know which quantity, the question that comes with it: **which instrument produced the
number?**

Try it on a sentence you might actually say. "My watch says I've been getting six and a half hours,
so I'm below the recommendation." It is about how much, so far so good. Now the instrument. The watch
is a consumer device, and lesson 1's comparison found such devices had high sensitivity and low
specificity: "epoch-by-epoch sensitivity was high (all ≥0.93), specificity was low-to-medium
(0.18–0.54)".[1] So they tend to count quiet wakefulness as sleep, and on a night with a lot of
lying awake that pushes the total up. How far, and whether it outweighs the sleep they miss, the
abstract does not say. That reasoning is this course's.[8]

And the recommendation's number rests on whatever instruments the cohorts behind lesson 3's
associations used, which this course did not read.[3] Nothing says they were watches. Comparing a
watch's figure to that recommendation may be comparing two instruments, and nobody has told you the
exchange rate between them.[8]

:::checkpoint A friend says: "Get bright light first thing in the morning and you'll sleep better." Run both habits on it.

Sort first. Measurable, in principle: a study could expose people to morning light and measure their sleep. This course read no such study.

Then the clock question. It is a claim about when, in its first half: light at a time. Lesson 4's light experiment reported that "Light exposure occurring in the early biological night induces a phase delay shift of the circadian pacemaker, whereas light exposure occurring in the late biological night induces a phase advance shift."[5] So for somebody whose waking falls in their late biological night, morning light would move the clock earlier. Whether that is "better" depends on which way they want to move, and whether their morning light is bright enough to matter is something nothing here measured.

The second half, "you'll sleep better", has switched quantity. It could mean earlier, longer or more pleasantly, and it does not say. That is the sort telling you the second half is unmeasurable as stated.
:::

## The refusal

Here's the question most readers came to this course hoping it would answer: **how much sleep do I
need?**

This course can't tell you. That is not modesty, and it is not a way of avoiding the subject. It is the
honest reading of three facts that sit side by side.

**The population recommendation is seven or more hours.** "Adults should sleep 7 or more hours per
night on a regular basis to promote optimal health."[2] It is a recommendation for a population, from
the field's peak bodies, and its list of outcomes is stated as associations.

**Some people sleep much less by nature.** [A university's
release](https://www.ucsf.edu/news/2019/08/415261/archive-after-10-year-search-scientists-find-second-short-sleep-gene)
on work by Ying-Hui Fu, Louis Ptáček and colleagues reports that "people who had inherited a
particular mutation in a gene called *DEC2* averaged only 6.25 hours of sleep per night; study
participants lacking the mutation averaged 8.06 hours", and that "this mutation is rare, so while it
helped explain some natural short sleepers, it couldn't account for all of them."[9] So the release
calls that one mutation rare, and says it does not explain every natural short sleeper. This course
read the release, not the papers, and the release reports how long people slept, not what they need.

**And feeling fine does not tell you which group you're in.** In lesson 2's experiment, the people
held to six hours in bed, which means less than six hours asleep, grew sleepier at first and then
barely sleepier, while their performance kept falling for a fortnight. The authors take that to
suggest the participants "were largely unaware of these increasing cognitive deficits".[4] "I feel
fine on six" is what somebody who naturally sleeps less would say, and on that reading it is also
what a restricted participant might have said while their performance fell.

:::checkpoint Put those three together. What personal number of hours do they give you?

None, and it is worth seeing exactly why.

The recommendation is a number for a population, and it says in the same statement that need varies between people. The short sleepers show that some of that variation is real and, in some cases, genetic. And the obvious thing a person could use to place themselves, how they feel, is the thing the experiment suggests does not track what restricted sleep costs.

So the three facts together rule out two easy answers. They rule out "everybody needs seven or more", because the statement itself says need varies and, on the release's account, some people naturally sleep much less. And they rule out "I feel fine so I'm one of them", because feeling fine is what both groups might report. What's left is not a number. This course's view is that it is more honest to leave you there than to give you one, in either direction.[8]
:::

That refusal cuts both ways, and both matter. It won't give you the reassuring number, that six is
fine for you. And it won't give you the alarming one either, that you're damaging yourself below
seven. Neither is something this course can know about one reader.

## Three things people get wrong

**"Somebody must have checked."** Somebody checked one chapter of lesson 7's book, and he reports it
took him more than 130 hours. For a claim you meet, the question is whether you can see who checked and how
far they read, which is what the read-level column above is for.

**"The science says eight hours."** The consensus statement says seven or more, sets no upper limit
for healthy adults, and says need varies. Eight is a number people remember; the panel printed a
different one.

**"If I feel fine, I am fine."** The restricted sleepers in lesson 2 grew only a little sleepier after
the first days while their measured performance fell for two weeks. How you feel is worth knowing,
and it is a different instrument from how you perform.

## Practice

:::exercise Trace one
Take 20 minutes, and stop at twenty whether or not the trail has ended.

Find one claim about sleep with a number in it: an app notification, a headline, a mattress
advertisement, something a colleague told you.

Write it down word for word, with where you found it. Then follow it back.

1. **What does this source cite?** If nothing, write "nothing", and you're finished.
2. **Open what it cites. Is the number there?** Not something like it.
3. **How much, or when?** And has the claim kept the same quantity as its source?
4. **Which instrument?** Electrodes, a research wrist device, a consumer device, a questionnaire, a
   diary, a hormone assay.

Write your finding in one sentence. **Five endings, and all of them are results**, in the form Memory
lesson 7 took from the courses before it: the number is there with its instrument and sample; the
number is there stripped of them; the trail ends in nothing; the trail leads somewhere you can't get
to; or twenty minutes ran out.
:::

:::exercise The decision
Take 30 minutes. This is the last thing the course asks, and if you did the project, it is section 4.

Decide what, if anything, to do about your own sleep. **Three endings, and all three are
legitimate.** Write whichever you choose in four lines.

**One: change one thing.** Name it, and name which quantity it is about: how much, or when. Say what
it rests on, from this course, at its read level. And say what it costs you, in time, in evenings,
in something else you'll stop doing.

**Two: keep something deliberately.** Not by default. Say what you're keeping, why, and what you're
accepting by keeping it. Somebody who keeps a six-hour night deliberately, having read lesson 2, is
in a different position from somebody who never thought about it.

**Three: stop deciding about it.** A legitimate ending, and for some readers the right one: the
evidence covers populations and does not reach your particular nights, and, in this course's view,
worrying about sleep has costs of its own.[8] Say what would make you pick the question up again.

Then one line, whichever you chose: **what would tell you it was the wrong call?**

This course does not recommend one of the three. And if what you've noticed about your own sleep
matches any of the reasons lesson 1 gave for seeing a doctor, that is a fourth ending, and it comes
before the other three.
:::
