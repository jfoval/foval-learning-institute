If you've read one popular book about sleep, it is probably this one. Matthew Walker's *Why We Sleep*,
published in 2017, is the best-selling book on the subject, and you may well have met its claims
second-hand without knowing where they came from.

**This course has not read it.** That is deliberate. What this lesson has instead is two documents
about it, both free, both short enough to read in a sitting: a long critique of its first chapter by
a writer called Alexey Guzey, and Walker's own written reply to questions about the book, including
Guzey's.

That pair is unusual, and it is why the lesson exists. A public argument about a book usually ends in
two camps shouting past each other. Here, the author answered in writing, point by point, and
**conceded some of it**. So you can watch specific claims change shape on the way from a research
literature into a paragraph, and then watch some of them change back.

This lesson reaches no verdict about anybody. It is not a takedown and it is not a defence. It is about
three sentences.

Two things each side says about its own purpose are worth having first, because they explain

Guzey on his scope: "I hope that going through one full chapter, rather than cherry-picking stuff
from across the book, demonstrated the density of errors in the book."[2] He reports spending "more

that the book, which he quotes, calls itself "intended to serve as a scientifically accurate
intervention".[2]

Walker on his aim: "The aim of the book, Why We Sleep, is to provide the general public access to a
broad collection of sleep research." And on the corrections: "I very much appreciate being made aware
of any errors in the book requiring revision. Furthermore, and as an essential part of what I see to
be good scholarly conduct, I have made a full set of corrections to the book. These corrections have
now been published in a second edition, which is now in print."[3] **This course has not seen the
second edition** and can't say what it changed.

## The first sentence: who said it

Here's the book's sentence, as Guzey quotes it from page 4: "[T]he World Health Organization (WHO)
has now declared a sleep loss epidemic throughout industrialized nations."[2]

Guzey's verdict: "This is false. The WHO never declared a sleep loss epidemic throughout
industrialized nations." He reports that the book's footnote for that sentence points to a National
Geographic documentary, that he "watched the entire 88-minute long film twice", and that it "never
mentions the WHO".[2]

Walker's reply: "The Centers for Disease Control (CDC) has stated that, "Insufficient sleep is a
public health epidemic."" And: "The book's misattribution of the CDC statement to the WHO will be
corrected in the next edition."[3]

So on this one, the two documents agree. The declaration was not the WHO's. A statement close to it
was made by the CDC, the United States' national public health agency, and the book credited it to
the World Health Organization.

:::predict Before you read on. Assume nobody involved was lying. How might a sentence credited to one body end up credited to another, in a book written by a researcher?

This course can only offer possibilities, because it was not there and neither document says how it happened.

A note taken while reading says "health authorities have called this an epidemic", and months later, drafting, the more famous authority is the one that comes to mind. Or the sentence is written from memory of a talk or an article that itself had it wrong. What these share is that each step is ordinary, and the error moves in one direction: towards the more authoritative-sounding name.

That is the first shape a claim takes on the way into a paragraph, and it is this course's reading, not a finding.
:::

Walker's reply adds one more sentence on it, which is his and should be kept: "Both organizations
have stated that inadequate and disrupted sleep is dangerous for human health."[3] That is a
different and much more general claim than a declared epidemic, and this course has not checked it.

## The second sentence: what it claims

The book's sentence, as Guzey quotes it from page 3: "Routinely sleeping less than six or seven
hours a night demolishes your immune system, more than doubling your risk of cancer."[2]

Guzey's verdict opens: "This is false. Walker does not cite any studies that support this assertion
anywhere in the book." He sets against it a 2018 review of cancer and sleep duration which, in his
words, "involved 1,550,524 participants and 86,201 cancer cases, found that neither short nor long
sleep duration was associated with increased cancer risk."[2] **This course has not read that review**,
so what it found is Guzey's report of it.

Walker's reply: "Short sleep is by no means associated with a higher risk of all forms of cancer.
Several studies, however, have indicated that short sleep is associated with a doubling of risk for
specific cancers". He lists four, and then: "A meta-analysis published after the book was written,
which examined 9 main forms of cancer (spanning a total of 20 subtype cancers), did not find an
association between an all-cancer category and sleep duration." And: "This will be reflected in
future editions of the book."[3]

This is the most useful exchange in the lesson, and it is worth doing slowly.

:::checkpoint Put the book's sentence next to the reply's. Name two things that changed between them.

The verb. "Demolishes" says short sleep does the damage. "Is associated with" reports a pattern in data. That is lesson 3's distinction exactly: the cohorts behind these figures show associations, and the book's sentence stated a cause.

The scope. "Your risk of cancer" covers all of it. The reply says the association is "by no means" with all forms, and names specific ones, while agreeing that a meta-analysis found no association for cancer as a whole.

So the author himself, in public, moved the claim from a cause of cancer in general to an association with some cancers. You don't need to have read any of the studies to see that, and this course has not.
:::

Notice where the two documents meet. Both report that pooled evidence didn't find short sleep
associated with cancer as a whole; whether they mean the same meta-analysis, this course can't tell.
They still disagree about what follows. Guzey reads it as the sentence being false. Walker reads it
as the sentence being too broad, with a narrower version still supported by the studies he lists.
**Those are two defensible readings of a narrower claim**, and this course, having read none of the
studies, does not choose.

That is the second shape a claim takes: a hedged association in a literature becomes a flat statement
about causes in a paragraph, and a scope that was specific becomes general.

## The third: the missing column

This one has no agreement in it, and it is the harder case.

A 2014 study of adolescent athletes reported injury rates by how many hours they slept. The book
printed an adaptation of its graph. Guzey, crediting the observation to somebody else ("this problem
was first noticed by Olli Haataja"), put it like this:

> "The 5 hours of sleep column – which is associated with lower chance of injury than 6 hours of sleep – has simply disappeared."[2]

The
appendix he put it in is headed "what do you do when a part of the graph contradicts your argument?
You cut it out, of course", which tells you how he reads it.

Walker's account, which deserves to be read whole: "As shown in the original study, as the athletes'
sleep amount progressively decreased from 9 to 6 hours, injury risk increased. This was depicted in a
graphic in the book. The study also reported a group of individuals getting 5 hours of sleep a night.
This group's injury risk was also high, though lower than the adjacent 6-hour group. However, noted
by the authors in communication, the 5 hour group was of a size small enough to challenge robust
estimate of injury risk, and was not represented in the book's graphic."[3]

And his remedy: "future editions of the book will include a new graphic. This figure will depict the
pooled injury risk of those getting less than 8 hours of sleep, relative to those getting 8 hours or
more."[3]

**This course has not read the study**, so it can't say how small the 5-hour group was.

:::predict Put both accounts at their strongest before you decide anything. What is the best case for leaving the column out, and the best case for putting it back?

For leaving it out. A very small group gives a very noisy estimate. Drawn as a bar the same width as the others, it looks as solid as they do, and a reader will read the dip as real when it may be chance. On Walker's account the study's own authors flagged it, and the pooled comparison he now proposes is the one they used for their statistics.

For putting it back. The reader can't judge a group's size if the group is not there. A dropped column takes the decision away from them, and it was the column that ran against the pattern the text describes. The fix for a noisy bar is to show its uncertainty or say it is small, not to remove it.

Both cases are about the reader, and both are reasonable. What the pooled graphic does is sidestep the argument: it shows the comparison the study's authors tested rather than a bar-by-bar picture.
:::

That is the third shape a claim takes: a small group that runs against a trend drops out, and the
pattern that is left reads cleaner than the data did. Whether that is a judgement about noise or a
loss of information depends on facts about the study that you'd have to go and check, which is the
point.

## Why each side does what it does

The lesson promised both cases at their strongest. Here they are in general, not about this book.

**Why an author writing for everybody compresses.** A book for a general reader can't carry every
confidence interval, every subgroup and every hedge, or nobody reads it. Its job, in Walker's words,
is "to provide the general public access to a broad collection of sleep research", and compression
is what makes a literature readable at all. Some errors will come with that. The author's side of
this case is that they should be corrected when found, which is what the reply says it did.

**Why a critic thinks the compression changed the claims.** A claim that is been compressed can
become a different claim: a cause instead of an association, all cancer instead of some, a clean
line instead of a messy one. If the book presents itself as scientifically accurate, the critic's
side of this case is that readers are entitled to hold it to that, and that the errors weren't
random: in the three cases here, each one made the book's case stronger than its sources.

And both sides agree on more than a reader might expect. Guzey has an appendix of "things I'm not
saying in this essay", and it includes "sleep is not important", "sleeping well is not important" and
"there are no people who naturally need 8 hours of sleep a night".[2] The dispute is not about
whether sleep matters.

## What this does and does not establish

**It establishes** that three specific sentences in a popular book went further than the sources
behind them, and that the author has conceded or narrowed two of them in writing.

**It does not establish** anything about the research in this course's other lessons. The experiment
in lesson 2, the consensus statement and the meta-analysis in lesson 3, the guidelines in lesson 6:
none of them is what either document disputes. "The book was criticised" and "sleep science is
unreliable" are different sentences.

**And it does not establish** anything about Walker as a scientist, or about Guzey's motives. This
course has read one chapter's worth of criticism and one reply, and neither the book nor the studies.
A verdict on a person from that would be exactly the kind of compression this lesson is about.

## Three things people get wrong

**"The book was debunked, so the science is wrong."** The dispute is about specific sentences in one
book. The studies this course is built on are not part of it.

**"The critic proved the claims false."** On the WHO sentence, the author agrees. On cancer, the
critic reports a review this course has not read, and the author narrows rather than withdraws. On the
graph, the two disagree about a study neither this course nor most readers have opened. "Argued, with
evidence, and partly conceded" is accurate. "Proved" is too strong for the second and third.

**"An author correcting a book means it was dishonest."** Correcting a book is what an author is
supposed to do when errors are found. Whether any particular error was careless, motivated or
honest is a question neither document can settle and this course does not try to.

## Practice

:::exercise Read the two documents side by side

Take 45 minutes.

Both are free and linked in the table above. Pick one section of Guzey's essay that this lesson
didn't cover, and find Walker's reply to the same point, if there is one.

1. Write down the book's sentence, as the essay quotes it.
2. Write down, in one line each, what the essay says and what the reply says.
3. Mark one place where you find the essay's case strong, and one where you find the reply's case
   strong. You have to find both.
4. Name which of this lesson's three shapes, if any, the sentence took: an attribution that moved, a
   hedged association that hardened, or a small group that dropped out.

If the reply does not address your section, that is worth noting too, and it is not a verdict.
:::

:::exercise Three checks on a paragraph

Take 20 minutes.

Take any paragraph of popular science on any subject: a book, an article, a talk transcript. Find the
single most confident sentence in it, and write down the three things you would check first.

- **Who said it**: is it credited to a named body, and can you find that body saying it?
- **What kind of claim it is**: a cause, or an association, and for all of something or part of it?
- **What's missing**: is there a chart, and if so, does the original have groups this one does not?

You don't have to do the checks. Knowing what they'd be is the skill.
:::
