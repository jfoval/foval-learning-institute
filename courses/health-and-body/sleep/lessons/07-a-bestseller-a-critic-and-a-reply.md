Matthew Walker's *Why We Sleep*, published in 2017, was a bestseller and widely praised,[2] and you
may well have met its claims second-hand without knowing where they came from.

**This course has not read it.** That is deliberate. What this lesson has instead is two documents
about it, both free and both online: a long critique by a writer called Alexey Guzey, mainly of the
book's first chapter, with appendices on others; and a post by Walker answering questions readers had
raised about the book "in reviews, online forums and direct emails".[3]

The post is not a reply to the essay. It does not name Guzey, and Guzey notes near the top of his
essay that it is "explicitly not a response to my essay" and that Walker "never responds to them
directly".[2] But several of its sections take up the same sentences the essay does, and those are
the ones this lesson uses. On three sentences, you can put the critic's account next to the author's
own written account and see where they meet. On some of it the author **concedes in writing**. So you
can watch specific claims change shape on the way from a research literature into a paragraph, and
then watch some of them change back.

This lesson reaches no verdict about anybody. It is not a takedown or a defence. It is about three
sentences.

**Neither document's underlying studies were read.** When Guzey cites a review, or Walker cites a
study, this lesson reports that they did, and does not report what the study found as though this
course had checked it.

What each says about its own purpose explains a lot of what follows.

Guzey on his scope: "I hope that going through one full chapter, rather than cherry-picking stuff
from across the book, demonstrated the density of errors in the book."[2] He reports spending "more
than 130 hours" on it. His thesis is that "the book consistently overstates the problem of lack of
sleep, sometimes egregiously so."[2] And his reason for holding a popular book to a scholarly standard
is that the book, which he quotes, calls itself "intended to serve as a scientifically accurate
intervention".[2]

Walker on his aim: "The aim of the book, Why We Sleep, is to provide the general public access to a
broad collection of sleep research." And on the corrections: "I very much appreciate being made aware
of any errors in the book requiring revision. Furthermore, and as an essential part of what I see to
be good scholarly conduct, I have made a full set of corrections to the book. These corrections have
now been published in a second edition, which is now in print."[3] **This course has not seen the
second edition** and can't say what it changed.

## The first sentence: who said it

The book's sentence, as Guzey quotes it from page 4: "[T]he World Health Organization (WHO) has now
declared a sleep loss epidemic throughout industrialized nations."[2]

Guzey's verdict: "This is false. The WHO never declared a sleep loss epidemic throughout
industrialized nations." He reports that the book's footnote for that sentence points to a National
Geographic documentary, that he "watched the entire 88-minute long film twice", and that it "never
mentions the WHO".[2]

Walker's post: "The Centers for Disease Control (CDC) has stated that, "Insufficient sleep is a
public health epidemic."" And: "The book's misattribution of the CDC statement to the WHO will be
corrected in the next edition."[3]

So the two documents agree on one thing: the declaration wasn't the WHO's. Walker gives the CDC, a
United States federal agency, as the source instead. Guzey reports, in an appendix, that a CDC page
with that title changed "epidemic" to "problem" "More than 2 years before Why We Sleep was
published".[2] This course has not checked the CDC's pages, so whether the CDC's statement still stood
when the book was written is a disagreement it reports and can't settle.

:::predict Before you read on. How might a sentence credited to one body end up credited to another, in a book written by a researcher?

Neither document knows. Guzey offers one possibility himself, "that Walker simply misremembered the organization (and added the "industrialized nations" bit)", and then gives his reasons for doubting it, including that the cited film never mentions either body.[2]

This course can add two more possibilities, and they're its own, not findings. A note taken while reading says "health authorities have called this an epidemic", and months later the more famous authority is the one that comes to mind. Or the sentence is written from memory of a talk or an article that itself had it wrong. What the innocent routes share is that each step is ordinary and the error moves one way, towards the more authoritative-sounding name. Guzey's reasons for doubt are the case that it wasn't innocent, and this course can't decide between them.

Notice Guzey's bracket, too. "Throughout industrialized nations" widens a statement by one country's agency to many countries. So this sentence took two shapes at once: the attribution moved up, and the scope got wider.
:::

Walker's post adds one more sentence, and it is a different, much more general claim: "Both
organizations have stated that inadequate and disrupted sleep is dangerous for human health."[3] He
supports the WHO half with a quotation from a researcher he describes as working at the WHO. This
course has not checked it.

## The second sentence: what it claims

The book's sentence, as Guzey quotes it from page 3: "Routinely sleeping less than six or seven
hours a night demolishes your immune system, more than doubling your risk of cancer."[2]

Guzey's verdict opens: "This is false. Walker does not cite any studies that support this assertion
anywhere in the book." On causes, he adds: "There do not appear to exist any experimental studies or
studies that would reasonably be able to establish causality, that would support this claim." And he
sets against it a 2018 review which, in his words, "involved 1,550,524 participants and 86,201 cancer
cases, found that neither short nor long sleep duration was associated with increased cancer
risk."[2] **This course has not read that review.**

Walker's post: "Short sleep is by no means associated with a higher risk of all forms of cancer.
Several studies, however, have indicated that short sleep is associated with a doubling of risk for
specific cancers". He lists four, and then: "A meta-analysis published after the book was written,
which examined 9 main forms of cancer (spanning a total of 20 subtype cancers), did not find an
association between an all-cancer category and sleep duration." And: "While there is ready evidence
associating certain cancers with short sleep, this study indicates that there are numerous cancers
that do not show such an association. This will be reflected in future editions of the book."[3]

He goes further on the verb than the book did: "It is not correct to suggest, based on
epidemiological findings, that sleeping less than 6 or 7 hours causes cancer." And the very next
sentence is his case against the critic's reading: "However, one similarly cannot state that
sleeping less than 6 hours a night does not double your risk of cancer. The same evidence cannot
establish causality in the negative: absence of evidence in observational epidemiology does not
necessarily imply evidence of absence." He also lists limits of the meta-analysis, among them that
its authors said its results "cannot be generalized to all cancer types".[3]

This is the most useful exchange in the lesson, and it is worth doing slowly.

:::checkpoint Put the book's sentence next to the post's. Name two things that changed between them.

The verb. "Demolishes" says short sleep does the damage. The post says the studies it lists report that short sleep "is associated with" some cancers, and says in terms that epidemiology can't show it "causes cancer". That is lesson 3's distinction, and the author draws it himself.

The scope. "Your risk of cancer" covers all of it. The post says the association is "by no means" with all forms, and names specific ones, while agreeing that a meta-analysis found no association for cancer as a whole.

So the author, in public, moved the claim from a cause of cancer in general to an association with some cancers. You don't need to have read any of the studies to see that, and this course has not.
:::

Notice where the two documents meet, and where they don't. Both say the evidence is observational and
can't show that short sleep causes cancer. Both report that pooled evidence did not find an
association with cancer as a whole; whether they mean the same meta-analysis, this course can't
tell. They still disagree about what follows. Guzey reads the sentence as false. Walker reads it as
too broad, with a narrower version supported by the studies he lists, and argues that finding no
association is not the same as showing there is none. **Those are two readings of the same evidence**,
and this course, having read none of the studies, does not choose between them.

That is the second shape a claim takes: an association in a literature becomes a flat statement about
causes in a paragraph, and a scope that was specific becomes general.

## The third: the missing column

This one has no agreement in it, and it is the harder case.

A 2014 study of adolescent athletes reported injury rates by how many hours they slept, and Walker
gives its size as 112 people.[3] The book, in its sixth chapter, printed an adaptation of its graph.
Guzey, crediting the observation to somebody else ("this problem was first noticed by Olli
Haataja"), put it like this:

> "The 5 hours of sleep column – which is associated with lower chance of injury than 6 hours of sleep – has simply disappeared."[2]

He adds that the 9-hour column, which the book kept, "is based on exactly 1 child being injured out of
6 children who reported sleeping for 9 hours".[2] The appendix he put it in is headed "what do you do
when a part of the graph contradicts your argument? You cut it out, of course", and elsewhere he
calls it "the clearest example of deliberate data manipulation".[2] That is a charge about intent, and
it is his.

Walker's account: "As shown in the original study, as the athletes' sleep amount progressively
decreased from 9 to 6 hours, injury risk increased. This was depicted in a graphic in the book. The
study also reported a group of individuals getting 5 hours of sleep a night. This group's injury risk
was also high, though lower than the adjacent 6-hour group. However, noted by the authors in
communication, the 5 hour group was of a size small enough to challenge robust estimate of injury
risk, and was not represented in the book's graphic." And: "Related, the authors averaged the data for
statistical power, comparing those getting less than 8-hours with those getting more than this
amount."[3]

And his remedy: "To counter any confusion, including power issues at either end of the sleep
duration distribution, and to better represent the statistical analysis of the study authors, future
editions of the book will include a new graphic. This figure will depict the pooled injury risk of
those getting less than 8 hours of sleep, relative to those getting 8 hours or more." He adds that
the revised book will cite a 2019 meta-analysis that "confirms this original result".[3]

**This course has not read the study or the meta-analysis**, so it can't say how small the 5-hour group
was.

:::predict Put both accounts at their strongest before you decide anything. What is the best case for leaving the column out, and the best case for putting it back?

For leaving it out. A very small group gives a very noisy estimate. Drawn as a bar the same width as the others, it looks as solid as they do, and a reader will read the dip as real when it may be chance. On Walker's account the study's authors told him the group was too small, and the pooled comparison he now proposes is the one they used for their statistics.

For putting it back. The reader can't judge a group's size if the group is not there. A dropped column takes the decision away from them, it was the column that ran against the pattern the text describes, and a small group at the other end, which happened to fit, was kept.

Both cases are about the reader, and neither is foolish. On Walker's account, the pooled graphic shows the comparison the study's authors tested. It also means no single bar is shown, at either end.
:::

That is the third shape a claim takes: a small group that runs against a trend drops out, and the
pattern that is left reads cleaner than the data did. Whether that was a judgement about noise or a
loss of information, or something worse, depends on facts about the study you'd have to go and
check, and on a question about intent that no document here can settle.

## Why each side does what it does

The lesson promised both cases at their strongest. Stated in general, not about this book, they run
like this.

**Why an author writing for everybody compresses.** A book for a general reader can't carry every
confidence interval, every subgroup and every hedge, or nobody reads it. Its job, in Walker's words,
is "to provide the general public access to a broad collection of sleep research", and compression is
what makes a literature readable at all. Some errors will come with that. The author's side of the
case is that they should be corrected when found, which the post says has been done in a second
edition.

**Why a critic thinks the compression changed the claims.** A claim that is been compressed can become
a different claim: a cause instead of an association, all cancer instead of some, a clean line
instead of a messy one. Guzey answers the popular-audience defence head on, calling it
"indefensible", on two grounds: the book's own claim to accuracy, and that "in 2019, Walker published
an academic paper that cited Why We Sleep 4 times just on its first page", so errors in the book
travel into the research literature.[2] And he argues the errors weren't random: the book, in his
words, "consistently overstates the problem of lack of sleep".[2] In the three cases here, each change
did run in the book's favour. That is this course's observation, from the two documents' own
accounts, and it does not tell you why.[4]

And the critic concedes more than a reader might expect. Guzey has an appendix of "things I'm not
saying in this essay", and it includes "sleep is not important", "sleeping well is not important" and
"there are no people who naturally need 8 hours of sleep a night".[2] The dispute is not about whether
sleep matters.

## What this does and does not establish

**It establishes** that the author accepts two of the three sentences need changing: the WHO
attribution is wrong, and the cancer sentence is broader than he now says the evidence supports. On
the third, the author and the critic disagree about what the graph should have shown, and this course
can't settle it.

**It does not establish** anything against the research in this course's other lessons. None of the
course's own sources is what either document disputes, and in fact each side leans on one of them:
Guzey cites lesson 3's meta-analysis in his own support, and Walker's post cites lesson 2's
experiment.[2][3] Guzey does add a caution that reaches lesson 3, that "Self-reported data is
notoriously unreliable", which is lesson 1's point about instruments. "The book was criticised" and
"sleep science is unreliable" are different sentences.

**And it does not establish** anything about Walker as a scientist or Guzey as a critic, or about
either man's motives. Guzey's essay does make a charge about motive, calling the graph "deliberate
data manipulation".[2] The post does not answer it, and this course can't judge it. It has read part of
one critique and part of one post, and neither the book nor the studies.

## Four things people get wrong

**"The book was debunked, so the science is wrong."** The dispute is about specific sentences in one
book. The studies this course is built on are not part of it, and both sides cite some of them.

**"The critic proved the claims false."** On the WHO sentence, the author agrees. On cancer, the
critic reports a review this course has not read, and the author narrows rather than withdraws, arguing
that no association is not proof of none. On the graph, the two disagree about a study most readers
haven't opened. "Argued, with evidence, and partly conceded" is accurate. "Proved" is too strong for
the second and third.

**"The author responded and corrected it, so the critic's case is answered."** The post does not name
the essay, and the critic says it never takes up his points directly. On the graph the two still
disagree, and the charge of intent is unanswered. The corrections are in a second edition this course
has not seen. A post that concedes some points leaves the others where they were.

**"An author correcting a book means it was dishonest."** Correcting a book is what an author is
supposed to do when errors are found. Whether any particular error was careless, motivated or honest
is a question neither document can settle and this course does not try to.

## Practice

:::exercise Read the two documents side by side

Take 45 minutes.

Both are free and linked in the table above. Pick one section of Guzey's essay that this lesson
did not cover, and find whether Walker's post takes up the same point.

1. Write down the book's sentence, as the essay quotes it.
2. Write down, in one line each, what the essay says and what the post says.
3. Mark one place where you find the essay's case strong, and one where you find the post's case
   strong. You have to find both.
4. Name which of this lesson's three shapes, if any, the sentence took: an attribution that moved, a
   hedged association that hardened, or a small group that dropped out.

If the post does not address your section, that is worth noting too, and it is not a verdict.
:::

:::exercise Three checks on a paragraph

Take 20 minutes.

Take any paragraph of popular science on any subject: a book, an article, a talk transcript. Find the
single most confident sentence in it, and write down the three things you would check first.

- **Who said it**: is it credited to a named body, and can you find that body saying it?
- **What kind of claim it is**: a cause, or an association, and for all of something or part of it?
- **What's missing**: is there a chart, and if so, does the original have groups this one does not?

You don't have to do the checks. Knowing what they'd be is the skill.
:::

## Connections

**Back.** Lesson 3's line between an association and a cause is the one the cancer sentence crossed,
and the author's post crossed back. **Note-Taking** lesson 3 followed a famous study that travelled without
its conditions; **Memory** lesson 4 traced a famous study to its source. This lesson does the same to
a book, with the unusual help of the author's own written account.

**Forward.** Lesson 8 asks you to read the next claim you meet about your own sleep. The three shapes
here are what to look for.
