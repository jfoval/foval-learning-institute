So your mid sleep on free days is a real fact about your sleep, and a proxy for a fact about your clock.

S2: And the clock can move?

S1: It can, and light moves it. A twenty twelve experiment gave participants one hour of bright white light, about eight thousand lux, at one of eighteen different points around the circadian cycle, and measured how far their clocks shifted using that melatonin measure. A matched group got the same hour in dim light and showed no discernible response, which is what shows the bright light was doing the work. The course read the abstract in full and one sentence of the introduction.

S2: How big was the shift?

S1: On the fitted curve, the difference between the biggest delay and the biggest advance was over two hours of clock time. Two point two oh hours, from one hour of light. And the direction is the part to hold. The paper's introduction says that light in the early biological night delays the clock, and light in the late biological night advances it.

S2: Biological night. Is that just night?

S1: No, and that's the whole lesson. Your biological night is the stretch of the cycle your clock treats as night. It usually overlaps your sleep, but it doesn't have to line up with the clock on the wall. That gloss is the course's own plain language, by the way, not a quotation.

S2: Let me try it, then. Ana gets an hour of bright light at ten at night. Ben gets his at six in the morning. Ten at night is early, so Ana's delayed. Six in the morning is late, so Ben's advanced.

If Ana's clock is set late, ten at night might fall before her biological night has even begun. If Ben wakes at eleven every morning, six might be in the middle of his night, not the end. The same clock time is a different phase in two different people. Ana and Ben are invented, and that reading of biological night is the course's, not the paper's.
