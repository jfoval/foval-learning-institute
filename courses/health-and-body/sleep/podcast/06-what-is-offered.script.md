S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Sleep, on the lesson called What is offered to somebody sleeping badly.

S2: And before anything, the lesson opens with a boundary, so let's say it too.

S1: Let's. This describes treatments and gives no advice about your own sleep, your own medicine, or any procedure to try. Nothing here supports starting, stopping or changing any medicine, prescribed or bought over the counter. And lesson one listed the signs that mean a doctor rather than a course. Loud snoring with pauses in breathing that somebody has seen. Falling asleep at the wheel. Severe sleeplessness that came on suddenly. And sleepiness that more sleep doesn't fix.

S2: So what is the lesson about?

S1: One thing at the far end of sleeping badly. A diagnosable disorder called chronic insomnia disorder, and what three clinical guidelines say should be offered for it. The American College of Physicians defines it as dissatisfaction with sleep quantity or quality, associated with difficulty getting to sleep or staying asleep, and waking early without being able to get back to sleep. And it says roughly six to ten percent of adults have insomnia that meets the diagnostic criteria.

S2: Now, the lesson says there's one idea that makes all three guidelines readable.

S1: The grade. A grade usually has two parts, and that two part summary is the course's. Strength of recommendation, which is how confident the panel is that following it is the right call for most patients. And quality of evidence, which is how good the studies behind it are.

S2: So if a treatment gets a weak recommendation, I'd assume it doesn't work very well.

S1: That's the assumption the lesson wants to stop. The twenty seventeen drug guideline from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine says a weak recommendation reflects a lower degree of certainty, but should not be construed as an indication of ineffectiveness. And it says the strengths don't refer to the size of the effect in a particular patient. So weak isn't it doesn't work, and strong isn't it works a lot.

S2: So what do the three recommend?

S1: Read the strength column top to bottom. Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, CBT I, gets a strong recommendation in both documents that grade it, on moderate quality evidence. Every medication line, fifteen if you count the physicians' one and the drug guideline's fourteen, is weak. Nothing about a drug is strong.

S2: So therapy beats pills.

S1: No, and the drug guideline itself says why not. Its abstract says downgrading the evidence for these treatments is predictable, because of who funds most drug trials and the risk of publication bias that goes with it, how few trials there are for any one drug, and how much their results vary. And the comparisons differ. Each drug was graded against no treatment. The CBT I trials compared it to wait list, minimal interventions, or placebo therapies. So the pattern tells you how certain three panels were. It isn't a head to head result.

S2: Then what does it support?

S1: Something narrower. The one panel that graded both, the physicians', was more confident about CBT I first than about adding a drug. Its first recommendation is that all adult patients receive CBT I as the initial treatment. Strong. Its second is about what happens if CBT I alone doesn't work, and it describes a conversation, not a prescription. A shared decision making approach, including a discussion of the benefits, harms, and costs of short term medication. Weak, on low quality evidence.

S2: So what is CBT I? It sounds like being told to relax.

S1: It isn't. It's a package. Cognitive therapy around sleep, behavioural methods such as sleep restriction and stimulus control, and education, such as sleep hygiene. And the strong grade rests mostly on studies where a trained professional delivered it.

S2: Here's my push, though. If I'm lying awake every night, I want something that works soon.

S1: And the same guideline that grades CBT I strongly makes that case for you. It says improvement usually isn't immediate, as it is with medication. Treatment typically runs four to eight visits, and both of those can be barriers to finishing it. It says patients may face higher out of pocket costs than for medications, or struggle to find a skilled provider where they live.

S2: So that's a real cost, stated by the guideline that grades it strongly.

S1: It is. And what it offers in return, in the same summary, is gains that are potentially durable over the long term without more treatment, and possibly less need for drugs, and so less risk of their side effects. Its own harms come early. Daytime fatigue and sleepiness, irritability, trouble concentrating, typically resolving by the end of treatment. The panel judged the benefits strongly outweighed those.

S2: You mentioned sleep restriction. What is that?

S1: A component that deliberately shortens the time a person spends in bed, to begin with. The guideline says early on it can bring more daytime sleepiness and trouble concentrating, which typically fade as time in bed is extended. And it says it may be contraindicated for some people, such as those in high risk jobs like drivers or heavy machinery operators, or those prone to mania, with poorly controlled seizures, or with excessive daytime sleepiness. It's the component a reader could most easily mistake for a tip. Lesson two found healthy adults held to fewer hours were largely unaware of their own decline. They weren't insomnia patients, so that's the course's reason for caution, not a finding about this treatment. But it's why the lesson says what sleep restriction is and gives no protocol for it.

S2: Last one. Sleep hygiene. Diet, caffeine, light, noise, the stuff every sleep article is made of. I've read that experts now say it's useless.

S1: The twenty twenty one behavioural guideline suggests clinicians not use sleep hygiene as a single component therapy for chronic insomnia disorder in adults. Conditional. Now take that apart. It doesn't say sleep hygiene is harmful. It doesn't say it has no effect. One trial found a higher responder rate than control, though CBT I beat it in the same trial, and the people who improved had also changed their sleep schedules unprompted. The panel rated the evidence low and judged its benefits on their own minimal. And it doesn't say leave it out. It says it may be part of a multicomponent treatment. So the fair version is this. For somebody with diagnosed chronic insomnia, a leaflet of sleep tips shouldn't be the whole of what they're offered. Much narrower than sleep hygiene doesn't work, and further still from don't bother.

S2: So if someone listening recognises themselves in all this?

S1: What the lesson supports is taking it to a clinician, with better questions. Whether CBT I is available to them and in what form, and what the plan would be if it doesn't work. Questions that ask, not tell.

S2: We've left out the drug guideline's full list, including its recommendation on melatonin and what that recommendation does and doesn't say. The full lesson is free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening.
