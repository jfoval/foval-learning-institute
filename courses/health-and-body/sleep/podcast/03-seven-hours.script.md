S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Sleep, on the lesson called Seven hours.

S2: Last time was one experiment on forty eight people. What's this one?

S1: A recommendation from two professional societies, and a study of one million, three hundred and eighty two thousand, nine hundred and ninety nine people. And the lesson's claim is that the bigger evidence is the weaker evidence about cause. Understanding why is, it says, the most useful thing in the course.

S2: Start with the recommendation.

S1: In twenty fifteen the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society published a joint consensus statement. The course read part of it. The recommendation, entire, is: adults should sleep seven or more hours per night on a regular basis to promote optimal health. Seven or more. Not eight, and the recommendation itself names no upper number.

S2: And what does it say short sleep does?

S1: It gives a long list. Sleeping less than seven hours per night on a regular basis is associated with adverse health outcomes, including weight gain and obesity, diabetes, hypertension, heart disease and stroke, depression, and increased risk of death. And a second list: impaired immune function, increased pain, impaired performance, increased errors, greater risk of accidents.

S2: That's a frightening list.

S1: Read the verb. Is associated with. Twice. A panel that meant something stronger would have written causes, and they had the option. Associated with is what you write when the evidence is observational. Nobody assigned anybody anything. What was recorded was what people already did and what later happened to them.

S2: So it leaves open which way round it goes.

S1: Exactly. An association between short sleep and diabetes fits short sleep contributing to diabetes, early diabetes disturbing sleep, or some third thing, shift work or poverty or pain, producing both. And the panel isn't being coy. It makes a recommendation. What it declines to do is claim a mechanism it doesn't have.

S2: What's the study underneath?

S1: The largest single piece is a two thousand and ten meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies. That means groups recruited before anything happened to them, measured, then followed. The course read fragments of its abstract and didn't open the paper. Sixteen studies, twenty seven independent cohort samples, one million three hundred and eighty two thousand nine hundred and ninety nine participants. Short sleep was associated with a greater risk of death, a relative risk of one point one two, with an interval from one point oh six to one point one eight. And long sleep was also associated with a greater risk of death, one point three oh, with an interval from one point two two to one point three eight.

S2: Long sleep too?

S1: Long sleep too, and it's the bigger number. The authors' conclusion is that both short and long duration of sleep are significant predictors of death in prospective population studies. And two phrases in the short sleep result cut opposite ways. No evidence of publication bias, which is a reason to take one point one two seriously at all. And heterogeneity between studies, meaning they disagreed more than chance explains, which is a reason to hold it loosely. The authors report both, in one sentence.

S2: How big is one point one two, in actual people?

S1: A relative risk multiplies a baseline, so you need one. The lesson invents one for the arithmetic, and says so. Suppose eight people per thousand die in a year. One point one two takes that to eight point nine six. About one extra death per thousand people per year. The long arm, one point three oh, takes it to ten point four, an excess of two point four. Two and a half times the short sleep excess. And that ratio is the one figure here that doesn't depend on the invented baseline.

S2: So the arm nobody worries about carries the bigger number. Why doesn't anybody worry?

S1: Because nobody believes spending longer in bed kills people. The standard explanation is reverse causation. Illness lengthens sleep, so people already unwell sleep longer and die sooner, and the sleep is a symptom. That's reasonable. As far as this course can tell it's the field's usual reading, and nothing the course read tests it.

S2: I can feel where this is going.

S1: Apply the same explanation to the short arm. It works just as well. Pain could wake someone already ill. A mood disorder could shorten sleep in one person and lengthen it in another. Poverty, or working two jobs, could shorten sleep and bring everything else too. The lesson is explicit that these are possibilities, not findings, and that the whole move is the course's own reasoning, not something either source says. But the point stands. Using reverse causation only where it's convenient is its own mistake.

S2: That feels unfair to the panel, though. They're not idiots.

S1: And the lesson gives their side at full strength. In fact it puts it first. A good cohort study adjusts for illness at the start and often throws out deaths in the early years, so that they were already ill can't explain the result. Whether these studies did that, the course can't tell you, because it didn't read the methods. And that cuts both ways. Second, the two kinds of evidence point the same way. Lesson two's experiment found a dose dependent cost to short sleep under control. These cohorts find short sleepers dying sooner. Neither proves the other, but weighing both together is reasonable. And third, a recommendation is a decision under uncertainty. Seven hours costs most people little, though more to somebody on nights or working two jobs, and advising it on an association while declining the mechanism is what public health does with the evidence it has.

S2: So what's left standing?

S1: The association is real. People the studies classified as short sleepers died sooner than the studies' reference group. That's a finding. What doesn't survive is the sentence, sleeping less will shorten your life, because it needs a direction the design can't see. And again, that's the course's reading. Where the studies drew the line between short and long, nothing the course read says.

S2: But a million people. Isn't that enough?

S1: Size fixes noise, not direction. Twenty seven samples with the same blind spot have it twenty seven times. And the opposite mistake is just as real. It's only an association, so ignore it, is wrong too. An association in that many people is among the larger pieces of evidence about human health there is. Refusing the direction isn't refusing the finding.
