S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Sleep, on the lesson called A bestseller, a critic, and a reply.

S2: The bestseller being Matthew Walker's Why We Sleep, from twenty seventeen.

S1: Yes. The first thing the lesson says is that the course hasn't read it. On purpose. What it has instead is two documents about it, both free online. A long critique by a writer called Alexey Guzey, mainly of the book's first chapter. And a post by Walker answering questions readers had raised about the book.

S2: So the post is Walker's reply to Guzey.

S1: No, and the lesson is careful about that. The post doesn't name Guzey, and Guzey himself notes it is explicitly not a response to his essay. But several of its sections take up the same sentences the essay does. So on three sentences you can put the critic's account next to the author's and see where they meet. And the lesson reaches no verdict about anybody. It isn't a takedown or a defence.

S2: Alright. Sentence one.

S1: As Guzey quotes it, the book says the World Health Organization has now declared a sleep loss epidemic throughout industrialized nations. Guzey says that's false. The WHO never declared it. And the book's footnote points to a National Geographic documentary, which he reports watching twice, and which never mentions the WHO.

S2: And Walker?

S1: Walker's post says the Centers for Disease Control has stated that insufficient sleep is a public health epidemic, and that the book's misattribution of the CDC statement to the WHO will be corrected in the next edition.

S2: So they agree.

S1: On one thing. The declaration wasn't the WHO's. Guzey then reports that a CDC page with that title changed epidemic to problem more than two years before the book came out. The course hasn't checked the CDC's pages, so whether the CDC's statement still stood when the book was written is a disagreement it reports and can't settle.

S2: Sentence two.

S1: As Guzey quotes it, routinely sleeping less than six or seven hours a night demolishes your immune system, more than doubling your risk of cancer. Guzey says it's false, that Walker doesn't cite any studies supporting it anywhere in the book, and that there don't appear to be studies able to establish causality that would support the claim. He sets against it a twenty eighteen review that he says found neither short nor long sleep associated with increased cancer risk. The course hasn't read that review.

S2: And Walker?

S1: He narrows it. Short sleep is by no means associated with a higher risk of all forms of cancer, he writes, but several studies have indicated it's associated with a doubling of risk for specific cancers, and he lists four. He says a meta-analysis published after the book was written didn't find an association for an all cancer category. And he says plainly that it's not correct to suggest, from epidemiological findings, that sleeping less than six or seven hours causes cancer.

S2: So he's conceding.

S1: Partly. Then his very next sentence, which the lesson calls his case against the critic's reading, says one similarly cannot state that sleeping less than six hours a night does not double your risk of cancer. The same evidence can't establish causality in the negative. Absence of evidence in observational epidemiology does not necessarily imply evidence of absence.

S2: Though the post never names Guzey.

S1: It doesn't. Now look at what changed between the book and the post. The verb went from demolishes, a cause, to is associated with. And the scope went from your risk of cancer, all of it, to some named cancers. Run the other way, that's one of the shapes a claim takes on its way into a paragraph: an association hardens into a cause, and some cancers become all of them.

S2: But they still disagree.

S1: They do. Both say the evidence is observational and can't show cause. Guzey reads the sentence as false. Walker reads it as too broad, with a narrower version supported, and argues that finding no association isn't showing there is none. Those are two readings of the same evidence, and the course, having read none of the studies, doesn't choose.

S2: And the third?

S1: The harder one, because there's no agreement in it. A twenty fourteen study of adolescent athletes reported injury rates by hours of sleep. Walker gives its size as a hundred and twelve people. The book printed an adaptation of its graph, and Guzey, crediting the observation to somebody else, says the five hours column, which had a lower chance of injury than six hours, has simply disappeared.

S2: What does Guzey point to?

S1: The column the book kept. He says the nine hour column is based on exactly one child being injured out of six children who reported sleeping nine hours. He calls it the clearest example of deliberate data manipulation. That's a charge about intent, and it's his.

S2: And Walker's account?

S1: That the five hour group's risk was also high, though lower than the six hour group, and that the study's authors told him it was small enough to challenge a robust estimate, and it wasn't in the graphic. And that the authors averaged the data, comparing less than eight hours with eight or more. Future editions, he says, will show that pooled comparison instead. The course hasn't read the study, so it can't say how small that group was.

S2: So who's right?

S1: The lesson puts both at their strongest. For leaving it out, a very small group gives a very noisy estimate, and drawn as a bar like the others it looks as solid as they do. For putting it back, the reader can't judge a group's size if it isn't there, and a small group at the other end that happened to fit was kept. Both cases are about the reader, and neither is foolish.

S2: And the charge of intent?

S1: No document here can settle it. The post doesn't answer it, and the course has read part of one critique, part of one post, and neither the book nor the studies.

S2: So what does all this establish?

S1: That the author accepts two of the three sentences need changing. The WHO attribution was wrong, and the cancer sentence is broader than he now says the evidence supports. On the graph they still disagree. What it doesn't establish is anything against the research in this course's other lessons. Each side actually cites one of them. And it establishes nothing about Walker as a scientist, or Guzey as a critic, or either man's motives. And the critic concedes more than you might expect. Guzey has an appendix of things he's not saying, and one of them is that sleep is not important. The dispute isn't about whether sleep matters.

S2: We've left out how an attribution might move from one body to another, and the lesson's general case for why a popular author compresses and why a critic thinks that changed the claims. The full lesson is free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening.
