S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Sleep, on the lesson called Fourteen nights.

S2: Fourteen nights of what?

S1: Of not enough sleep, on purpose. Almost everything you've been told about sleep is an association. Somebody asked a lot of people how long they sleep, waited, and counted what happened. That's the next lesson. This one is the other kind. One experiment, forty eight people, sleep taken away by an amount somebody chose. It's the strongest evidence in the course, and it's also the narrowest.

S2: Who ran it?

S1: A team published it in two thousand and three. And the course read the abstract and did not open the paper, so everything I say is held at that level. People were randomised to one of three doses, four, six or eight hours in bed a night, kept up for fourteen days in a row. A separate experiment had three nights with no sleep at all. The forty eight were healthy adults aged twenty one to thirty eight, and that's the total across both experiments. How many were in each arm isn't in the abstract. Standardised laboratory, monitored continuously, and all other sleep prohibited.

S2: Can I stop you on one word? You said hours in bed.

S1: That's the unit, and the lesson holds it the whole way. Nobody sleeps every minute they're in bed. So somebody who actually sleeps six hours is getting more sleep than the six hour arm did, not the same. Every six hours in this episode means six hours in bed.

S2: Got it. And the eight hour group is the normal sleepers?

S1: No, and that's worth noticing. It's a group held at eight hours in bed, which for some of them is more than they usually get and for others less. That's what a dose is. So it's a dose response experiment, not the sleepy against the rested.

S2: What happened?

S1: The abstract says restricting sleep periods to four or six hours a night over fourteen days produced significant cumulative, dose dependent deficits in cognitive performance on all tasks. Cumulative means the deficit kept growing across the fortnight rather than arriving on night two and sitting still. Dose dependent means four was worse than six, and six worse than eight.

S2: What were the tasks?

S1: That's the first thing the course can't tell you. The abstract says all tasks and never names them. So the lesson can't say what they were, how long they took, or whether any of them looks like anything you do. A lesson that filled that in would be making it up.

S2: Okay. Did the people know they were getting worse?

S1: That's the finding I'd have guessed wrong. The study asked them how sleepy they felt. The sleepiness ratings did not significantly differentiate the six hour and four hour conditions. The performance separated those two groups, and the ratings didn't.

S2: So the gauge people actually use to decide if they slept enough wasn't tracking the thing that was changing.

S1: And here I want the authors' exact words, because they're careful. Sleepiness ratings suggest that subjects were largely unaware of these increasing cognitive deficits, which may explain why the impact of chronic sleep restriction on waking cognitive functions is often assumed to be benign. Suggest, and may explain. That's the authors offering an account of why a belief is common, not a measurement of the belief.

S2: So here's the question everybody actually wants answered. Is six hours enough?

S1: The honest answer has two halves. On what this experiment measured, six hours in bed was not enough, and the abstract reports no point at which the deficits stopped building. That's a real result, on healthy twenty one to thirty eight year olds, in a laboratory, on tasks the abstract doesn't name. But the question you asked is whether six hours is enough for you, doing your work, at your age, with everything the laboratory removed. This study can't answer that. And what makes the gap uncomfortable is the sleepiness result. The one measurement you'd use to answer it for yourself is the one that didn't separate six hours from four. Feeling fine is not evidence either way. That's the course's reading, not the paper's. And it's not a reason to assume you're impaired.

S2: There's a line from this paper I think I've heard. Six hours is as bad as two nights with no sleep?

S1: That's the sentence that gets quoted out of this paper, and the version you heard is missing words. The two that matter are up to. The authors wrote that chronic restriction of sleep to six hours or less per night produced cognitive performance deficits equivalent to up to two nights of total sleep deprivation. Up to. It's a ceiling, over fourteen nights, on unnamed tasks, in a laboratory. It's not a conversion rate for your own week. And it's separate from another result, that three nights with no sleep at all cost more per hour of sleep lost than the fortnight of short nights did.

S2: And there's a number with decimals in this one, isn't there?

S1: Fifteen point eight four hours. The abstract says a statistical model revealed that lapses in behavioural alertness were nearly linearly related to the cumulative time people had been awake beyond fifteen point eight four hours, with a standard error of nought point seven three hours. A statistical model revealed. That's the authors telling you where the figure came from. It's a parameter fitted across four experimental conditions. Nobody watched anyone cross a threshold at fifteen point eight four hours, and it isn't any one person's number.

S2: So if somebody tells me that's the longest a person can safely stay awake?

S1: Two things wrong, and a third. It isn't an observed threshold, it's fitted. It isn't about a person, it describes forty eight people across conditions. And safely isn't in the sentence the paper wrote. Lapses were modelled. Safety is a claim about consequences nobody here measured. The same goes for anyone saying this study proves short sleep is dangerous. Danger is a health claim, and no health outcome was measured here.
