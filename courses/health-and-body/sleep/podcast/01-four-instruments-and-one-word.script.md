S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're starting the course called Sleep, with its first lesson. Four instruments and one word.

S2: Which word?

S1: Sleep. You've done it every night of your life and you've never once measured it, because sleep is the one activity where the participant isn't available to watch. So everything anybody knows about it came through an instrument. There are four in common use, they don't measure the same thing, and the word sleep gets used for all four without anybody saying which one produced the number.

S2: Go on then. Four.

S1: Polysomnography first. Electrodes on the scalp and face, plus breathing and heart traces, overnight in a laboratory, scored by a technician in thirty second blocks. It's what the gold standard means here, and everything else is checked against it. Second, actigraphy, a research device at the wrist that infers sleep from not moving. It knows nothing about your brain and everything about whether your arm moved. Third, a consumer device. A watch, a ring, a sensor by the bed, feeding movement and heart rate into a model the manufacturer hasn't published. And fourth, a sleep diary. You write down when you think you fell asleep and woke. It's the only one that measures what you experienced, which isn't a weakness so much as a different subject.

S2: Where did that list come from?

S1: I should say what the lesson says. Grouping them as four is the course's own organisation, and the descriptions are general background, not something its sources lay out.

S2: So which of them is lying to me?

S1: That's where the lesson goes next. In twenty twenty one a team put thirty four healthy young adults in a sleep laboratory for three nights in a row, wired for polysomnography, and measured them at the same time with a research actigraph and some consumer devices. Seven of those in all, four at the wrist and three beside or under the bed. And the course read the abstract of that paper and did not open it, so everything I say about it is held at that level.

S2: Noted. What did the abstract find?

S1: Two numbers that have to be read together. Sensitivity was high, zero point nine three or above on every device. That's the share of the minutes you were genuinely asleep that the device called asleep. So they caught almost all your sleep. Specificity, the share of genuinely awake minutes called awake, was low to medium, from zero point one eight to zero point five four.

S2: So between about a fifth and a half of the time I was lying there awake, it noticed.

S1: And scored the rest as sleep. Catching sleep is the easy case. Lying still with your eyes shut and asleep, and lying still with your eyes shut and awake, look almost identical to anything reading movement.

S2: That sounds like a case against cheap watches.

S1: The same abstract cuts the other way, and the lesson says it would be dishonest to leave it out. Most of the consumer devices did as well as or better than the research actigraph on telling sleep from wake, and the authors call their performance promising. So the low specificity isn't a complaint about cheap watches. It's what comes of measuring sleep by movement at all.

S2: Make it concrete for me.

S1: The lesson works one night, and the arithmetic is the course's own. Eight hours in bed, seven asleep, one hour awake. At a specificity of zero point five four the device finds about thirty two of those sixty awake minutes and calls twenty eight of them sleep. At zero point one eight it finds about eleven and calls forty nine of them sleep. And the lesson is careful here. That picture holds sensitivity at a perfect one to show one effect on its own. In reality some genuine sleep goes the other way too, and on a night with little time awake that can outweigh the overcount. Which wins depends on how long you lay awake, and the abstract doesn't let you work that out.

S2: But on a bad night, the lean is towards reporting more sleep than I got.

S1: That's the course's reading, yes. On the night with a lot of lying awake, which is the night you'd most want it right. So if your watch says you slept well and you feel you didn't, the instrument's weakness points exactly that way.

S2: Here's what I don't get, though. Then how does anything get sold as over ninety percent accurate?

S1: Without anybody lying, too. Take the same night. Four hundred and twenty minutes asleep, sixty awake. A device with no sensor at all, that just said asleep every single minute, would be right four hundred and twenty times out of four hundred and eighty. That's eighty seven and a half percent accurate, and it knows nothing.

S2: Because most of the night really is sleep.

S1: Right. So one accuracy figure on a mostly sleep night is close to meaningless, and that's why the paper reports the pair. And the abstract's last clause matters too. The devices tended to do worse on nights with poorer or disrupted sleep. The lesson adds a sentence it labels as its own, and half of it a guess rather than a measurement: the people most likely to buy a tracker are the people whose nights are disrupted, and those are the nights the devices read worst.

S2: Okay, a practical one. My diary says I fell asleep at half past eleven. My watch says ten o'clock. Which is wrong?

S1: Neither, and the question has the wrong shape. The diary records when you experienced falling asleep, reported from the wrong side of the event. The watch records when a model decided your movement and heart rate looked like sleep. Two quantities, no reason to agree. A big gap would fit a long quiet stretch of lying awake, which is exactly what specificity says devices miss. How often that's the explanation, the course hasn't read anything about. And polysomnography would have called it by the electrodes, which would be a third number again. None of the three is your sleep. Each one is a measurement of it.

S2: And if something is actually wrong with my sleep?

S1: Then the lesson is plain. Sleep disorders are a clinical literature this course hasn't searched, and it won't improvise. If you snore loudly and somebody has seen you stop breathing, if you've fallen asleep at the wheel, if severe sleeplessness has come on suddenly, or if sleeping more doesn't fix your sleepiness, those are reasons to see a doctor. And the course gives no medical advice of any kind.

S2: The full lesson has a table of the sources behind the course's figures and exactly how far in each was read, what else it leaves out on purpose, and two exercises, one of which starts the course project with a prediction you write down before lesson two. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Next time you meet a number about sleep, ask which instrument made it.
