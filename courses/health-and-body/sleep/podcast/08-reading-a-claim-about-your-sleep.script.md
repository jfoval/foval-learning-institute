S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Sleep, on its last lesson, Reading a claim about your sleep.

S2: And the lesson describes itself as two jobs and one refusal.

S1: It does. The first job is two habits for the next claim about sleep you meet. The second is a decision about your own sleep, which you make and the course doesn't. And the refusal is why the lesson exists at all.

S2: Habits first, then.

S1: Habit one is the institute's standing sort, which listeners who've done the other closing lessons will know. Three categories. Measurable, meaning a study could check it. Unmeasurable as stated, meaning it uses a term nobody has pinned down. And false, meaning it's been checked and didn't hold. The lesson uses specimens it wrote itself, and the sorting is the course's reasoning.

S2: Give me one.

S1: Adults need seven or more hours a night.

S2: That sounds measurable. It's a number on every sleep website I've read.

S1: Measurable, yes, but look at what kind of sentence it is first. It comes from a consensus statement, and the statement's verb is should. Adults should sleep seven or more hours per night on a regular basis to promote optimal health. That's a recommendation, a panel's decision about a literature, and the outcomes it lists are introduced with is associated with. The specimen drops the should and turns advice into a fact about your body.

S2: Next.

S1: Your body repairs itself while you sleep.

S2: Repairs what?

S1: Exactly the question. Repairs what, measured how, compared with what? Nothing in it says what would count against it. So it's unmeasurable as stated. That doesn't make it wrong. It makes it a framing.

S2: And false?

S1: Everybody needs eight hours. False, as a statement of what the field says. The consensus statement says seven or more, sets no upper limit for healthy adults, and says individual sleep need varies, with genetic, behavioural, medical and environmental factors. Eight is a number people remember. The panel printed seven or more.

S2: Habit two.

S1: The clock question, which is what this course adds. Is this claim about how much sleep, or about when? Lesson four introduced it. They're different quantities, with different instruments and different gold standards. Electrodes in a laboratory for how much. A hormone assay under dim light for when. And a sentence about one licenses nothing about the other.

S2: And once you know which?

S1: The next question. Which instrument produced the number? Try a sentence somebody might actually say. My watch says I've been getting six and a half hours, so I'm below the recommendation.

S2: That's about how much. So it passes the first question.

S1: So far so good. Now the instrument. Lesson one's comparison found consumer devices had high sensitivity and low to medium specificity. They tend to count quiet wakefulness as sleep, so on a night with a lot of lying awake, that pushes the total up. How far, and whether it outweighs the sleep they miss, the abstract doesn't say. That reasoning is the course's.

S2: And then there's the recommendation on the other side of the sentence.

S1: Which rests on whatever instruments the cohorts behind lesson three's associations used, and the course didn't read how they measured sleep. Nothing says they were watches. So, in the course's reading, comparing a watch's figure to that recommendation may be comparing two instruments, and nobody has told you the exchange rate.

S2: Alright. The refusal.

S1: Here's the question most people came to the course hoping it would answer. How much sleep do I need? And the course says it can't tell you. Not out of modesty. It's the honest reading of three facts that sit side by side.

S2: Go on.

S1: One. The population recommendation is seven or more hours, from the field's peak bodies, with its outcomes stated as associations. Two. Some people sleep much less by nature. A university news release on work by Ying-Hui Fu, Louis Ptáček and colleagues reports that people who'd inherited a particular mutation, in a gene called DEC2, averaged only six point two five hours of sleep a night, against eight point nought six for participants without it. And the release says that mutation is rare and couldn't account for every natural short sleeper.

S2: So six and a quarter hours is what those people need?

S1: No, and that's worth being careful about. The course read the release, not the papers, and the release reports how long people slept, not what they need.

S2: And the third fact?

S1: Feeling fine doesn't tell you which group you're in. In lesson two's experiment, people held to six hours in bed, which means less than six hours asleep, rated themselves sleepier at first, and then only slightly sleepier, while their measured performance kept falling for a fortnight. The authors take that to suggest they were largely unaware of these increasing cognitive deficits.

S2: So I feel fine on six is what a natural short sleeper would say.

S1: And on that reading it's also what one of those restricted participants might have said while their performance fell.

S2: So put the three together and I get what number?

S1: None. The recommendation is for a population, and the same statement says need varies. The short sleepers show some of that variation is real and, in some cases, genetic. And the obvious way to place yourself, how you feel, is the thing the experiment suggests doesn't track what restricted sleep costs.

S2: That can sound like a dodge.

S1: The lesson says it isn't a way of avoiding the subject, and the refusal cuts both ways. The course won't give you the reassuring number, that six is fine for you. And it won't give you the alarming one either, that you're damaging yourself below seven. Neither is something it can know about one reader. The course's view is that leaving you there is more honest than giving you a number in either direction.

S2: Then the decision.

S1: Three endings, and the lesson calls all three legitimate. One, change one thing. Name it, say whether it's about how much or when, what it rests on, and what it costs you. Two, keep something deliberately. Somebody who keeps a six hour night having read lesson two is in a different position from somebody who never thought about it. Three, stop deciding. The evidence covers populations and doesn't reach your particular nights, and in the course's view, worrying about sleep has costs of its own.

S2: And whichever you choose?

S1: One more line. What would tell you it was the wrong call? The course doesn't recommend any of the three. And if what you've noticed about your own sleep matches any of the reasons lesson one gave for seeing a doctor, that's a fourth ending, and it comes before the other three.
