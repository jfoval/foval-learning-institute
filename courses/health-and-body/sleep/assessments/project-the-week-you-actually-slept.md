Every figure in this course arrived through an instrument, and lesson 1 said there are four of them
measuring four different things. **This project puts two of them on the same nights and compares
them**, which is the only experiment in this course you can run yourself.

It takes about four hours: half an hour at the start, ten minutes a day for a week, and about two
hours at the end, three weeks after you begin.

## Read this before lesson 2, not after lesson 6

Section 1 is the prediction, and lesson 1 asks for it before you have read anything else. **It can't
be written later**, because knowing what the comparison usually shows changes what you would
predict.

If you have already read further and want to do the project anyway, say so at the top of section 1
and write the prediction now. **A spoilt prediction honestly labelled is worth more than a clean one
that is not true**, and saying which is section 4's job in any case.

## The problem this project has, and how it solves it

**No section here can have a key**, because the answer is your week and nobody else has seen it. And
unlike most projects, you cannot even check your own answer: there's no third instrument here to
settle a disagreement between the first two.

**That is the point rather than a flaw.** You are going to end the week with two numbers for the
same nights and no way to decide which is right, which is exactly the position every figure in this
course came from. What you can do is say how far apart they are, and where.

**One consequence, said up front so it does not feel like a trick.** The project cannot be improved
by sleeping better during the week. A week arranged to produce tidy numbers gives you a comparison
of two instruments on a week you don't normally have.

## What you hand in

**One plain text or Markdown file, roughly 1,200 to 2,000 words, in four sections.** Plain text for
the reason **Digital Literacy**'s project gives.

## Section 1: the prediction, dated

Written before lesson 2 and not edited afterwards. Four things.

1. **How long you sleep on a work night**, your best estimate, in hours and minutes.
2. **How long you sleep on a free night**, the same.
3. **The midpoint of your sleep on a free night.** Halfway between falling asleep and waking. Lesson
   4 is where this gets its name; for now it is a number.
4. **How confident you are**, out of ten, where ten is certain. One number for all three.

**Do not edit a word of it afterwards**, including the parts you come to think are wrong. Those are
section 4's material.

## Section 2: the log

**One week. Every night, two records, made independently.**

The word independently is doing the work. **Write your own estimate down before you look at the
device**, every morning, or the comparison is worth nothing: once you have seen the device's number
you cannot un-see it, and what you write will be a memory of that number rather than of the night.

Four things a night.

1. **When you think you fell asleep.** Your estimate, written before you check anything.
2. **When you woke for the last time.** The same.
3. **How the night felt**, out of five. One number, no explanation.
4. **What the device said.** Total sleep, and the time it says you fell asleep, if it gives one.

**If you have no device**, do the diary twice instead: once on waking, and once at the end of the
day without looking at the morning's entry. That is a comparison of one instrument against itself at
two delays, it is a real measurement, and it answers a question the two-device version cannot.

Then one line at the end of the week: **whether logging changed what you did**. It usually does, and
saying so is better than pretending it didn't.

## Section 3: the comparison

**Night by night, not averaged.** The averaging is where the interesting part disappears.

For each night, three things.

1. **The two totals**, side by side.
2. **The gap between them**, in minutes, with a sign: did the device say more or less than you did?
3. **What the night was like**, from your out-of-five rating.

Then the arithmetic, and then four paragraphs.

**The two averages.** Your average estimate for the week, and the device's. Put your section 1
prediction beside both.

**The biggest gaps.** Which three nights did the two records differ most on, and what were those
nights like? **Lesson 1's source predicts that the worst agreement comes on the most disrupted
nights.** Say whether yours did.

**The direction.** Add up the signed gaps. **A device with the specificity figures lesson 1 quotes
should report more sleep than you did**, on most nights. Say whether yours did, and by how much on
average.

**And the night you cannot explain.** There is usually one where the two records disagree and
nothing about the night accounts for it. Write a paragraph on it. **A reader who has no such night
should say so**, which is also a result.

## Section 4: the decision, and what it costs

Lesson 8 gives you a way of sorting a claim and then a decision to make with it. **Make it here, on
your own sleep.**

Reach one of three endings.

**Ending one: change something.** Name it specifically enough that somebody could tell in a month
whether you had done it. Then name what it costs: minutes, something you will stop doing, or an hour
of your evening.

**Ending two: keep something deliberately.** You sleep six and a half hours, you have read all eight
lessons, and you are going to carry on. That is a decision as long as you write down why and what
evidence you are leaning on.

**Ending three: stop deciding about it.** If your week showed two instruments agreeing within twenty
minutes and nothing you would want to change, a sleep policy is a solution to a problem you do not
have.

For whichever you choose, three things.

1. **The decision**, in one or two sentences.
2. **What it rests on.** Your own numbers from section 3, and any finding from the course you are
   leaning on. **If a finding does not stretch as far as your decision, say so** and make the
   decision anyway with the gap named. Every experiment in this course was healthy adults in a
   laboratory, and you are not.
3. **What it costs.**

**And one line on what would tell you it was the wrong call**, which is the thing lesson 8 asks for
and the thing almost nobody writes.

## Rubric

| Criterion | What full marks look like |
|---|---|
| The prediction is untouched | Section 1 is dated, has all four numbers, and was not edited after the week began |
| The two records are independent | The diary entry for each night was written before that night's device figure was looked at, and the file says so |
| The log is complete rather than tidy | Bad nights and missed entries are recorded as carefully as good ones, and gaps are marked as gaps |
| The comparison is night by night | Differences given per night with a sign, not summarised into one average |
| The instruments are named and limited | Both records are described for what they measure, and neither is treated as the truth about the night |
| Scope is respected | No figure from the course is used past what it was measured on, and section 4 names the gap between a laboratory and you |
| The decision names its cost | Section 4 has something real on the other side of it |

**Two of those seven are about honesty rather than analysis**, and that is deliberate. A course whose
subject is what instruments can and cannot see would be funny in the wrong way if its project
rewarded two numbers that agreed.

## If the project is spoilt

**It happens and it is not a disaster.** If you never wrote the prediction, or looked at the device
before writing the diary, or missed three nights, the project can still be done and you say at the
top what went wrong.

**What you lose depends on which one.** A missing prediction costs you section 1 and nothing else:
log the week, compare it, and decide. **Looking at the device first costs you the comparison**,
because the two records are no longer independent, and the honest repair is to start the week again
rather than to write up a week where one number was copied from the other.

**The one thing not worth doing is reconstructing a week from memory.** You have now read a course
about how differently two records can report the same night, and a reconstructed log will agree with
whichever number you looked at last.

## A note on what this project is not

**It is not a health check.** It measures how far two records of the same nights disagree, which is a
fact about instruments. It is not a sleep study, it cannot detect a sleep disorder, and a week of it
says nothing about your health.

**If something is wrong with your sleep, lesson 1's list is the one to read**, and the answer there
is a doctor rather than a spreadsheet.
