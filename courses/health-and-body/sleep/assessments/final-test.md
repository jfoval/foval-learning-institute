  # --- Instruments, and what each measures (L1) ---
  - q: >-
      In the comparison of consumer sleep trackers against polysomnography, sensitivity was 0.93 or
      higher and specificity was between 0.18 and 0.54. What does that pair mean?
    options:
      - The devices often called sleep wake, but almost always recognised real wake as wake
      - The devices were accurate in both directions, within a few percent of the electrodes
      - The devices almost always caught real sleep, and often called quiet wake sleep too
      - The devices measured sleep stages well, but were unreliable about total sleep time
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Sensitivity is how often real sleep was called sleep, and it was high; specificity is how
      often real wake was called wake, and it was low to medium. So the devices rarely missed sleep
      and often counted wakeful stillness as sleep. The abstract calls their stage estimates
      inconsistent, which rules out the last option.
  - q: >-
      A new sleep app is tested on 30 healthy students over two ordinary nights and agrees well
      with the electrodes. A reader with broken, restless nights wants to know if it will work for
      them. What does lesson 1 suggest?
    options:
      - The test may flatter it, since devices tested so far did worse on disrupted nights
      - It should work equally well, since agreement with the electrodes was already shown
      - It will work better for them, since restless nights give the device more movement
      - Nothing at all, since a test on students cannot say anything about any other adult
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The comparison lesson 1 read found devices "tended to perform worse on nights with
      poorer/disrupted sleep", so a test on ordinary nights is likely to be the flattering case.
      That's a reason for caution about this reader, not proof the app will fail, and a test on
      students does tell you something, just not the thing they need.
  - q: >-
      An app tells you your body clock is running two hours late, because your watch recorded you
      falling asleep around 1 a.m. all week. Which question from this course does that claim fail?
    options:
      - The sample question, since the app's norms were built on other people's weeks
      - The scope question, since a single week is too short to say anything about you
      - The design question, since nobody compared you with a group that slept earlier
      - The clock question, since a sleep tracker isn't an instrument for body-clock timing
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      A body clock's timing is measured by a hormone assay under dim light, and a questionnaire
      approximates it; a wrist device estimates when you seemed to be asleep, and lesson 1 showed it
      does even that imperfectly. When you fell asleep and where your clock sits are different
      quantities, so the claim changed instrument without saying so.

  # --- The dose-response experiment (L2) ---
  - q: >-
      What did the fourteen-night restriction experiment show, and on whom?
    options:
      - That any sleep under eight hours damages long-term health, in a large national cohort
      - That 4 or 6 hours in bed cut performance cumulatively, in 48 healthy adults aged 21 to 38
      - That 6 hours is enough for most adults, since only the 4-hour group's performance fell
      - That performance fell for three days and then recovered, in 48 volunteers of all ages
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Randomised to 4, 6 or 8 hours in bed for 14 nights, the restricted groups showed cumulative,
      dose-dependent deficits on all tasks. The six-hour group declined too, the decline didn't
      level off, the participants were healthy adults of a narrow age band, and the outcome was
      performance over days, not long-term health.
  - q: >-
      A manager says her team works fine on six hours a night, because none of them has said they
      feel tired. What does the restriction experiment say about that reasoning?
    options:
      - It's sound, since sleepiness is the most sensitive sign of lost sleep that there is
      - It's sound for six hours, since only the four-hour group showed any decline at all
      - It proves the team is impaired, since the experiment measured people just like them
      - It's weak, since people held to six hours rated themselves barely sleepier than before
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Sleepiness ratings rose once and then barely moved, and didn't separate six hours from four,
      while measured performance kept falling; the authors say participants were "largely unaware".
      So not feeling tired can't settle it. It doesn't prove the team is impaired either, since a
      lab study of 48 people isn't a measurement of them.
  - q: >-
      A senior colleague tells a new hire: "I've done six hours a night for twenty years and I'm
      fine, so I'm obviously one of the people who need less." Which pair of facts from this course
      means the colleague can't know that?
    options:
      - People held to six hours in the lab felt fine too, and genuine short sleepers are rare
      - Nobody can stay healthy on six hours, and the recommendation is seven for everyone
      - Short-sleep genes are common, and feeling fine is the best evidence of carrying one
      - Twenty years is too short to see an effect, and the lab studied only young adults
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The restricted participants rated themselves barely sleepier while their performance fell,
      so feeling fine is what both groups would report; and the mutation linked to natural short
      sleep is described as rare. Together they give no way to place one person, which is the
      closing lesson's refusal. Some people do need less, so "nobody can" is wrong.

  # --- The recommendation and the cohorts (L3) ---
  - q: >-
      A meta-analysis reports that short sleepers had a relative risk of death of 1.12 compared
      with a reference group. What is the best plain reading?
    options:
      - Short sleepers had a 12 percent chance of dying during the studies
      - Short sleep causes a 12 percent rise in the risk of dying early
      - Short sleepers died about 12 percent more often, as an association
      - Short sleepers lived 12 percent fewer years than everyone else did
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      A relative risk compares rates: 1.12 means deaths were about 12 percent more frequent among
      short sleepers than in the reference group, observed in cohorts. It isn't an absolute chance
      of dying, it isn't a cause, and it says nothing about years of life.
  - q: >-
      The same meta-analysis found a larger relative risk for long sleep, 1.30, than for short. The
      usual explanation is that illness makes people sleep longer. What does this course say that
      explanation does to the short-sleep figure?
    options:
      - Nothing, since reverse causation is only possible for long sleep and not for short
      - The same kind of explanation is open for the short arm, since illness can shorten sleep
      - It confirms it, since explaining away the long arm leaves the short arm as the cause
      - It overturns it, since a figure that can be explained away was never real at all
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      If illness can lengthen sleep, illness and other conditions can shorten it, so the same kind
      of explanation is available for the short arm. That doesn't make either association unreal;
      it means neither is shown to be a cause by cohorts alone.
  - q: >-
      A cohort study reports that people sleeping under six hours had a relative risk of 1.12 for
      an outcome. A magazine later writes that short sleep "raises your risk by 12 percent". What
      has happened to the claim on the way?
    options:
      - An attribution moved to a more famous body than the one that said it
      - A small group was dropped so the remaining pattern looks cleaner
      - An association became something done to you, and its scope widened
      - A number was rounded up in the direction that suits the argument
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      "Raises your risk" turns a pattern observed in cohorts into something short sleep does to
      you, the reader: the verb changed from association to cause, and the scope widened from those
      people to everybody. The number itself survived, nothing was dropped, and nobody's name moved.

  # --- Timing, light and IARC (L4) ---
  - q: >-
      Two people get an hour of bright light at 5 in the morning. One ends up with an earlier body
      clock, the other with a later one. How can that be?
    options:
      - It can't, since the same light at the same time must move both clocks alike
      - One of them must have had brighter light than the other, whatever they reported
      - Light always delays the clock, so the one who moved earlier did so for other reasons
      - 5 a.m. fell late in one person's biological night and early in the other's
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Light late in the biological night advances the clock and light early in it delays it, and
      the same clock time can fall at different points in two people's cycles. Nothing requires a
      difference in brightness, and light doesn't always delay.
  - q: >-
      One hour of bright light produced about 40 percent of the effect that 6.7 hours produced. A
      product claims its lamp works "seven times better" if used for seven hours instead of one.
      What does the light experiment suggest?
    options:
      - The claim is unlikely, since the first hour did far more than its share
      - The claim is roughly right, since light's effect adds up hour by hour
      - The claim is too modest, since longer exposure multiplies the effect
      - The claim is untestable, since no study has used more than one hour
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Fifteen percent of the exposure produced forty percent of the effect, a non-linear response,
      so extra hours bought less than the first. The experiment compared one hour with 6.7, so the
      claim can be weighed against it, and nothing there multiplies.
  - q: >-
      A newspaper says night shift work is "officially a cancer risk, as bad as" another Group 2A
      agent. What does this course say the classification tells a reader?
    options:
      - That both agents raise cancer risk by the same amount, since they share a group
      - How strong the evidence is that something can cause cancer, not how much risk it adds
      - That night work has been proven to cause cancer, since the group requires proof
      - That night workers should change jobs, since the agency's grouping recommends it
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      IARC's groups grade the strength of the evidence, and 2A is the second highest of four. They
      carry no risk figure, so two agents in one group needn't carry similar risks; 2A is
      "probably", not proven; and the classification makes no recommendation about anybody's job.

  # --- Sleep and memory (L5) ---
  - q: >-
      A study has one group learn word lists at 8 in the evening and sleep, and another learn at 8
      in the morning and stay awake, both tested twelve hours later. The sleep group does better.
      Besides sleep, what could explain the difference?
    options:
      - Nothing, since both groups waited exactly twelve hours between learning and the test
      - The wake group forgot less, since being awake gives more chances to rehearse the lists
      - The groups learned and were tested at different times of day from each other
      - The sleep group studied harder, since evening learners are known to be more motivated
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      This is the night-against-day design, and its groups are on different clocks: one learns in
      the evening and is tested in the morning, the other the reverse. Equal intervals don't remove
      that. The rehearsal option would predict the opposite result, and nothing says anything about
      motivation.
  - q: >-
      A pooled estimate of the sleep benefit for one design is g = 0.10, with an interval from
      -0.25 to 0.45, from twelve samples. What is the fairest reading?
    options:
      - This design hasn't detected a benefit and can't rule out a moderate one either
      - This design shows sleep has no effect on memory, since the interval includes zero
      - This design shows a small but reliable benefit of about a tenth of a deviation
      - This design is flawed, since other designs in the literature found a benefit
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      An interval running from below zero to 0.45 contains no effect and a moderate one, so it
      settles neither. "No effect" reads a failure to find as a finding; "reliable" ignores that
      zero is inside the interval; and a design isn't flawed because its answer differs.

  # --- Guidelines and their grades (L6) ---
  - q: >-
      A guideline gives a talking therapy a strong recommendation and a medicine a weak one. A
      reader concludes the therapy works better. What is the best reply from lesson 6?
    options:
      - That's correct, since a strong grade is only given to the more effective treatment
      - That's wrong, since weak grades are given to treatments shown not to work in trials
      - The grades can't be compared, since strong and weak mean different things for drugs
      - Grades reflect certainty and trade-offs, and the two weren't compared head to head
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Grades weigh the quality of evidence, benefits against harms and patient values, and the drug
      guideline says a weak grade "should not be construed as an indication of ineffectiveness". In
      lesson 6's documents the drugs and CBT-I were each compared with other things, not with each
      other.
  - q: >-
      A friend with years of poor sleep reads that sleep hygiene "doesn't work" and throws out every
      habit she had. What does the 2021 guideline actually say?
    options:
      - That sleep hygiene is harmful and should be avoided by people with insomnia
      - That on its own it shouldn't be the treatment for diagnosed chronic insomnia
      - That sleep hygiene is the right first treatment, since it's cheap and safe
      - That sleep hygiene has been shown to have no effect in any trial of any kind
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The recommendation is against sleep hygiene as a single-component therapy for chronic
      insomnia disorder, on low-quality evidence, while saying it may be part of a package and that
      some common-sense principles may help. It isn't called harmful, and one trial found a higher
      responder rate than a wait-list.

  # --- Compression of a claim (L7) ---
  - q: >-
      A blog quotes a sentence about screen time correctly, but credits it to a national medical
      academy. It actually came from a single researcher's conference talk. What is the most useful
      thing lesson 7 suggests doing?
    options:
      - Find the original source and see who said it, and whether the academy ever did
      - Dismiss the sentence, since a wrong attribution means the claim itself is false
      - Accept the sentence, since the wording is correct whoever turns out to have said it
      - Assume the blogger lied, since attributions don't move to famous bodies by accident
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      An attribution moving to a more authoritative name is one of the shapes a claim takes, and the
      remedy is to check who said it. A wrong attribution doesn't make the sentence false, a correct
      wording doesn't make it the academy's, and nothing here establishes intent either way.

  # --- Sorting a claim (L8) ---
  - q: >-
      Sort this sentence: "Deep sleep flushes the toxins out of your brain, so skimping on it
      poisons you." What is the best sorting?
    options:
      - Measurable as a whole, since every part of it has already been tested and confirmed
      - False as a whole, since this course read no evidence about deep sleep at all
      - Two claims welded, a mechanism to check and then a harm with no quantity in it
      - Unmeasurable as a whole, since nothing about the brain can be measured during sleep
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The first half makes a claim about what happens during deep sleep that a study could check,
      and this course read nothing on it. The second half's "poisons you" names no quantity, dose or
      outcome, which makes it unmeasurable as stated. Each half needs its own verdict, and not
      having read about something isn't evidence it's false.

Most of the questions give you a claim, a study or a situation that isn't in any lesson and ask you
to apply what the course taught to it. A few ask what a named source found. Where a figure appears
