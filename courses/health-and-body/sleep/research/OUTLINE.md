# Outline: Sleep

*Stage 2, 2026-09-19. Built from `research/SOURCES.md`, which was written in three passes the same
day: the core literature, then the circadian gap, then light. Sequence for how the ideas build.*

## How many lessons, and why

**Eight.** The subject divides cleanly into four questions and a closing pair, and the count comes
from the questions rather than from a target.

1. **What is this measured with?** One lesson, first, because every number in the course carries the
   limits of its instrument and because the course has an unusually good source on exactly that
   (SOURCES §6). It also does the job Note-Taking lesson 1 and Memory lesson 1 did: it sets the scope
   and the refusals before any figure appears.
2. **What happens when you take sleep away?** One lesson, on the one randomised dose-response
   experiment in the file (§1). This is the course's strongest evidence and its narrowest.
3. **What is the recommendation, and what is it made of?** One lesson, on the consensus statement and
   the mortality meta-analysis (§2, §3), which is where the course's central distinction lives.
4. **When, rather than how much?** One lesson, on timing: chronotype, the phase response curve, and
   shift work (§12, §14, §13).
5. **What does sleep do for memory?** One lesson, because this course's two prerequisites are about
   learning and memory and a reader arrives expecting it (§7).
6. **What is offered to somebody sleeping badly?** One lesson, on the three guidelines (§4, §5, §11).
7. **What happened when a bestseller compressed this literature?** One lesson (§8). This is the
   neutrality set-piece and it is the only lesson in the course built on documents a reader can check
   end to end in an afternoon.
8. **How do you read the next claim you meet?** One closing lesson, in the institute's standing form.

**A ninth lesson on sleep disorders was considered and rejected.** Apnoea, narcolepsy, restless legs
and the parasomnias are a clinical literature this course has not searched, and a lesson built on
nothing would be exactly the failure Personal Finance lesson 6 recorded. Lesson 1 names them as out
of scope and says where to go instead, which is a doctor.

## What is deliberately left out, and said so in the lessons

- **Sleep disorders**: apnoea, narcolepsy, restless legs, parasomnias. Not searched. Named in lesson
  1 with the safety line attached.
- **Children and adolescents**, including school start times. A separate literature with live policy
  arguments. The course's prerequisites and every source in it are adult.
- **Screens, lamps and ordinary indoor light.** SOURCES §14 covers one hour of 8000 lux in a
  laboratory and nothing else. **Lesson 4 says the course has no evidence about screens and the
  course then never mentions them again**, which is the honest treatment of the single most
  advised-about thing in the subject.
- **Dreams and dream content.** Nothing in the file.
- **Naps.** The only thing the file has is a design category inside a meta-analysis read at
  search-summary level, which is not enough to teach from.
- **Supplements other than melatonin**, which appears only because a named guideline names it.

## Six decisions, settled here so no lesson re-opens them

**1. Every figure states its read level at the point of use.** Two abstracts in this course were read
verbatim in full (§1, §6, and §14 makes three); the rest are fragments, guideline recommendations, or
search summaries. The scope table in lesson 1 carries the levels, and a lesson repeats the level
wherever it prints a number, in the form Note-Taking used.

**2. No lesson gives medical advice.** The treatment lesson reports what named guidelines recommend,
with their grades, and stops. **It does not tell a reader what to take, what to stop, or how to run
sleep restriction therapy**, and it carries the safety lines from SOURCES' safety section where a
reader might take description for instruction.

**3. The line between the experimental and the observational literature is drawn every time it is
crossed.** "Associated with" is the consensus statement's own verb and the course keeps it. A lesson
using §2's outcome list or §3's relative risks says, in the sentence, that these are associations in
cohorts and not results of an experiment.

**4. The `unread:` line exempts Walker for lesson 07 only.** `SOURCES.md` names Matthew Walker and
Kryger as unread. Lesson 7 must name the book to discuss the dispute, so the line becomes
`<!-- unread: Walker (except 07), Kryger -->` **before lesson 7 is drafted**. Every quotation from
the book in that lesson reaches the course through the critic's essay, at one remove, and the lesson
says so at each one.

**5. Shift workers are people with jobs.** Lesson 4 carries the IARC classification. It does not
imply that night work is a lifestyle error, does not tell anybody to change shifts, and states what
the course cannot say: how much risk, for whom, and what to do. A nurse reading that lesson should
finish it better informed and not accused.

**6. The course's own addition to the institute's claim sort is the clock question.** Memory added
the sample, Focus and Deep Work added the instrument, Note-Taking added the setting. **This course
adds: is this claim about how much, or about when?** Almost every claim a reader meets is about
duration, the two are measured by different instruments with different gold standards, and a sentence
about one licenses nothing about the other. It is introduced in lesson 4 and used in lesson 8.

## Lesson sequence

### 1. Four instruments and one word (~80 min)

**Objectives.**
- Name the four instruments sleep research uses and say what each one can and cannot measure
- Read a sensitivity and specificity pair together and say what the two numbers mean about a device
- State what this course read, at what level, and what it therefore cannot say

**Core ideas.** Everybody uses one word for four different measurements. Polysomnography is
electrodes, a laboratory and a scored record. Actigraphy is movement at the wrist. A consumer device
is movement plus heart rate through a model nobody outside the company has seen. A sleep diary is a
person remembering. **The course's scope table** goes here, in the form Note-Taking lesson 1 used:
each source, what it measured, on whom, and how far this course got into it.

**Mechanism to explain.** Why a device that guesses "asleep" is right most of the time: most of the
night is sleep, so a detector biased towards sleep scores well on accuracy and badly on the thing you
care about. That is what §6's pair says, and the two numbers have to be read together.

**Worked examples.** (a) Simple: §6's own figures. Sensitivity at or above 0.93 across every device;
specificity from 0.18 to 0.54. Work out what that means for a night in which you lay awake for an
hour. (b) With a wrinkle, and a gap the reader fills before the answer: a constructed case where a
diary and a wrist device disagree about the same night by ninety minutes, and the reader says which
is wrong. **The answer is that the question is wrong**: they measure different things, and the
disagreement is data.

**Misconceptions.** "My tracker knows how much I slept." "Sleep stages on a watch are measured."
(§6: "Device sleep stage assessments were inconsistent.") "A laboratory measurement is the real
number, so my normal nights are like that."

**Practice.** (1) Take one night, write down your own estimate of when you fell asleep and how long
you slept, and then say which instrument would have to exist to check it. (2) Find a sleep claim with
a number in it and name the instrument behind the number; if you cannot, that is the result.

**Sources.** §6 for the device figures; §1, §2, §3 and §14 for the scope table's read levels; §10
named and not used; the safety lines for what is out of scope.

**Connects.** Back to **Focus and Deep Work** lesson 1, which is the instrument question, and to
**Memory** lesson 7, which is the sample question. Forward to every lesson, all of which carry a
number from a named instrument.

**Spaced review of:** nothing yet; this is lesson 1.

### 2. Fourteen nights at four, six and eight hours (~85 min)

**Objectives.**
- State the design of the dose-response experiment and what it measured
- Explain what "cumulative, dose-dependent deficits" means in terms a reader can restate
- Say what the experiment licenses and what it does not, given its participants and its outcome

**Core ideas.** SOURCES §1, the spine of the course. Forty-eight healthy adults aged 21 to 38,
randomised to 4, 6 or 8 hours in bed for **fourteen consecutive nights**, with a separate arm of
three nights with no sleep at all, under continuous laboratory monitoring. Cognitive performance fell
cumulatively and by dose, on all tasks. **And the finding that makes the lesson**: subjective
sleepiness responded at once and then barely moved, and "did not significantly differentiate the 6 h
and 4 h conditions". People at four hours felt about as sleepy as people at six and performed much
worse.

**Mechanism to explain.** The difference between an acute response and a cumulative one, which is
what "dose-response" and "over 14 consecutive days" are doing in the same sentence. And the
difference between a measured outcome and a model parameter: the 15.84 hour figure comes from "a
statistical model revealed", which the abstract says in terms.

**Worked examples.** (a) Simple: read the Results sentence clause by clause and say, for each clause,
what was measured and on whom. (b) With a wrinkle: a constructed reader who has slept six hours a
night for a fortnight and reports feeling fine. What does this study license about them, and what
does it not? **It licenses an expectation about unnamed cognitive tasks in a laboratory and
nothing about their driving, their work or their health**, and saying exactly that is the exercise.
**The abstract names no task**, so no lesson may name one; an earlier draft of this outline said
"reaction-time task" and that was an inference, corrected 2026-09-19.

**Misconceptions.** "Six hours is fine for me, I've adapted." "Sleep debt is a metaphor." (The paper
proposes the opposite, in its own words, and the lesson quotes the proposal as a proposal.) "This
proves short sleep is dangerous."

**Practice.** (1) A claim-to-design exercise: given the finding, write the study you would have to
run to show that six hours harms *health* rather than performance, and notice how much bigger it is.
(2) Predict, before reading the answer, what the subjective-sleepiness result was, then read it.

**Sources.** §1 throughout, at abstract level, which the lesson states twice.

**Connects.** Back to lesson 1's instruments: this one used polysomnography overnight and
performance tasks the abstract does not name.
Forward to lesson 3, which is the same subject measured a completely different way.

**Spaced review of:** lesson 1's instrument question, applied to two measures that moved differently.

### 3. Seven hours, and what that number is made of (~85 min)

**Objectives.**
- State the consensus recommendation and identify the verb it uses about health outcomes
- Compare an experimental finding with an observational one on the same subject and say what each
  licenses
- Explain what the long-sleep arm of the mortality curve is and why it matters to reading the short
  arm

**Core ideas.** SOURCES §2 quoted: "Adults should sleep 7 or more hours per night on a regular basis
to promote optimal health", the full list of outcomes short sleep "is associated with", the
uncertainty about more than nine hours, and the sentence about individual variation. Then SOURCES §3:
16 studies, 27 cohort samples, **1,382,999 participants**, short sleep RR **1.12** [1.06 to 1.18],
long sleep RR **1.30** [1.22 to 1.38]. **The lesson is the pair.** Lesson 2's evidence is causal,
narrow and about a fortnight; this evidence is associational, enormous and about years, and the two
get spoken in one breath.

**Mechanism to explain.** What a relative risk of 1.12 is, in terms somebody can hold: twelve per cent
more of whatever the baseline rate was, not twelve per cent of anything absolute. **Logic and
Argument** taught converting relative to absolute and this course uses it rather than re-teaching it.
And reverse causation, worked on the long arm and then turned on the short arm, which is the move
that makes the lesson.

**Worked examples.** (a) Simple: take one outcome from §2's list and write the two sentences it
supports, the associational one and the causal one, and say which the source licenses. (b) With a
wrinkle: the long-sleep arm. Its risk is *larger* than the short-sleep arm's. Nobody thinks sleep
kills. The standard explanation is that illness lengthens sleep. **Now apply that same explanation to
the short arm and see what survives.**

**Misconceptions.** "Everybody needs eight hours." (The recommendation says seven or more and names
no upper bound.) "Sleeping too long is dangerous." "Short sleep causes heart disease." "The
recommendation is a target to hit exactly."

**Practice.** (1) Convert one of §3's relative risks into what it would mean for a stated baseline
rate, and say what you would need to know to do it for yourself. (2) Find a health claim about sleep
in the wild and mark, in its own words, every place it turns an association into a cause.

**Sources.** §2 verbatim, §3 in fragments, with both read levels stated. §1 for the contrast.

**Connects.** Back to lesson 2 and to **Logic and Argument**'s relative-to-absolute conversion.
Forward to lesson 7, where a bestseller does exactly what this lesson warns about.

**Spaced review of:** lesson 2's design, set beside a cohort study.

### 4. When, not how long (~85 min)

**Objectives.**
- Distinguish a claim about sleep duration from a claim about sleep timing, and name the instrument
  each rests on
- State what light does to circadian timing, including the direction reversal and the non-linearity
- State what an IARC Group 2A classification does and does not say

**Core ideas.** Timing is a second quantity and almost nothing a reader has met is about it. SOURCES
§12 gives chronotype, the mid-point of sleep on free days corrected for oversleep, and the laboratory
gold standard, dim-light melatonin onset. SOURCES §14 gives what light does: one hour at 8000 lux
moved the clock by a fitted peak-to-trough amplitude of 2.20 hours, in the direction that depends on
when it landed. SOURCES §13 gives the one place where timing meets a hard outcome: night shift work,
IARC Group 2A.

**Mechanism to explain.** A phase response curve, in words: the same stimulus at different points in
the cycle produces opposite effects, so "light is good for you" and "light is bad for you" are both
incomplete sentences. And what "limited evidence in humans, sufficient evidence in animals" means in
IARC's grading, which is a statement about evidence strength rather than about risk size.

**Worked examples.** (a) Simple: two people get an hour of bright light, one at ten at night and one
at six in the morning, and their clocks move opposite ways. Work out which way each moves from the
quoted sentence. (b) With a wrinkle: the non-linearity. One hour produced about 40 per cent of the
shift that 6.7 hours produced, on 15 per cent of the exposure. **What does that imply about the
second hour, and what does it imply about a light box used for eight?**

**Misconceptions.** "Night owls are lazy." "Blue light before bed ruins your sleep." (**The course
has read no measurement of what a screen does and says so here, once, and then never mentions screens
again.**) "Group 2A means shift work gives you cancer." "Social jetlag is a diagnosis."

**Practice.** (1) Work out your own mid-sleep on free days from last week, and say what it is and is
not evidence of. (2) Take a piece of light advice you have been given and write down the two things
it would have to specify to be checkable.

**Sources.** §12 and §14 at their stated read levels; §13 at search-summary level, with the
classification's grades quoted and no risk figure attached.

**Connects.** Back to lesson 1's instruments, which gains a fifth: a hormone assay under constant
routine. Forward to lesson 8, where the clock question joins the institute's sort.

**Spaced review of:** lesson 1's instrument question; lesson 3's associational language, applied to
IARC's wording.

### 5. What sleep does for memory (~75 min)

**Objectives.**
- State what the pooled sleep benefit in episodic memory is, and at what read level this course holds
  it
- Explain why the size of the benefit depends on which design produced it
- Say what this course adds to **Memory** and **How to Learn Anything** and what it leaves to them

**Core ideas.** SOURCES §7, at search-summary level, which the lesson says in its first paragraph and
again at every figure: 823 effect sizes, 271 independent samples, 177 articles from 1967 to 2019, an
overall benefit of about g = 0.44, **and a moderator pattern that is the actual finding**. The
benefit is largest in designs that compare a day awake with a night asleep, smaller for naps, smaller
again for total sleep deprivation without recovery, and not significant for total sleep deprivation
with recovery nights.

**Mechanism to explain.** Why the day-wake against night-sleep design confounds sleep with time of
day: one group learns in the morning and is tested in the evening, the other the reverse, so anything
that varies across the day rides along with the sleep. **That reading is this course's own** and the
lesson labels it as an inference rather than as the authors' statement.

**Worked examples.** (a) Simple: sort the four design types by how much of the confound each removes,
and notice that the order matches the effect sizes. (b) With a wrinkle: a constructed study claiming
that a nap "doubled" recall. What design was it, and which of the four does it belong to?

**Misconceptions.** "Sleep consolidates memory, that's settled." "Pull an all-nighter and you lose
what you learned." (The design that tests exactly that, with recovery, is the one that found
nothing.) "Naps are as good as a night."

**Practice.** (1) Take one claim about sleep and learning and say which of the four designs would
have to have produced it. (2) Write the one sentence about sleep and memory you would be willing to
defend, with its read level attached.

**Sources.** §7 only, at search-summary level, stated repeatedly. The lesson prints **no figure from
the paper's results** beyond the four the summary supports.

**Connects.** Back to **How to Learn Anything** lessons 3 and 4 and to **Memory** throughout; neither
is re-taught. Forward to lesson 8.

**Spaced review of:** lesson 1's read levels; lesson 3's association-and-cause distinction.

### 6. What is offered to somebody sleeping badly (~85 min)

**Objectives.**
- State what chronic insomnia disorder is, how common it is, and what the first-line treatment is
- Compare the recommendation strengths across three guidelines and say what the pattern shows
- Identify the point at which a description of a treatment would become advice, and stop before it

**Core ideas.** SOURCES §4: the definition, 6 to 10 per cent of adults, and the two recommendations
quoted in full with their grades. SOURCES §5: the behavioural guideline's strong recommendation for
CBT-I, its conditional ones, and its sentence about sleep hygiene. SOURCES §11: fourteen
recommendations, **every one weak**, eight for a drug and six against one.

**The lesson's spine is the grade pattern**, which no retelling carries: a talking therapy with a
strong recommendation on moderate-quality evidence, and a pharmacopoeia with fourteen weak ones and
none strong. That is a fact about three documents, and the lesson presents it as one.

**Mechanism to explain.** What a guideline grade is: strength of recommendation and quality of
evidence are two separate axes, and a weak recommendation is not a finding of ineffectiveness, which
§11 says in its own words. Teaching that is what stops the lesson being read as "the pills don't
work".

**Worked examples.** (a) Simple: put the three guidelines' recommendations in one table with their
grades and read the column. (b) With a wrinkle: sleep hygiene. The 2021 guideline says it is not an
effective stand-alone therapy for the disorder. **Work out precisely what that does and does not
say**, because it is narrower than "sleep hygiene doesn't work" and much narrower than "don't
bother".

**Misconceptions.** "Melatonin is the safe natural option." "Sleeping pills fix insomnia." "Good
sleep hygiene is the treatment." "CBT-I is just being told to relax." (§4 lists its components in the
guideline's own words.)

**Practice.** (1) Take one over-the-counter sleep product and find which, if any, of the three
guidelines names it, and what grade it carries. (2) Write down what you would ask a clinician, given
what the guidelines say, without deciding anything yourself.

**Sources.** §4 verbatim, §5 at publisher-summary level, §11 in part. **The safety section of
`SOURCES.md` is mandatory reading for whoever drafts this lesson** and its lines go in the body,
not in a footnote.

**Connects.** Back to lesson 1's scope. Forward to lesson 8's decision.

**Spaced review of:** lesson 3's associational language, now in a guideline's grading.

### 7. A bestseller, a critic, and a reply (~85 min)

**Objectives.**
- Trace one claim from a research literature into a popular paragraph and name what changed
- State what a documented dispute about a book does and does not establish about the field
- Describe the strongest version of the case on each side

**Core ideas.** SOURCES §8, the three documents. A bestselling book this course has not opened; a
long, specific public critique; and the author's own written reply. **The reply concedes two of the
charges in writing**, which is what makes the case teachable rather than a row: the WHO attribution
was wrong and will be corrected, and the missing column of a graph is explained by the author with a
reason the reader can weigh.

**Mechanism to explain.** How a claim changes shape on the way from a literature into a paragraph:
an attribution moves from one body to a more famous one; a column with a small sample is dropped and
the remaining shape reads cleaner than the data; a hedged association becomes a flat statement.
**None of those requires dishonesty**, which is the part a reader will resist and the part the lesson
must land.

**Worked examples.** (a) Simple: the WHO attribution. The claim, the correction, and the two
sentences side by side. (b) With a wrinkle: the missing column. The critic's account, the author's
account, and the question of what a reader should do when a small subgroup runs against the trend.
**Both accounts are given at their strongest and the lesson reaches no verdict on the person.**

**Misconceptions.** "The book is discredited, so the science is wrong." (**Sources 1 to 6 are not in
dispute**, and the lesson says so plainly.) "The critic proved the claims false." (He argued they
were unsupported; this course has read none of his underlying sources and says so.) "An author
correcting a book means it was dishonest."

**Practice.** (1) Read the two documents side by side, both free, and mark one claim each way: one
where you find the critic's case strong and one where you find the reply's strong. (2) Take a
popular-science paragraph on any subject and name the three things you would check first.

**Sources.** §8a not read at all, §8b and §8c read in part at the levels stated. **Every quotation
from the book reaches this lesson through the critic's essay and the lesson says so at each one.**
**The `unread:` exemption for Walker keys to this lesson number and nothing else.**

**Connects.** Back to lesson 3, whose distinction is the one at issue; back to **Note-Taking** lesson
3, where a famous study travelled without its conditions, and to **Memory** lesson 4's trace.

**Spaced review of:** lessons 2 and 3's figures, now seen being compressed.

### 8. Reading a claim about your sleep (~85 min)

**Objectives.**
- Sort a claim about sleep into measurable, unmeasurable as stated, or false, and say what would
  settle it
- Ask the clock question of a claim: is it about how much, or about when, and which instrument
  measured it
- Decide what, if anything, to change about your own sleep, and say what the decision rests on and
  what it costs

**Core ideas.** The institute's standing three-way sort, used rather than re-taught, in the form
**Digital Literacy** lesson 8 gave it. **Then this course's addition: the clock question.** Duration
and timing are different quantities with different instruments and different gold standards, and a
sentence about one licenses nothing about the other. Then the sources side by side with what each
licenses. Then the refusal.

**The refusal is specific to this course and it is the reason lesson 8 exists.** **This course cannot
tell any reader how much sleep they need.** The population recommendation is seven or more hours
(§2). A rare minority demonstrably need about six and a quarter and are fine (§9). And the
participants in §1 who were not fine also reported feeling fine. **Those three facts sit together and
no reading of them produces a personal number**, which is a harder refusal than any course before this
one has had to make, because a reader who wants a number will take one.

**Worked examples.** (a) Four claims sorted, one of each kind plus one that is two claims welded, each
worked, all invented for the lesson and labelled as invented. (b) The source table with what each one
licenses, in the form **Memory** lesson 7 used, with the clock question as a column.

**Misconceptions.** "Somebody must have checked." "The science says eight hours." "If I feel fine I
am fine." (§1's participants reported exactly that.)

**Practice.** (1) Trace one claim about sleep back to its source in twenty minutes, with endings
named, including the ending where the time runs out. (2) **The decision**: one thing to change, keep
deliberately, or stop deciding about, with what it rests on and what it costs, and one line on what
would tell you it was the wrong call.

**Sources.** All. **Connects:** back to the whole course; out to nothing on the path.

**Spaced review of:** everything, which is what a closing lesson is.

### Project: the week you actually slept

**About four hours, spread over three weeks.** A prediction, a week of measurement by two instruments
at once, and a comparison. **It is deliberately not the same shape as Note-Taking's project**: that
one counted a behaviour, this one compares two measurements of the same nights, which is this
course's own subject.

**Section 1, the prediction, written before lesson 2 and not edited afterwards.** How long you sleep
on a work night and on a free night, your mid-sleep on free days, and how confident you are out of
ten. It is the exercise in lesson 1 and it cannot be written later.

**Section 2, the log.** One week. Every night, two records made independently: a diary line written
before you look at anything, and whatever device or phone you already have. **A reader with no device
does the diary twice, once on waking and once at the end of the day**, which is a different comparison
and an equally good one. Four things a night: when you think you fell asleep, when you woke, how you
rated the night out of five, and what the device said.

**Section 3, the comparison.** The two records side by side, night by night. Where they agree, where
they differ, and by how much. Then: which nights did they differ most on, and what were those nights
like? §6 predicts that the worst agreement comes on the most disrupted nights, and **a reader whose
data disagrees with that has found something worth writing down**.

**Section 4, the decision, and what it costs.** Lesson 8's three endings. Change something, keep
something deliberately, or stop deciding about it, with what it rests on, what it costs, and one line
on what would tell you it was the wrong call.

**Rubric, six rows**: the prediction is untouched; both records are complete rather than tidy;
the comparison is night by night rather than averaged; the instruments are named and their limits
stated; scope is respected, with no figure used past what it was measured on; and the decision names
its cost. **Two of the six are about honesty rather than analysis**, as in the courses before it.

## Neutrality notes

**This is a sensitive domain under standards 3.4** and the neutrality audit is mandatory before
publish.

- **Lesson 3** is where a course like this usually fails: it is easy to write the consensus
  recommendation as settled fact and the mortality figures as its proof. Both are quoted with the
  verb they came with, and the long-sleep arm is given the same reverse-causation reading as the
  short arm.
- **Lesson 4** touches people's livelihoods. Counted before reading for tone: shift work gets its
  classification, its evidence grades, its caveat, and no advice. There is no sentence in which a
  night worker is doing something wrong.
- **Lesson 6** is about treatments people are taking now. A weak recommendation is not a finding of
  ineffectiveness and the lesson says so in the guideline's own words; nobody is told to stop
  anything.
- **Lesson 7** is the set-piece and the ideological Turing test runs in both directions. A reader
  should finish it able to say why an author writing for a general audience compresses, and why a
  critic thinks the compression changed the claims. **No verdict on either person.**
- **Lesson 8's refusal** is the neutrality safeguard for the whole course: it refuses to give a
  reader a personal number in either direction, including the reassuring one.
