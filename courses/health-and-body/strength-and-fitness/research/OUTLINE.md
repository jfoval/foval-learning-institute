# Outline: Strength and Fitness

*Stage 2, 2026-09-24. Built from `research/SOURCES.md` (Parts R1 to R5 and the synthesis at its foot).
Sequenced for how the ideas build. A sensitive domain under standards 3.4 (medicine): every lesson
gets two fresh-context reviewers, as Nutrition and Mental Fitness did, and the neutrality audit is
mandatory before publish.*

## How many lessons, and why

**Ten.** (Planned as nine; lesson 4 was split at its named seam on 2026-09-24 when its first
draft measured 140 minutes.) The subject has two halves that the taxonomy row names together, "programming, technique,
progression", and a health course's obligation to say what the evidence is. SOURCES' first finding
decides the shape: **the big effect is starting, and the small effects are the arguments**, so the
course teaches the reader to start and to keep going before it teaches them to argue, and it teaches
the arguments as a way of reading evidence rather than as a list of optimisations.

1. **How much, and what it rests on.** The guidelines, the curve, and the three sentences (changes
   the body / active people live longer / you will live longer). Scope, callout, screening.
2. **How hard: effort you can measure.** METs, the talk test, 0 to 10, heart rate and its error,
   perceived exertion, repetitions in reserve, and what a watch can and cannot tell you.
3. **Building endurance.** VO2max, what adapts, continuous against intervals, dose and
   "non-responders", zone 2 as a claim, and starting to walk or run without getting hurt.
4. **How strength adapts.** Starting as the big effect, neural then muscular, specificity. The
   course's question introduced: trained whom, for how long, measured on what?
5. **What the variables do.** Load, sets, effort and frequency, individual variation, and how the
   advice changed between ACSM 2009 and 2026.
6. **Technique: six movements and the rules about them.** Squat, hinge, horizontal and vertical push, horizontal and vertical
   pull (the NSCA's six; the lunge sits in its squat family, and no carry is taught because nothing
   read covers one);
   what each cue is for; what the famous rules rest on; how often lifting actually hurts people.
7. **Writing and progressing a plan.** A first plan built from ACSM 2026, how to progress it, what a
   log is for, stalls, deloads, periodisation, cardio alongside, stopping and starting again.
8. **Warm-up, stretching, soreness and recovery.** What each is for on the evidence, the soreness
   that is normal and the one that is an emergency, and the recovery industry.
9. **Training across a life.** Women, older adults, pregnancy, children, and the common conditions,
   each as "yes, with conditions", with the conditions attached.
10. **Reading a claim about your training.** The closing lesson: the sort with this course's question
   joined, the popular claims, practitioners' frameworks, and a decision.

**Why not fewer.** Endurance and strength adapt to different things (R2, R3), and a lesson that did
both would drop one side's mechanism. Technique and programming are separate skills with separate
evidence (R4 Part C, R2). **Why not more.** A separate lesson on screening would be thin (R4.1 to
R4.3 fit inside lesson 1); flexibility, soreness and recovery share one question, what each is for,
and fit one lesson; the special populations are safety pointers with conditions, not a clinical
course, and fit one.

**Lesson length.** Most should land between about 50 and 80 measured minutes. Lesson 7 carries
the most material and may run to about 90. **Measured minutes include the exercises and the
Sources list**, so a lesson can measure past 100 while its prose reads in about an hour. Lessons 1
to 3 and 5 measured 100 to 125 after Stage 4; each reviewer judged the length earned (lesson 2 by a
real walk, lesson 3 by two desk exercises of 36 minutes and a 33-note Sources list) and named the
padding, which the fixes cut. Lesson 3 at 125 is the longest; its seam, if a later review wants a
split, is between how endurance adapts and starting to walk or run.

## What is deliberately left out, and said so in lesson 1

- **Energy balance, exercise for weight loss, and protein.** Taught in Nutrition (its lessons 3 and
  5). This course states the boundary with the ACSM 2009 weight position's own hedge ("only modest
  weight loss", R5.27) and the PAG's "generally independent of body weight" (R1), and points there.
- **Exercise for depression and anxiety.** Mental Fitness lesson 5. Pointed to, not re-taught.
- **Sport-specific training, athletes, power and speed development**, competition lifting and
  bodybuilding. The evidence read is about healthy adults, mostly beginners.
- **Clinical exercise prescription** (cardiac rehabilitation, diabetes management, arthritis
  programmes, physiotherapy). Named in lesson 9 with where to go.
- **Supplements and drugs**, including creatine and anabolic steroids. Nutrition lesson 10 owns
  supplements; the creatine stand in R2.20 has declared industry conflicts. Creatine is named once,
  in lesson 10, as a claim with a stated conflict, or not at all.
- **Sleep and recovery tracking.** Sleep owns it; lesson 8 points there in one line.
- **Body image and appearance goals.** Value questions the course did not research; lesson 10 names
  them as reasons people train that the course does not adjudicate.

## Eight decisions, settled here so no lesson re-opens them

**1. The same callout is on every lesson, word for word, directly after the opening paragraph, and
a check enforces it.** The text is in SOURCES' "Safety-critical guidance":

> :::callout Before you train
> This course is education, not advice about your own body. If you have heart, kidney or metabolic disease such as diabetes, are pregnant, or have symptoms like chest discomfort, fainting or unusual breathlessness, talk to a doctor before you start or step up training. Stop and get medical help straight away for pain or pressure in the chest, neck, jaw or arms, dizziness, palpitations, or breathlessness out of all proportion to the effort, and call your local emergency number for chest pain. Cola-coloured urine, or muscle pain and swelling far worse than the session explains, needs a doctor the same day.
> :::

Sources for each clause: the disease categories and symptom list (ACSM 2015 via Whitfield 2017,
R4.1); stop and seek evaluation (AHA 2023, ADA 2016, NIA, R4.3); emergency for chest pain (NHS,
R4.3); pregnancy (ACOG 804's "thorough clinical evaluation", R4.20; PAR-Q+, R4.2); cola-coloured
urine and pain out of proportion (CHAMP, R4.4). The check is in `scripts/build.mjs` beside
Nutrition's, and its sentence is in `REPETITION_EXEMPT`. This is standards 2.5's "education, not
personalised advice" note, carried on every lesson because a reader may arrive at lesson 6 from a
search engine.

**2. "Trained whom, for how long, measured on what?" is this course's addition to the institute's
claim sort.** Memory added the sample, Focus and Deep Work the instrument, Note-Taking the setting,
Sleep the clock, Mental Fitness what the comparison group got, Nutrition "instead of what?". **A
claim about training is a claim about a population, a duration and an outcome measure**, and the
exercise literature's defaults are narrow: 8 to 12 weeks, young, often male, untrained, tested on
the lift they practised (R2.16, R2.5), or 9 men in a lab (R3.4c). Introduced in lesson 4 with the
specificity finding, used in every evidence lesson after it, joined to the sort in lesson 10. It
overlaps Memory's sample question, and lesson 4 says the difference in one sentence: the sample asks
who; this asks who, for how long, and scored on which test.

**3. The course teaches people to train, within named general-population guidance.** Nutrition
refuses prescriptions; this course cannot, because its taxonomy row is "programming, technique,
progression". The line: lessons show how to build, run and progress a plan **inside what WHO 2020,
ACSM 2026 and the NHS recommend for healthy adults**, label every choice with what it rests on, and
never prescribe for a condition, a pregnancy, a child or an injury. Those get the callout and a
pointer to a doctor, a physiotherapist or a qualified exercise professional.

**4. Every figure carries its read level and its scope at the point of use.** Most trials were read at
abstract level (SOURCES, "Open concerns"). A lesson says "the abstract reports" where that is all that
was read, and never states a detail below it. ACSM 2026 (R2.2) and Generation 100 (R1.9) were read in
full and are the course's anchors for that reason.

**5. Weight is a boundary, not a topic.** No lesson sets a weight, body-fat or calorie goal. Lesson 1
states the boundary; lesson 10 handles spot reduction as a claim about where fat comes off, not as
advice about losing it.

**6. Practitioners are frameworks, labelled.** Rippetoe (R5.8), Renaissance Periodization (R5.9) and
Stronger By Science (R5.10) are quoted in their own words where a lesson uses them, their commercial
interests stated once in the lesson that first uses each, and each set beside what the trials
measured. The SOURCE_NOTES treatment of Hormozi is the model: admire the framework and still ask for
the test.

**7. Two things are said at full strength on both sides and never resolved.** Whether activity
causes longer life (lesson 1; only one sceptic's own statement was read, Karvinen, R1.10, and the
lesson says so), and whether rounding the lower back under load is harmful (lesson 6; the caution
side read at abstract level only, R4.9). Zone 2 (lesson 3) has **no proponent statement read** and
the lesson says so rather than improvising one.

**8. Units.** Kilograms with pounds in brackets at first use in each lesson; heart rate in beats a
minute; "per cent" in bodies and quizzes alike (Sleep's course-wide review found them mixed).

## Lesson sequence

### 1. How much, and what it rests on (~70 min)
- **Objectives:** state what WHO 2020 and the US and UK guidelines recommend for adults, including
  muscle strengthening, and the shape of the curve they all lead with; distinguish the three
  sentences (exercise changes the body / active people live longer / becoming active will make you
  live longer) and name the kind of study that licenses each; explain what Generation 100 found and
  what its authors conclude from it; say what this course covers, what it leaves out, who should ask
  a doctor first, and where to go instead.
- **Core ideas:** WHO's 150 to 300 moderate or 75 to 150 vigorous minutes, strengthening on two or
  more days, "some physical activity is better than none", bouts of any length (R1.1, R1.2); the US
  and UK versions and the UK's order (R1.3, R1.4); the curve's steep start (R1.4 PAGAC); how few
  adults meet the strengthening guideline (R1.6, R2.2's ~30%); WHO's "high certainty" is for
  association, upgraded for size and dose-response (R1.1 methods); Generation 100 (R1.9); twins,
  reverse causation and MR as the other evidence (R1.10, R1.11); the screening model: who needs a
  doctor first and why fewer than before (R4.1, R4.2 PAR-Q+ linked, not reproduced).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why a trial of exercise and death is so hard to run (death is rare,
  follow-up long, controls exercise too, volunteers are healthy), in Generation 100's own words; why
  an observational association can be large and consistent and still not settle cause.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a reader who walks 20 minutes most days and does nothing for
  strength, placed against WHO's recommendations, minute by minute. (b) wrinkle: Generation 100's
  power calculation against what happened (expected 10% deaths, observed 4.7% in controls), and why
  the exploratory HIIT hazard ratio of 0.63 (0.33 to 1.20) cannot carry the authors' final sentence.
- **Misconceptions:** "exercise under 10 minutes doesn't count"; "if you can't do 150 there's no
  point"; "the guidelines are about cardio"; "trials prove exercise makes you live longer" and its
  mirror "it's all confounding"; "everyone must see a doctor before starting to walk".
- **Practice:** a week's log of the reader's own activity sorted into moderate, vigorous and
  strengthening against WHO; sort six headlines into the three sentences.
- **Sources:** R1.1 to R1.4, R1.6, R1.9 to R1.11, R4.1 to R4.3; R5.27 and PAG for the weight
  boundary.
- **Connects:** Logic and Argument (correlation and cause), Mental Fitness (the comparison group:
  Generation 100's controls exercised), Nutrition lesson 1 (cohorts and trials). Leads to lesson 2,
  which asks what "moderate" means.
- **Spaced review of:** Memory's sample question (who volunteered for Generation 100).

### 2. How hard: effort you can measure (~55 min)
- **Objectives:** define moderate and vigorous intensity in METs and in relative terms, and place an
  activity on each; estimate maximum heart rate by a formula and state how far a formula can miss one
  person; use the talk test, a 0 to 10 scale and repetitions in reserve to set effort, and say what
  each rests on; say what a wrist device measures well and badly.
- **Core ideas:** METs and the 2024 Compendium's bands (3.0 to 5.9, 6.0 and over) and its own warning
  that values are not individual (R3.2a); CDC's relative scale and talk test (R3.2b); 220 minus age's
  history (R3.3a), Tanaka's 208 minus 0.7 times age (R3.3b, abstract level), Martin 2025's individual
  limits of about ±18 to 24 beats (R3.3c); Borg's scale via Generation 100's anchors (R3.3d, and say
  where the anchors reached the course from); the talk test's link to the ventilatory threshold
  (R3.3e); RIR and how far off people are (R2.10, Halperin: about one rep); wearables: heart rate
  mostly acceptable, calories not (R3.9).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why a relative measure travels better than an absolute one (the same
  brisk walk is moderate for one person and vigorous for another); why breathing and speech track a
  real physiological boundary.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a 50-year-old's predicted maximum by both formulas and the range
  that Martin's limits put around it. (b) wrinkle: two people walking together at the same MET value,
  one who can sing and one who can barely speak: which of them is working "moderately"?
- **Misconceptions:** "maximum heart rate is 220 minus your age"; "my watch knows how many calories I
  burned"; "RPE times ten is your heart rate" (no source read: say the course found none); "if you
  can talk, you're not working".
- **Practice:** a talk test and a 0 to 10 rating on the reader's own walk at three paces, logged; a
  set of a bodyweight exercise with an RIR guess, then continued to check it (with the callout's stop
  signs restated).
- **Sources:** R3.2, R3.3, R2.10, R3.9.
- **Connects:** lesson 1's moderate and vigorous minutes; Sleep lesson 1's devices (sensitivity and
  specificity), which this lesson points to rather than repeats. Leads to lesson 3.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's curve (the bands matter less than moving at all).

### 3. Building endurance (~70 min)
- **Objectives:** explain what VO2max is, what limits it and what adapts with training; compare what
  continuous and interval training do to VO2max, with the population and duration each finding comes
  from; explain what "non-responder" means and what the dose evidence says about it; plan a first
  month of walking or running and say what the injury evidence does and does not support.
- **Core ideas:** VO2max and its limits (R3.1a, b; central and peripheral adaptations, R3.1c);
  Milanović (18 to 45, +1.2 mL/kg/min for intervals, abstract), Weston (273 patients, 6 of 10 trials
  from one group), Gillen (9 sprint, 10 continuous, 6 control, sedentary men, 12 weeks) (R3.4a to c);
  Generation 100's HIIT adherence of about 50% (R3.4d); Biddle and Batterham's debate in both voices
  (R3.4e); HERITAGE, Montero and Lundby, Ross 2015, and the statistics of non-response (R3.5, R3.6);
  zone 2 (R3.7, contested, no proponent read); Couch to 5K ends at 30 minutes, not 5 km (R3.8a);
  17.8 injuries per 1,000 hours in novices (R3.8c); the 10% rule tested (R3.8d); pronation (R3.8g);
  steps and where 10,000 came from (R1.7).
- **Mechanism to explain:** oxygen from air to mitochondria and which links training moves; why
  intervals can reach high intensities that continuous work cannot sustain.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: the Gillen headline ("one minute") taken apart by who, how long and
  measured on what. (b) wrinkle: the GRONORUN trial, where a gentler build-up did not reduce injuries:
  what it did and did not show about pacing a beginner.
- **Misconceptions:** "you need an hour of cardio"; "HIIT is simply better"; "some people just don't
  respond"; "never increase more than 10% a week"; "overpronators need special shoes"; "10,000 steps".
- **Practice:** write a four-week walking or run-walk plan using the talk test from lesson 2 and say
  which parts rest on evidence and which on convention; reread a HIIT headline with the course's
  question.
- **Sources:** R3.1, R3.4 to R3.8, R1.7, R1.8 (fitness and mortality, observational, one paragraph).
- **Connects:** lesson 2's measures; lesson 1's three sentences (fitness predicts survival: which
  sentence is that?). Leads to lesson 4, which asks the same questions of strength.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's Generation 100 (its HIIT arm reappears), lesson 2's talk test.

### 4. How strength adapts (~70 min) and 5. What the variables do (~70 min)

Drafted as one lesson and split at the seam named above: lesson 4 takes the objectives on neural
adaptation, specificity and the course's question, with "starting is the big effect" and individual
variation; lesson 5 takes
load, weekly sets, proximity to failure and frequency, and the 2009 to 2026
change in ACSM's advice, and opens by recalling lesson 4's question in a sentence. The plan below is
the joint one both halves were drafted from.

- **Objectives:** explain why early strength gains are largely neural and why progress slows; explain
  specificity and apply "trained whom, for how long, measured on what?" to a training claim; state
  what load, weekly sets, proximity to failure and frequency each do for strength and for muscle
  size, with the evidence's population and duration; state ACSM 2026's primary recommendation and
  what it says did not consistently matter.
- **Core ideas:** starting is the big effect (R2.2, R2.3); neural adaptation first (R2.4, with the
  disagreement on how many weeks); specificity (R2.5, Spitz d = 1.84 against 0.8; R2.13); load (R2.6:
  heavy for 1RM, similar growth across loads when sets are hard); volume and diminishing returns
  (R2.7, R2.8); frequency with volume equated (R2.9); failure optional, "near-failure" or 2 to 3 RIR in ACSM 2026's words (R2.10, R2.2);
  individual variation (R2.12, Hubal 0 to +250%); who these studies trained (R2.16); the 2026 stand
  against the 2009 one (R2.1, R2.2) as how guidance moves. The muscle-structure image (R5 Part H).
- **Mechanism to explain:** the nervous system learning to recruit and coordinate before muscle
  fibres grow; why a test on the trained lift overstates general strength.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: an 8-week trial's headline gain in bench press 1RM, re-read as
  specificity plus neural learning plus some muscle. (b) wrinkle: the load finding, where the same
  meta-analytic evidence says heavy wins for strength and loads are similar for growth: which answer
  you get depends on what was measured.
- **Misconceptions:** "you must lift heavy to build muscle"; "every set to failure"; "more is always
  better"; "early gains mean your muscles grew"; "train each muscle twice or it won't grow"; "women
  gain less" (R2.14, short studies).
- **Practice:** apply the course's question to three described studies; estimate the reader's own
  RIR on a set and check it.
- **Sources:** R2.1 to R2.14, R2.16, R2.19 (named, not resolved).
- **Connects:** lesson 2's RIR; lesson 3's dose and non-response, now for strength. Lesson 4 leads to lesson 5; lesson 5 leads to lesson 6
  (how to do the lifts) and lesson 7 (how to arrange them). **Introduces decision 2.**
- **Spaced review of:** Memory's sample question and Mental Fitness's comparison group, joined here.

### 6. Technique: six movements and the rules about them (~75 min)
- **Objectives:** describe the NSCA's six patterns (squat, hip hinge, horizontal and vertical push,
  horizontal and vertical pull), with the cue
  that matters most in each and what it is for; evaluate a technique rule against what the study
  behind it measured; state how often lifting injures people compared with other sports, with the
  population; explain breath-holding under load and who should avoid it.
- **Core ideas:** the NSCA's six-pattern teaching article (R5.31) as the frame; videos V1 to V6 (R5
  Part G) embedded where each pattern is taught; knees past toes (R4.8: hip and knee torque, seven
  men); lumbar flexion (R4.9, both sides, contested, with the pig-spine mechanism and the human outcome
  data); Valsalva and blood pressure (R4.10: five bodybuilders, 320/250 mmHg peaks, and the guidance);
  belts (R4.11: no gym evidence read); injury rates (R4.5, competitors, per 1,000 hours); strength
  work preventing sports injuries (R4.7); start below what you could do (R4.4).
- **Mechanism to explain:** a technique rule moves load between joints rather than removing it; what
  bracing does and what holding the breath does to blood pressure.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: the goblet squat taught start to finish, with the reason for each
  cue. (b) wrinkle: the knees-past-toes rule, where following it lowers knee torque and raises hip and
  back torque: a trade, not a safety fix.
- **Misconceptions:** "lifting is dangerous"; "never let your knees pass your toes"; "any rounding of
  the back will injure you" and its mirror "back position never matters"; "machines are safer".
- **Practice:** film or mirror-check one bodyweight pattern against the lesson's checklist; a
  worked-example gap on the hinge where the reader writes the cue before it is shown.
- **Sources:** R5.31, R5 Part G, R4.5, R4.7 to R4.11, R4.4, R2.13.
- **Connects:** lesson 4's specificity (a technique is a skill, and skills are specific). Leads to
  lesson 7.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 2's RIR (technique breaks down near failure) and lesson 1's screening.

### 7. Writing and progressing a plan (~85 min)
- **Objectives:** build a first full-body plan for a healthy beginner from ACSM 2026 and WHO, and say
  what each choice rests on; progress it using a log, and say what to change when progress stalls;
  compare a practitioner's framework (novice linear progression, volume landmarks) with what the
  trials measured; explain what happens when training stops and restarts, and whether aerobic work
  interferes.
- **Core ideas:** ACSM 2026's primary recommendation and its four-region view, labelled as the
  authors' view (R2.2); two sets per exercise, 2 to 3 RIR (ACSM 2026's "2–3 repetitions in reserve"); progression "likely a requirement only for
  those seeking continued longer term progress" (R2.2); double progression as one simple method,
  labelled as convention; Rippetoe's novice effect and his programme in his words, with its scope
  (young men) and interest (R5.8); RP's landmarks with "starting points, not gospel" and the soreness
  input problem (R5.9, R5.16); Nuckols on the two readings of the volume evidence (R5.10);
  periodisation (R2.11, R2.2); concurrent training (R2.15); detraining and regain (R2.17); Steele's
  six-year minimal-dose records (R2.16) as a long-term, uncontrolled picture; the 1RM estimate with
  its provenance (R2.18). Protein is a pointer to Nutrition lesson 5.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why gains slow (diminishing returns as the reader approaches their own
  limit; neural learning used up), and why a log beats memory for deciding what to change.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a two-day plan, four regions, two sets each, built line by line
  from the stand. (b) wrinkle: a log showing a six-week stall, and three candidate explanations
  (sleep, effort, the plan) with what each would predict in the next two weeks.
- **Misconceptions:** "you need a complicated periodised plan"; "cardio kills your gains"; "if you
  stop, you lose it all"; "more days is better"; "a programme that worked for someone else will work
  the same for you".
- **Practice:** write the reader's own plan on one page, with a column saying what each line rests on;
  a worked-example gap in the progression table.
- **Sources:** R2.2, R2.8, R2.10, R2.11, R2.15 to R2.18, R5.8 to R5.10, R5.16.
- **Connects:** lessons 4 to 6; the project starts here. Leads to lesson 8.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 4's diminishing returns and specificity; lesson 3's dose.

### 8. Warm-up, stretching, soreness and recovery (~60 min)
- **Objectives:** explain what a warm-up and stretching each do and do not do, on the evidence;
  distinguish ordinary delayed soreness from exertional rhabdomyolysis by its signs; evaluate a
  recovery method by asking whether it changes how you feel or how you adapt; explain what lactate is
  and is not.
- **Core ideas:** Behm 2016 (holds under and over 60 s, abstract), Thacker and Lauersen on injury
  (stretching RR 0.963), resistance training against stretching for range of motion (R4.12 to R4.14);
  FIFA 11+ (R4.15); DOMS, its timing and the repeated-bout effect (R4.16, R3.10e); lactate as fuel,
  and the physiologists' residual dispute kept out of the lesson's claims (R3.10); rhabdomyolysis
  signs and the spin-class series (R4.4); cold-water immersion reducing gains in one trial of 21 men
  (R4.17); massage and foam rolling (R4.18); overtraining (R4.19, abstract); sleep pointer.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why a warm-up raises performance; why soreness fades with repetition even
  though the training still works.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a ten-minute warm-up built from general movement and lighter sets
  of the session's first lift. (b) wrinkle: two people after a first spin class, one sore and one with
  cola-coloured urine on day two: the signs that separate them and what each should do.
- **Misconceptions:** "stretch before exercise to prevent injury"; "stretching after stops soreness";
  "lactic acid causes the soreness"; "if you're not sore it didn't work" (the course's inference,
  labelled); "ice baths help you grow"; "lifting makes you stiff".
- **Practice:** rebuild the reader's warm-up from lesson 7's plan; classify five recovery products by
  "feel or adapt?" with the evidence named.
- **Sources:** R4.4, R4.12 to R4.19, R3.10.
- **Connects:** lesson 7's plan; Sleep for recovery and sleep. Leads to lesson 9.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 4's specificity (range of motion is trained like anything else).

### 9. Training across a life (~65 min)
- **Objectives:** state what the evidence read shows about strength training for women, for older
  adults and for people with low bone mass, with the population and conditions of each finding;
  state what ACOG and the Canadian guideline recommend in pregnancy and the signs to stop; state what
  the evidence says about supervised youth training and growth plates; say, for back pain, type 2
  diabetes, high blood pressure and knee osteoarthritis, what the named guidance supports and when a
  professional has to be involved.
- **Core ideas:** Roberts 2020 (relative gains, short studies) and Hubal (R2.14, R5.15); older adults'
  gains to 80 and beyond (R2.14) and falls (R4.21, Cochrane uncertain for resistance alone); LIFTMOR
  "under highly supervised conditions" against AHA's low to moderate loads for severe osteoporosis
  (R4.21, contested only if scope is dropped); pregnancy (R4.20, Box 3 signs); youth (R4.22,
  supervision as the condition); low back pain (R4.23); diabetes (R4.24, hypoglycaemia and silent
  ischaemia); hypertension (R4.10, R4.24); knee OA (R4.25); AHA 2023 as a frame (R4.26).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why the same stimulus is adapted to at every age, and why the conditions
  attached to each permission are about what can go wrong in that body rather than about capacity.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a 72-year-old who wants to start, taken through screening, what the
  falls evidence supports, and what a first month could look like inside the guidance. (b) wrinkle:
  LIFTMOR's result placed beside AHA's advice for severe osteoporosis: both are right within their
  scope, and the lesson shows what dropping the scope does.
- **Misconceptions:** "lifting makes women bulky"; "older people can't gain strength"; "women with
  osteoporosis must not lift"; "children shouldn't lift, it stunts growth"; "rest a bad back";
  "pregnant women shouldn't lift".
- **Practice:** for four short cases, say what the guidance supports, what condition attaches, and who
  the person should ask; none of the cases is solved by the reader alone.
- **Sources:** R2.14, R5.15, R4.20 to R4.26, R4.10, R4.2.
- **Connects:** lesson 1's screening, lesson 4's evidence base (who was trained). Leads to lesson 10.
- **Spaced review of:** decision 2's question, applied to populations the trials under-sample.

### 10. Reading a claim about your training (~60 min)
- **Objectives:** sort a claim about training into established, contested or value, and ask of it
  "trained whom, for how long, measured on what?"; evaluate five popular claims against the tests
  recorded for them; say what this course cannot tell a reader about their own body and who can;

## Neutrality notes

Sensitive domain (medicine). The risk here is not political; it is (a) overclaiming exercise as
medicine, (b) the fitness industry's framing leaking in as fact, and (c) weight and body talk.
- **Causation and longevity** (lesson 1): both sides in their own words; count citations and last
  words.
- **HIIT, zone 2, failure, periodisation, lumbar flexion**: each side in its proponents' words where
  read; where only one side was read, the lesson says so.
- **Practitioners** (lesson 7): quoted in their own words, interest stated once, no sneering.
- **Weight and appearance**: decision 5; no goals set, value questions named and not adjudicated.
- **Populations**: women, older adults, pregnant people and children described by what the evidence
  shows and its conditions, never as fragile and never as without limits.
- **Institutions**: ACSM 2026's disclosed advisory-board tie and its authors' overlap with Currier 2023
  (R2.2) are stated once where the stand is first used, as a declared interest, without insinuation.
