Every trial in this course took some people, measured them, trained them for a set number of weeks
and measured them again. This project does the same to one person, you, and then asks of your own
result the question lesson 4 taught you to ask of everyone else's: **trained whom, for how long,
measured on what?** You screen yourself, write a plan, take three measures you can repeat, train and
keep a log for eight weeks, take the same measures again, and write a report on what the change can
and can't tell you. The minutes above count about eight hours of desk and test time over ten weeks,
not the training itself: an hour and a half for screening and the plan, the same for two baseline
sessions, fifteen minutes a week for the log, the same again for two re-tests, and two hours for the
report.

:::callout Before you train
This course is education, not advice about your own body. If you have heart, kidney or metabolic disease such as diabetes, are pregnant, or have symptoms like chest discomfort, fainting or unusual breathlessness, talk to a doctor before you start or step up training. Stop and get medical help straight away for pain or pressure in the chest, neck, jaw or arms, dizziness, palpitations, or breathlessness out of all proportion to the effort, and call your local emergency number for chest pain. Cola-coloured urine, or muscle pain and swelling far worse than the session explains, needs a doctor the same day.
:::

## Where it starts

The project starts from lesson 7, "Writing and progressing a plan", because the plan you run is that
lesson's last exercise, "Your plan on one page". The other lessons supply the tools. Lesson 1, "How
much, and what it rests on", gives you the guidelines and the screening form. Lesson 2, "How hard:
effort you can measure", gives you the talk test, the 0 to 10 scale, your three-pace rows and reps in
reserve. Lesson 3, "Building endurance", gives you your first four weeks of walking. Lesson 5, "What
the variables do", gives you the practice-effect exercise, which this project repeats at full length.
Lesson 6, "Technique", gives you the checks for each movement.

Keep reading while you train. Lesson 8's warm-up belongs in your sessions once you have read it, and
nothing in the later lessons spoils anything here.

## No section has a key

The answer is your eight weeks, and nobody else has seen them. There is no model report to check
yours against, and you can't check your own result either. You will end with a before, an after,
and no way of knowing what would have happened without the plan, because there is no comparison
group. That is the position of every small trial in this course with its control group taken away,
and the report is where you say exactly what that costs.

One consequence, said up front so it doesn't feel like a trick. A big improvement does not score
higher than a small one. A re-test that barely moved, written up honestly, can take full marks, and a
large gain that the report credits entirely to new muscle can't.

## Why eight weeks

Lesson 4's table of who these studies trained is the reason. The fifteen studies in the failure
meta-analysis ran a median of eight weeks, from 6 to 14. The thirteen in the free-weight and machine
analysis averaged about nine, and the volume analysis's studies about ten and a half, in its
preprint.[1] Eight weeks makes your trial the length of a typical one in this literature. So when
your report asks "for how long?", your answer is the same as theirs, and on that count at least the
comparison between your result and theirs is fair.

## What you hand in

One plain text or Markdown file, roughly 2,000 to 3,000 words, in five parts, plus the log. The log
can sit in the same file, in a spreadsheet, or in clear photographs of paper pages.

## Part 1: screening

Before anything else, fill in the [PAR-Q+](https://eparmedx.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/PARQPlus2025Fillable.pdf),
the free screening form lesson 1 described. It runs to four pages, and you complete it yourself, on
paper or on screen. Its terms say "You must use the entire questionnaire and NO changes are
permitted", so this project links to it and describes it rather than reprinting any of it.[2] Use
the whole form, from the link.

Its first page asks seven general questions about your health, which lesson 1 summarised. A yes to
any of them sends you to follow-up pages organised by condition, and a yes there directs you to seek
further information, pointing to its companion form, the ePARmed-X+, or to a qualified exercise
professional.[2]

This project's rule is stricter than the form: **any "yes" stops the project here** until you have
talked to a doctor or a qualified exercise professional. Some people who answer yes would be cleared
by the form's own follow-up pages, and that is the form working as designed. The project takes the
cautious line anyway, for the reason lesson 1 gave for its callout: whether a condition matters is a
clinical judgement, and a course can't make it for you. After that conversation, what you were told
becomes part of your plan. It goes in as a line of its own, the "rests on" column names the person
who said it, and it overrides anything in this brief.

If every answer is no, the form says you're cleared to become more active, and tells you to start
slowly and build up gradually. It adds that if you're over 45 and not used to regular vigorous to
maximal effort, you should consult a qualified exercise professional before exercising at that
intensity.[2] Nothing in this project asks for vigorous effort. Every measure is taken at a pace you
can talk at, or stopped with reps to spare, and your plan should stay that way unless you have had
that consultation.

Three more things from the form. It says to delay becoming more active if you have a temporary
illness such as a cold or fever, if you're pregnant, or if your health changes. And a completed form
is valid for twelve months at most, and becomes invalid if your condition changes.[2] So if
something changes during the eight weeks, stop and fill it in again.

Then reread the callout above, separately from the form. If anything in its sentence about who
should talk to a doctor first applies to you, the same rule holds: the project waits for that
conversation, even if every answer on the form was no.

What to write for Part 1: the date you completed the form, and either "all no" or the kind of
professional you talked to, the date, and any conditions they set. You don't need to write down your
answers or any medical details. The file has to show that screening happened and what it changed,
and nothing more.

## Part 2: the plan, on one page

This is lesson 7's exercise, finished and kept. If you wrote it when you read lesson 7, check it
against the list below and bring it up to date. If you didn't, write it now.

- Days: at least two strength sessions a week, with a day or more between them. That is the ACSM
  2026 stand's primary recommendation and WHO's "2 or more days a week" for muscle strengthening, as
  lessons 1 and 7 showed.[3][4]
- Four slots: upper push, upper pull, lower push, lower pull, the four body regions the stand's
  authors offer as their view.[3] For each, a home or gym version from lesson 6 or 7 that you can do about
  8 to 12 times with good form. **Start below what you could do**: if you're unsure between two
  versions, pick the easier one.
- Numbers: two sets per exercise, stopping with 2 to 3 reps in reserve, and your progression rule in
  one sentence. Lesson 7's double progression is one such rule, and lesson 7 labels it a convention.
- Your aerobic week: lesson 3's first four weeks, then weeks 5 to 8 continued by the same rules you
  used there, with most sessions at the pace where you can talk but not sing. WHO's 150 to 300
  moderate minutes a week is the direction of travel, not the week-one target: lesson 1's curve is
  steepest at the start, and some activity is better than none.[4]
- A warm-up, however short, before each session. Lesson 7 allowed a few minutes of easy movement,
  and lesson 8 teaches it properly.
- One line you would change if you stalled, and what you would expect to see if the change worked.

Then the column that makes it a plan and not a list. Beside every line, write what it rests on, in
lesson 7's four words: **guidance** (a named body recommends it), **evidence** (trials measured it),
**view** (authors say so and label it their opinion) or **convention** (a sensible choice nobody has
tested). Name the source wherever there is one. Lesson 7 told you roughly what you will find:
guidance behind the days, the sets and the effort, a view behind the four regions, and convention
behind a good deal of the rest.

A layout that fits on one page:

Three things the plan must not contain. No weight, body-fat or calorie goal, anywhere: lesson 1 drew
that boundary for the whole course, and a plan that carries one has become a different project.
Nothing for a condition, a pregnancy or an injury, beyond what a professional told you in Part 1.
And no test of your maximum: lesson 7 would not suggest one in week one, and nothing in this project
needs one.

## Part 3: three baselines

A **baseline** is useful only if the re-test can repeat it exactly. So each measure below comes with
a recipe, and the first time you do it you write your own recipe down in full: which chair, which
counter, which bottles, which route, what time of day, which shoes. The re-test follows your written
recipe, not your memory of it.

Take each baseline twice, in the week before the plan starts, two to four days apart. Lesson 3
suggested testing more than once at each end as a way of shrinking the noise in a before-and-after
comparison, and this project takes the suggestion. The two baselines give you something else as
well. Nothing was trained between them, so any difference between them is the test being learned
and the day being different. Call that difference your **yardstick** and keep it for the report.
Using it this way is this project's design, not a finding.[10]

Do the three measures in the same order both times, on a day that was not physically hard for some
other reason, and note anything unusual: a poor night, a cold coming on, a heatwave.

### Measure A: the walk

1. Choose a route that takes about fifteen to twenty minutes at your brisk pace, as flat as you can
   find, on a safe surface in daylight, with a start and a finish you can't mistake: a gate, a
   lamppost, a corner. A loop and an out-and-back both work. Describe it precisely enough that you
   would walk exactly the same route in ten weeks.
2. Walk easily for five minutes first, as your lesson 3 plan does.
3. Walk the route at the pace where you can talk but not sing, the row from your lesson 2 exercise
   where singing stopped. That is the CDC's description of moderate effort, as lesson 2 quoted
   it.[5] Check it every few minutes: say a sentence out loud, then try a line of a song. If you
   can only manage a few words at a time, you have gone past it, so ease off.
4. Record three things: the time from start to finish, your 0 to 10 rating at the end, and the
   conditions (time of day, weather, shoes).

If you wear a watch you can add its heart rate, and lesson 2's advice holds: notice how it moves,
not what it says. If you take a medicine for your heart or blood pressure, leave heart rate out
entirely; lesson 2 explained why beta-blockers make heart-rate zones misleading. And the callout's
signs stop the walk wherever you are.

Why time at a fixed effort? Lesson 2's point was that the same activity takes a smaller share of a
fitter person's capacity. If your fitness changes, the pace that feels like talking but not singing
may change with it, and the route time is where that would show. That is this project's reasoning
from lesson 2, and whether it shows up in your eight weeks is one of the things your report finds
out.

### Measure B: two lifts, at a set number of reps in reserve

1. Choose two movements from your plan, one lower body and one upper body. A squat to a sturdy chair
   and an elevated press-up are the obvious pair, but use what your plan uses.
2. Fix everything you can. The same chair, with its height written down. The same counter or table
   for the press-up. A strip of tape on the floor where your feet go. The same load, recorded
   exactly: "backpack, four 1.5-litre bottles", not "the backpack". The same pace for each rep, one
   you can count, such as about a second up and two down. The count is a convention; what matters is
   that it is the same count both times.
3. Set up safely. A chair with no wheels, its back against a wall, on a floor that won't slip. A dry,
   solid counter that won't move. Warm up with a few minutes of easy movement and a few easy reps of
   each movement.
4. Do one set of each, stopping when you believe you have 2 left, and count the reps. Keep breathing
   throughout, out as you push or stand, and don't hold your breath as the reps get hard; lesson 6
   showed what breath-holding under effort does to blood pressure. Don't carry on past your stopping
   point to check your guess. That check belongs in lesson 7's stall test, not here.
5. Rest three minutes between the two movements, the rest lesson 5's exercise used, and do them in
   the same order every time.
6. Record the version, the load, the reps and your reps-in-reserve guess for each.

Stop at once for sharp pain in a joint or for any of the callout's signs. If you pass 15 reps at the
first baseline, the version is too easy to measure well, because guesses about reps in reserve were
slightly better in sets of 12 or fewer.[6] Choose a harder version, and take both baselines with
that one.

### Measure C: one everyday task

Choose one ordinary thing you would like to find easier, and define it exactly. Two that work:

- Stairs. The same flight or flights every time, at the pace you would normally climb them, not a
  race, with a hand on the rail. Record the time, your 0 to 10 rating at the top, and whether you
  could talk but not sing when you got there.
- Carrying. Lesson 6 taught no carry, because nothing the course read covers one, so use only a load
  you already carry in ordinary life, such as two bags of shopping. Make it the same load each time:
  water bottles make that easy, since a litre weighs about a kilogram (2.2 lb), as lesson 7
  said.[10] Carry it the same distance, and record your 0 to 10 rating at the end and whether you
  had to put it down.

Whatever you choose gets a number you can count or time, a 0 to 10 rating, and the same recipe both
times. The callout's signs stop it, as they stop everything else here. And it can't be a number on a
scale or a clothes size: that is the weight boundary again.

### Optional: one test you won't practise

If you want to see lesson 4's specificity finding in yourself, add lesson 5's wall sit as a fourth
measure, taken exactly as that exercise described, and never practise it during the eight weeks.
Lesson 5's cautions come with it: the depth mark on the wall, a way out by sliding up or walking your
feet in, steady breathing, and, if you have high blood pressure, a doctor's view before you try it.

### Before week 1: a prediction

With both baselines written down, write one line for each measure: which way you expect it to move by
week 9, and roughly how far. Then add a line on which measure you expect to change most. Date it,
and don't edit it afterwards, including the parts that turn out wrong. Those are the report's
material.

## Part 4: eight weeks, and the log

Run the plan. Lesson 7 set out what a log is for; this is what yours records.

- Every strength session, one line per exercise: the date, the exercise and version, the load, the
  reps in each set, your reps-in-reserve guess, and a notes column for anything unusual, such as a
  bad night, a cold or a twinge. That is lesson 7's log, unchanged.
- Every aerobic session: the date, what you did, the minutes, your talk-test result and your 0 to 10
  rating.
- Once a week, one line: your moderate minutes plus twice your vigorous minutes, set against WHO's
  150 (lesson 1's arithmetic), and your strength days set against two.[4] Then any change you made
  to the plan that week, with the reason and what you expect it to do. Lesson 7's stall test is the
  model: change one thing, write the prediction before you make the change, and see what happens.

Missed sessions are logged as missed. A blank line in the right place is part of the record. A log
with no gaps usually means something was filled in later, and lesson 7's case for writing things
down depended on the record being made at the time.

Don't re-run the baseline measures during the eight weeks, and keep the test route out of your
ordinary walks if you can. If you do walk it, note that you did. Both would make the re-test more a
measure of practice at the test, which Part 5 has to account for anyway.

Four safety lines, one for each way the eight weeks can go wrong.

1. The first sessions are a handful of careful sets. The rhabdomyolysis guideline lesson 7 quoted
   lists "High-intensity, repetitive, and/or prolonged exercise unmatched to fitness level" among its
   contributing factors.[7] Being sore for a day or two after a new session is common, and lesson 8
   explains it. Muscle pain and swelling far worse than the session explains, or cola-coloured urine,
   needs a doctor the same day.
2. Every session keeps the set-up and the breathing from Part 3: a stable chair against a wall, a
   counter that won't move, and breathing out through the hard part of each rep.
3. After a break, restart a few steps back. A cold, a trip or a bad fortnight will happen to some
   readers. Lesson 7's rule is to restart at the load and version you used several weeks before you
   stopped, and to climb back by the same progression. Log the break and the restart. If the break
   was illness, the PAR-Q+'s rule about waiting until you feel better comes first.[2]
4. The callout's signs end the session, whatever week it is. Get help first. Then log what happened,
   and don't go back to the plan until a doctor has said you can.

## Part 5: the re-test and the report

### The re-test

In week 9, take the measures again, twice, two to four days apart, following your written recipe
exactly. That means the same route, chair, counter, load, version, pace and time of day as the
baselines, even though your plan has moved on. The **re-test** measures change on the baseline's
terms, and a re-test on your week-8 load would measure something else.

The safety lines are the same as at baseline: a stable set-up, breathing out through each rep,
stopping at 2 in reserve, and stopping at once for the callout's signs. One thing will be different.
A load that was hard in week 0 may now be easy, so the set may run long, past 12 reps, where guesses
about reps in reserve were a little worse.[6] Stop at 2 in reserve anyway, and write down how long
the set was. It belongs in the report.

### The report

Six sections. The course's question runs through the middle three.

**1. The numbers.** One table: each measure, both baselines, both re-tests, and the change, taken as
the average of the two re-tests against the average of the two baselines. Work out the per cent
change the way lesson 5's exercise did: the re-test minus the baseline, divided by the baseline,
times 100. Put your yardstick beside each change, and your Part 3 prediction beside that.

**2. Trained whom?** One person. Describe yourself the way a methods section describes its
participants, as far as you're comfortable: age, how active you were before, whether you had lifted
before, what screening found. Then set that description beside lesson 4's table. How close are you
to the typical participant in this literature? And turn it round. Lesson 4 showed people on the same
programme responding very differently, so your result is one point from a wide spread. It tells
nobody else what they would get, any more than their average told you.

**3. For how long?** Eight weeks, the typical trial's length. Using lesson 4's section on the first
weeks, say what that suggests about where your lift gains came from, and why you can't split them
between the nervous system learning the movement and anything else. Keep lesson 4's warning in view:
whether muscle growth causes strength gains is disputed, so "my muscles grew" isn't something your
numbers can show. Then give your adherence from the log, sessions done out of sessions planned,
because eight weeks on paper and eight weeks trained aren't always the same length.

**4. Measured on what?** The longest section. Take each measure in turn and weigh four things
against its change.

- **Specificity.** If you trained twice a week, you practised both lifts at least sixteen times, and
  the test was those lifts. In Spitz's meta-analysis, strength rose with an effect size of 1.84 on
  the practised movement and 0.8 on an unpractised test of the same muscle.[8] The stand adds the
  other half: a considerable carryover to general muscular performance in people who aren't
  advanced.[3] So your lift change is real, and it overstates how much stronger you are at
  everything else. Did your everyday task, which the plan did not train directly, move? If you did
  the wall sit, compare the two.
- **Learning the test.** Your two baselines had nothing but a test between them, so their difference
  is the part of any change you could get from taking the test at all. Lesson 5's exercise raised
  the same problem over two weeks. The lifts have a subtler version. You have spent eight weeks
  practising the reps-in-reserve guess, and if your guesses changed, say because you learned you
  had more left than you thought, then your week-9 set may stop at a different real distance from
  failure than your week-0 set did. The reps would then change for a reason that has nothing to do
  with strength. That is this project's inference, not a finding, and it is worth a paragraph.
- **Noise.** On average, people in Halperin's review underpredicted the reps they had left by about
  one.[6] One rep either way is a large share of a ten-rep set. The walk has weather and wind in it,
  and the stairs have how your day went. Atkinson and Batterham's point from lesson 3 applies to
  you: within-subject random variation is "inevitable even with 'gold-standard' measurement
  tools/protocols".[9] Is your change bigger than your yardstick? If it isn't, say you can't tell
  it from noise, and say it plainly.
- **No comparison group.** You don't know what would have happened to your numbers over the same
  ten weeks without the plan. The season changed, your work changed, and you may have walked more
  simply because you were logging. Atkinson and Batterham's second point is that a true individual
  response can be quantified only against a comparator arm, and you have none.[9] Mental Fitness's
  question, what did the comparison group get, has the bleakest answer here: there wasn't one.

**5. What the re-test can show, and what it can't.** Two paragraphs. Use lesson 1's three
sentences. At most, your eight weeks bear on the first, that exercise changes the body, for one body,
on these measures. They say nothing about whether active people live longer, or whether becoming
active will make you live longer. Nor can they tell you whether your plan was better than another
plan: lesson 4 showed that most comparisons between one programme and another could not settle that
even across many trials. What they can show is narrower and still worth having: that you ran a plan
for eight weeks, how often you actually trained, which measures moved by more than your yardstick,
and what happened at the points where you changed something.

**6. What next.** One decision about the next eight weeks, of one of three kinds. Keep the plan as it
is, deliberately. Change one thing, named precisely enough that someone could tell in a month whether
you had done it, with lesson 7's prediction attached. Or stop measuring, if the log showed a habit
that runs without it. Whichever you choose, write what it rests on (your numbers, and any finding
from the course, with the gap between that finding's population and you named), what it costs in
time or anything else, and one line on what would tell you it was the wrong call. Lesson 10 comes
back to this decision.

|---|---|---|---|

| The baselines | Three measures, each taken twice, each with a written recipe precise enough for a stranger to repeat; the prediction is dated and untouched | Measures taken once, or recipes that leave out something that matters (the chair, the load, the route) | Baselines reconstructed from memory, or measures that can't be repeated |
| The log | Every session and every missed session recorded at the time, with weekly lines and each change's reason and prediction | Gaps that aren't marked as gaps, or changes made without a written reason | A log written up afterwards |
| The re-test | Repeated to the recipe, twice, on the baseline's terms, with any departure recorded | Repeated once, or with a departure that isn't recorded | Re-tested on the week-8 load or version, so it measures something else |
| The report | Specificity, learning the test, noise and the missing comparison all weighed against each measure; changes set against the yardstick; the three sentences used to say what the result can and can't show | The limits listed but not applied measure by measure, or one of the four missing | A change credited entirely to getting stronger or fitter, with no limits named |
| What next | A decision, what it rests on, what it costs, and what would show it was wrong | A decision without its cost or its test | No decision |

Three of those seven parts are about honesty rather than fitness, and that is deliberate. A course
whose question is what a training result was measured on would be funny in the wrong way if its
project rewarded the biggest number.

## If the eight weeks go wrong

They often do, and it isn't a disaster. A project with three missed weeks, logged as missed, can
still be written up well; say at the top of the report what went wrong.

What you lose depends on what happened. A lost baseline costs you the comparison for that measure,
and the honest repair is to report the others and say so. Don't rebuild a baseline from memory:
you will remember it as whatever makes the re-test look sensible. Missed weeks cost you adherence,
which the report records, and a break means lesson 7's restart rule before you carry on. A pain that
changes how you move means stopping that session, and seeing a doctor or physiotherapist if it
persists, as lesson 3's exercise said.

A callout sign ends the project until a doctor says otherwise. Write up what you have and what
happened, and stop there. An eight-week project that stopped at week 3 for a good reason, and says
so, is a complete piece of work.

## A note on what this project is not

It is not a fitness assessment or a medical check. It measures three things about one person on
their own terms, and nothing in it can tell you whether your heart, your joints or anything else is
healthy. It isn't medical clearance either: that is what Part 1 was for, and the PAR-Q+ and the
professionals it points to are the instruments for it. And it is not evidence for anyone else. Your
result is a sample of one, which is the thing the report exists to say.
