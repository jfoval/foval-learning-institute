  # --- The curve, the three sentences, screening (L1) ---
  - q: >-
      A post says: "Under 150 minutes a week you're wasting your time. The benefits start at the
      guideline." Going by the dose-response curve the guidelines lead with, how does that stand?
    options:
      - Nearly right, since WHO says gains below 150 minutes are real but far too small to count
      - It's backwards, since the curve is steepest at the low end and some activity beats none
      - Nearly right, though minutes below 150 do count if they come in bouts of ten minutes or more
      - Right for walking, though running minutes count double and so reach the line sooner
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      WHO says "there is no lower threshold for benefit, and the greatest benefits are seen at the
      lower end of the dose-response curve", and "Doing some physical activity is better than doing
      none"; the UK calls its levels "not absolute thresholds". So the post has it backwards: the
      first minutes do the most. Nothing read calls gains below 150 too small to count. The rule
      about bouts of ten minutes is the one the US dropped in 2018 and WHO in 2020. And a vigorous
      minute does count about double, but that doesn't turn 150 into a starting line for anybody.
  - q: >-
      A trial randomises 2,000 adults aged 40 to 60 to a year of twice-weekly strength training or
      to carrying on as before. Leg strength rises in the trained group. Nine people die in one
      group and ten in the other, and a headline says: "Lifting doesn't help you live longer." Using
      lesson 1's three sentences, what does the trial show?
    options:
      - That the third is false, since randomising people is the design that can settle a cause
      - The first, and it tilts slightly against the third, though a year is too short to be sure
      - None of the three, since a single year is too short a time to measure a gain in strength
      - The first, and with so few deaths it can't test the third either way, randomised or not
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      A randomised trial with a short-term measure of the body is the evidence for "exercise changes
      the body", and a year is plenty for that. For the third sentence the trial counted nineteen
      deaths in all. Generation 100's authors gave the reason that's a problem: death is "relatively
      rare in young and middle aged people", so a trial needs a large sample and long follow-up, and
      fewer deaths means wider uncertainty around any difference. Nine against ten sits well inside
      that uncertainty, so it doesn't tilt either way. Nor can the null show the third sentence
      false, which is what Generation 100's authors said of their own.
      Randomising is needed for cause, and it isn't enough without deaths to count.
  - q: >-
      A woman of 56 has type 2 diabetes and no symptoms. For three years she has walked briskly for
      40 minutes on four days a week, and now she wants to add running intervals. On ACSM's 2015
      screening model, what's the right next step?
    options:
      - Keep walking as she does, and get medical clearance before adding the vigorous intervals
      - Stop walking until she's cleared, since known diabetes needs clearance before any exercise
      - Add the intervals under a trainer's eye, since supervision takes the place of clearance for her
      - Add the intervals slowly, since the 2015 model dropped diabetes along with risk factors
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Her walking meets the model's definition of exercising, at least 30 minutes of planned
      moderate exercise on at least three days a week for three months or more, and type 2 diabetes
      is what its "metabolic disease" means. That branch is "encouraged to continue light to
      moderate intensity exercise without clearance, but should have medical clearance before
      progressing to vigorous intensity exercise". Clearance before any exercise is the branch for
      people with known disease who aren't exercising yet. A trainer appears nowhere in the model:
      what her branch asks for before vigorous work is a doctor's clearance. And the model dropped
      risk-factor profiling, not known disease. The box above, the same one that opens every lesson,
      says it too: talk to a doctor before you step up.

  # --- Effort you can measure (L2) ---
  - q: >-
      A 40-year-old's app sets her training target at 80 per cent of her maximum heart rate: 220
      minus 40 is 180, so 144. Tanaka's formula also gives 180 at her age. Using the limits lesson 2
      gave for a single formula, where could her real 80 per cent plausibly sit?
    options:
      - At about 144 give or take a few beats, since two different formulas agree on her maximum
      - Somewhere above 144, since 220 minus age underestimates the maximum of most adults
      - Anywhere from about 128 to 160, since her maximum itself could be 20 beats either way
      - Anywhere from about 124 to 164, since the target carries the same 20 beats as the maximum
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Martin's study put the limits at about 20 beats either side for each formula, so her real
      maximum could be anywhere from about 160 to 200. Eighty per cent of each end is 128 and 160,
      the same working lesson 2 did at 70 per cent. The 20 beats shrink with the percentage, which is
      where the 124 to 164 range goes wrong. Two formulas agreeing says nothing about her, since both
      predict the average 40-year-old. And Tanaka's abstract said the old formula underestimates
      older adults, while Martin found no average bias for it in adults aged 18 to 68, so nothing
      read puts her above 144.
  - q: >-
      A man of 62 takes a beta-blocker for his blood pressure, and his doctor has cleared him to walk
      briskly. His new watch sets heart-rate zones for his walks. On lesson 2, what should he do?
    options:
      - Use Tanaka's formula for the zones instead, since it had the smallest average error in 2025
      - Aim a little lower in each zone, since the medicine pushes his heart rate up during effort
      - Keep the zones, once his doctor has checked that the watch reads his pulse accurately
      - Judge effort by the talk test or the 0 to 10 scale, and ask his doctor about any targets
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Martin's authors note that medicines such as beta-blockers "can blunt" the heart's response to
      exercise, "rendering HR-based zones misleading". Lesson 2's advice for anyone taking a medicine
      for the heart or blood pressure is the talk test and the 0 to 10 scale, with targets a question
      for the doctor. A better formula doesn't fix a blunted response, and the medicine blunts the
      rise rather than pushing it up. Checking the watch misses the point: wrist heart rate was
      usually within a few per cent in the lab studies, so the pulse may well be right. The trouble
      is what zones built on that pulse assume about his heart.

  # --- Building endurance (L3) ---
  - q: >-
      A healthy 30-year-old runs steadily for 45 minutes three times a week. She reads that interval
      training is "far better for fitness" and wonders whether her runs are wasted. What does the
      evidence in lesson 3 say?
    options:
      - Partly wasted, since the review of patients suggests intervals roughly double anyone's gain
      - "Not wasted: in healthy adults under 45 both raised VO2max a lot, and intervals a bit more"
      - Not wasted, since pooled trials found the two exactly equal once the time was matched
      - Wasted unless she slows to zone 2, which the critics' review found best for fitness
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Milanović's meta-analysis of healthy adults aged 18 to 45 found both kinds of training raised
      VO2max a lot, with intervals ahead by about 1.2 mL/kg/min, a third of a MET, which its abstract
      calls "possibly small". The review of patients found the larger gap, in 273 people with heart
      and metabolic disease, six of its ten studies from one group, so reading it as anyone's gain
      moves the finding to people it didn't study. Nothing read found the two exactly equal. And the
      zone 2 review concluded the opposite of "best": the evidence "does not support Zone 2 training
      as the optimal intensity". Whether people stick to intervals is the live argument, and it's
      about the public, not about her runs.
  - q: >-
      A 50-year-old stopped running two years ago with a calf injury that has since healed. He's
      about to restart with a nine-week run-walk plan. Which change to his plan does lesson 3's
      evidence give most reason for?
    options:
      - Extra patience, since a previous injury was the risk that stood out in beginner runners
      - Stability shoes as a precaution, since an old injury may mean his feet roll too far inwards
      - A strict 10 per cent weekly cap, since the trial found that rule cut injuries in novices
      - Another year's wait, since novices' injury risk ran highest in their first 8 to 13 weeks
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      In Relph's study of a modified Couch to 5K, a previous injury raised the odds of a new one, with
      a very wide interval, and lesson 3's reading for a beginner was to treat an old injury as a
      reason for extra patience, repeating a week when it felt hard. The shoe study found moderate
      pronation wasn't linked to more injuries in a neutral shoe, so an old injury is no case for
      stability shoes. The graded trial found no effect of the 10 per cent rule, and its authors
      thought the two plans may have differed too little to show one. Waiting doesn't get him past
      the early risk: Videbæk's authors suggest novices who run 8 to 13 weeks without injury may have
      adapted, and only running gets anyone there. And if a pain changes how he runs, lesson 3's
      suggestion is to stop that session and see a doctor or physiotherapist if it persists.

  # --- Specificity and the course's question (L4) ---
  - q: >-
      Women in their sixties trained on a knee-extension machine twice a week for ten weeks. Their
      one-rep maximum on the machine rose 40 per cent, and their knee strength on a force-measuring
      chair they had never used rose 15 per cent. An advert says: "Ten weeks on our machine makes
      you 40 per cent stronger." What does specificity say about the two figures?
    options:
      - The advert is fair, since the one-rep maximum is the standard strength test in the field
      - Only the 15 per cent is real, since a gain on a practised lift is skill and not strength
      - One of the tests was probably run badly, since the two should agree if strength rose
      - The 40 per cent is on the practised machine, and a smaller gain elsewhere is expected
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Spitz's meta-analysis found gains on the movement people trained ran at more than twice the
      effect size of gains on an unpractised test of the same muscle, so a smaller figure on the
      unfamiliar chair is what specificity predicts. That doesn't make the 40 per cent fake: the
      practised lift overstates the gain, it doesn't invent it, and the 2026 stand reports "a
      considerable carryover" in people who aren't advanced. The one-rep maximum is the usual test,
      which is why so much of the evidence is the practised lift, tested, and that's the reason to
      ask what the advert's figure was measured on. The two tests disagreeing is the finding, not a
      fault in either.
  - q: >-
      An app company reports that 20,000 of its users, average age 45, doubled the squat loads they
      logged over three years, and calls it proof that its programme works. Put the course's
      question to it. What is this record?
    options:
      - Moderate evidence it works, since its size partly makes up for the missing comparison group
      - Worthless, since loads that users type in themselves can't be counted as data at all
      - A long record of users who kept logging, on loads they typed in, without a comparison group
      - Good evidence for older lifters, since at 45 the users are older than most trials trained
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Whom: people who kept using the app and logging, not everyone who started. How long: three
      years, which is long. Measured on what: loads the users recorded, on no test anyone
      standardised. And there's no group that didn't use the app, so, like the six-year company
      records in lesson 4, it's a picture of what happened to those people, not a test of what caused
      it. Lesson 4's first finding applies as well: against doing nothing almost every programme
      wins, and against another programme almost nothing can be told apart. Size and length don't
      make up any part of the missing comparison; twenty thousand people with none are still a
      picture. A self-recorded log isn't worthless, since lesson 7 builds a plan on one. And an
      older sample tells you whom the record describes, not what caused it.

  # --- Load and measure, failure (L5) ---
  - q: >-
      A twelve-week trial compares sets of about 4 reps with a heavy load against sets of about 30
      with a light one, every set taken to failure. The heavy group's one-rep maximum rises twice as
      much, and an advert says: "Light weights are useless for strength." How does lesson 5 read
      that?
    options:
      - Heavy won on a test like heavy training; on unpractised tests the gap wasn't clear
      - Mostly fair, since heavy's edge would probably show up on any strength test they used
      - Unfair, since load makes no difference to strength once every set is taken to failure
      - Fair, and light loads will build less muscle too, since size and strength go together
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Schoenfeld's meta-analysis found 1RM gains "significantly greater in favor of high- vs.
      low-load training", and "no significant differences" on an isometric test neither group had
      practised. A 2026 meta-analysis was "inconclusive" on strength measured on unfamiliar tests,
      leaning towards heavy, so "any strength test" claims more than either found. Heavy loads build
      a bigger 1RM, partly as practice at lifting heavy, and a light group gets stronger too, which
      "useless" ignores. Heavy's 1RM win is real, so "no difference" is wrong. And muscle growth was
      similar across loads when sets were very hard; whether growth drives strength is a dispute
      lesson 4 left open.
  - q: >-
      An instructor runs a strength class for beginners in their seventies and has everyone take
      every set to failure, "because that's where the gains are". What does lesson 5's evidence say?
    options:
      - Sound, since the failure meta-analysis found that it did no harm to strength or size gains
      - Failure wasn't needed for gains, and the stand warns it may be unwise for older people
      - Sound for growth, since it's settled that the last reps before failure add the muscle
      - Probably unwise, since trials in older adults found failure slowed their strength gains
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Grgic's meta-analysis concluded that training to failure "does not seem to be required for
      gains in strength and muscle size", and the ACSM stand says it "is not necessary for benefits
      to occur". The same stand warns that lifting to fatigue "may also be" inadvisable for older
      people, because of risks to vascular health and injury when form breaks down. No harm in
      Grgic's analysis meant no harm to the gains, in young adults; it said nothing about safety in
      people in their seventies. Whether the last reps add a little muscle is contested: Robinson's
      analysis, Refalo's and the stand read it differently. And nothing read found failure slowing
      strength gains in older adults; the stand's caution is about blood vessels and form, not about
      the gains.

  # --- Technique and its rules (L6) ---
  - q: >-
      A gym instructor tells a new member to wear a lifting belt for every deadlift, "because belts
      prevent back injuries". What did lesson 6 find behind that?
    options:
      - Well supported for lifters, since the review of belts at work found they reduce lifting stress
      - Probably harmful over time, since the review of workers found that belts weaken the body
      - Untested in gym lifters, and the reviews of workers found too little evidence to back belts
      - Supported for heavy lifts, since strength athletes wear belts and report fewer injuries
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The course found no sports-science review of belts. The 1995 review of belts at work said they
      "seem to reduce lifting stress", then that they "may" give a false sense of security and "may"
      weaken the body, and ended that more research was needed "before any conclusions can be
      drawn". A 1999 paper, citing NIOSH, found "insufficient" evidence to recommend back belts in
      industry. So less lifting stress isn't fewer injuries, a weakening the review said "may" happen
      isn't a finding that it does, and athletes wearing belts isn't a test of anything. On that
      evidence the course's judgement was that a beginner doesn't need one.
  - q: >-
      Practising the back-to-wall hinge at home, a man feels a sharp pain low in his back on every
      rep. A friend watching says his back is curling and suggests a cue: push the hips back
      further. What does lesson 6 say he should do?
    options:
      - Try the cue for another set, since a curling back is the fault the drill exists to catch
      - Stop the movement, since pain isn't a fault a cue fixes, and get it seen if it persists
      - Add a light kettlebell, since the teaching article says load tends to improve technique
      - Stop for today and try the cue tomorrow, since the curl is the likely cause of the pain
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The NSCA article says pain, range of motion deficits or overt weakness "may negatively affect
      movement performance", and that suspected pathology means "referral to a physical therapist or
      physician". Lesson 6's rule for every pattern follows from it: if a movement hurts, stop it
      rather than cueing your way through it, and if the pain doesn't settle, see a physiotherapist
      or a doctor. The curl may be real and the cue may be good, but a cue is for a technique fault,
      not for pain, and nothing lets a friend decide the curl caused it; trying the cue tomorrow
      still treats the pain as a fault. Loading early is advice for learning a movement that doesn't
      hurt.

  # --- Writing and progressing a plan (L7) ---
  - q: >-
      A reader fills in the "rests on" column of her plan. Which line has she labelled the way
      lesson 7 would label it?
    options:
      - "Two sets per exercise: guidance, which the stand bases on trials where two beat one"
      - "Four body regions: evidence, since the stand's pooled trials compared the regions"
      - "Double progression: guidance, since the 2026 stand names it as the rule for novices"
      - "Two rest days between sessions: evidence, from trials that tested it in beginners"
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The stand advises "at least two sets per exercise", and its reason is that "two sets are
      superior to one set", so lesson 7 labelled that line guidance, from evidence. The four regions
      are the authors' view, labelled "In the view of the authors" straight after they say the
      pooled evidence didn't compare regions. Double progression is this course's convention, and
      its nearest relative is the 2009 stand's rule, an expert recommendation nothing read tested;
      the 2026 stand names no loading rule. And the rest between sessions is older ACSM guidance,
      read at second hand and untested for beginners.
  - q: >-
      A friend in her first month of lifting says she has to follow a periodised plan from day one,
      "or you waste your early gains". What does the evidence read in this course say?
    options:
      - She's right, since Moesgaard's review found that undulating plans beat linear ones in novices
      - She's probably wrong, since periodising helped only trained lifters in the trials read here
      - It points both ways for beginners, and the stand rates it less important than once thought
      - She's right, since Williams's review found variation vital and larger gains in novices
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      For beginners the evidence cuts both ways. Williams's analysis found larger 1RM gains in
      untrained people, but it didn't hold volume equal, so some of that could be more work.
      Moesgaard's, with volume equal, found undulating beat linear "only the case for trained and not
      previously untrained individuals". And the stand's summary is that "periodization is less
      important than previously hypothesized", while it notes one review slightly favoured
      periodised plans for strength. So neither "she's right" option holds, and "helped only trained
      lifters" takes Moesgaard's result for the whole question and drops Williams's, on a point the
      stand itself said "could not be determined". Lesson 7 left blocks out of its plan for a
      practical reason, not an evidential one.

  # --- Soreness, rhabdomyolysis and recovery (L8) ---
  - q: >-
      Two days after an unfamiliar class full of pull-ups and push-ups, a woman's upper arms are
      swollen and tight. Pressing on them hurts far more than the class explains, and she can't
      straighten her elbows fully, even when a friend tries to straighten them for her. Her urine
      looks normal. What should she do?
    options:
      - Do some light arm work, since exercise is the most effective relief for ordinary soreness
      - Rest her arms for a day or two, and see a doctor if her urine turns dark in the meantime
      - Rest until day seven, since only pain lasting past 5 to 7 days needs a doctor's view
      - See a doctor today, since the swelling and loss of movement are each signs on the list
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The military guideline's six signs include pain "out of proportion to what one would normally
      expect from the activity", "Muscle swelling" and "Significant limitation in active and passive
      range of motion", and she has all three. Any one of them is reason to see a doctor the same
      day, as the callout's last sentence says, so waiting for her urine to darken waits past signs
      she already has. Ordinary soreness can bring some stiffness and swelling, which is why the
      list's qualifiers matter, and hers meet them; the guideline also says the two "can have
      overlapping symptoms", so normal urine doesn't clear her. Exercise eased ordinary soreness for
      a while in Cheung's review, but once a sign appears the advice turns to rest decided with a
      doctor. The 5-to-7-day sign is one of six, not the only one. And pull-ups and push-ups are both
      in the guideline's examples of eccentric exercise.
  - q: >-
      A sports clinic sells weekly massage to lifters "to make your training build more strength".
      What does lesson 8's evidence say about that claim?
    options:
      - "Untested in what was read: massage eased soreness in single sessions; gains went unmeasured"
      - It's supported, since massage came out as the most effective method in the recovery review
      - It's false, since the recovery review found that massage worked only the way a placebo does
      - It's unlikely, since cold water cut strength gains and massage probably works that way
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Dupuy's review found massage "the most effective method for reducing DOMS and perceived
      fatigue", after single recovery sessions, and it couldn't rule out placebo. Those are feel
      outcomes. Nothing read tested whether massage changes strength or muscle over weeks, so
      "build more strength" is untested in anything read, which is neither supported nor false.
      Being best at easing soreness doesn't make it best at building anything. A placebo effect that
      "could not be eliminated" isn't a finding that placebo was all of it. And Roberts's trial
      tested cold water against active recovery; nothing read says massage acts the way cold water
      does.

  # --- Training across a life (L9) ---
  - q: >-
      A man of 45 has had low back pain for a year and hasn't had it examined. He reads that
      exercise helps back pain and plans to start heavy deadlifts next week. What does lesson 9's
      evidence say about his plan?
    options:
      - Go ahead, since the Cochrane review found exercise cut the pain by a clinically important amount
      - Start with light deadlifts instead, since the review found exercise probably eases this pain
      - Go ahead, since deadlifts were the exercise the review found works best for chronic back pain
      - The review is about non-specific pain, and whether his is that kind needs examining first
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Hayden's review found exercise "probably effective" for pain in chronic non-specific low back
      pain, by just over the authors' threshold for mattering, and less for function. Non-specific
      means no identified cause such as a fracture, a tumour or a trapped nerve, and telling whether
      his pain is that kind is a job for a doctor or a physiotherapist, so on lesson 9's reading of
      the review's scope his plan starts with an examination, not a bar. Going ahead drops that
      scope, and so does starting light, which changes the load and leaves the pain unexamined. The
      review doesn't say which exercise is best, deadlifts or any other. And rest isn't what the
      evidence supports either: the lifting studies in lesson 6 found low-quality evidence that
      bending while lifting was not a risk factor for back pain, not that it caused it.

  # --- Reading a claim: the address and the sort (L10) ---
  - q: >-
      A study of trained young men found muscle size still rising at 30 sets per muscle a week over
      eight weeks. A website retells it as: "Beginners over 50: do 30 sets a week for eight weeks
      for the most muscle growth." Which of the course's three questions finds what changed?
    options:
      - For how long, since eight weeks is too short for a study of weekly sets to be trusted
      - Trained whom, since the study's trained young men have become beginners over 50
      - Measured on what, since muscle size can't be measured closely enough to set a number
      - None, mostly, since the numbers carried over and the advice is roughly what it found
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The address moved on one answer. The weeks, the outcome and the number all carried over, which
      is what makes a moved address hard to see: the numbers survive the move, so "roughly what it
      found" is the move itself. The people didn't carry over. Trained young men became beginners
      over 50, a group the study never trained. That doesn't make the advice wrong for them, since
      the stand's authors report that training experience had "a minimal impact" in their analysis,
      but nobody measured it there, and Nuckols put the point of rapidly diminishing returns likely
      quite a bit lower for novices. Eight weeks is typical of this literature, not a reason to
      dismiss it, and muscle size is measured by scans and biopsies throughout the course. There's a
      safety point too: a beginner jumping straight to a high volume is the "exercise unmatched to
      fitness level" the rhabdomyolysis guideline warns about.
  - q: >-
      A running club's coach tells new members: "Stretching before a run prevents injuries. The
      science on that is settled." Using lesson 10's sort and lesson 8's evidence, how should the
      claim be graded?
    options:
      - Probably established, since stretching researchers still recommend it in a warm-up for injuries
      - False, since the reviews that pooled injury counts showed stretching has no effect at all
      - "Contested: pooled injury reviews found no clear effect, yet stretching researchers back it"
      - A value question, since whether to stretch comes down to what each runner prefers to do
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      It's measurable, it has been measured, and the evidence splits. Thacker's and Lauersen's
      reviews, which pooled injury counts, found no significant effect of stretching on injuries in
      general, and Thacker's wording is "not sufficient evidence to endorse or discontinue". Behm's
      review and Alizadeh's still recommend stretching within a warm-up partly for muscle injuries,
      the second resting on one earlier study. So "settled" fails in both directions: a
      recommendation isn't a pooled finding, however many researchers make it, and no clear effect
      isn't proof of none. Whether a runner enjoys stretching is a preference, but whether it
      prevents injuries is a question evidence can answer, which makes it empirical, not a value
      question.

:::callout Before you train
This course is education, not advice about your own body. If you have heart, kidney or metabolic disease such as diabetes, are pregnant, or have symptoms like chest discomfort, fainting or unusual breathlessness, talk to a doctor before you start or step up training. Stop and get medical help straight away for pain or pressure in the chest, neck, jaw or arms, dizziness, palpitations, or breathlessness out of all proportion to the effort, and call your local emergency number for chest pain. Cola-coloured urine, or muscle pain and swelling far worse than the session explains, needs a doctor the same day.
:::

Nearly every question gives you a claim, a study, a plan or a person that isn't in any lesson, and
asks you to apply what the course taught to it. The people, adverts and studies in the questions are
invented; the explanations draw on the real ones the lessons read. Where a figure appears it's in the
question, because the course is about what a figure licenses, not about memorising it.

Lesson 1 carries three questions, lesson 9 one and the rest two each. Lesson 10's second question is a
claim from lesson 8 run through lesson 10's sort, so read that one with both lessons in mind. Several
questions touch claims the course calls contested: whether the last reps before failure add muscle,
periodisation for beginners, zone 2, and stretching and injuries. None of them asks you which side is
right. They ask what the evidence shows, for whom, and how far it reaches.

None of the questions is about you, and none of the answers is advice about your own body. A few are
about pain or symptoms, during training or after it, and in every one of those the right answer is to
stop, or not to start, and get the right person to look. The box above says who, and how soon. If
anything in it applies to you, that comes before this test.
