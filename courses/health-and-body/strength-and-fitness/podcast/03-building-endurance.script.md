S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Strength and Fitness, on the lesson called Building endurance.

S2: The box at the top first, as every lesson in this course has it.

S1: This course is education, not advice about your own body. If you have heart, kidney or metabolic disease such as diabetes, are pregnant, or have symptoms like chest discomfort, fainting or unusual breathlessness, talk to a doctor before you start or step up training. Stop and get medical help straight away for pain or pressure in the chest, neck, jaw or arms, dizziness, palpitations, or breathlessness out of all proportion to the effort, and for chest pain call your local emergency number. Cola-coloured urine, or muscle pain and swelling far worse than the session explains, needs a doctor the same day.

S2: The lesson opens with a claim I think I've heard.

S1: Put the way it tends to get retold: one minute of hard exercise does the work of forty five minutes of steady exercise. That's the course's own paraphrase, not a headline it collected. It comes from a real study, done well, and the study says a good deal less.

S2: What did the study actually do?

S1: Gillen and colleagues compared two programmes over twelve weeks on a laboratory bike, three times a week. One was three twenty-second all-out sprints with easy pedalling between, ten minutes a session with the warm-up and cool-down. The other was forty five minutes of steady cycling at about seventy per cent of maximum heart rate. Their summary calls the sprint protocol "as effective as" the steady one for insulin sensitivity, fitness and the mitochondria in the muscle, "in previously inactive men."

S2: So where does the retelling go wrong?

S1: Ask three questions, which lesson four turns into the course's standing question. Trained whom? Sedentary men, assigned to groups; the paper doesn't describe randomisation. Nine finished sprinting and ten riding steadily, with six controls, twenty seven years old on average. No women. For how long? Twelve weeks, supervised, in a lab. Measured on what? Peak oxygen uptake, which rose about nineteen per cent in both groups, plus insulin sensitivity and an enzyme from muscle biopsies. What it couldn't show is whether it works for women, or older people, or anyone training unsupervised at home, or beyond twelve weeks, or whether people would keep it up. And with nine and ten men a group, "as effective as" means the study found no difference, not that it proved the two were equal. The authors raised adherence themselves and asked for "longer and more comprehensive studies". And an all-out sprint isn't where a beginner starts.

S2: So is the argument over?

S1: The argument has moved. In twenty fifteen Biddle and Batterham wrote a debate paper, each writing his own side, and they agreed in the abstract that efficacy "is not in question. Rather, the effectiveness of this form of exercise is at stake." Efficacy means it works when people do it as prescribed. Effectiveness means it works when you recommend it to a population who then live their lives.

S2: Biddle's side first.

S1: He's against: "There is currently no evidence supporting HIT as a viable public health strategy." His reasons are that the studies are efficacy-only, short and small, and few are randomised trials. And on how it feels: "we need to boost people's positive feelings about exercise. Making it harder and more painful is unlikely to do this." He leans on a model of how exercise feels, called dual mode theory. In plain terms, the closer you get to your limit, the worse it feels, for nearly everybody.

S2: And Batterham?

S1: He's for, and he answered that point directly: "The bottom line is that dual mode theory applies to continuous exercise above the ventilatory threshold, not to low-volume HIT with its built-in recovery periods." His reason is that looking forward to the recovery, and the recovery itself, feel better than continuous vigorous work. His conclusion is that "practical, enjoyable and scalable HIT protocols exist that could form a viable population health strategy".

S2: Has anyone tested it over years?

S1: The longest real-world test sits between them. In Generation one hundred, the trial from lesson one, about half of each group was keeping to its prescription after five years, forty seven per cent for intervals and fifty one for steady training, in mostly healthy volunteers aged seventy to seventy seven, supervised or guided. So the lesson sorts it three ways. That intervals raise fitness in trials, as much as steady training or a little more, in less time, is established. Whether the public will do them is contested, with evidence on both sides. And whether health services should promote them is partly a value question, which the course doesn't settle.

S2: Now the people who swear training does nothing for them.

S1: The lesson gives three explanations. The first says it's in the genes. The HERITAGE Family Study put four hundred and eighty one sedentary white adults from ninety eight families through twenty weeks of the same cycle training. The average gain in maximal oxygen uptake was about four hundred millilitres a minute, but some people gained little or nothing and others more than a litre a minute. The authors concluded that trainability "is highly familial and includes a significant genetic component." The study had no group that didn't train.

S2: The second?

S1: Dose. Montero and Lundby put seventy eight untrained young men, who chose their own groups, through one to five sixty-minute sessions a week for six weeks. At one session a week, sixty nine per cent were non-responders on maximal cycling power. At four or five, none. Those who hadn't responded then did six more weeks with two extra sessions, and in the authors' words, "non-response was eliminated in all individuals." Their own limitation: "Findings were obtained from a sample of healthy young males."

S2: And the third?

S1: Measurement. Every fitness test varies from day to day. Atkinson and Batterham say that variation is "sometimes so substantial that it explains all apparent individual response differences." To measure how much people truly differ, you need a group who didn't train, to see how much scores wander on their own. Williamson, Atkinson and Batterham searched over a hundred and eighty HERITAGE publications and "could not find a comparator arm in any of these studies." And the lesson turns that test on the dose study too. Montero and Lundby had no untrained group either, so their individual counts are made just the way the statisticians warn about. What their design adds is that the same men trained more and all of them then improved. And the course read no reply from the HERITAGE investigators, so their side of that argument isn't given.

S2: So how does it sort out?

S1: That people's gains differ is established. That some people can't improve at all, given enough training, is contested, and on the sources read it's weakly supported. How much of the difference is genetic is contested too.
