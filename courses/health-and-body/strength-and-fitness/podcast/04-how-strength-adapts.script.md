S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Strength and Fitness, on the lesson called How strength adapts.

S2: The box at the top of the lesson, briefly, before anyone picks up a weight.

S1: This course is education, not advice about your own body. If you have heart, kidney or metabolic disease such as diabetes, are pregnant, or have symptoms like chest discomfort, fainting or unusual breathlessness, talk to a doctor before you start or step up training. Stop and get medical help straight away for pain or pressure in the chest, neck, jaw or arms, dizziness, palpitations, or breathlessness out of all proportion to the effort, and for chest pain call your local emergency number. Cola-coloured urine, or muscle pain and swelling far worse than the session explains, needs a doctor the same day.

S2: The lesson's first heading is Starting is the big effect. What's behind that?

S1: A twenty twenty three network meta-analysis led by Currier, which compares many programmes at once. Its strength network had one hundred and seventy eight studies. Every programme beat doing nothing, for strength and for muscle size. But between programmes, different loads, sets, sessions a week, the credible interval contained zero for, in their words, "a striking" ninety one per cent of the comparisons, one hundred and one of one hundred and eleven. The analysis couldn't tell them apart.

S2: So the programme doesn't matter?

S1: The authors stop you there themselves. The evidence "does not permit definitive, statistically valid conclusions about the equivalency" of the programmes. Not shown to differ isn't the same as shown to be equal. And the American College of Sports Medicine's twenty twenty six position stand, which pooled one hundred and thirty seven systematic reviews, says that few resistance training prescription variables "affected primary adaptations." So training beats not training, and arguing about the recipe buys you much less than starting does, at least over the weeks these trials ran, in mostly untrained people.

S2: Are those two papers independent?

S1: Not entirely. Two of the stand's authors, Currier and Phillips, also led the twenty twenty three analysis. The lesson says that's no reason to distrust the findings, but it's a thing you'd want to know.

S2: Now the beginner's puzzle. The weights go up every week and nothing looks any bigger.

S1: The standard explanation is that the nervous system changes first. In nineteen seventy nine Moritani and deVries trained seven young men and eight women for eight weeks, and concluded that neural factors accounted for the larger share of the early gain, "with hypertrophy becoming the dominant factor after the first three to five weeks." Hypertrophy is muscle growth. A twenty twenty one review by Škarabot and colleagues keeps the hedge: gains in the first two to four weeks "are thought to be primarily underpinned by neural adaptations". After training, motor units switch on at lower forces and fire faster, though "the precise site of putative neural adaptations remains unclear." With two timings that don't quite agree, the lesson's honest summary is the first few weeks, rather than a number.

S2: But after that, bigger muscles make you stronger. Surely that part's obvious.

S1: It's a live dispute. One research group, led by Loenneke, argues that "at present, there is no experimental evidence that would support any paradigm in which muscle hypertrophy is a mechanism for increasing strength with exercise." Their reason is that size and strength come apart too often, after exercise, drugs and ageing, "suggesting that these changes may be completely separate phenomena." And they're careful about what they aren't saying: "We are not dismissing the potential importance of maintaining muscle mass, particularly in clinical populations."

S2: And the other side?

S1: Taber, Vigotsky, Nuckols and Haun argue growth is one contributing cause among several. They grant part of the case: "We concede that strength can increase without increases in muscle size", and that an increase in size may not bring an increase in strength. They conclude "that in the long-term muscular hypertrophy contributes to strength", and add that "short-term studies utilizing untrained subjects may not fully capture the influence of hypertrophy on strength."

S2: Who does the course side with?

S1: Neither. On the lesson's reading, both agree bigger and stronger tend to go together, and either can change without the other. They disagree about what the mismatches mean. And the lesson flags a gap: the course read the Loenneke group's paper at abstract level only, and didn't open their own paper in the published exchange, while it read the Taber paper in full. So Taber's is the fuller statement only because it's the one the course could read.

S2: The lesson also asks, stronger at what?

S1: Specificity. A twenty twenty three meta-analysis by Spitz and colleagues pooled twelve studies that tested strength twice: in the movement people trained, and on a machine they hadn't practised, using a muscle they had trained. On the practised movement, the effect size was one point eight four. On the unpractised test, nought point eight, with wide intervals on both. So the gain on the practised lift was more than twice as big, in effect-size terms.

S2: So my bigger bench press is mostly skill?

S1: Some of it, and the stand gives the other half: there's "a considerable carryover of training effects on general muscular performance in various domains in nonadvanced trainees". The practised lift overstates the gain. It doesn't invent it. But notice what the stand's own strength numbers mean. Unless it says otherwise, strength was the one-repetition maximum, the most weight you can lift once, "in the same mode as that in which the training was performed." Most of its strength evidence is the practised lift, tested.

S2: Which is where the course's question comes from.

S1: Trained whom, for how long, measured on what? From here on the course asks it of every training claim. And when the lesson lines up the meta-analyses' own descriptions of the studies they pooled, apart from one limited to older adults, the typical study is two to three months long, in untrained people in their twenties, more often men. And, from what we just said, tested on the lift they practised.

S2: So the findings don't apply to me unless I'm a twenty-five-year-old beginner?

S1: The stand's authors would add a caution. In their twenty twenty three analysis, they write, "training experience had a minimal impact on strength and hypertrophy outcomes", and they call their recommendations "evidence-based across all ages". So the question doesn't say the findings are wrong for you. It says what they were measured on.

S2: And people vary around those averages?

S1: Enormously. Hubal and colleagues had five hundred and eighty five people train the muscles that bend one elbow for twelve weeks. Gains in their one-rep maximum ranged from nought to two hundred and fifty per cent. Same programme, same twelve weeks. So your result may land far from the average in either direction, and nothing in this course can predict it. That spread gave rise to the idea of the non-responder, which the lesson calls contested. Setting two studies side by side, it shows the label is partly a claim about what was measured and for how long. One slow measure over a few weeks doesn't make you a non-responder.

It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.
