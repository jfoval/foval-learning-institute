S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Strength and Fitness, on the lesson called Reading a claim about your training. It's the last lesson in the course.

S2: So the box at the top, one last time.

S1: This course is education, not advice about your own body. If you have heart, kidney or metabolic disease such as diabetes, are pregnant, or have symptoms like chest discomfort, fainting or unusual breathlessness, talk to a doctor before you start or step up training. Stop and get medical help straight away for pain or pressure in the chest, neck, jaw or arms, dizziness, palpitations, or breathlessness out of all proportion to the effort, and call your local emergency number for chest pain. Cola-coloured urine, or muscle pain and swelling far worse than the session explains, needs a doctor the same day.

S2: How do you read a claim, then?

S1: With the institute's sort, from earlier courses. Three bins. Measurable, where some result could count against the claim. Unmeasurable as stated. And false, where somebody checked and it didn't hold. A measured claim then gets a grade: established, contested, or a value question, one that evidence can inform and cannot decide. And a measurable claim that nobody has tested gets no grade at all. Not tested in anything read is its own verdict, and it isn't the same as false.

S2: Where does this course's question come in?

S1: At the grade. The question is: trained whom, for how long, measured on what? In training research a grade is rarely established in general. It's established for some people, over some number of weeks, on some test. The course calls those three answers the grade's address, and that word is its own. A claim can drop its address, so a small laboratory study becomes "science says". Or it can move it, so a finding about trained cyclists on a laboratory bike is retold as advice for you over months. That's harder to see, because the numbers survive the move.

S2: Give me one.

S1: Ab exercises burn belly fat. The trade name is spot reduction, and it's measurable: train one part, and measure fat there against a part you didn't train. The first test, by Vispute and colleagues, put twenty four sedentary adults on seven abdominal exercises, five days a week for six weeks, or on nothing. No fat measure changed significantly. Their conclusion: "Six weeks of abdominal exercise training alone was not sufficient to reduce abdominal subcutaneous fat and other measures of body composition". Keep the word alone. It leaves open what ab work does alongside a diet that cuts energy, which the trial didn't test. And at the end the exercise group managed forty seven curl-ups on average, against thirty two. The training worked on what it trained.

S2: That's one small trial, though.

S1: So there's a better design, each person as their own control. Ramírez-Campillo and colleagues had eleven young adults train only one leg for twelve weeks, nine hundred and sixty to twelve hundred leg presses a session at ten to thirty per cent of their one-rep maximum, while the other leg did nothing. Whole-body fat fell five point one per cent, but fat didn't change significantly in either leg. It fell in the arms and the trunk. Then a twenty twenty two meta-analysis, whose authors include two from that trial, pooled thirteen of these one-limb studies: one thousand, one hundred and fifty eight people aged fourteen to seventy one, over two to twenty weeks. The effects were consistent, it says, "meaning that spot reduction was not observed".

S2: So the claim is false.

S1: False, with its grade split by address. For the idea that working a part takes fat off that part, the finding is established, in limbs, over up to twenty weeks. For the belly itself there's one six-week trial. No pooled study trained the trunk, so past those lines, not tested is the honest phrase. And one sentence in that paper sorts differently from its finding. It says the popular belief "is probably derived from wishful thinking and convenient marketing strategies". Nothing in the analysis measured belief or marketing. It may be right. But it's the authors' opinion, and a sentence in a good paper doesn't inherit the paper's grade.

S2: Now the fat-burning zone on the gym machine.

S1: It rests on a real measurement. Achten, Gleeson and Jeukendrup tested eighteen moderately trained cyclists on a laboratory bike, analysing their breath. They wanted the intensity of fastest fat burning, which they called Fatmax, and it came at sixty four per cent of their maximal oxygen uptake, lesson three's measure of aerobic fitness, which was about seventy four per cent of maximum heart rate. Above eighty nine per cent of that maximal uptake, fat's contribution became negligible. That's established physiology. The course didn't read where the machines' bands come from, so setting the two side by side is its own comparison, not a history. The machine says: exercise in this band and you'll lose more body fat. Whom: you, not a trained cyclist. How long: weeks and months, not one test. Measured on what: the fat on your body, not the fuel in your breath. All three answers have moved.

S2: Has anyone tested the moved version?

S1: Wewege and colleagues pooled thirteen trials comparing interval training, HIIT, with moderate continuous training, MICT, in overweight or obese adults aged eighteen to forty five, over about ten weeks. Both reduced fat mass, and "There were no significant differences between HIIT and MICT for any body composition measure", though the intervals took about forty per cent less training time. A small trial of twenty young women dieting for four weeks found that doing their cardio fasted, which raises the share of fat burned, made no significant difference either. So the machine's claim is not supported by the trials read, at their address.

S2: Some people train to look a certain way. Does the course judge that?

S1: No. Appearance goals are reasons people train, and whether any of them is a good reason is a value question the course didn't research and doesn't judge in either direction. "Training for looks is shallow" and "you should want to look fit" are both value claims, and neither is the course's to make. It keeps one line from lesson one: it sets no weight, body-fat or calorie goal. And if food, eating, exercise or your weight has started to feel out of your control, tell a doctor, or call Beat on oh eight oh eight, eight oh one, oh six seven seven in the UK, or ANAD's peer-support helpline on one, eight eight eight, three seven five, seven seven six seven in the US. The NHS lists "exercising too much" among the signs of an eating disorder.

S2: And what can't the course tell you?

S1: Whether a symptom is safe to train through, how you'll respond, your own numbers, and a plan for a condition. For those there's a doctor, for anything in the box, and emergency services for chest pain. A qualified exercise professional, whom lesson one's screening form names; the form never says which qualifications count, so ask. A physiotherapist or doctor, for a pain that changes how you move and doesn't settle. And someone who can watch you move. Going to any of them isn't a failure of the course or of you. And the refusal runs both ways: the course won't tell you that you're fine to push on, and it won't tell you that you're at risk.
