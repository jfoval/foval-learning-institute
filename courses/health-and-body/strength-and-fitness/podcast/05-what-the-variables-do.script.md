S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Strength and Fitness, on the lesson called What the variables do.

S2: As always, the box at the top first.

S1: This course is education, not advice about your own body. If you have heart, kidney or metabolic disease such as diabetes, are pregnant, or have symptoms like chest discomfort, fainting or unusual breathlessness, talk to a doctor before you start or step up training. Stop and get medical help straight away for pain or pressure in the chest, neck, jaw or arms, dizziness, palpitations, or breathlessness out of all proportion to the effort, and for chest pain call your local emergency number. Cola-coloured urine, or muscle pain and swelling far worse than the session explains, needs a doctor the same day.

S2: This one takes on the gym arguments. How heavy, how many sets, to failure or not.

S1: And the lesson's promise is that each has a real answer, and for most of them the answer is smaller than the argument. One thing to hold first: there are two outcomes, not one. Strength here almost always means the one-repetition maximum on the trained lift. Muscle size is the muscle itself, measured from a scan or a biopsy. The arguments often have one answer for strength and a different one for size.

S2: Load first. Heavy or light?

S1: A twenty seventeen meta-analysis by Schoenfeld and colleagues pooled twenty one studies in which every set went to failure, comparing loads of sixty per cent of your one-rep maximum or less with heavier ones. Its conclusion: "maximal strength benefits are obtained from the use of heavy loads while muscle hypertrophy can be equally achieved across a spectrum of loading ranges."

S2: So heavy lifting makes you stronger. Full stop?

S1: Stronger on the one-rep test. Schoenfeld's analysis also tested strength a second way, pushing against something that doesn't move, which, in studies that trained with weights, neither group would have practised. On that test there were "no significant differences". That's lesson four's specificity at work. A twenty twenty review from the Spitz group put it directly: the differences between heavy and light "can be reduced when the group training with a low load is allowed additional exposure to the maximal strength test." So part of heavy training's advantage may be practice at lifting heavy.

S2: And for muscle size, anything goes?

S1: With a condition. The American College of Sports Medicine's twenty twenty six position stand lists loads from thirty to a hundred per cent of the one-rep maximum among the things that didn't change growth. But in the studies behind the light-load finding, every set was taken to failure. Light loads build muscle when the sets are very hard. So "you don't need to lift heavy to build muscle" is fair, with that condition attached. And for a bigger one-rep maximum, the lesson's fair summary is to practise lifting heavy. Both findings come mostly from young adults over a few weeks.

S2: Sets, then. How much is enough?

S1: The biggest analysis is Pelland and colleagues', sixty seven studies and two thousand and fifty eight people, published in twenty twenty six. Gains in size and strength both rise with the number of sets, but "both best-fit models suggest diminishing returns, with the diminishing returns for strength being considerably more pronounced." The course read that published abstract, and the full text of an earlier preprint that hadn't been peer reviewed, whose figures may differ. For strength, the preprint says "one set was identified as the minimum effective dose", with detectable extra gains up to about four sets a week, counting a set that works a muscle only indirectly as half. For size there was "no clear plateau", though few studies have looked past about twenty five of those sets a week. And the authors' own hedge on all of it: "multiple dose-response forms are compatible with the present analysis".

S2: So a set or two is all anyone needs?

S1: That's the mirror of "more is always better", and the lesson says it fits the strength curve and drops the size one. Strength flattens early. Size keeps creeping up, each extra set buying less. The stand says "the exact number of sets required to optimize adaptations cannot be ascertained." Its practical line: "healthy adults are advised to complete at least two sets per exercise."

S2: Now failure. My training partner says the last two reps are the only ones that count.

S1: A twenty twenty two meta-analysis by Grgic and colleagues, in young adults over a median of eight weeks, concluded: "Training to muscle failure does not seem to be required for gains in strength and muscle size. However, training in this manner does not seem to have detrimental effects on these adaptations, either." Whether going closer to failure adds muscle is contested, and the lesson gives three positions in their own words. Robinson and colleagues estimated how close to failure each study's sets had been, and found "changes in muscle size increased as sets were terminated closer to failure", adding that "caution is warranted when interpreting the present analysis due to its exploratory nature." Refalo and colleagues found "a trivial advantage" for failure, and with failure strictly defined, "no advantage for either" approach. And the stand: going to failure "does not enhance gains in strength, hypertrophy, and power, and so is not necessary for benefits to occur."

S2: So my partner's wrong?

S1: "The only ones that count" goes past all three. They agree a set doesn't have to reach failure to count. What's open is whether those last reps add a little muscle. The stand's own target is two to three reps in reserve, though it admits the exact target can't yet be pinned down. It also warns that going to failure may be inadvisable for some people, older adults for example, because of the strain on blood vessels and the risk of injury when form breaks down.

S2: The lesson also tracks how the official advice changed.

S1: The twenty oh nine stand was detailed. For novices, loads of an eight to twelve repetition maximum, and two to three days a week, rising to four or five for advanced lifters. It also said its recommendations "should be applied in context and should be contingent upon an individual's target goals, physical capacity, and training status." The twenty twenty six authors say the twenty oh two and twenty oh nine stands were criticised for lacking evidence-based rigour. Their example: the twenty oh nine advice on how often novices should train was graded the highest quality of evidence, yet on their account rested on three studies with fifty nine participants in all. That's their description, and the course didn't open the twenty oh nine text to check it.

S2: And the new advice?

S1: Much shorter. Its primary recommendation is that healthy adults train "with high effort", "at least twice weekly, with all major muscle groups being engaged." Then something the authors mark as a view, not a finding. R T is resistance training, and R T x their shorthand for a training prescription: "we propose that individualizing programs to increase RT participation is, from our perspective, more important than conforming to specific RTx criteria outlined in previous Position Stands." They allow that the older details "may be an appropriate starting point for some individuals". Whether guidance should aim to get more people training, or to get the most out of those already training, is partly a question of values. The course read no written case for the more prescriptive side beyond the twenty oh nine stand itself, so it leaves that question open.
