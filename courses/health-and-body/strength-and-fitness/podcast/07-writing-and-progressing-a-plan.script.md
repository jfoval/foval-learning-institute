S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Strength and Fitness, on the lesson called Writing and progressing a plan.

S2: Before we write anything, the box at the top of every lesson in this course.

S1: This course is education, not advice about your own body. If you have heart, kidney or metabolic disease such as diabetes, are pregnant, or have symptoms like chest discomfort, fainting or unusual breathlessness, talk to a doctor before you start or step up training. Stop and get medical help straight away for pain or pressure in the chest, neck, jaw or arms, dizziness, palpitations, or breathlessness out of all proportion to the effort, and call your local emergency number for chest pain. Cola-coloured urine, or muscle pain and swelling far worse than the session explains, needs a doctor the same day.

S2: So where does a plan start?

S1: With the American College of Sports Medicine's twenty twenty six position stand, from lessons four and five. Its primary recommendation is resistance training with high effort, at least twice a week, with all major muscle groups engaged. That's a target, not a plan. It doesn't say what all major muscle groups means when you're standing in a kitchen. The stand does offer an answer, carefully. It says the evidence it pooled didn't compare body regions at all. Then, opening with the words "In the view of the authors", it says that working the upper and lower body, with a push and a pull for each, four regions, is sufficient to target major muscle groups.

S2: So that part's an opinion.

S1: A considered one, from thirteen authors who had just pooled a hundred and thirty seven systematic reviews, and it's the one the lesson builds on. But in your plan it goes down as a view, not as evidence. That's the habit the lesson teaches: beside every line, write what it rests on. Guidance, where a named body recommends it. Evidence, where trials measured it. View, where authors say so and label it as their opinion. Or convention, a sensible choice nobody has tested.

S2: And the rest of the numbers?

S1: From lesson five. At least two sets per exercise. Sets near failure, or a target of two to three reps in reserve, with the stand's admission that the exact target can't yet be pinned down. It also says lifting to failure may be inadvisable for some groups, giving older people as its example, so the plan stops short on purpose. And at least two sessions a week, which is also the World Health Organization's floor.

S2: Surely the big one is progression. You have to keep adding weight.

S1: Here the stand may surprise people who've lifted for years. It says that, quote, "progression is not necessary to achieve beneficial outcomes, and overload, or more accurately, increasing the stimulus in some manner, is likely a requirement only for those seeking continued longer term progress." Straight after, it adds that in some groups, because they're inexperienced and have to start with low loads, progression is needed for a meaningful benefit at all, and that whether to keep progressing can be a personal decision. And its conclusion still says healthy adults "should perform progressive RT", meaning resistance training.

S2: Let's build one, then.

S1: The lesson's example is Dele, who is made up for teaching. He's thirty eight, healthy, answered no to every question on lesson one's screening form, and has never lifted anything on purpose. No gym: a sturdy chair, a kitchen table, a backpack and some water bottles. Two sessions, say Monday and Thursday; the gap follows older ACSM advice that nothing read here tested for beginners. For the four slots, a squat to a chair, a press-up with his hands on the kitchen counter, a hip hinge that becomes a backpack deadlift, and a one-arm backpack row. Those last two are the course's own substitutes. For each, a version he can do for eight to twelve reps with two or three still left.

S2: Where does eight to twelve come from?

S1: It's borrowed from the twenty oh nine stand, which advised novices to use loads they could lift at most eight to twelve times. Stopping two or three short puts Dele a little lighter, so his range is a convention adapted from that. And there's a more cautious start on record. The American Heart Association says a new programme's first intensity should be forty to sixty per cent of your one-rep maximum, the most you can lift once. That's lighter than the loads Dele works up to. His first sessions, a set or two of about five, well short of hard, sit nearer it, and if you'd rather follow the AHA, keep to loads you could lift many more times for the first few weeks.

S2: Then how does the plan move forward?

S1: Hold the effort steady and it almost happens by itself. If Dele stops every set at two to three in reserve and he gets stronger, the same set gets easier. To stay on target he has to do more reps, add weight, or move to a harder version. The course calls its rule double progression, and says plainly that it's a convention: work within eight to twelve, and when every set reaches twelve at the effort target, make it harder and drop back towards the bottom. Its closest relative is the twenty oh nine stand's rule, an increase of two to ten per cent in load once you can do one or two reps over the target, and that was an expert recommendation, not a trial finding.

S2: Walk me through his log.

S1: His row, with six kilos in the backpack. Session three, ten reps then nine. Session four, eleven and ten. Session five, twelve and eleven. Session six, twelve and twelve, both with three in reserve. So he adds a one and a half litre bottle, and session seven might read seven and a half kilos, nine reps and eight.

S2: That's a big jump.

S1: Twenty five per cent, far above the two to ten. At home, load comes in whole bottles, and a press-up only gets harder in whole steps of surface height. That's the practical reason for a rep range: a coarse jump in load is absorbed by a drop in reps, and then the reps climb back.

S2: One more fear. You stop for a couple of months and it's all gone.

S1: A twenty twenty study trained thirty older men for twelve weeks, stopped them for twelve, then trained them again. They found "a modest loss of strength and power", five to fifteen per cent, and less than eight weeks of retraining got them back to their trained one-rep maximum. A twenty thirteen meta-analysis of a hundred and three studies adds two conditions: the longer the break, the more you lose, and the effect was larger in people over sixty five. Going back, the rule is the course's safety spine: start below what you could do. The military guideline on muscle breakdown lists exercise "unmatched to fitness level" among its contributing factors, and your old numbers belong to a fitness level you don't have right now.
