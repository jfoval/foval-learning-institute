S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Strength and Fitness, on the lesson called How hard.

S2: First, the box that opens every lesson in this course.

S1: This course is education, not advice about your own body. If you have heart, kidney or metabolic disease such as diabetes, are pregnant, or have symptoms like chest discomfort, fainting or unusual breathlessness, talk to a doctor before you start or step up training. Stop and get medical help straight away for pain or pressure in the chest, neck, jaw or arms, dizziness, palpitations, or breathlessness out of all proportion to the effort, and for chest pain call your local emergency number. Cola-coloured urine, or muscle pain and swelling far worse than the session explains, needs a doctor the same day.

S2: So. Lesson one gave us minutes of moderate and vigorous activity. What do those words actually mean?

S1: The lesson says two questions hide in the word intensity. Absolute intensity is how much energy the activity costs, whoever does it. Relative intensity is how big a share of your own capacity it takes. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the CDC, put the reason for needing both in one line: "an activity that is vigorous for a person of average fitness may only be moderate for a very fit person."

S2: Start with absolute.

S1: The unit is the MET, the metabolic equivalent. In the CDC's words, "One MET equals the energy or oxygen used while sitting quietly." The twenty twenty four Adult Compendium of Physical Activities turned measured energy costs into a table of one thousand, one hundred and fourteen activities. Moderate is three to five point nine METs, vigorous is six and over. Brisk walking for exercise is four point eight. Running a twelve-minute mile is eight point five. But the Compendium itself says its values are a starting point, not a person's own energy cost.

S2: Then relative. How do I measure my own effort?

S1: The CDC gives a nought to ten scale, where nought is sitting and ten is maximal effort. Moderate is five or six, and vigorous begins at seven or eight. But the most practical measure needs no scale at all: the talk test. The CDC again: "a person doing moderate-intensity aerobic activity can talk, but not sing, during the activity. A person doing vigorous-intensity activity cannot say more than a few words without pausing for a breath."

S2: That sounds like folk wisdom.

S1: It's been tested against gas analysis. In two thousand and four Persinger and colleagues put sixteen healthy volunteers through tests where the effort rose step by step on a treadmill and a bike. Their abstract reports that "At the point where speech first became difficult, exercise intensity was almost exactly equivalent to ventilatory threshold." The lesson supplies the background: that's roughly the point where your breathing starts to rise faster than the work does. But this sample is small, sixteen people, and the classic studies come from one research group, so the lesson holds the talk test as a good tool rather than a precise one.

S2: Now heart rate itself. Two hundred and twenty minus your age.

S1: Two exercise physiologists, Robergs and Landwehr, went looking for the study behind it in two thousand and two. In their words: "Surprisingly, there is no published record of research for this equation." Their verdict, which is their judgement in a commentary, not a consensus statement, is that the formula "has no scientific merit for use in exercise physiology and related fields."

S2: So there's a better one?

S1: The best known is Tanaka's, from pooled group averages of three hundred and fifty one studies: two hundred and eight, minus nought point seven times your age. But in twenty twenty five Martin and colleagues compared seven formulas with the maximum actually measured on a treadmill in two hundred and thirty adults. Tanaka's had the smallest average error, about seven beats. Two hundred and twenty minus age showed no significant bias on average, and the authors say it "exhibited arguably the most consistent performance across the sample", supporting "its continued use as a practical and generalizable option". So the lesson says the newest data don't crown a winner, and both miss individuals by the same wide margin. For each formula, the authors put the limits of agreement at about twenty beats either side. That's the range within which ninety five per cent of people's measured maximums fell.

S2: What does that do to a real person?

S1: Take a fifty-year-old the lesson made up. Two hundred and twenty minus fifty is one hundred and seventy. Tanaka gives one hundred and seventy three. Nearly the same. Now put twenty beats either side of one hundred and seventy three, and their real maximum could be anywhere from about one hundred and fifty three to one hundred and ninety three. Tell them to train at seventy per cent of maximum, and that's about one hundred and seven beats at the low end and one hundred and thirty five at the high end. Twenty eight beats apart. Two people born the same year could get either target, and neither knows which one they are.

S2: So the formula's useless.

S1: That's the mirror mistake. Martin's authors call age-based formulas "useful for population-level benchmarking and for setting initial training targets". A formula gives you a sensible place to start. What it can't give you is your own number. And their sample was mostly recreational athletes, one hundred and seventy four men and fifty six women in one university lab, and they call the analysis exploratory. One caution too. Medicines such as beta-blockers, the authors note, "can blunt" the heart's response to exercise. If you take a medicine for your heart or blood pressure, the lesson says the talk test and the nought to ten scale are the measures to use, and your doctor is the person to ask about targets.

S2: The weight room, then. Nobody sings through a set of squats.

S1: Lifters use repetitions in reserve, R I R for short: when you finish a set, how many more good reps could you have done? The American College of Sports Medicine's twenty twenty six position stand recommends "high effort", from sets taken to near failure or "a target of two to three repetitions in reserve". It also says "there is insufficient evidence to quantify exact RIR and perceived exertion targets". A range, not a precise dose.

S2: Can people even judge that?

S1: Halperin and colleagues pooled twelve studies of four hundred and fourteen people. People tended to underpredict how many reps they had left, by nought point nine five repetitions, "but with considerable heterogeneity". So on average they had about one more rep in them than they thought, and the studies differed a lot. Guesses were slightly better near failure and in sets of twelve reps or fewer. In the authors' words, "It remains to be determined whether the observed degree of inaccuracy should be considered acceptable." The lesson adds its own inference: aim for two to three in reserve and underpredict like the average person, and you're probably still inside or just above the range, a forgiving direction to be wrong in.
