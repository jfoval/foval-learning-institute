S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Strength and Fitness, on the lesson called Training across a life.

S2: The lesson says the box at the top of every lesson matters more here than anywhere else in the course.

S1: It does. This course is education, not advice about your own body. If you have heart, kidney or metabolic disease such as diabetes, are pregnant, or have symptoms like chest discomfort, fainting or unusual breathlessness, talk to a doctor before you start or step up training. Stop and get medical help straight away for pain or pressure in the chest, neck, jaw or arms, dizziness, palpitations, or breathlessness out of all proportion to the effort, and call your local emergency number for chest pain. Cola-coloured urine, or muscle pain and swelling far worse than the session explains, needs a doctor the same day. And the lesson adds its own line: none of it is a plan for your pregnancy, your child, your blood sugar or your back. The plan for those is made with someone who can examine you.

S2: So who is this lesson about?

S1: People who are usually told not to lift: older people, pregnant women, children, people with a bad back or a condition. For most of them the guidance the course read says yes, with conditions, and the lesson gives you the permission and the condition together, because either one on its own misleads. And each group has its own evidence, because the main strength literature trained untrained people in their twenties, more often men, for two or three months.

S2: Older adults first. Can they still gain?

S1: A twenty ten meta-analysis by Peterson and colleagues pooled forty seven studies of one thousand and seventy nine people aged fifty or over. Strength rose by twenty four to thirty three per cent across four machine lifts, roughly a quarter to a third. One caution from the course: where people were tested on the machines they trained on, which is usual in this field, part of the gain is skill at the machine. So read it as older adults getting much better at the lifts they trained, which is still a large and useful result. And one condition, from the twenty twenty six ACSM stand: it may be that lifting to fatigue is inadvisable for some groups, older people among them, because of risks to vascular health and of injury from poor form. Lesson five showed failure wasn't needed for gains, so stopping short gives up little.

S2: For a lot of older people the real reason is not falling.

S1: The best single test is a twenty nineteen Cochrane review of a hundred and eight trials, with twenty three thousand, four hundred and seven people aged sixty and over living in the community. Across all types of exercise against a control, exercise reduced the rate of falls by twenty three per cent, on high-certainty evidence. Balance and functional exercise, meaning practice at balance and at everyday movements like standing up and stepping, reduced falls with high certainty. Programmes combining several types, most commonly balance and functional work plus resistance exercise, probably reduced them. And for programmes that are primarily resistance training, the review's words are "We are uncertain".

S2: Uncertain, meaning it doesn't work?

S1: Meaning the review couldn't tell. The American Heart Association is more confident. It says resistance training "can reduce the risk of falls and injury from falls" in older adults, and that programmes with balance challenges built in "may be the most effective". That's a narrative statement citing one reference, and the Cochrane review is the graded test. Where the two overlap they agree: strength work with balance built in. So if falls are your reason, lifting alone isn't what the evidence tested best.

S2: Pregnancy.

S1: The two guidelines the course read encourage it, with conditions. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists: "Women with uncomplicated pregnancies should be encouraged to engage in aerobic and strength-conditioning exercises before, during, and after pregnancy." The Canadian guideline says all women without contraindication should be physically active throughout pregnancy, with resistance training among the activities. The condition is in those words, uncomplicated and without contraindication, and that isn't something you can decide from a course. ACOG puts a thorough clinical evaluation first.

S2: What rules it out?

S1: The Canadian list of reasons to avoid strenuous exercise altogether includes the waters breaking early, premature labour, unexplained persistent vaginal bleeding, preeclampsia and uncontrolled high blood pressure. Even then, it says, women may continue their usual daily activities, just not more strenuous ones. ACOG's practical conditions: stay well hydrated, avoid long periods lying flat on your back, and avoid contact activities with a high risk of abdominal trauma or imbalance. The Canadian guideline adds: if exercising on your back makes you light-headed, nauseous or unwell, change position. ACOG says rating how hard it feels may be a better guide than heart rate, and offers the talk test. And if you were inactive before pregnancy, progress more gradually.

S2: Is there a cautious side?

S1: ACOG itself gives it. Resistance training wasn't considered safe in the early guidelines on exercise in pregnancy, because of potential injury and a possible slowing of the baby's heart rate from breath-holding under strain. "Consequently, there is sparse literature on this topic." For elite athletes who want to keep training hard, it advises getting approval from their health care providers and considering lighter loads than before pregnancy. That's written for elite athletes, not every pregnant lifter, but it tells you the permission is broad and the evidence on hard resistance training in pregnancy is thin.

S2: And the signs to stop?

S1: ACOG lists ten. Vaginal bleeding. Abdominal pain. Regular painful contractions. Amniotic fluid leakage. Breathlessness before exertion. Dizziness. Headache. Chest pain. Muscle weakness affecting balance. Calf pain or swelling. Any one of them means stop the session and contact whoever is looking after the pregnancy.

S2: Children. Doesn't lifting stunt growth?

S1: A twenty ten review by Faigenbaum and Myer, of resistance training in young athletes, says injury to the growth cartilage "has not been reported in any prospective youth resistance training study that provided professional guidance and instruction", and that there is "no evidence that resistance training will negatively impact growth in height during childhood and adolescence". The worry came from real case reports, and the review says most of those injuries were caused by improper technique, poorly chosen loads or lack of qualified adult supervision. So the permission and the condition come in one sentence: resistance training "can be a safe, effective and worthwhile activity for children and adolescents provided that qualified professionals supervise all training sessions and provide age-appropriate instruction on proper lifting procedures and safe training guidelines." A supervised school or club programme is what the evidence describes. A bar and plates in the garage is not. And the course doesn't write a programme for a child; the coach does.
