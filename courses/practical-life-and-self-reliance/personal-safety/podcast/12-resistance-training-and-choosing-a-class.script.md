S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Personal Safety, on the lesson called Resistance training, and choosing a class.

S2: Odd, for a course that keeps saying it isn't a self-defence class.

S1: And it says so again. This is education, not legal advice and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger right now, call nine nine nine in the UK or nine one one in the US. Much of this lesson discusses research on rape and sexual assault, and the full lesson has a content note with support lines. And before any evidence, one thing it won't do. It won't suggest that anyone who was assaulted should have taken a class, or that a class they took should have worked. A trial's result is an average over hundreds of people, never a measure of what one person could have done.

S2: So what's the best evidence?

S1: A programme called Enhanced Assess, Acknowledge, Act, or EAAA. Senn and colleagues tested it in a randomised trial at three Canadian universities. First-year women were assigned at random either to the programme, four three-hour units, or to a session with brochures, which was common university practice. The word to notice is acquaintances. The programme was built for the risk young women at university face mainly from men they know, not a stranger in a car park.

S2: And the result?

S1: Over a year, nine point eight per cent of the brochure group were raped, against five point two per cent of the programme group. The headline is a forty-six per cent cut.

S2: Forty-six per cent sounds enormous.

S1: So the lesson reads it three ways, in the course's own arithmetic. Relative, forty-six per cent. Absolute, four point six percentage points, so about four or five fewer women in every hundred. And one in how many: about one completed rape prevented in a year for every twenty-two women who took the programme. Then the interval. It runs from six point eight per cent to sixty-nine point one: the data fit a little good, or a great deal. One in twenty-two could be anything from about one in twelve to about one in a hundred and seventy.

S2: And that's for first-year university women in Canada.

S1: Volunteers, reporting in a survey, over one year, against brochures. In the course's labels, tested for them. For anyone else, an inference.

S2: Did it last?

S1: The authors followed some of the women for two years, the ones who joined in the first year of recruitment. The gap in completed rape narrowed a little and held, at three point seven points. But the difference was no longer statistically significant at eighteen months or two years. Attempted rape held, at about sixty-three or sixty-four per cent, significant throughout. The authors say a booster may be needed in the second year.

S2: There's an obvious objection, though. If she fights him off, doesn't he just go and find someone else?

S1: The authors print that critique themselves, and answer that they suspect perpetrators may learn lessons that change what they do next. And they grant the point that matters most: which explanation is right is an empirical question. The trial measured what happened to the women in it, not to other women the same men met afterwards.

S2: And underneath, a bigger argument.

S1: A value question, and the course describes it without choosing. Put at its strongest in the course's own words, since it hasn't read a critic making the case at length, one side is that when people at risk are taught to fend off the people who harm them, the work of prevention lands on those at risk, and even a programme that works may leave the offenders untouched. The other says that, on its proponents' reading, this approach has the strongest trial evidence of any campus programme, and holding it back until wider change arrives leaves women without the one tool shown to help them now. What both sides share, as the course read them, is that the responsibility is the offender's. The trial's authors say interventions for women must not inadvertently suggest that women are responsible for sexual assault.

S2: Say I want to learn something anyway. What can a page do?

S1: Teach you to recognise trouble, what fear does, and how to choose a class. It can't teach a strike or an escape. A class is where practice happens. American workplace safety guidance for health and social care says self-defence training should include a hands-on component, and carrying that beyond those workplaces is the course's step. And from the police studies in lesson ten, the course infers that practising against a partner who pushes back, with your consent and in steps you choose, may prepare you better than watching. Sensible, untested.

S2: And if I'm a man?

S1: That's the gap, said out loud. The research behind the course found no controlled evidence on self-defence classes for men, or for general assault or robbery. That gap includes men who have been sexually assaulted, whom lesson eleven names. In England and Wales, men were more likely than women to be victims of violence by a stranger. But no evidence isn't evidence that classes don't work, because a search that turns up nothing is weak evidence either way. The honest label is practitioner opinion.

S2: So how do I choose?

S1: The course names no martial art, style or school. Its own checklist, labelled sensible, untested, includes: covers avoidance, talking and getting away, not only fighting. Covers people you know. Practice with a partner, consented and in steps. Never blames survivors. Lets you opt out of any drill. And is candid about what a short course can't do. What not to count as evidence: a black belt, a long lineage, years in business, or a promise you'll never be a victim.

S2: Give me a case.

S1: Dominic, thirty-four, works nights at a parcel depot on the edge of Exeter and walks across a big car park at about three in the morning. Three classes, invented for the lesson. Class A: a two-hour seminar, five moves that end any fight, taught by a seventh-degree master. Class B: four three-hour sessions for shift workers, noticing trouble, talking it down, getting away, padded partners, a session on the law, drills you choose, a refresher six months later. Class C: combat fitness, three evenings a week.

S2: A goes first.

S1: On the first item: nothing on avoiding, talking or leaving, and a promise no source supports. C may be good exercise, but on its own description it isn't a self-defence class. B meets most of the list, though it says nothing about people you know. And what B can't promise is that he'll be safer in that car park, because nothing controlled that the course found covers a man and a stranger.

S2: And for anyone who's been assaulted?

S1: You don't have to take a class, and not taking one isn't something anyone can hold against you. And if you took a class and were assaulted anyway, the class didn't fail because of you, and nor did you.

It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening.
