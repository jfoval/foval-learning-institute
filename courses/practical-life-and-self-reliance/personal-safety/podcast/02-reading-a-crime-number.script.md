S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in the course called Personal Safety, lesson two. Reading a crime number.

S2: The same reminder as last time, briefly?

S1: This course is education. It isn't legal advice, and it isn't a self-defence class. If you're in danger now, call nine nine nine in the UK or nine one one in the US.

S2: So. Suppose my local paper runs the headline, violent crime jumps twenty percent in our area. I walk home after dark. Should I change anything?

S1: You can't answer that yet, and neither can the paper, until you know what was counted. A crime number is never a plain count of crimes. It's what survives a series of filters. Whether the victim told anyone, whether the police wrote it down, who the source could reach, how the offence was defined, which months it covers, and what it was divided by. Change any one and the number moves, with no change in how many people were hurt.

S2: Start with who counts.

S1: There are two main ways. Count what the police recorded, or pick a sample of people and ask them what happened to them. The US and England and Wales both do both. In the US the survey is the National Crime Victimization Survey, and because it works by interviewing victims, there's one crime it can never count.

S2: Homicide.

S1: Right. The police side, the FBI's figures, include homicide, crimes against businesses, and crimes against children under twelve, none of which the survey reaches. In England and Wales, the Office for National Statistics publishes both and says what each is for. The survey is better for long-term trends in violence, theft, criminal damage, fraud and computer misuse. Police figures give more insight into rarer, more harmful offences reported to the police, and it names homicide, offences involving weapons, and robbery.

S2: Once I know who counted, what else?

S1: What kind of number it is. The US survey defines three. Victimizations, the number of times people were victims, so someone robbed twice counts twice. Incidents, the criminal acts, so one robbery of two friends is one incident and two victimizations. And prevalence, the unique people who were victims, so someone robbed twice counts once. For twenty twenty four, the rate of violent victimization was twenty three point three per thousand people aged twelve and over, and the prevalence was one point four five percent. Put as counts of people, out of every thousand, about fifteen were victims of violence at least once, and between them they had about twenty three victimizations. The gap is people hurt more than once.

S2: Give me a case where the filters change the answer.

S1: A short brief from the Bureau of Justice Statistics gives three rates of violent crime for the US in twenty twenty four, leaving out simple assault. The survey's rate, eight point nine per thousand. The survey's rate for the part victims said reached the police, five point three. And the police-data rate, three point seven. The largest is well over twice the smallest, and all three are correct.

S2: How can all three be correct?

S1: Take them one filter at a time. From eight point nine to five point three is the reporting filter. Same interviews, same people, same offences. Five point three just keeps the crimes the victim said reached the police. From five point three to three point seven is the source filter, and several things change at once. The survey divides by people aged twelve and over, the police rate by all residents, a bigger group, which pushes the police rate down. But the police figure also includes businesses, young children and homicide, which push it up. The brief doesn't say how the two net out.

S2: How much crime never reaches the police at all?

S1: In the US survey for twenty twenty four, about forty eight percent of violent victimizations were reported, and thirty percent of property crime. So of every ten violent crimes the survey found, about five reached the police. In England and Wales, across the survey's main set of crimes, leaving out fraud and computer misuse, an estimated thirty six percent. The two cover different crimes, so they don't compare the countries. And for fraud, the Federal Trade Commission cites a study finding only four point eight percent of victims of mass-market consumer fraud complained to a Better Business Bureau or a government body. About one in twenty.

S2: And once a crime is reported, it's counted?

S1: Not necessarily. In twenty fourteen the inspectorate of police in England and Wales audited recording and found over eight hundred thousand reported crimes going unrecorded each year. That's nineteen percent. For violence against the person it was thirty three percent, and twenty six for sexual offences. About one reported violent crime in three wasn't making it into the count.

S2: So when forces tighten up their recording...

S1: The police count rises even if the number of attacks stays the same. The statistics office says increases in the police count over the last ten years have been largely influenced by improvements in recording standards, and that changes in recording have affected the reliability of police figures, particularly for violent crime, making them a better indicator of police activity than of crime trends. The survey's estimate of violence was thirty three percent lower in the year to March twenty twenty six than a decade earlier. So your local twenty percent could be the same attacks, with more of them written down.

S2: In the US, isn't all this a political fight?

S1: Whether US crime rose or fell after twenty twenty became one, and the course takes no side on whose account was right. The Council on Criminal Justice, which describes itself as independent and nonpartisan, found the two US measures show similar long-term trends for serious violent crime, and that much of the difference comes from methods rather than inaccurate data. Over a few recent years they pointed different ways, and both the FBI and the Bureau of Justice Statistics describe them as different tools, not a true figure and a false one.

S2: Bring it back to my headline.

S1: Five questions. Who counted? A police count or a survey. What did they count? Which offences, and victimizations, incidents or people. Whose harm, and where? The whole area, or the streets you use. When? One year against the last, or a run of years. And per how many? Is twenty percent a rise from fifty recorded offences to sixty, where one busy weekend could do it? If the headline can't answer those, it isn't on its own a reason to change your walk home. If it's a police count rising over several years in the offences you'd actually meet on that walk, it's worth reading lessons four to seven with it in mind. The course labels that reading sensible, untested. It tells you how much weight a number can bear, not what to do.
