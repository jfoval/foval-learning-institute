S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Personal Safety, on the lesson called Stepping in for someone else.

S2: So this time it isn't happening to me.

S1: Right. You're three seats back, or across the car park, with a few seconds to decide whether to do anything. The usual line first. This is education, not legal advice and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger right now, call nine nine nine in the UK or nine one one in the US. And the lesson adds something for everyone in it. The person harassing or attacking someone is responsible for what they do. A bystander who froze, or looked away, didn't cause it, and nor did one who stepped in and got hurt. If you've been in that moment, nothing here is a verdict on what you did.

S2: Start with the big fear. Do the people who step in get hurt?

S1: Liebst and colleagues went through ninety-three recordings of real public disputes and followed four hundred and seventeen people who stepped in. Three point six per cent were physically harmed. On the course's arithmetic, that's about fifteen people. Their words were that interveners were rarely physically harmed.

S2: That's lower than I'd have guessed.

S1: Two cautions, and they're the course's, not the authors'. The abstract doesn't say how serious the harm was. And those people stepped in at moments of their own choosing. So three point six per cent describes those moments. It isn't a promise about stepping into any fight at all.

S2: Does it hold everywhere?

S1: No, and that's the newest study in the lesson. Lindegaard and colleagues coded forty-three robberies of shops and businesses. Bystanders helped in about thirty-five per cent of them, against ninety point nine per cent in the public assault footage. And more bystanders didn't mean more help. The authors describe robberies as a setting with fewer bystanders and more weapons, and the course won't supply a reason they didn't give. A number about street fights isn't a number about every emergency.

S2: So if I do want to help, what does helping look like?

S1: One widely taught scheme comes from Right To Be, a US organisation that trains people to respond to harassment in public. Five Ds. Distract, Delegate, Document, Delay and Direct. Four of them are indirect. Distract means ignoring the person doing it and talking to the person being harassed about something else entirely, or just getting in the way. Delegate is asking someone else for help, a bus driver, say. Document is recording or taking notes, but only if someone else is already helping and you're safe, and never posting it without permission. Delay is checking in afterwards. And Direct, naming the behaviour, comes with the page's own warning: use it with caution.

S2: Is any of that proven?

S1: The course found no controlled test of the five Ds. In its labels, they're practitioner opinion. The strongest test is of Green Dot, the programme the first three Ds came from. Twenty-six Kentucky high schools, randomly assigned. Sexual violence perpetration, as students reported it in anonymous surveys, was seventeen per cent lower in the Green Dot schools in year three, and twenty-one per cent lower in year four, though the range around the year three figure comes close to no difference at its top. But that's a school-wide programme of talks and training, over years. It isn't a result for one person on a bus. And whether reading about the five Ds makes anyone more likely to help, or safer when they do, nobody has measured.

S2: Walk me through the bus.

S1: Nearly eleven at night, Southampton. You're two rows behind a young woman on her phone. A man across the aisle has been making comments to her for several stops. She moved seats once, and he moved too. She hasn't answered him. None of this is her doing. Start with Direct, because it's the one people picture. Right To Be has four questions. Are you safe? Is she? Does it seem unlikely to escalate? Can you tell whether she wants someone to speak up?

S2: And he's already followed her once.

S1: So you haven't got four yeses, and on the page's own test this isn't the moment for Direct. Distract costs least. You take the empty seat across from her, which puts you between them, and talk to her about anything else. If that doesn't end it, tell the driver at the next stop. Then Delay: once he's gone, are you okay, I saw what he was doing, and it wasn't okay. And if he hits her, grabs her or threatens to hurt her, someone may be in immediate danger, and GOV.UK's page says that's a nine nine nine call.

S2: The lesson's second case is darker.

S1: It's an armed robbery in a shop, and in the full lesson there's a content note before it, with support lines. Theo is third in the queue in Albany, New York, holding a carton of milk. A man puts a knife on the counter and tells the cashier, Joel, to empty the till. Everything from the bus pulls Theo towards doing something. Here the sources point the other way. Help in about a third of robberies, not nine in ten. American workplace safety recommendations tell employers that staff may be trained to hand money over without resistance. And what the man wants is property. London's Metropolitan Police put it plainly: property can be replaced, you can't. Here it isn't even Theo's.

S2: So Theo does what?

S1: The course's reading, labelled sensible, untested. Stay out of it, leave quietly if he can, call nine one one once it's safe, and check on Joel afterwards. And suppose the other customer stood completely still the whole time. Did she do anything wrong? No. Freezing is automatic, not chosen. The responsibility lies with the man with the knife.

S2: Last question. Should the bystander on the bus call the police?

S1: This is a real disagreement, mostly about values, and the course takes neither side. Right To Be says don't, unless the person being harassed has asked you to. Its reason is that some people and communities aren't safe or comfortable with the police. As the course reads it, the person being harassed lives with what a police response brings, so the choice should be theirs. The official pages start from the situation instead. GOV.UK says call nine nine nine if a crime is in progress or someone is in immediate danger. None of them says to ask the victim first. As the course reads them, a crime in progress, or someone in danger, is itself the reason to call. And put at its strongest, asking the person in front of the one doing it can put them on the spot.

S2: So where does that leave me?

S1: Two things don't depend on the argument. Where someone is being assaulted or is in immediate danger, it's a call. And both sides put safety first. The real difference is harassment where nobody's in immediate danger, and who decides then.
