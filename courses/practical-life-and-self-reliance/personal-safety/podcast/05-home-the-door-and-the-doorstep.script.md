S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Personal Safety, on the lesson called Home, the door and the doorstep.

S2: Before we start, the thing this course says every time.

S1: This is education. It isn't legal advice, and it isn't a self-defence class. If you're in danger right now, call nine nine nine in the UK or nine one one in the US. And responsibility for a crime lies with the person who commits it. A lock changes how hard a house is to get into. It has nothing to do with who chose to break in.

S2: So why start with burglary?

S1: It has the course's strongest evidence. In the nineteen nineties, burglary in England and Wales began a long fall, and criminologists argued about why. Tseloni and colleagues tested one explanation, the security hypothesis: households fitted better locks and lights, and burglars found fewer houses they could get into. They used the Crime Survey for England and Wales, which asks households both whether they were burgled and which security devices they had.

S2: How do you test that from a survey?

S1: With a neat prediction. The survey sorts burglaries into forced entries, like a lock forced or glass broken, and unforced ones, through a door or window left open or unlocked. If security caused the fall, only the forced ones should drop, because a lock only matters to a burglar who has to get past it. That's what they found. Forced entries fell from thirty-one per thousand households in nineteen ninety-three to seven per thousand by the survey year two thousand and six to two thousand and seven, a fall of seventy-seven per cent. Unforced burglaries stayed at seven per thousand. The authors concluded there is strong evidence that security caused the decline. But nobody randomly assigned locks to houses. It's a strong comparison, not a trial.

S2: And this is where the famous number comes in. Almost fifty times.

S1: The team measured what they call a security protection factor. It compares the burglary risk of households with certain devices against households with none of the devices the survey asked about. Window locks and door double or deadlocks together had a factor of thirteen. Add outside lights on a sensor, thirty-four. And all four, window locks, indoor lights on a timer, door double or deadlocks, and external lights on a sensor, forty-nine. They call that combination WIDE. In their words, almost fifty times lower risk of burglary with entry than no security.

S2: So if I fit all four, I'm fifty times safer.

S1: That's the misreading, and it goes wrong three ways. First, it's against no security at all. In that data, under one household in twenty had none of the devices, and they had become nearly eight times as likely as the average household to be burgled. So it isn't what WIDE does for a typical home. Second, it compares households that may differ in other ways; it isn't a trial. Third, the survey counts whether devices were there, not how good they were or whether they were used. A window lock left unlocked every night is still a window lock in the survey.

S2: I notice there's no burglar alarm in that list.

S1: That's the one piece of home security that's really disputed. In the same England and Wales data, alarms weren't identified as contributing. A two thousand and fifteen paper by Tilley and colleagues went further: alarms had become associated with increased rather than decreased risk. They called it counter-intuitive and said it needs treating cautiously.

S2: Alarms attract burglars?

S1: The authors don't say that. One explanation they call plausible is timing. Some households fit an alarm after a burglary, and a household burgled once is at higher risk of being burgled again. So an alarm may mark a home that was already a target. They tested none of their explanations, and said it would be premature to conclude alarms are counter-productive. On the other side, in a US survey of four hundred and twenty-two people imprisoned for burglary, sixty per cent said they'd look for another target if there was an alarm. The course read the university's release, not the report. Both can hold, because they measured different things: what burglars say they'd do, and which houses actually got burgled. So in this course's labels, alarms are contested. Locks and lights in combination are not.

S2: I'd have said burglars come when nobody's home.

S1: In England and Wales, more often than not, someone was home. In the survey tables from the year ending March twenty fifteen to the year ending March twenty twenty-five, someone was at home in between fifty-one and sixty-eight per cent of burglaries where the burglar got in. And the door wasn't locked in between eighteen and thirty-five per cent of them.

S2: So keep the outside doors locked when you're in.

S1: That step is the course's, labelled sensible, untested, because nobody has trialled it. And if you think someone's inside, the Suzy Lamplugh Trust says call the police, and don't go and investigate by yourself. If you come home and suspect someone has been in, don't go in. Sensible, untested again. And anyone who did go and look did nothing wrong. The burglar made that moment.

S2: Then the doorstep.

S1: The course's own point is that a lock only works on someone who has to get past it, and a con at the door gets you to open it. Picture Harold, seventy-nine, living alone. A man says he's from the water company, there's a problem with the mains in the street, and he needs to check the kitchen tap straight away. And Harold can see a second man by his back gate.

S2: That worries me.

S1: It should. The Lamplugh Trust warns that a bogus caller will try to distract you while an accomplice robs you, often through a back door. So Harold keeps the door between them, and locks the back door first. Then he asks for ID, and here's the rule. He closes the door and rings the number printed on his own bill, not any number on the card.

S2: And if the man says there's no time for that?

S1: Then he's telling Harold something. The US Federal Trade Commission lists four signs of a scam: they pretend to be from an organisation you know, there's a problem or a prize, they pressure you to act now, and they tell you to pay in a specific way. At the door, on the course's reading, the payment is getting inside. Urgency is the tell, and the call-back rule is the answer, at the door as on the phone. The call-back rule is sensible, untested; the ID and accomplice steps are practitioner opinion.
