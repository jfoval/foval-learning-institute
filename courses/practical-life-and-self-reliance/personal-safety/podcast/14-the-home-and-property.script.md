S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Personal Safety, on the lesson called The home and property.

S2: Two things get said about this, and I've heard both. Your home is your castle and you can do anything to someone who breaks in. And you'll be prosecuted for defending yourself against a burglar.

S1: The lesson says neither is what the law says. But the usual line first. This course is education, not legal advice and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger right now, call nine nine nine in the UK or nine one one in the US. And every legal statement here carries the lesson's label: this is general information, not legal advice. The law differs by state, and between England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland.

S2: Where does it start?

S1: Before any law, with the phone. The householders' leaflet for England and Wales, from the prosecutors and the police chiefs, opens: wherever possible you should call the police. The course labels that advice sensible and untested. Everything after is about the moment when calling wasn't possible, or wasn't enough. And that moment is there because the law asks about it afterwards, not because anyone who ends up face to face with an intruder did something wrong. The burglar made that moment.

S2: So what's special about the home in England and Wales?

S1: Lesson thirteen gave the ordinary rule: force isn't reasonable if it was disproportionate. In twenty thirteen Parliament added a different line for one kind of case. In a householder case, force isn't reasonable if it was grossly disproportionate. The Government's reason, as the prosecutors report it, is that the threat from an intruder is most immediate when he's entering or has entered someone's home. And the High Court, hearing a challenge, found the rule compatible with the state's duty to protect the right to life.

S2: Is that the right call?

S1: The lesson says whether the home should get this extra room is a value question, and it describes Parliament's answer without taking a side. The reasons it gives are the Government's and the court's. It read no source that argues against the rule, so the case against isn't set out.

S2: Okay. So at home, anything short of grossly disproportionate is fine?

S1: That's what an appeal argued, and the Court of Appeal disagreed, in a twenty seventeen case called R v Ray. The course read the prosecutors' summary, not the judgment. On that summary, a jury asks two questions in order. First, was the force grossly disproportionate, as the householder believed things to be? If it was, self-defence fails. Second, if it wasn't, was it reasonable in all the circumstances as they believed them to be? So the rule takes grossly disproportionate force off the table, and leaves a jury free to find force reasonable in a home that it might have called disproportionate on a street.

S2: Does it cover my garden?

S1: No. The force has to be used in or partly in the building, which covers meeting someone climbing through a window, but not a confrontation wholly outside, in the garden. And it covers self-defence, defending yourself or someone else, never defending property.

S2: What does the leaflet say to an ordinary person?

S1: Do you have to wait to be attacked? No, not if you are in your own home and in fear for yourself or others. Where it tips over: knocking an intruder unconscious and then going on to kick and punch them repeatedly would be more likely to be considered grossly disproportionate. And afterwards, the police have a duty to investigate a death or injury, and the leaflet also says very few householders have ever been prosecuted. It gives no number, and the course doesn't supply one.

S2: And Scotland?

S1: No householder rule. An intruder in a Scottish home is judged by the ordinary questions: imminent danger believed on reasonable grounds, no cruel excess, and no reasonable means of escape, one the person could reasonably be expected to take.

S2: And the US has castle doctrine.

S1: Put in different ways in each text. Florida says a person in a home where they have a right to be has no duty to retreat, and adds a presumption. Against someone unlawfully and forcibly entering, or who has entered, a home or an occupied vehicle, a person using force that could kill or badly injure is presumed to have held a reasonable fear of imminent peril of death or great bodily harm, if they knew or had reason to believe it was happening or had happened. But it has four exceptions, and the first is that it doesn't apply against someone with a right to be there, like an owner or tenant, unless there's an injunction for protection from domestic violence or a written pretrial supervision order of no contact against them. If someone you live with is frightening you, the lesson on domestic abuse and stalking starts with the helplines. Texas has a presumption of the same kind.

S2: So a presumption is basically a green light.

S1: The course reads it otherwise. A presumption goes to whether the fear or belief was reasonable, and the rest of each section still applies. And whether presumptions like these go too far or not far enough is another value question, which the course describes without taking a side.

S2: Give me a night at home.

S1: Joanne lives alone. At two in the morning she hears someone climbing in through the kitchen window. She calls nine nine nine from the landing. Before the police arrive, the man comes up the stairs towards her, and she swings the heavy bedside lamp she's holding. The lesson gives no verdict, only questions. In England and Wales: is it a householder case, where it's her belief that he was a trespasser that counts, and then Ray's two questions. In Scotland: imminent danger on reasonable grounds, cruel excess, and a means of escape she could reasonably be expected to take, from the top of her own stairs at night. None of them asks whether she should have stayed in bed or had a lamp to hand.

S2: Last part. What if it's only my stuff at risk?

S1: Then the places part company. In England and Wales, defending property is never a householder case. Disproportionate force to protect property is still unlawful, the leaflet says, but you can use reasonable force to recover your property and make a citizen's arrest. Scotland's self-defence reaches only personal danger, not material loss, and the course read no other Scots rule on it. And Texas may be the rule you've heard of. It allows deadly force for property only when three things all hold: force is justified in the first place, they reasonably believe deadly force is immediately necessary to prevent listed crimes such as burglary, robbery, or theft during the nighttime, or to stop someone escaping with the property straight after a burglary, a robbery or a theft during the nighttime, and they reasonably believe the property can't be protected or recovered any other way, or that less force would bring a substantial risk of death or serious injury.
