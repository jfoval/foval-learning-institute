S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Personal Safety, on the lesson called What fear does to you, and the robbery decision.

S2: The one everybody's wondered about. What would I do if someone demanded my bag?

S1: And the lesson's honest answer is that nobody knows in advance. First the usual line. This is education, not legal advice and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger right now, call nine nine nine in the UK or nine one one in the US. And the lesson's responsibility sentence matters most here. Someone who was robbed and froze, or handed everything over, or fought, didn't cause what happened to them, and none of those was the wrong response. The lesson describes averages. It never grades one person's moment.

S2: So start with the body. What does a sudden threat do?

S1: A review by Kozlowska and colleagues describes a set of defence responses it calls innate and automatic, in an order. Arousal first. Then fight or flight. Freezing is fight or flight put on hold. And a state where the body can't move for a while, called tonic immobility, is a last resort, when there's no escape and active defences have failed.

S2: So freezing isn't giving up.

S1: That's the idea people most often get wrong. Roelofs, reviewing the animal and human research, calls freezing a brake on the motor system, not a passive state. In the animal work the heart slows while the body takes in the situation and gets ready to act. But she adds a caution. Most of that is animals, and in people how freezing works is still largely unexplored. So: freezing is automatic, not chosen, it has a job, and the human details are still being worked out.

S2: What about the senses? People talk about tunnel vision.

S1: The best-known figures come from police officers. Artwohl, a police psychologist, surveyed a hundred and fifty-seven officers who'd been in shootings. Eighty-four per cent reported hearing sounds less than normal. Seventy-nine per cent, tunnel vision. Fifty-two per cent lost memory for part of the event. And twenty-one per cent said they'd seen, heard or experienced something that didn't really happen.

S2: How much weight do those numbers take?

S1: She supplies some caveats herself. A voluntary written survey, not an experiment, and about two thirds of the officers filled it in after a group debriefing, where, the course adds, they may have taken on details they heard there. And carrying officers' figures to a person at a cash machine is the course's step, so it carries the direction, not the numbers. Expect to see less, hear less, and remember in pieces. It isn't lying, and it isn't a sign anything else is wrong with you.

S2: And skill? Can training fix it?

S1: Two small Dutch experiments with police separate a practised movement from a decision. In one, officers' shooting got less accurate when an opponent fired soap cartridges back. The group that then trained against someone shooting back held its accuracy, still four months later, though the authors say only that such training may help. In the other, officers under threat more often shot at a suspect with no gun, and after three weeks of practice those mistakes persisted. So the course draws one inference, labelled sensible, untested: make some decisions in advance, so fewer are left for the moment.

S2: Which brings us to the decision. Hand it over, or resist?

S1: And in the full lesson there's a content note before this part, with support lines. It's contested, so each side gets its own words. First the law, as general information, not legal advice, and it differs by place. No source the course read suggests the law anywhere it covers requires a robbery victim to hand property over, or to resist. The case for handing over is the official advice. London's Metropolitan Police: property can be replaced, you can't. The Washington, DC police brochure says don't resist. And American workplace safety recommendations for late-night shops list handing over money or valuables without resistance among topics staff training may include.

S2: And the other side?

S1: It rests mainly on the big American crime victim survey, reanalysed with more care about which came first. Tark and Kleck looked at twenty-seven thousand five hundred and ninety-five incidents and found self-protection in general, forceful and not, reduced property loss and injury compared with not resisting. And in crimes with both resistance and injury, injury followed resistance in only ten per cent. Guerette and Santana found greater resistance cut the odds of a robbery being completed by ninety-three per cent in some cases. But that outcome is whether the crime was completed, not whether anyone was hurt.

S2: Two honest bodies of evidence pointing opposite ways. How?

S1: Because of a flaw both share. The dead aren't in the surveys; they're interviews with people who lived. People choose when to resist, so resisters may look better off partly because of where they resisted, and someone already being hit may fight back because they're being hit. The order of events is rarely known. And nobody can randomly assign victims to comply or resist. On the course's reading, the disagreement is mostly about what each side could see.

S2: Is there anything they agree on?

S1: One narrow case. A weapon shown, and all that's wanted is property. The three official sources the course read all point towards handing it over, and the resistance studies don't single that case out. That last part is the course's reading of their abstracts. Take Sanjay, at a cash machine in Derby at about eleven at night. Two men, one either side, one holding a knife low, wanting his wallet and phone. He can't walk away. That's the narrow case. His body may go still for a second, he may see the knife sharply and little else. None of that is failure, and however he responds, the robbers are responsible.

S2: And if he'd pushed one man away and run?

S1: He didn't choose wrongly either. The course never grades one person's choice in that moment, and the evidence that could doesn't exist.

S2: What changes if they want me, not my things?

S1: Then the official pages say nothing. Denise, in a parking garage in Wilmington, Delaware. A man steps between her and the stairs, says he has a gun, tells her to get into his car. The demand is for her, so the narrow case doesn't apply. The only evidence left is the resistance research, and on average, among survivors, it points towards resisting, resisting without force among it. It can't say what would work here. She may freeze. None of that is a choice, and whatever happens is his responsibility. The lesson doesn't say what happened next; any ending would seem to grade her.
