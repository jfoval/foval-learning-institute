S1: It's education. It isn't legal advice, and it isn't a self-defence class. If you're in danger now, call nine nine nine in the UK or nine one one in the US. If a partner, ex-partner or family member is frightening you, the lesson on domestic abuse and stalking starts with the helplines. And responsibility for a crime lies with the person who commits it. Precautions change the odds, not the blame.

S2: So where does it start?

S1: With a walk you no longer take after dark, or a car park you go the long way round. In October twenty twenty five, thirty one percent of Americans told Gallup there was an area within a mile of home where they'd be afraid to walk alone at night. The lesson doesn't say that fear is wrong. It asks what the fear is tracking.

S2: Where do you begin with that?

S1: In a study published in nineteen eighty three, two criminologists, Warr and Stafford, asked residents of Seattle how much they feared each of a list of crimes. Guess what came top.

S2: Murder, surely.

S1: Residential burglary came top. Murder was low on the list. And Warr's explanation is the most useful idea in the lesson. Fear isn't set by how serious a crime is. In his words, it's a multiplicative function of the perceived seriousness and the perceived risk. To be strongly feared, a crime has to seem both serious and likely.

S2: Why does multiplying matter so much?

S1: Add two factors and a big one can carry a small one. Multiply them, and a factor near zero drags the whole thing towards zero. Murder is seen as very serious and very unlikely. Burglary is seen as fairly serious and fairly likely. So the middling crime wins.

S2: And are people any good at judging the likely part?

S1: Not at the sizes. Warr reports that people overestimate how often rare crimes happen and underestimate the common ones, though they get the order right. And the news is chosen for seriousness, so in his words, the media are most likely to report precisely those crimes that are least likely to occur. Learn about crime from the news and you're reading the list upside down.

S2: So fear of crime is irrational.

S1: Warr says that's the wrong question. He calls rationality an ill-advised notion here. You can check likelihood against data, because risk has a rate. But seriousness depends on what you value. His examples include a lost wedding album and a recording of a dead parent. Nobody can check that against a table. So the course's stance is this. When it tells you how common a crime is, it's informing the likelihood half of your fear. The seriousness half is yours.

S2: Is there anywhere the fear and the risk agree?

S1: Fraud. Of the crimes Gallup asked about in that October poll, the only two a majority of Americans worry about frequently or occasionally are identity theft, at sixty nine percent, and being tricked by scammers, at fifty three. And fraud is the commonest crime the England and Wales survey measures, four and a half million incidents in the year to March twenty twenty six.

S2: If fear is information, is less of it always better?

S1: Warr asks you to imagine a dial. Turn it down and people drop precautions that were doing them good. Turn it up and they shrink their own lives, and the commonest reaction he names in the United States is spatial avoidance, staying away from places that seem dangerous.

S2: How do you tell which end you're at?

S1: Two findings help. In a British survey, about a third of people said they were very or fairly worried about being robbed. Then new questions in part of that survey asked how often, in the past year, they had actually worried about it. Eighty five percent hadn't worried once, and Gray, Jackson and Farrall concluded that the usual question magnifies everyday fear. The course draws a practical step from that, and labels it sensible and untested. Don't ask yourself how worried you are. Ask how many times you worried last month, and what you were doing.

S2: And the second?

S1: Jackson and Gray separate a functional worry, which prompts routine precaution, from a dysfunctional one, which erodes your quality of life. Turning that into a test is the course's step, not theirs. Does the worry lead to a precaution? Does the precaution make you feel safer? And does your life stay about the size it was? Three yeses is functional. A no on the third is the kind that costs.

S2: Give me a person.

S1: Derek is seventy. For years he walked ten minutes to a quiz night every Thursday. Three weeks ago the paper reported a mugging near the club's car park, and he hasn't been back. His seriousness isn't just a lost wallet. It's a broken hip and not coping alone afterwards, and on Warr's argument nobody can tell him that valuation is wrong. The likelihood is a different matter. One report is thin evidence, so the question is what he'd have guessed about that walk a month ago, from years of Thursdays.

S2: And what has staying in cost him?

S1: His main evening out, and the people there. It fails the third question. And Jackson and Stafford suggested a feedback model where worry harms health and poorer health feeds the worry. A functional version might be a lift home or a walking companion, sensible and untested. But the choice is Derek's. The course separates what he values from what he's guessing. It doesn't tell him to go back.

S2: What about the classic line, you're more likely to die falling down the stairs?

S1: The US figures for twenty twenty four point that way. Unintentional falls killed fourteen point two people per hundred thousand, and homicide five point nine, so the fall death rate was about two point four times the homicide rate. But Warr passes on advice from risk researchers to avoid comparisons that look noncomparable, and on the course's reading violence is. Another person chooses it, and it carries dread and a sense of being wronged. A comparison can inform. It can't dismiss.

S2: Last thing. How will the course tell me what to trust?

S1: Every piece of advice carries one of four labels. Tested, where the outcome that matters was measured. Sensible, untested. Contested, where the evidence points more than one way. A trial on police officers is tested for police. For you, it's an inference.
