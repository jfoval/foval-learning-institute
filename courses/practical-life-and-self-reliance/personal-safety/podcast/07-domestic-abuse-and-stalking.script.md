S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Personal Safety, on the lesson called Domestic abuse and stalking.

S2: First, a word about what's in this one. This episode deals with abuse by a partner, an ex-partner or a family member, and with stalking, and it touches on killings of women by partners. The full lesson opens with the helplines, for each part of the UK and for the US.

S1: And the course's usual line. This is education, not legal advice and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger right now, call nine nine nine in the UK or nine one one in the US. And from the top of the lesson: if someone might be watching your phone or computer, you can close the page and come back later on a device they can't get at.

S2: So where does it start?

S1: With two sentences from the services. Refuge, which runs the National Domestic Abuse Helpline in England, says: we will never tell you what to do. And the US National Domestic Violence Hotline says you are the expert in your situation. So the lesson describes. It doesn't direct. It doesn't tell anyone to stay or to go.

S2: What do the services mean by abuse? A lot of people picture being hit.

S1: Refuge calls the idea that it only counts if it's physical or sexual violence a common myth: many women experience domestic abuse without ever being physically harmed. Its definition is an incident or a pattern of behaviour used by someone to control or obtain power over their partner or ex-partner. The US Hotline's is close. Two words carry both. Pattern, so the harm is in the repetition, and one incident can look small when the whole isn't. And control, so the question isn't whether any one act was violent, but what the acts together are for.

S2: How would someone inside it know?

S1: Refuge's page on coercive control offers questions, because it can be hard to see from inside. Does your partner isolate you from friends and family, monitor how you spend your time, keep control of your money? And its helpline site has a shorter test: anyone forced to change their behaviour because they are frightened of their partner or ex-partner's reaction is experiencing abuse.

S2: How common is this?

S1: In England and Wales, the crime survey estimated that seven point eight per cent of adults aged sixteen and over experienced domestic abuse in the year ending March twenty twenty-five. Nine point one per cent of women and six point five per cent of men. Those figures come from a part of the survey people fill in privately, because the Office for National Statistics says victims may not disclose it to an interviewer.

S2: And stalking?

S1: The Suzy Lamplugh Trust defines it as a pattern of repeated, unwanted behaviour that causes you to feel distressed or scared. It can happen with or without a fear of violence, and it needn't be dramatic: regularly sending flowers or gifts can be part of it. About forty-five per cent of the people who contact the National Stalking Helpline, which the Trust runs, are being stalked by ex-partners. That's the helpline's callers, not everyone who's stalked, so it can't be turned into a national share.

S2: Make it concrete.

S1: Kerry, in England. Her partner checks her phone most evenings, has started turning up at her work unannounced, and tells her which friends she can see. He's never hit her, and she isn't sure it counts. Each of those is close to one of the services' examples. But none of their pages gives a score or says this counts. Refuge's shorter test is a question only Kerry can answer, and the course won't answer it for her.

S2: And if she did ring a helpline?

S1: Refuge's page says an adviser will listen, offer confidential information without judging her, help her make a plan if she'd like one, and never tell her what to do. She doesn't have to be sure it counts first. Since he checks her phone, the course reads Refuge's advice as covering a call or a chat too: a phone or computer he can't get at, at a safe time. Every one of those is an option, not a step she ought to take. What she does next, including nothing for now, is hers.

S2: The lesson then turns to a study of killings.

S1: Campbell and colleagues, two thousand and three. In eleven US cities, they compared two hundred and twenty women killed by a partner with three hundred and forty-three abused women who were not killed. So it asks what separates the most dangerous abusive relationships from other abusive ones. In the model where it first appeared, the combination of a highly controlling abuser and separation after living together went with about nine times the odds of being killed.

S2: Nine times sounds very precise.

S1: It isn't. It's an odds ratio, not a chance of being killed, and the study gives no absolute risk. The interval runs from about three to about twenty-five. Once the abuser's earlier threats were counted, the estimate fell to about four. The women who were killed couldn't be asked, so people who knew them answered. It's an urban sample, from the US, published more than twenty years ago. And it's an association. It doesn't say why. The finding is strong that the combination goes with higher risk, and weak on how much higher.

S2: Which is part of why the lesson won't tell anyone to walk out.

S1: One of three reasons. The first is Campbell's finding. The second is how the services frame leaving: Refuge calls it a process, and tells friends not to tell a woman to leave or criticise her for staying, because she has to make that decision in her own time. The third is the one we started with. Telling someone to leave is someone else deciding. None of that is an argument for staying either. It's an argument for planning, with help, on the person's own timing.

S2: What's a safety plan?

S1: The Hotline calls it a personalised, practical plan to improve your safety while experiencing abuse, preparing to leave an abusive situation, or after you leave. Both it and Refuge will make one with you, and the course's own point is that a plan made on the phone with an adviser doesn't have to exist on paper, or on a device, where it could be found. In this course's labels, safety planning is practitioner opinion.

S2: And if the person I'm worried about is my sister?

S1: The same rule holds. Refuge warns that intervening directly can be dangerous for you and her. Speak in private, believe her, tell her it's not her fault, and don't ask why she hasn't left.

It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.
