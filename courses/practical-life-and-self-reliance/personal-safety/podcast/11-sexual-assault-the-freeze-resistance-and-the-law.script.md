S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Personal Safety, on the lesson called Sexual assault: the freeze, resistance and the law.

S2: And before anything else, a note. This whole episode is about rape and sexual assault: what happens to the body, the law, and the research on resisting. The full lesson opens with support lines for each part of the UK and for the US. You can stop listening at any point.

S1: And the usual line. This is education, not legal advice and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger right now, call nine nine nine in the UK or nine one one in the US. Then the lesson's first sentence. The only cause of a rape or a sexual assault is the person who commits it. Not where someone was, not who they trusted, not what they said or didn't say, and not what their body did in the moment.

S2: What their body did. That's the freeze.

S1: Many people's bodies go still during an assault. They find they can't move, can't speak, can't fight. That stillness is automatic, not chosen, and it isn't agreement. A survivor who didn't fight, didn't scream or wasn't injured did nothing wrong. Those sentences aren't comfort in place of evidence. Each rests on a source.

S2: Then who were the sources about?

S1: Almost entirely women. Men are raped and sexually assaulted too. The Office for National Statistics estimated, for the year ending March twenty twenty-five, that in England and Wales eight point two per cent of women and nought point seven per cent of men aged sixteen and over had experienced rape or assault by penetration, including attempts, since turning sixteen, a figure from half the usual sample that it says to treat with caution. Where a finding comes from women, the lesson doesn't stretch it to men.

S2: So, the freeze. How common is it?

S1: The only study the course found that counted it was done after rape. Researchers at the Karolinska Institute in Stockholm assessed two hundred and ninety-eight women who came to an emergency clinic within a month of being raped. Seventy per cent reported significant tonic immobility during the assault, and forty-eight per cent reported the extreme form. The study defines it as a state of involuntary, temporary motor inhibition. The body stops moving, for a while, and not by decision.

S2: That's most of them.

S1: With three limits. It's one sample of women at one clinic in one city, so it can't speak for men or anywhere else. It's what the women reported afterwards on a scale, not something anyone watched. And it found tonic immobility associated with post-traumatic stress and severe depression six months later, but that's an association across a group, not a forecast for any one person.

S2: So how does the law see a body that didn't move?

S1: This is general information, not legal advice, and the law differs by state, and between England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. In England and Wales, section seventy-four of the Sexual Offences Act two thousand and three says a person consents if they agree by choice, and have the freedom and capacity to make that choice. Put that beside the Stockholm definition, which is the course's own step, and it settles itself. A state your body goes into without your choice can't be an agreement by choice.

S2: And the prosecutors?

S1: The Crown Prosecution Service has an annex for its own lawyers on rape myths. Two entries: that a real victim would never freeze but would fight back, and that if the victim didn't scream, fight or get injured, it couldn't have been rape. Both are marked false. It says the brain under threat uses survival responses the victim won't necessarily control, not just fight or flight, but flop, freeze or befriend. And one short sentence carries a great deal: consent and submission are different. On the CPS's summary of a case, nobody has to have shown or said refusal. The course read only the law for England and Wales, so don't assume those rules apply in Scotland, Northern Ireland or any US state.

S2: Now the resistance research. I'm nervous about this part.

S1: So is the lesson. Everything here is about what tends to happen, on average, across many incidents. None of it is a standard anyone was supposed to meet. If you froze, or went along with it to stay alive, nothing in these studies says what was possible for you. And if you fought back and were raped anyway, or hurt, nothing here says you resisted wrongly or not enough.

S2: So what did they find?

S1: Tark and Kleck used the US National Crime Victimization Survey, for nineteen ninety-two to two thousand and two, and took account of whether any injury came before or after the victim resisted. Their abstract says further injuries after resistance, especially serious ones, were rare. And that most kinds of resistance, forceful and not, reduced the risk of the rape being completed, and made no significant difference to further injury.

S2: No significant difference isn't the same as safe.

S1: Exactly. It means the study didn't find a difference big enough to rule out chance. And reduced risk is a change in the odds across many incidents, not a promise about one. Nobody can be assigned at random to resist. And the course adds its own inference: the freeze might sit behind the numbers. If the attacks where bodies shut down differ from those where people could resist, the two groups would differ in the attacks they faced, not only in what they did, and that could bear on whether the rape was completed. No study read here measures that, and a freeze isn't a measure of how bad an attack was either. It's the body's response to the threat as the person met it.

S2: The lesson ends on a friend.

S1: Hana, who lives in England, tells you over coffee that a man she'd been seeing for a few weeks assaulted her at his flat. She froze, didn't push him away, didn't say anything. So maybe it wasn't really assault. Rape Crisis England and Wales says the most important thing is to listen and believe, then let the person say what they need, and not to ask why they didn't run or fight. That's practitioner opinion, from long experience. You might say: I believe you, and I'm glad you told me. Freezing is something bodies do on their own when they're terrified, and it isn't agreeing to anything. Whatever you want to do next, or not do, I'll back you.

S2: And whether she goes to the police?

S1: Hers to decide, in her own time. Whether a court would call it rape is a court's question, not yours. What you can say is that freezing doesn't make it her fault, and doesn't mean she agreed.
