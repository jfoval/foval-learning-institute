S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Personal Safety, on the lesson called Carrying something for protection.

S2: Before anything else, the usual line.

S1: This course is education, not legal advice and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger right now, call nine nine nine in the UK or nine one one in the US. Every legal point here is general information, not legal advice. The law differs by state, and between England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. The lesson gives no advice on choosing, buying, carrying or using anything, guns included. And the second half talks about research on killings, including of women by partners, and on deaths linked to guns in the home. The full lesson has support lines before that part.

S2: Someone's been frightened, and thinks about carrying something next time. A heavy torch, say.

S1: In England and Wales, "for self-defence" is the problem. The main law is the Prevention of Crime Act nineteen fifty three. It's an offence to have an offensive weapon with you in a public place without lawful authority or reasonable excuse, and it's for the person charged to show the excuse. An offensive weapon is anything made to injure, adapted to injure, or neither of those but carried with the intention of injuring someone.

S2: So the torch becomes the problem if I carry it to hit someone with, even if only to hit back when I'm attacked.

S1: That's the third route. And the prosecutors' guidance adds, in brackets, that the intention can be "either offensively or defensively".

S2: But what if I'm genuinely afraid? Someone's been following me.

S1: The guidance says carrying a weapon merely because of the possibility of a constant or enduring threat, "even where genuine", isn't self-defence or a reasonable excuse. Exceptionally, it may be reasonable in anticipation of an attack that's imminent and specific, closely connected in time and purpose. So the rule doesn't doubt the fear is real. It says a real, continuing fear isn't enough.

S2: Pepper spray, though. That's surely different.

S1: In England, Wales and Scotland, the Firearms Act nineteen sixty eight prohibits any weapon designed or adapted to discharge a noxious liquid, gas or other thing. So pepper spray is a prohibited weapon. Possession is the offence, even one kept in a drawer at home. And purpose makes no difference: there's no self-defence version of a prohibited weapon.

S2: Then guns.

S1: Every question in this part is contested, in the course's labels: the evidence points more than one way, and serious researchers disagree. The fairest single statement the course found is from a twenty thirteen report from the US National Academies: "Defensive use of guns by crime victims is a common occurrence, although the exact number remains disputed".

S2: Disputed by how much?

S1: More than twenty times. As the report gives it, the government's victim survey puts it at sixty thousand to a hundred and twenty thousand defensive uses a year. A nineteen ninety five paper by Kleck and Gertz, from their own national telephone survey, puts it at about two point two to two point five million.

S2: How can both be real surveys?

S1: Each side has an answer, in its own words. Hemenway, replying to the Kleck and Gertz paper, argues that surveys "can wildly overestimate rare events which have some social desirability". Saying you used a gun to ward off a criminal makes you look good, and when the true event is rare, a few wrong yeses swamp the true ones. So the high estimates overcount. Kleck and Gertz, in their answer to him, argue that the government survey "never directly asks" about defensive gun use. It only gives people a chance to volunteer it in answer to a general question about protecting themselves, so it undercounts.

S2: What would settle it?

S1: On the course's reading of both objections, a survey that asks directly and is built to measure false positives on a rare event. Nobody has yet run one to both sides' satisfaction. Until then, rare and common are each a position in a dispute, not a finding.

S2: And a gun kept at home? Does it make a household safer?

S1: The largest pooled study the course read, by Anglemyer and colleagues in twenty fourteen, combined sixteen studies and found access to a gun in the home went with a pooled odds ratio of two for being killed, and a higher one for suicide. Anyone who's struggling right now can find crisis lines in the full lesson. The authors word it as an association, and since every study pooled was observational, it shows the two went together, not that one caused the other. The course read the abstract only.

S2: And the other side of that one?

S1: Critics have published on how these studies are done. The course didn't read that work, and says the gap is its own, not theirs. What it can give: the National Academies report says studies found consistently lower injury rates among crime victims who used a gun than among those who protected themselves other ways, though effectiveness likely varies and those findings need confirming or discounting. And in Kleck and Gertz's survey most defensive uses involved no shot: only twenty four percent claimed to have fired their gun.

S2: And a woman who's being abused?

S1: Lesson seven's study, by Campbell, of women killed by partners. The abuser's access to a gun stood up when other factors were taken into account, an odds ratio of five point four four in the paper's fifth model. In a later model, once his use of a gun in the worst incident was added, access alone was no longer significant; the authors say the use apparently carried the effect of the access. That's the clearest finding in this section, and it points one way. On a victim's own gun, the study is much thinner, and it says so. Among women living apart from the abuser, sole access to a gun went with a lower risk in the model where it first appeared, and stopped being significant once relationship factors were added. The authors say only five percent of women were in that group, and "there was no clear evidence of protective effects". And no clear evidence isn't evidence of no effect, either way.

S2: So what do people who work with survivors say?

S1: They disagree, and the lesson gives each as a position. The US National Domestic Violence Hotline sees dangers in a gun in a home where there's abuse, above all one an abusive partner can reach, and says: "we do not advocate for survivors of domestic violence to purchase a gun." It also tells the reader, "you are the expert in your situation". Allyson Hottinger, a survivor and advocate profiled in a National Rifle Association journal, urges fellow survivors to get a concealed carry permit if comfortable. In her words, "if it came down to him or me, I was no longer going to be a victim of his abuse." And: "It might not be an option for everyone, but for the majority of survivors, it is."

S2: And the course's view?

S1: Whether a particular person should keep a gun is a value question with an empirical core. The course describes it and doesn't answer it.
