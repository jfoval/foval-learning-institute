S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Personal Safety, on the lesson called Talking it down.

S2: Mostly about arguments?

S1: Mostly. A refusal at a counter, a queue that's gone on too long. Most end with nothing worse than a raised voice. The lesson is about the few minutes when someone's angry with you and you can't walk away yet. First, the usual line. This is education, not legal advice and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger right now, call nine nine nine in the UK or nine one one in the US. And none of this is advice for dealing with a partner, ex-partner or family member who frightens you. The lesson on domestic abuse and stalking is for that, and it starts with the helplines.

S2: Why not just walk away?

S1: Leaving comes before talking. The course's order of options, its own arrangement rather than a tested sequence, is avoid, leave, talk, comply or resist, get away. Talking is third, and leaving stays open at every rung, even mid-conversation. So on the course's reading, talking is for two moments. When you can't leave yet: you're behind a counter, or the person stands between you and the door. Or when someone depends on you staying. And the aim isn't to win. It's to get everyone out of the moment without anyone getting hurt.

S2: Where does the advice come from?

S1: Two documents, neither written for you. NICE, the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, published guidance in twenty fifteen for health and social care staff. One staff member should take the lead in talking. Control the visible signs of your own anxiety or frustration, like posture and eye contact. Communicate respect and empathy throughout.

S2: And the other?

S1: Project BETA, a two thousand and twelve consensus statement from a workgroup of the American Association for Emergency Psychiatry. It says studies on verbal de-escalation are few, so its guidelines come from the authors' consensus and a review of the limited literature. In this course's labels that's practitioner opinion, from unusually well-qualified practitioners. Keep at least two arm's lengths away. Both people should be able to leave without feeling blocked. Hands visible and not clenched. Stand at an angle. Don't stare. Short sentences. State limits in a matter-of-fact way, not as a threat. And don't do anything that could be taken as humiliating.

S2: And if they're just wrong?

S1: You don't have to lie. Project BETA gives three honest ways to agree: with the truth, in principle, or with the odds, meaning most people would feel the same. And if there's no honest way to agree, agree to disagree.

S2: Walk me through one.

S1: Leah, on a pharmacy counter in Preston. A man wants a medicine that, under the pharmacy's rules, she may not sell without the pharmacist agreeing, and the pharmacist is busy. He's been waiting twenty minutes, raises his voice, jabs a finger at the counter. Her colleague Kwame comes over, then steps back and lets Leah talk: one voice. He watches, and fetches help if needed. Leah stays back from the counter, turns slightly, hands open, looks at him without staring. Then short sentences, agreeing with what's true. You've been waiting a long time. I'm sorry about that.

S2: And the shouting?

S1: A limit, said matter-of-factly. I want to help you. I can't do that while you're shouting at me. Then a real choice. I can ask the pharmacist to see you as soon as she's free, or tell you which pharmacy nearby is open late. Which would be better? Both let him leave with something. And if he leans over the counter or comes round the end of it, the conversation has changed. She steps back, and Kwame calls for help. Every step borrows from guidance for health and care staff, so for Leah it's the course's inference, not a tested result.

S2: So does any of it work?

S1: Police in Louisville, Kentucky, were trained in a de-escalation course, ICAT, rolled out to patrol divisions in an order chosen at random. Published in twenty twenty-two: use of force incidents fell twenty-eight point one per cent, citizen injuries twenty-six point three per cent, and officer injuries thirty-six per cent. A replication in Indianapolis, with a shorter course, found force and injuries to the public fell again, but officer injuries showed no significant change.

S2: And outside policing?

S1: Much thinner. A twenty eighteen Cochrane review of de-escalation for aggression in adults found one eligible study, and the reviewers say they cannot say whether de-escalation techniques are effective. A review of nine studies of training for hospital nurses found the nurses came out more confident and more knowledgeable, but no significant change in how often patients were aggressive. Feeling more confident isn't the same as being attacked less.

S2: So how does the lesson sort it?

S1: Established: health and workplace guidance recommends de-escalation, and ICAT reduced police use of force in two US cities. Expert consensus, without trial evidence: the specific techniques. And not known: whether training members of the public reduces violence against them. That you'll handle an angry stranger better after this is the course's inference. Reasonable, and untested.

S2: How do you know when to stop talking?

S1: The course gives four signals, its own synthesis, labelled sensible, untested. The distance closes. Others join. A weapon appears. Or the demand changes, from the parking space or the queue to your property, or to you. Any one of them is the signal to stop talking and leave, or, if you can't leave, to move to the next rung.

S2: And one where it turns?

S1: Farid, in a supermarket car park in Omaha, lifting his three-year-old out of her car seat. A man says loudly he'd been waiting for that space. Farid can't leave his daughter half out of the car. So he stays at least two arm's lengths away, hands visible, angled, and agrees with the odds: I didn't see you. Anyone would be annoyed after waiting for a space. He offers a way out: I'll move once she's back in. It works for about a minute. Then the man's passenger gets out and walks round behind Farid.

S2: Two signals at once.

S1: Someone's joined, and he's placed himself where Farid can't see him. Time to stop talking and leave. One calm line, I'm going to go, then into the car, lock it and drive away, or carry her into the supermarket, where there are staff and other people. Sensible, untested, in the course's labels. Which is safer depends on where the two men are standing, and only Farid can see that. If either shows a weapon or follows him, that's a nine one one call. And leaving isn't losing. If anything had followed, the responsibility would lie with the two men who chose to crowd him.
