S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in the course called Personal Safety, lesson four. Opportunity and attention.

S2: The reminder first.

S1: This course is education. It isn't legal advice, and it isn't a self-defence class. If you're in danger now, call nine nine nine in the UK or nine one one in the US. And a frame for lowering your own odds is never a verdict on anyone who was harmed. Responsibility lies with the person who commits the crime.

S2: So what's the frame?

S1: You can't change who's out tonight. You can change some of the circumstances you walk into. In nineteen seventy nine two sociologists, Lawrence Cohen and Marcus Felson, set out to explain why US crime rose so much between nineteen forty seven and nineteen seventy four. They didn't look for more criminals. They looked at how ordinary people lived. Their idea, in one sentence: most criminal acts require convergence in space and time of likely offenders, suitable targets and the absence of capable guardians against crime.

S2: Three things have to meet.

S1: At one place and one time. And they made a deliberate turn. Rather than the characteristics of offenders, they concentrated on the circumstances. The theory doesn't need to know who is a criminal or what they look like, only what the situation is. That gives you three levers. Be a less suitable target: less on show, less within reach. Add guardianship: someone with you, or someone who knows your plan and will act if it breaks. And change the convergence: a different route, time or routine.

S2: Is there a practice built on that?

S1: Situational crime prevention. R. V. Clarke, one of its founders, defined it, in part, as opportunity-reducing measures directed at highly specific forms of crime that make crime more difficult and risky, or less rewarding and excusable, as judged by a wide range of offenders. Cornish and Clarke later laid out a grid of twenty five techniques. Under extend guardianship it lists, take routine precautions: go out in group at night, leave signs of occupancy, carry phone.

S2: Any catch?

S1: Two cautions. Using a grid written for planners on your own walk home is the course's step, not Clarke's. It tells you where to look, not what works for you. And not all of it is settled. Burglar alarms are in the grid, and lesson five sets out the dispute over whether an alarm protects a home at all.

S2: Give me someone to try it on.

S1: Martin is a hospital porter. Most nights his shift ends at eleven, and he walks ten minutes to a bus stop. The short way cuts across the staff car park and down a service lane; the longer way follows the lit road past the front entrance and adds about three minutes. He usually takes the short cut, on the phone to his partner, the only time in the day they get to talk. Nothing here says Martin is at fault for anything.

S2: Lever one, the target.

S1: A phone in his hand, worth taking, and his attention on the call. The change is to make the call from the bus stop and walk with the phone in his pocket. Lever two, guardianship: walk with a colleague, ask whether security will walk staff out, or message from the bus. Lever three, the route. The lit road has more people and staff, more potential guardians, and three minutes is the price. All of those are labelled sensible, untested. And it passes lesson one's test, because none of it shrinks his life.

S2: You said his attention was on the call. Is there evidence for that?

S1: The clearest the course found has a clown in it. In a study published in twenty ten, Hyman and colleagues had a clown ride a unicycle round a sculpture on a campus square, and asked walkers whether they'd seen it. Pairs noticed most, seventy one percent. People with music players, sixty one. People walking alone, fifty one. And people on a phone, twenty five percent.

S2: So music didn't hurt.

S1: Read that as no less, not more. The groups were small and the paper runs no test on that difference. Now the limits. One campus, one spring afternoon, a hundred and fifty one walkers, only twenty four on a phone. Nobody was assigned to a group, and the authors say something about people who choose to phone while walking may be the cause, not the call. And it measured noticing a clown, not whether anyone was followed or robbed. So putting the phone away somewhere late or unfamiliar is sensible, untested.

S2: What about walk confidently and you won't be picked?

S1: This part touches on sexual violence and on killings of women, and the full lesson has the helplines. In one study, people saw walkers as nothing but points of light at the joints, and could still sort them by how easy they'd be to attack. But the authors were careful. Their raters were told to judge ease of attack, and nobody attacked anyone. To call the raters accurate you'd have to show the people rated easiest really were easiest to attack, and the study didn't do that. No study the course read measured whether changing your walk lowers your risk, or whether real attackers choose that way. On prevention, the honest answer is, not shown.

S2: And if someone says a person was attacked because of how she walked?

S1: The evidence can't carry that. The studies measured how raters judged walkers. And lesson three showed that in both England and Wales and the US most women who are killed are killed by someone they know, and in England and Wales, of victims of rape or assault by penetration since age sixteen, fewer than one in five were attacked by a stranger. How someone walks has no obvious bearing on harm from a partner at home. That's the course's reasoning, not a finding of the walking studies. Under all of it: responsibility lies with the person who attacked.

S2: So what about trust your gut?

S1: The Suzy Lamplugh Trust tells people who think they're being followed to trust their instincts and act. The course labels that practitioner opinion. Then it makes a rule of its own, labelled sensible, untested. A warning sign is something a person does, never how they look. Someone who keeps coming closer after you've stepped back. Someone who treats no as the start of a negotiation. Someone who places themselves between you and the only way out, or tries to move you somewhere less visible.

S2: And never how they look.

S1: Never race, religion, class, clothes, accent, or looking out of place. A feeling set off by how someone looks can carry prejudice as easily as pattern. A feeling set off by what someone is doing is about the situation you're in. And none of this is a test that anyone harmed failed. Plenty of harm comes with no warning a person could read.
