S2: The walk home, the late train, the first night somewhere new.

S1: That's its ground. First, the usual reminder. This is education, not legal advice and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger right now, call nine nine nine in the UK or nine one one in the US. And this lesson leans on one sentence harder than most: responsibility for a crime lies with the person who commits it. A route changes the odds of meeting someone who has decided to hurt a person. It has nothing to do with who made that decision.

S2: So what's the advice, as it's usually given?

S1: The lesson starts with what the bodies actually say. The Suzy Lamplugh Trust, the UK personal safety charity: before you go out, consider how you are going to get home, and try to use well-lit, busy streets and the route you know best. The UK Foreign Office: leave an itinerary with someone at home and agree how regularly you'll be in contact. Read together, it's mostly three things. Plan before you go. Have someone who knows the plan. Stay where there are people and light.

S2: And how much of that has been tested?

S1: Almost none. And the Lamplugh Trust is honest about it. Its advice pages carry a disclaimer saying, in effect, that following the advice won't eliminate aggression and violence; it helps you assess and lower the risk. The lesson calls it the most honest sentence in any of the advice it read.

S2: Lighting must have something behind it, though.

S1: It has the nearest thing. A review by Welsh and Farrington, published by the Campbell Collaboration in two thousand and eight, pooled thirteen studies from the UK and the US. Crime fell by twenty-one per cent in areas where street lighting was improved, compared with similar areas where it wasn't. Thirty-eight per cent in the UK studies, seven in the US ones, though the American studies were older and several counted only night-time crime.

S2: So attackers avoid light because they can be seen.

S1: Here's the surprise. If that were the whole story, daytime crime shouldn't change, because nothing about daytime visibility changed. But night-time crime didn't fall more than daytime crime. So the summary suggests community pride may play a crucial role: better lighting signals that someone has invested in a place. That's a suggestion from the pattern, not a tested mechanism, and the course read the summary, not the full review. And it measured crime across an area, not the risk to you walking one street on one night. So choosing the lit road is sensible, untested.

S2: What about cameras?

S1: A review by Piza and colleagues in twenty nineteen found CCTV is associated with a significant and modest decrease in crime, with the largest effects in car parks. But for your own safety a camera is at best a record. It isn't someone who'll step in, and it isn't a capable guardian unless a person is watching and able to act.

S2: So if nothing's tested, why pass any of it on?

S1: Because most of it rests on one mechanism with solid theory behind it. The capable guardian, from lesson four. A guardian comes in two kinds. Someone who's with you, and someone who isn't there but knows your plan and will act if it breaks. And that gives the rule this lesson builds everything on. A plan is only as useful as what happens when it breaks.

S2: Give me the difference.

S1: Two friends each let someone know before a night out. One texts her brother: out tonight, don't wait up. The other texts her flatmate: leaving the bar at eleven, home by half past eleven, I'll message when I'm in, and if you haven't heard by midnight, ring me, and if I don't answer, ring the bar.

S2: The second one has a moment when silence means something.

S1: A time, a place, and a thing to do. Or take Owen, twenty, walking twenty minutes home from a party at about one in the morning. He isn't worried, but the figures aren't on his side. Lesson three showed that in England and Wales, in the year to March twenty twenty-four, violence from strangers fell on one per cent of men against nought point four per cent of women, and on two point two per cent of people aged sixteen to twenty-four. So he decides how he's getting home before he goes out. If his flatmate hasn't heard from him by two, she rings him, and if he doesn't answer, she rings the friend whose party it is. And he agrees a home message, and a message if plans change, so a missed one really means something.

S2: And if you think someone's following you?

S1: The Lamplugh Trust has a short sequence. Cross the road, turning to see who's behind you. If you're still being followed, keep moving. Head to a busy area and tell people what's happening. Call the police if you need to. The course labels that practitioner opinion; nobody has tested it. And if you need passers-by, the Trust says you're more likely to get help if you shout a specific instruction, such as call the police.

S2: And travel?

S1: The same idea far from home. The Foreign Office recommends booking your first night's accommodation before you go, because you're at your most vulnerable when you first arrive, likely tired and unsure of your surroundings. And it has a line worth keeping: being safe is more important than being polite.
