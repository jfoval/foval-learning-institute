S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Personal Safety, on the lesson called The law on using force.

S2: Which is a big one, so the usual line first.

S1: This course is education, not legal advice and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger right now, call nine nine nine in the UK or nine one one in the US. And for everything legal in this episode, the lesson's own label: this is general information, not legal advice. The law differs by state, and between England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and it changes.

S2: So what's the lesson for?

S1: Most people who ever use force on someone do it in a second or two, frightened, with no time to think about law. The law does its thinking afterwards, and it asks the same few questions almost everywhere. The lesson sets them out for England and Wales, Scotland, and three US states, New York, Florida and Texas. It tells you the questions. It doesn't tell you what you may do in your own situation. Nothing on a page can.

S2: What are the questions?

S1: Four ideas. Necessity: was force needed at all? Proportion: did it match the threat? Imminence: was the threat happening now, not tomorrow and not already over? And belief: the law judges much of it as the situation looked to the defender, within limits that differ from place to place.

S2: What limits?

S1: Say someone you're arguing with reaches fast into his jacket, and you're sure it's a knife. It was his keys. In England and Wales, under section seventy six of a two thousand and eight act, an honest belief counts even if the mistake wasn't a reasonable one to make. Unreasonableness still matters, but as evidence of whether you really held the belief. With one exception: a mistake caused by intoxication you brought on yourself, drink for instance, doesn't count. In Scotland the belief has to rest on reasonable grounds. And New York, Florida and Texas all say "reasonably believes".

S2: Now the one everyone's heard of. Do I have to run?

S1: Here they really part company. The neutral names come from the research group RAND. Traditionally there was a duty to retreat where safe retreat was available. Castle doctrine removes it in your own home. Stand your ground removes it in some cases outside the home. That's the supporters' own term; opponents call them shoot first laws. England and Wales: the chance to retreat is a factor, not a duty. Scotland comes closest to requiring it: violence as a last resort, and if there was a safe means of escape, one the person could reasonably be expected to take, that's the course he should have taken. New York: a duty only before deadly force, against deadly force, where you know you can retreat with complete personal safety, and none in your own home if you didn't start it. Florida and Texas: no duty, in the cases the statutes set out.

S2: England and Wales has no duty to retreat? I'd have bet it did.

S1: Lots of people would. And the lesson is careful here. Escape keeps coming up because the law asks about it, afterwards. That isn't a judgement on anyone who didn't take a way out, or didn't see one.

S2: But in practice?

S1: Even a page written for people who carry guns says it. Jo Deering, writing for NRA Women against duty to retreat laws, still says: if you are able to leave a situation without having to use force, I highly recommend you do so. And the course adds its own inference, labelled sensible and untested. Every question in this lesson is asked about force you used. If you got away, none of them is asked about you.

S2: When does force stop counting as self-defence?

S1: One way is time. The lesson's case is a man called Dale in a takeaway queue. Another man shoves him, steps between him and the door and lifts a bottle, and Dale pushes him away. The man drops the bottle and runs out. A minute later Dale chases him onto the pavement and punches him. The lesson gives no verdict on either moment. It says the questions change, because the thing the defence rests on, a threat happening now, may be gone.

S2: Back to stand your ground. Is it a good idea?

S1: The lesson splits that in two. Whether the law ought to require someone to retreat before using deadly force, when they safely could, is a value question. It weighs the defender's freedom, the danger that a retreat was not, in Deering's words, safe or reasonable in the moment, and the risk of being second guessed later, against the attacker's life and a confrontation ending without a death. Evidence can inform it but can't settle it, so the course takes no side, and gives each side in its own words. For stand your ground, Deering at NRA Women. A duty to retreat, she writes, is one more thing to have to think about in what might be the most stressful moment of your life. And: it's very easy for a prosecutor to, with the benefit of hindsight, point out all kinds of ways you could have retreated that you didn't notice at the time or which weren't safe or reasonable in the moment.

S2: And for a duty to retreat?

S1: Giffords Law Center says these laws allow a person to use deadly force in public even if they know they could safely avoid any need for violence by simply stepping away, and that these laws too often allow individuals to use lethal force as a first step, rather than as a last resort. Its conclusion: as a result, these laws encourage the escalation of violence in everyday conflicts.

S2: Then there's the question evidence can answer.

S1: Whether the laws change how many people are killed. RAND finds supportive evidence, its strongest grade, that stand your ground laws increase total and firearm homicides. On defensive gun use its grade is inconclusive. It notes that almost all the research looked only at statutes, not case law. States with a similar rule through their courts may end up in the comparison group, which may shrink the measured effects. But it adds that statutes may affect how people behave differently from case law, in which case the focus on statutes might be justified.

S2: So the evidence, as RAND grades it, points one way on homicides, and the argument's still open.

S1: Yes. Someone can accept RAND's finding and still hold that the law shouldn't make a person under attack weigh a retreat. Someone else can hold that a duty to retreat is right whatever the figures show. And one point the supporters and the statute agree on: stand your ground removes the duty to retreat, not the other requirements. Florida still needs a reasonable belief that deadly force is necessary to prevent imminent death, great bodily harm or a forcible felony.

S2: And after force is used?

S1: That's lesson sixteen. The one piece of advice this course gives, labelled sensible and untested, is to get a lawyer's advice. What to say to police is legal advice, and it depends on where you are.
