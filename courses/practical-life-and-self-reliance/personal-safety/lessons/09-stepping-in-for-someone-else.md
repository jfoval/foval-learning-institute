Most of the time, when something goes wrong in public, you aren't the person it's happening to. You're
the one three seats back, or across the car park, or behind someone at a counter, with a few
seconds to decide whether to do anything and what. This lesson is about that decision: five ways of
helping that one widely used training teaches, what researchers watching real incidents have seen
bystanders do, how often the people who step in get hurt, one kind of emergency that works
differently, and a real disagreement about when to call the police.

:::callout Before you rely on this
This course is education, not legal advice, and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger now, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US. On a 999 call from a mobile, if you can't speak, cough or tap if you can and press 55 when you're prompted; in the US you can text 911 where that's available, but call if you can. If a partner, ex-partner or family member is frightening you, the lesson on domestic abuse and stalking starts with the helplines. Responsibility for a crime lies with the person who commits it: precautions change the odds, not the blame. The law differs between England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

The responsibility sentence in that box covers two people in this lesson. The person harassing or
attacking someone is responsible for what they do. And a bystander who froze, looked away or couldn't
think what to say didn't cause it. If you have been on either end of that moment, harassed while
a carriage full of people studied their phones, or one of the people studying a phone and thinking
about it since, nothing below is a verdict on it.

## Where First Aid and CPR left off

First Aid and CPR lesson 1 covered the ground this lesson starts from, so here it is in four lines
rather than again. The 1964 story of 38 witnesses who watched a murder and did nothing was wrong, as
the *New York Times* itself later wrote. The bystander effect is real in laboratory studies, and
modest, and it weakened when people saw a situation as dangerous. When Philpot and colleagues watched
219 CCTV clips of public conflicts in three countries, at least one bystander stepped in in 90.9% of
them. And that lesson's practical advice was to give people jobs: point at one person and name the
task.

What it left open is what this lesson takes up. What does stepping in look like when the emergency
isn't a collapse? Do the people who step in get hurt? Does the 90.9% hold for every kind of emergency?
And who decides whether the police come?

## What watching real incidents shows

Several research groups have gone past asking people what they would do and looked at what people did.

:::predict Researchers coded what third parties did in 42 aggressive incidents caught on CCTV in public places. When bystanders got involved, do you think they more often calmed things down or made them worse?

Calmed them. Levine, Taylor and Best, writing in *Psychological Science* in 2011, found that "third parties were more likely to take conciliatory actions than to escalate violence", and that "conciliatory behaviors are more successful when carried out by multiple third parties than when carried out by one person."[1]
:::

That second finding needs setting beside lesson 8, which gave you NICE's and Project BETA's rule of
one voice: when several people talk at an angry person at once, it confuses and escalates. Levine's
finding sounds like the opposite. This course read only the abstract of
[Levine's paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611398495), which does not list what counted as a
conciliatory action and gives no reason why several people did better than one, so the two can't be
matched point for point. This course's reading, and it's only a reading: the one-voice rule is about
how many people do the talking, and Levine's finding is about how many people act to calm a conflict.
Several people can do that with one of them talking and the rest standing near, fetching help or
keeping watch, which was roughly Kwame's job at the pharmacy counter in lesson 8.

The second study came from nights of observation in bars rather than from cameras. Parks and
colleagues, in a [2013 paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3939823/), studied 860
incidents of verbal and physical aggression in large bars and clubs in Toronto. Third parties were
more likely to get involved when the aggression was more severe, and the features that predicted
their involvement predicted calm, non-aggressive involvement more strongly than aggressive
involvement.[2] The authors read that as bystanders responding to the danger of serious harm. It fits
what First Aid and CPR lesson 1 reported from Fischer's pooled laboratory studies, that the bystander
effect weakened when a situation looked dangerous. This course read the abstract only.

### Do the people who step in get hurt?

:::predict Liebst and colleagues went through 93 CCTV recordings of real public disputes and followed 417 bystanders who stepped in. What share of them do you think were physically harmed?

3.6%. In the authors' words, "interveners were rarely physically harmed", at that rate.[3] On this course's arithmetic that is about 15 of the 417. Bystanders who didn't step in were almost never hurt.
:::

The study was first posted as a preprint in 2020 and published in the journal
[*Psychology of Violence*](https://doi.org/10.1037/vio0000299) in 2021. This course read the
abstract of each, and both give the same 3.6%. The published abstract adds three things. The harm
was no more likely in the more severe conflicts. Whether the person stepping in was connected to one
side of the conflict predicted being hurt, a link the authors rate as only moderately dependable,
and the abstract does not say which way it ran. And the intervener's gender was, in their words, a
highly fragile risk factor, which this course reads as: not a result to rely on.[3]

Two cautions, and they are this course's reading rather than the authors'. First, the abstract does
not say how serious the harm was, or which countries the footage came from. Second, the people on
those recordings chose to step in, in moments they judged they could. So 3.6% is the rate for people
who made that judgement, not a promise about stepping into any fight at all. Lesson 10, "What fear
does to you, and the robbery decision", meets the same problem in the robbery data, where the people
who resist are the ones who judged they could.

Even so, the finding cuts against one of the commonest reasons people give for doing nothing. In the
disputes these cameras recorded, stepping in was usually not what got someone hurt.

### A robbery in a shop is different

Philpot's 90.9% came from public conflicts: aggression, arguments and fights. Lindegaard and
colleagues asked whether it holds somewhere else.

:::predict They applied the same way of coding footage to 43 robberies of shops and other businesses, caught on CCTV. In what share of those do you think bystanders did something to help the person being robbed?

About a third: "in 35% of the events", against 90.9% for public assaults. And more bystanders did not go with more help, as they had in the street footage. The authors' conclusion: "researchers should be wary of generalizing bystander intervention rates across emergency types."[4]
:::

The study was [published online](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224545.2026.2729059) in *The Journal of
Social Psychology* on 15 September 2026, and this course read its abstract only. The abstract
describes commercial robberies as a setting with fewer bystanders and more weapons than the street
assaults.[4] It doesn't test why people helped less there, and this course won't supply a reason
the authors did not give. What it does tell you is that a number about street fights is not a number
about every emergency, which is the question "measured on whom, and measured as what?" once more.

## Right To Be's five Ds

One widely taught scheme for stepping in comes from Right To Be, a US organisation formerly called
Hollaback!, which trains people to respond to harassment. Its page on the
[5Ds of Bystander Intervention](https://righttobe.org/guides/bystander-intervention-training/) opens
on harassment in public, including harassment about someone's race, religion, immigration status,
gender presentation, sexual orientation, size, age or disability. The five are "Distract, Delegate,
Document, Delay, and Direct", and the page says "four of them are indirect methods of
intervention."[5]

They grew in stages. In the organisation's account: "In 2012, we partnered with the bystander program
Green Dot, which pioneered the Three Ds ... In 2015, we expanded those to include Delay, and in 2017,
we expanded them again to include Document."[5] Green Dot comes back in the next section, because it
is the part of this family that has been tested.

| The D | What Right To Be says it is | One of its examples |
|---|---|---|
| Distract | "Its aim is simply to derail the incident of harassment by interrupting it." | "Pretend to be lost and ask the person being harassed to give you directions" |
| Delegate | "Delegation is asking a third party for help with intervening in harassment." | Someone in charge of the space, such as "a store supervisor, bus driver, or a transit employee" |
| Document | "Documentation involves either recording or taking notes on an instance of harassment." | Its two rules, below |
| Delay | Checking in on the person "after the fact" | "Ask them if they're okay, and let them know you saw what happened and it wasn't okay." |
| Direct | Responding directly, and "Use this one with caution" | "Please stop right now." |

All quotations in the table are from Right To Be's page.[5]

**Distract** has two keys: "Ignore the person who is harassing, and engage directly with the person
who is being harassed", and "Don't talk about or refer to the harassment that's happening. Instead,
talk about something completely unrelated."[5] Asking the time, asking for directions, or simply
"Get in the way." Nobody has to know you're intervening, which is the point.

**Delegate** starts with finding someone "ready and willing to help. Often, a great choice is the
person right next to you." Then: "try to tell them as clearly as possible what you're witnessing and
how you'd like them to help."[5] That's First Aid and CPR lesson 1's named job again, arrived at from
a different direction: not "can someone do something?", but "you, can you stand with me while I go
and talk to her?". What the page says about delegating to the police is the last section of this
lesson.

**Document** comes with two rules, and the first is the one people skip. "Assess the situation. Is
anyone helping the person being harassed? If not, use another of the 5Ds." The second: "ALWAYS ask the
person who was harassed what they want to do with your recording and/or notes. NEVER post it online or
use it without their permission."[5] The page gives its reasons at more length: filming someone's
harm while nobody helps them can add to it, and footage posted without consent can make a person
visible in a way they never chose.

**Delay** is for when the moment has passed, or you could not act in it. "Even if we can't act in
the moment, we can still make a difference for someone who's been harassed by checking in on them
after the fact." Its examples include "Offer to accompany them to their destination or sit with them
for a while."[5]

**Direct** is the one the page hedges most. "Use this one with caution, because Direct intervention
can be risky", and it gives the reason: the person harassing may turn on the bystander, or escalate
in another way.[5] It gives four questions to ask first: "1. Are you physically safe? 2. Is the
person being harassed physically safe? 3. Does it seem unlikely that the situation will escalate? 4.
Can you tell if the person being harassed wants someone to speak up?" And: "If you can answer yes to
all of these questions, you might choose a direct response."[5] Even then, "keep it short and
succinct", and the page adds that you shouldn't get drawn into an argument with the person doing
it. That is lesson 8 again: Project BETA's advice not to challenge, and Quinn's rules as MacYoung
passes them on.

The page ends on a note about safety: "We don't ever want you to get hurt while trying to help someone
out. Always prioritize safety".[5] The claim that the 5Ds are "designed to be safe and not to escalate
situations" is Right To Be's account of what they were built to do, not a measured result.[5] In this
course's labels, the 5Ds are practitioner opinion: a framework from people with long experience of
teaching it, which the next section shows nobody has put to a controlled test.

One boundary, because the page does not draw it. The 5Ds are written for harassment in public. If
what you see looks like someone being controlled or hurt by a partner or family member, the lesson
"Domestic abuse and stalking" has the services' own advice for people who want to help, and its lines
take calls from people worried about someone else.

## What has been measured

Here the lesson slows down, because one claim made for bystander training is that it is proven, and
the evidence is uneven.

**The 5Ds themselves.** This course found no controlled evaluation of them. The nearest thing is a
[2022 press release](https://web.archive.org/web/20251205135030/https://righttobe.org/article/press-release-67-of-people-have-used-bystander-intervention-strategies-in-the-workplace-after-taking-right-to-bes-training/)
from Right To Be about its workplace training, reporting a nine-month study by a consultancy, with "67% of attendees reporting applying the bystander intervention skills" after
seeing harassment.[6] Run that through the course's question. Measured on whom: people who took one
workplace training. Measured as what: what they said, afterwards, that they had done. There was no
comparison group, so nobody knows how many would have stepped in anyway, and this course read the
release, not the report behind it. The release calls the 5Ds proven; this study cannot show that.

**Green Dot**, the programme the first three Ds came from, has the strongest test. Coker and
colleagues ran a cluster randomised trial in 26 Kentucky high schools, published in
[2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5737001/): schools, not pupils, were randomly assigned
to get Green Dot or to wait for it. Green Dot there meant school-wide talks plus bystander training
for students who were influential with their peers, and the outcome was violence that students
reported in anonymous surveys, counted school by school. For sexual violence perpetration the rate
ratios, intervention schools against control schools, were "0.83 (95% CI=0.70, 0.99) in Year 3 and
0.79 (95% CI=0.67, 0.94) in Year 4".[7]

A rate ratio of 0.83 means the intervention schools reported 17% less sexual violence perpetration
than the control schools that year, and the brackets are the range the true figure probably lies in.
Both ranges stay below 1, so both years show a fall, though the Year 3 range comes close to 1 at its
top. The abstract says similar patterns showed up for victimisation, sexual harassment, stalking and
dating violence. An earlier study of three US college campuses, one with Green Dot and two without,
found victimisation "17% lower" on the Green Dot campus, but that study was not randomised, so the
campuses may have differed in other ways.[8]

**Bystander programmes in general.** Kettrey and Marx pooled 15 high-quality studies of bystander
programmes for preventing sexual assault among college students and found "a significant, desirable
effect" on bystander efficacy (how capable people felt of stepping in), on their intentions, and on
intervening itself.[9] A second meta-analysis by the same two authors, of 14 studies, found the
programmes raised bystander intervention but had no significant effect on the sexual assault
perpetration the participants reported.[10] This course read both abstracts only.

Put together, and sorted the way this course sorts every claim:

- **Observed on camera or by observers:** bystanders usually step into public conflicts, usually to
  calm them, and the ones who stepped in were rarely hurt, in the footage studied. In shop robberies
  they helped far less often.
- **Tested:** Green Dot's whole programme, in Kentucky high schools, cut the violence students
  reported. That is a result for a school-wide programme, not for one person on a bus.
- **Practitioner opinion:** the 5Ds, and the claim that indirect options are the safer ones.
- **Not known:** whether reading about the 5Ds makes anyone more likely to help, or safer when they
  do. Nobody has measured it.

:::checkpoint A colleague organising a lunchtime session says: "Bystander training is proven. There was a randomised trial." Using the course's question, measured on whom and measured as what, what would you ask before agreeing?

Which programme, and on whom. The randomised trial is Green Dot's, in Kentucky high schools, and it measured school-wide violence reported in anonymous student surveys, after a programme of talks and training over years. That is good evidence for that programme in those schools. A lunchtime session on the 5Ds for office staff is a different programme, a much lighter dose and a different population, and the only study of Right To Be's own workplace training asked people what they had done, with no comparison group. A fair version of the colleague's sentence: "One bystander programme has been tested in schools and worked; this session has not been tested, and it may still be worth an hour."
:::

## The late bus

The first case, worked through. It's nearly eleven at night and you're on a bus in Southampton, in
England, two rows behind a young woman who is looking at her phone. A man across the aisle has been
making comments to her for several stops. She moved seats once, towards the front, and he moved too.
She hasn't answered him. Nobody else has said anything.

Nothing here is her doing, and the case is written forward: what are your options now?

**Direct first, because it is the one people picture.** Run Right To Be's four questions. Are you
physically safe? Probably, but you don't know him. Is she? You don't know. Does it seem unlikely to
escalate? He has already followed her once. Can you tell whether she wants someone to speak up? Not
yet. Four yeses are the page's condition for Direct, and you have none you're sure of, so on Right
To Be's own test this isn't the moment for it.[5]

**Distract costs least.** You move to the empty seat across from her, which also puts you between
them, and talk to her about something unrelated, ignoring him. That is both of Right To Be's keys
for Distract, and "Get in the way" as well.[5] The risk is that he turns his attention to you.
Right To Be's view is that the indirect Ds carry less of that risk than Direct; nobody has measured
it. In this course's labels, practitioner opinion.

**Delegate is there too.** The driver is exactly the person Right To Be names: someone with authority
in the space.[5] If Distract doesn't end it, you could go forward at the next stop and tell the
driver plainly what is happening and what you would like, which is the page's second key for
Delegate.

**Document** is not the first choice. Nobody else is helping her, and the page's first rule says
that in that case you use another D.[5]

So you choose Distract, then Delay: once he has gone, or she is off the bus, you check in.

:::checkpoint Your turn. Write the first thing you would say to her as you sit down across from her, following Right To Be's two keys for Distract. Then write one sentence for afterwards, for Delay. One short sentence each.

For Distract, something that has nothing to do with him: "Sorry, do you know if this one stops near the station?" or "Is it always this slow at night?" It ignores him and does not mention what he has been saying, which are the two keys. If yours said "Is he bothering you?", notice that it refers to the harassment, which the page says not to do while it is happening; that question belongs to Delay.

For Delay, Right To Be's own example is close to ideal: "Are you okay? I saw what he was doing, and it wasn't okay." Then an offer she can take or leave, such as sitting with her until her stop. Whether she wants any of that is her call. She may want nothing but to get home, and that's fine.
:::

**When you would switch.** If he touches her, blocks her in her seat, or threatens her, it has
stopped being harassment you can interrupt with a question about the station. GOV.UK's page on
contacting the police says: "Call 999 if you are reporting a crime that is in progress or if someone
is in immediate danger."[11] Tell the driver, who can stop the bus and call, or call yourself, on
speaker, as First Aid and CPR lesson 2 taught. What Right To Be says about the police, and why people
disagree about it, is the last section of this lesson. And if you once did nothing on a bus like this
and still think about it, this lesson is not marking that moment either. A plan made in advance,
which is what this section is, is what makes the next one easier.

## Third in the queue

The second case has a wrinkle. Theo is third in the queue at a convenience store in Albany, New York,
late on a weekday evening, holding a carton of milk. The man at the front puts a knife on the counter
and tells the cashier, Joel, to empty the till. There's one other customer, between Theo and the
man.

Everything the bus taught pulls Theo towards doing something. This is where it pulls the wrong way,
for three reasons.

**The evidence is different here.** Lindegaard's footage of shop robberies found help in 35% of them,
not 90.9%, in a setting its authors describe as having fewer bystanders and more weapons.[4] The
abstract does not say what happened to the people who helped, and this course will not guess.

**The cashier has probably been told what to do.** In the US, the Occupational Safety and Health
Administration's [recommendations for late-night retail](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/osha3153.pdf)
say staff training should include "Specific instructions on how to respond to a robbery such as
turning over money or valuables without resistance".[13] That is official guidance to employers, from
2009, and it is written for staff, not customers. Reading it as a reason for a customer to stay out of
the way is this course's step: if Joel's plan is to hand the money over and let the man go, a customer
who steps in cuts across that plan and puts himself inside a situation built to end quickly. Sensible,
untested, in this course's labels.

**What the man wants is property.** Lesson 10, "What fear does to you, and the robbery decision",
takes up the case of a weapon shown and property demanded, where every official source this course
read agrees on handing it over. The Metropolitan Police put it plainly in its own advice on street
robbery: "Property can be replaced, you can't."[14] Here the property is not even Theo's.

So what helps? This course's reading of the sources, labelled sensible, untested:

- **Staying out of it**, and leaving if he can do that quietly, without moving towards the man or
  drawing his attention. If leaving would mean walking past him, staying still is also staying out
  of it.
- **Calling when it's safe to.** Once the man has gone, or once Theo is outside, it's a 911 call.
  911.gov lists what the call-taker may ask, including the location and details such as a physical
  description of a person who may have committed a crime.[15] Noticing what the man did, what he took
  and which way he went is useful. Lesson 10 explains why memory under that kind of stress is less
  exact than it feels, so nobody should expect a perfect description of themselves.
- **Delay.** Afterwards, checking on Joel is the same D as on the bus. Being robbed at work is
  frightening whatever the training said.

:::checkpoint Suppose the other customer, the one between Theo and the man, stands completely still through the whole thing, does not call, and cannot afterwards say what the man was wearing. Did she do anything wrong?

No. Standing still is one of the things a frightened body does, and lesson 10 explains why freezing is common and involuntary. She stayed out of a robbery she could not have stopped, which is what the official advice for the people behind the counter points towards anyway. The responsibility for everything that happened in that shop lies with the man with the knife. Nobody in the queue chose wrongly, whatever they did.
:::

## Should a bystander call the police?

Back to the bus, and to Delegate. Many people would say the obvious third party is the police. Right
To Be doesn't, and this is a question people genuinely disagree about. It's partly about facts and
mostly about values: whose decision it should be. The course sets out both positions and takes
neither.

### Right To Be's position

On its 5Ds page, Right To Be answers the question directly: "you should not contact the police unless
you've checked with the person being harassed and they've explicitly asked you to call the police on
their behalf."[5] Its reasons, in summary, are these: some people are not comfortable or safe with
police involvement, and for many people and communities a history of mistreatment and violent
escalation by law enforcement has led to fear and mistrust. It names communities of colour and
undocumented people among those who "may rightfully feel unsafe in the hands of police".[5] Put in
one line, as this course reads it: the person being harassed is the one who lives with the
consequences of a police response, so the choice should be theirs. The same page's Delay section
suggests offering to help the person make a report, if they want to.

That is an organisation's stated position, and this course reports it as that. Right To Be's page is
written about harassment. It does not say how its rule applies when someone is being violently
assaulted in front of you.

### The official position

Official public pages start from the other end: the situation, not the permission. GOV.UK's page
says: "Call 999 if you are reporting a crime that is in progress or if someone is in immediate
danger."[11] In the US, 911.gov describes an emergency as any situation that needs immediate help
from the police, fire service or ambulance, and says to dial 911 straight away.[15] The Office for
Victims of Crime tells victims themselves: "If at any time you feel that you are in immediate danger,
we strongly urge you to call your local police department's emergency number (911)."[16] None of
these pages says a bystander should ask the victim first. Put in one line, as this course reads them:
a crime in progress, or someone in danger, is itself the reason to call, whoever is on the receiving
end.

There is one more fact both sides would want on the table, and it is the course's to add. This is
general information, not legal advice. The law differs by state, and between England and Wales,
Scotland and Northern Ireland. In England, Wales and Scotland, a claim for criminal injuries
compensation requires the violent crime to have been reported to the police, though Victim Support
helps people whether or not they have reported. The same scheme says a person injured taking a
"justified and exceptional" risk trying to stop a crime may also be able to claim.[17] A report can
be made later, by the person themselves, so none of this settles who should call in the moment. It
does show that a report can matter to the person harmed, which is part of why both sides care about
who makes it.

### What does not depend on the argument

Two things hold whichever position you take. First, where someone is being assaulted or is in
immediate danger, GOV.UK's own words make it a 999 call, and Right To Be's page, written about
harassment, does not address that case; the disagreement is sharpest over harassment that is not
violent. Second, both positions put safety first: Right To Be's "Always prioritize safety", and the
official pages' "immediate danger".[5][11] Where they differ is in who gets to decide about the police
when nobody is in immediate danger, and that is a question about values, which this course describes
and does not answer.

:::checkpoint A new case. On a train, a man is shouting insults at a woman about her religion. He has not touched her. She is looking straight ahead. How would each position guide a bystander who is thinking of calling the police, and what would change both answers at once?

On Right To Be's position, the bystander does not call yet: they check with her first, perhaps by sitting with her (Distract) and asking quietly, and call only if she asks them to. On the official pages' position, the question is whether a crime is in progress or someone is in immediate danger, and nothing on those pages asks the bystander to check with her first. Whether shouting insults at someone is itself a crime is a legal question this course does not answer. What changes both answers at once: if he moves to hit her, grabs her or blocks her in, someone is in immediate danger, and GOV.UK's own words make that a 999 call (911 in the US), while Right To Be's page, written about harassment, does not address that case.
:::

The call itself is First Aid and CPR lesson 2's: phone on speaker, location first, and don't hang up
until the call handler says so.

## What people get wrong

**"Crowds mean nobody helps."** First Aid and CPR lesson 1 corrected this for public conflicts:
someone stepped in in 90.9% of them. Add the exception: in shop robberies, help came in 35% of cases,
and more people did not mean more help.[4]

**"Filming is always helping."** Right To Be's rule is to film only if someone is already helping,
and never to post or use a recording without the person's permission.[5]

**"Stepping in means confronting the person."** Four of the five Ds involve no confrontation at all,
and Right To Be treats the fifth, Direct, as the one to use with caution.[5]

**"People who step in usually get hurt."** In the public disputes Liebst and colleagues studied, 3.6%
of people who stepped in were physically harmed.[3] That is the rate for people who chose their
moment, and it is not zero.

**"More people means more talking."** Several people acting to calm a conflict did better than one
in Levine's footage,[1] but lesson 8's one-voice rule still applies to who does the talking.

**"Calling the police is always right"**, and its mirror, **"calling the police is never a
bystander's decision"**. Each is one side of a disagreement about values. Where someone is in
immediate danger, the official pages are clear; over harassment that is not violent, Right To Be and
the official pages start from different places, and the course takes neither side.

## Practice

:::exercise Four scenes, and your own rule

Take 25 minutes over this, on paper. It is all planning and writing. Nothing here asks you to step into a real situation to practise, to confront or follow anyone, to call 999, 911 or the police to try it out, or to film anyone. If a scene below is close to something that happened to you, skip it.

For each scene, write three things: which D you would choose and why, in one line; the first sentence you would say, and to whom; and what would make you switch to a 999 or 911 call.

1. In a supermarket queue, a man behind a woman with a pushchair keeps telling her, loudly, that she is holding everyone up and should not have come out with a baby. She is red in the face and trying to pack her bags. The cashier looks uncomfortable.

2. Outside a takeaway late at night, two young men are shoving a third, who is on his own, saying nothing and trying to walk away. People are watching from inside.

3. At a work party, a senior colleague keeps putting his arm round a new junior colleague, who keeps stepping away from him. Your manager is across the room.

4. In a car park, a woman who was shouted at by a driver is being comforted by someone else. The driver has gone. You recorded the last part of it on your phone.

Then write your own rule, in one sentence, for when you would call 999 or 911 as a bystander. Afterwards, say which of the two positions in this lesson your rule sits closer to, and why. There is no right answer to that part.
:::

:::checkpoint Compare your answers. Each scene has more than one good choice; these are examples.

1. Distract or Delegate. Distract: speak to her, not him, about something unrelated ("Do you want a hand with those bags?"). Delegate: the cashier is someone with authority in the space; "Could you call a supervisor over?" Switch to a call if he threatens her or puts a hand on her or the pushchair.

2. This one is different. A man is being physically attacked while he tries to leave, which is someone in immediate danger in GOV.UK's words, and Direct fails Right To Be's first two questions. The official pages make it a 999 or 911 call, and Delegate works alongside it: point at one of the people watching and give them the job ("You, call 999 and put it on speaker"), as First Aid and CPR lesson 1 taught. Afterwards, Delay: check on him.

3. Delegate or Distract. Your manager is the person with authority; tell them clearly what you are seeing and what you would like ("Can you come and pull her into our conversation?"). Or Distract: go over and ask her something unrelated. Switch to a call if it becomes an assault or a threat.

4. Document, with Right To Be's second rule: ask her what she wants done with the recording, and do not post or share it without her permission. Delay: ask if she is okay. Nobody is in danger now, so no call, unless she asks you to help her report it.

For your rule, check two things: does it cover the case where someone is in immediate danger, and does it say what you would do when they are not? If it covers only one, it is half a rule.
:::
