Perhaps there's a walk you no longer take after dark, a car park you go the long way round, or an
evening out you dropped after a story in the local paper. Decisions like those are what this course
is about, and you are far from alone in making them: in October 2025, 31% of Americans told
[Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/697124/crime-seen-less-serious-second-straight-year.aspx) that
there was an area within a mile of their home where they would be afraid to walk alone at night.[1]
Most harm people fear is rarer, and most harm they actually run into is more ordinary, than the
headlines suggest.

That is not the same as saying the fear is wrong, and this lesson will not say it. It asks what fear
of crime is tracking, what it costs you and what it buys you, how to set a fear beside an ordinary
risk honestly, and what the rest of this course can and cannot do for you.

## What makes a crime frightening

In a study published in 1983, the criminologists Warr and Stafford asked residents
of Seattle how much they feared each of a list of specific crimes in everyday life, from homicide
and robbery to minor property and public order offences.[3]

:::predict Before you read on: which offence do you think came top of their fear list? And where do you think murder came?

Residential burglary outranked every other offence. Murder was low on the list. Warr, writing about
the study in 2000, says the ranking still startles people, and that the later offence-by-offence
surveys have generally found the same order, where they asked about comparable offences.[3]
:::

The explanation is the most useful idea in this lesson. Fear is not set by how serious a crime is.
In Warr's words, in a [free 2000 chapter for the National Institute of Justice](https://www.ojp.gov/criminal_justice2000/vol_4/04i.pdf), "the degree of fear attached to particular crimes is a multiplicative function of
the perceived seriousness and perceived risk of the offenses. To generate strong fear, an offense
must be perceived as both serious and likely to occur."[3] Burglary is seen as fairly serious and
fairly likely. Murder is seen as very serious and very unlikely.

Multiplicative is the word doing the work. Add two factors and a large one can carry a small one.
Multiply them and a factor near zero drags the whole product towards zero, however large the other
is. That is why the most terrible crime of all does not dominate most people's everyday fear, and why
a crime you would rate as middling can.

Both factors are perceptions. People are not good at the sizes: Warr reports that they "tended to
systematically overestimate the frequency of rare offenses while underestimating the frequency of
common ones", while being accurate about which crimes are commoner than which.[3] News is chosen
for seriousness, so "the media are most likely to report precisely those crimes that are least
likely to occur".[3] If what you know about crime comes mostly from reports of it, you are looking
at the list upside down.

:::checkpoint Try the idea on two cases this lesson has not used. A man dreads the thought of being kidnapped for ransom on holiday, though he has never heard of it happening to anyone he knows. He barely thinks about his car being broken into, which happens on his street every few weeks. On Warr's account, which fear is likely to be the stronger in his everyday life, and what would change the car one?

The car could easily win. Kidnap is dreadful and remote, a large factor times a tiny one, while a break-in is middling and likely. A change in either factor would change his fear of car crime: a break-in to his own car raises the likelihood he perceives, and caring less about what he leaves in it lowers the seriousness. His dread of kidnap is real too; dread alone, with a likelihood near zero, just doesn't make for much everyday fear.
:::

## Fear is information, not a mistake

A lot of writing about fear of crime asks whether it is rational. Warr thinks that is the wrong
question: "When applied to fear of crime, however, the concept of rationality is an ill-advised
notion."[3]

His reason is the seriousness factor. You can check perceived likelihood against data, because risk
has a rate. But seriousness depends on what a person values, and Warr's examples are a lost wedding
album, a recording of a dead parent, the life of a favourite uncle.[3] Nobody can check those
valuations against a table. Social scientists, he writes, can judge the perceptions behind people's
reactions to crime and measure the gap between perception and reality. "They are in no position,
however, to tell people the value they should place on the elements of their lives and how much
they should fear their loss or destruction."[3]

So fear itself is not the error. Warr is blunt: "Fear, then, is not intrinsically bad. It is when
fear is out of proportion to objective risk that it becomes dysfunctional."[3]

This gives the course its stance on numbers. When it tells you how common a crime is, it is giving
you information about the likelihood half of your fear, the half that can be checked. It is not
giving you a verdict on the seriousness half, which is yours. That division is this course's reading
of Warr, and every data lesson from here on is written to it.

## The gap between what people believe and what the data show

The likelihood half is where people's beliefs drift, and they drift in a particular direction.

Gallup has asked Americans how serious crime is, both nationally and where they live, since 2000,
and in every one of those polls they rated crime nationally as more serious than crime where they
live. In the words of Frank Newport, a senior scientist at Gallup, writing in an
[opinion piece](https://news.gallup.com/opinion/polling-matters/698624/exploring-local-positivity-bias-crime-perceptions.aspx),
"Americans’ views of the seriousness of crime nationwide over this 25-year period have averaged 43
percentage points higher than their views of local crime."[4] Newport's explanation is that local
views rest on direct experience and national views on "dramatic events and broader
narratives".[4] That is one writer's account, not a finding, but it fits Warr's point about the
news. Politics moves the national number too: Gallup reports that people whose party holds the
White House are "generally less likely to report an increase in crime", for supporters of both
parties.[1]

England and Wales show the same drift. The Crime Survey for England and Wales asks a large sample
of people what happened to them, whether or not they told the police; lesson 2, "Reading a crime
number", explains it. In the year to March 2016, 60% of people asked thought crime had gone up in
the country, up from 57% the year before but down from 84% in the year to March 2009, while the
survey itself showed falls.[5] The [Office for National
Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/articles/publicperceptionsofcrimeinenglandandwales/yearendingmarch2016)
(ONS) concluded that "individuals’ perceptions of crime on a national level do not typically match
well to reality", while about their immediate local area "perceptions seem to match reality more
closely".[5] Its survey, it added, doesn't cover every crime people may have in mind.[5] By the
survey's estimate for the year to March 2026, violence with or without injury was 78% below its
1995 peak.[2]

:::predict Gallup's October 2025 poll also asked Americans how often they worry about a list of specific crimes. Only two of them were worried about frequently or occasionally by a majority. Which two do you think they were?

Identity theft and scams. "Overall, Americans worry most about being the victim of identity theft (69%) and being tricked into providing financial information to scammers (53%). These are the only crimes included in the survey about which majorities say they worry frequently or occasionally."[1]
:::

Here fear and risk agree. Fraud is the commonest crime the England and Wales survey measures: 4.5
million incidents in the year to March 2026, nearly half of the 9.6 million in its main headline
count, with 7.8% of adults a victim.[2] In the US, the Federal Trade Commission [told
Congress](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/ftc-testimony-jec-hearing-on-the-rising-scam-economy.pdf)
that "In 2025, consumers submitted 3 million fraud reports to CSN and reported $15.9 billion in
losses", CSN being its database of reports, which holds only what people report.[6] So on this
course's reading of these sources, the gap is widest for violence and narrowest for fraud. Lesson
3, "Who is harmed, by whom, where and when", gives the fuller picture.

## What fear costs, and what it buys

If fear is information, you might think less of it is always better. Warr asks you to imagine a dial
that sets a society's fear of crime. Turn it down and people stop taking precautions that were doing
them good: "by relieving fear, we stand the chance of increasing public injury."[3] Turn it up and
people take precautions they do not need and shrink their own lives. "At the extreme is a “fortress
society” in which citizens withdraw from public life altogether".[3]

The shrinking is the commonest response. Warr: "the single most common reaction to fear of crime in
the United States is spatial avoidance; that is, staying away from places that are perceived to be
dangerous".[3] He also notes that fear sells: "There are entire industries in the United States
that rely on fear of crime to sell products and services."[3] Some firms, he adds, are careful in
their claims and others exaggerate the risks. This course recommends no products.

The same shrinking shows up in Great Britain. In an ONS survey in February and March 2022, among
people who felt very or fairly unsafe in at least one setting, the share of men who had stopped
walking in quiet places such as parks after dark in the past month had risen from 18% in June 2021
to 24%, and of women from 32% to 37%. Between the same two surveys the share who had stopped going to busy
public places by day fell, from 38% to 23%.[11]

:::predict About a third of people in the 2003 to 2004 British Crime Survey, which covered England and Wales, said they were very or fairly worried about being robbed. New questions in one part of the survey then asked a different thing: how often, in the past year, had they actually worried about it? What share do you think said they hadn't worried about it once?

85%. Only 5% of all respondents had worried about robbery once a month or more.[7] Gray, Jackson and Farrall concluded that the standard "how worried are you?" question magnifies the everyday experience of fear, and proposed that worry about crime is often better seen as a diffuse anxiety about risk than as a run of actual worrying moments.[7]
:::

That finding was about survey questions, but this course draws a practical step from it, sensible,
untested in its labels. Asking yourself how worried you are about something invites the diffuse
version. Asking how many times you actually worried about it last month, and what you were doing at
the time, gets closer to what the worry is costing and when it bites.

### Two kinds of worry

Jackson and Gray drew the line this course uses. They separate a dysfunctional worry,
which erodes quality of life, from a functional worry, which motivates vigilance and routine
precaution.[8] In a survey of seven London wards, published in 2010, about a quarter of the people
who said they were worried about crime described their worry as something close to problem-solving:
they took precautions, the precautions made them feel safer, and neither the precautions nor the
worry reduced the quality of their lives.[8]

Turning their grouping into a test you run on yourself is this course's step, not theirs. Does the
worry lead to a precaution? Does the precaution make you feel safer? And does the whole thing,
worry and precaution together, leave your life about the size it was? Three yeses is functional
worry. A no on the third is the kind that costs. A worry that leads to no precaution is neither yet.

The cost is not only lost evenings. Jackson and Stafford, using Whitehall II, a British study that
follows the same people over years, found poorer mental and physical health predicting more worry
about crime. Adding earlier studies, they suggested a feedback model in which worry harms health,
which in turn heightens the worry.[9]

Mental Fitness lesson 4 showed where a clinician's assessment of worry starts: worry that is
excessive and hard to control. If your worry about crime looks like that, that lesson's route is the
one to take, and this course is not a substitute for it.

## Derek and the Thursday club

Here's the first case, worked through. Derek is seventy and lives in a market town in England. For
years he went to a quiz night at a social club every Thursday evening, a ten-minute walk from his
house. Three weeks ago the local paper reported that a man had been mugged near the club's car park.
Derek hasn't been back since.

Start with Warr's two factors, and keep them separate.

**Seriousness.** For Derek this is not only about losing a wallet. He's thinking about being knocked
down, a broken hip, and not coping on his own afterwards. That is his valuation, and on Warr's
argument nobody can tell him it is wrong. On Warr's account, that is why an ordinary street robbery
can weigh heavier for him than for someone who would expect to get up and walk away.

**Likelihood.** Here the gap between local and national perception is useful. Newport thinks local
views hold up better because they rest on direct experience. The report about the car park is real
information about that car park, and it is also one event, which is a thin basis for a likelihood.
The question worth asking Derek is what he'd have guessed about that walk a month ago, from his own
years of Thursdays. Lesson 3 gives the one England and Wales figure this course found for his age
group, which is eight years old and leaves out robbery, and the course will not guess beyond it.

:::checkpoint Now the third step, before you read on. What has Derek's avoidance cost him, and what does this lesson's evidence suggest about that cost?

He has lost his main weekly evening out, and with it the people he saw there. On this course's test, drawn from Jackson and Gray, his worry has produced a precaution (staying in) that may make him feel safer, but it fails the third question, because his life has got smaller. Jackson and Stafford's suggested feedback model says that cost may not stay still: worry can harm health, and poorer health can feed the worry. So the avoidance is not free, even if it lowers his risk of that one crime.
:::

What would a functional version look like? For Derek, it might be a lift home from another member,
or walking back with someone who lives his way, and a charged phone in his pocket. Each of those is
sensible, untested, in this course's labels: nobody has measured whether they prevent a mugging,
and the claim that they keep his Thursdays is this course's inference from Jackson and Gray's work,
not a finding.

The choice is Derek's. The course's job was to separate what he values from what he's guessing, and
to show him what the guess is costing. It is not to tell him to go back.

The death rate from falls was about 2.4 times the homicide rate.[12][13] Fall deaths weigh heaviest
late in life: CDC's rate for adults aged 65 and over in 2024 was 78.4 per 100,000, adjusted for the
spread of ages within the group, so a different measure from the all-ages figure.[14] That is not a
reason for an older reader to fear crime less. It is one more reason the comparison is incomplete.

Great Britain and England and Wales can't be set side by side as neatly. The Department for
Transport counted 1,602 road deaths in Great Britain in 2024.[15] The Home Office's record of every
homicide, as ONS reports it, counted 522 homicide victims in England and Wales in the year to March
2025.[16] Great Britain includes Scotland and the homicide figure does not, and one is a calendar
year while the other runs to March. This course gives no British falls figure, because it did not
read an official one.

Now the caution. Warr passes on advice from three risk-communication researchers, Vincent Covello,
Detlof von Winterfeldt and Paul Slovic: "Avoid comparisons of risk that may appear to the audience
to be noncomparable because of different qualitative characteristics".[3] Their example was the risk
of smoking set against the risk of living near a nuclear power plant.

Violence is noncomparable with a fall in at least three ways, on this course's reading. Another
person chooses it. It carries a dread and a sense of being wronged that a slip on the stairs does
not. And precautions change the odds of some of it, which is what lessons 4 to 6 look at, while
responsibility stays with the person who chooses it. So "you're more likely to die in a fall, so
stop worrying" is the careless version, and a reader who has been attacked will rightly feel
dismissed by it. The careful version says: here is the size of this risk beside some others you
accept every day, and here is why the size isn't the whole of it.

## Priya's walk home

The second case has a wrinkle. Priya is twenty-six and lives in a city in England. She says: "I know
the numbers say I'm more at risk from people I know, but I still feel unsafe walking home."

Start with what fails her. The falls comparison does nothing for Priya: the fall death rate is highest
far above her age, and in any case it is the noncomparable kind. Telling her that her fear is
irrational fails too, for Warr's reason.

Then what explains her. Warr describes offences that arrive together in the imagination. "What for
men is the perceived risk of robbery would for many women be the perceived risk of robbery, plus
rape, plus additional injury".[3] On the multiplicative account, that raises the seriousness of
many street crimes she thinks about, so her fear can be strong even where the likelihood of any one
crime is low.

And her experience is data too. In the year to March 2026 the crime survey estimated that 10.0% of
adults in England and Wales had experienced harassment.[2]
[Scott](https://www.westerncriminology.org/documents/WCR/v04n3/article_pdfs/scott.pdf), studying the
1993 Statistics Canada survey of violence against women, found women's fear best explained by
"women having had negative experiences with strangers. Negative experiences include being followed,
receiving unwanted attention, and having received obscene phone calls."[17] If Priya has been
followed or shouted at on her way home, that is information about her streets, even if nothing else
happened.

Lesson 3 gives her the England and Wales figures for women, including the finding she
half-remembers about people she knows, and says that no source this course read gives a figure for
women of her age together. "Domestic abuse and stalking" covers harm from a partner, ex-partner or
family member, and stalking. Lesson
6, "Out and away: streets, travel and meeting people", gives her the evidence on routes, travel and
the street, with each piece of advice labelled. The course won't tell her to stay in, and it won't
tell her the street doesn't matter.

## Four readings of one gap

The groups who are harmed least by street crime, women and older people in the classic version,
often report fearing it most. Men are part of this picture too: in England and Wales, violence by
strangers falls more often on men than on women, and most often on people aged 16 to 24, and lesson
3 gives the figures. Criminologists call the gap the fear-victimisation paradox, and they don't
agree about it. It is a contested empirical question, and this course sets out four positions
without choosing between them.

**The paradox is found.** The gap is real, and it has a structure. Warr suggests that much of the
difference between men's and women's fear at the same perceived risk comes from offences that
arrive together: when rape is part of what's feared, the same likelihood of robbery weighs against a
worse outcome.[3] On this reading the fear is not mistaken; it is weighting a worse outcome.

**The paradox is an artefact.** The standard questions exaggerate fear. Gray, Jackson and Farrall's
robbery figures are the evidence: ask how often people worried, and the share who report fear falls
sharply.[7] A different kind of evidence points the same way. A 2020 study of 409 randomly chosen
residents of inner Sydney found that once their views of their neighbourhood and their experience
of crime were taken into account, gender and age had little to do with their worry about crime.[10]
That was one city and 409 people.

**The paradox is an undercount.** Women's victimisation is hidden from the surveys that measure
risk, so the risk side is too low. Feminist criminologists, as Scott summarises them, argue that
"conventional criminology tends to look at street crime and not crimes happening behind closed doors
between non-strangers and thereby undermines the detection of crimes of violence against
women."[17] There is official support for part of this: ONS says the questions its interviewers ask
face to face underestimate domestic abuse, and prefers the part of the survey people fill in
privately.[2]

**The paradox is precaution.** Lower victimisation may partly be the result of the fear, because
people who fear a place stay out of it. Warr makes the general point that those most affected by a
fear, of flying for instance, may rarely feel it, because they've arranged their lives to avoid its
source.[3] Nobody this course read has tested the idea for crime. It is a hypothesis.

What would settle it? On this course's reading, repeated measures of actual worrying moments,
collected alongside confidential victimisation questions from the same people over time. Until then,
all four stand, and each tells Priya something different: that her fear is weighting a worse harm,
that a standard survey question may be recording a general sense of risk rather than how often she
actually worries, that her risk is larger than a survey would record, or that her caution is part
of why her risk looks low.

## The course, and how it labels advice

This course is built on one value commitment, stated in the box at the top: responsibility for a
crime lies with the person who commits it, and precautions change the odds, not the blame. That is a
value, not a finding, and the institute makes it openly. Every position this lesson describes is
consistent with it, as far as this course has read, and some say it outright. The team behind a
randomised trial of a sexual assault resistance programme for university women, which lesson 12,
"Resistance training, and choosing a class", teaches, wrote that their
[programme](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5821219/) "makes clear that perpetrators are
entirely responsible for the crimes they commit".[19]

Some researchers argue that safety advice can shade into blaming victims anyway. Vera-Gray and Kelly
describe the "safety work" mandated for women and girls in public as limiting women's space for action
and making them responsible for preventing violence.[20] That critique is a mainstream position in
UK research on violence against women, and lesson 6 gives it in full beside the case for teaching
skills.

### The ladder

For a threatening encounter, the course follows an order of options: avoid, leave, talk, comply or
resist, get away, with leaving allowed at every rung. That order is not advice for abuse by a
partner, ex-partner or family member. "Domestic abuse and stalking" is about the services' help and
safety planning for that, and for stalking. Lessons 4 to 6 are about lowering the odds at home, on
the street and when travelling. Lessons 8 and 9, "Talking
it down" and "Stepping in for someone else", are about calming a situation and helping someone else
in one. Lessons 10 to 12 are about what fear does to you in the moment and the decision to hand
something over or resist. Then come two lessons on the law, "The law on using force" and "The home,
property, and carrying something for protection", and "Afterwards" ends the course.

### Four labels

Every piece of practical advice in the course carries one of four labels, in the sentence, not as a
badge:

- **Tested**: a trial, or a strong comparison, measured the outcome that matters. Lesson 5's
  comparison of home security devices in England and Wales is one; a trial that cut police use of
  force, in "Talking it down", is another, for police.
- **Sensible, untested**: it follows from established theory or evidence nearby, and nobody has
  measured the outcome. Plan your route; tell someone where you're going.
- **Contested**: the evidence points in more than one direction. Whether a burglar alarm protects a
  home is one.
- **Practitioner opinion**: experienced people teach it and no study stands behind it.

A value question, such as who should carry the burden of preventing a crime, does not get a label.
The course describes each side in its own terms and doesn't resolve it.

The labels answer the question this course asks of every claim: **measured on whom, and measured as
what?** Personal safety advice fails in one way more than any other. It outruns what was measured. A
trial on police officers is tested for police, and for you it is an inference. A study of how raters
judge people walking is not a study of who gets attacked. A crime figure is always a police count or
a survey estimate, of some population, over some period, and lesson 2 teaches you to read one.

### Where the law and the data apply

Crime figures in this course are for England and Wales, from ONS, and for the US, from the Bureau of
Justice Statistics and the FBI, with US deaths from CDC death records; Great Britain only where a
source covers it. Scotland and Northern Ireland count their
crime separately, and the course did not read their figures.

The law is set out for England and Wales, for Scotland, and for named US states, side by side and
never ranked, with the Model Penal Code labelled as a model that is not law anywhere by itself.
Northern Ireland's law is different again. Every time the course states a legal rule, it says:
"This is general information, not legal advice. The law differs by state, and between England and
Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland." It never tells you what you may lawfully do in your own
situation.

### What the course leaves out

So you know before you go looking:

- **Technique.** No strike, release, hold or escape is taught or drawn. A page cannot teach one, and
  lesson 12 is about choosing a class that can.
- **Anything about choosing, carrying, using or storing a firearm, and any product**: no sprays,
  alarms, apps or locks by brand.
- **Medical care after an assault**, which First Aid and CPR covers, and **mental health after a
  crime**, which Mental Fitness lesson 7 and First Aid lesson 16 cover.
- **Online security and the con in depth**, which Digital Literacy lessons 10 and 11 and Personal
  Finance lesson 10 teach. This course keeps the doorstep, meeting people from online, and
  technology used to track or control a partner.
- **Child protection**, beyond one section in lesson 6 on talking to children.
- **Ordinary conflict**, which belongs to Conflict Resolution, and relationships, which belong to
  Relationships and Family.
- **Northern Ireland law**, beyond one point in "The law on using force", and **employers' duties**
  for violence at work.
- **Topics this course didn't research**: terrorism and responses to mass violence, drink spiking,
  hate crime, online harassment beyond tracking and stalkerware, human trafficking, and road safety
  beyond the comparison above. Nothing in the course should be read as covering them.

## What people get wrong

**"Crime is rising."** In October 2025, 49% of Americans told Gallup crime in the country had risen
over the past year, the lowest share since at least 2018,[1] and ONS finds national perceptions "do
not typically match well to reality".[5] Lesson 2 shows how to check.

**"People who are afraid are being irrational."** Warr calls rationality "an ill-advised notion"
here, because fear depends on what a person values.[3]

**"Less fear is always better."** Turn fear down too far and people drop precautions that were
helping them. Warr's dial has two bad ends.

**"You're more likely to die falling down the stairs, so stop worrying."** The numbers may be right
and the conclusion still careless: violence differs in kind.

**"Safety advice and victim-blaming are the same thing," or "they have nothing to do with each
other."** Both are too quick. Vera-Gray and Kelly argue that the safety work mandated for women limits
their freedom and makes them responsible for preventing violence. The course's commitment is that
responsibility stays with the person who commits the crime. Whether advice to individuals can avoid
shifting the burden is a value question, and lesson 6 gives both sides.

## Practice

:::exercise A fear inventory, and six pieces of advice

Take 20 minutes over these, on paper, for yourself. Nothing here asks you to test anything in the world, and you shouldn't: no walking a route to see how it feels, no calling anyone to try it.

If one of your worries is about a partner, ex-partner or family member, leave it off the paper, and do not write it anywhere they could find it. The lesson "Domestic abuse and stalking" starts with the helplines and is the place for it.

1. List three harms you worry about, for yourself or your household. For each, score seriousness and likelihood separately, from 1 to 5, then multiply them, and see whether the ranking matches the order you'd have put them in by feel. For one of the three, name an ordinary risk you accept without much thought, and write one quality that makes the two hard to compare.

2. For each, write what you do about it now, and what that costs you in time, money and freedom. Be specific: "I don't go to X after Y o'clock" is more useful than "I'm careful".

3. For each, ask the three questions this course drew from Jackson and Gray, and mark it functional, dysfunctional, or, if it leads to no precaution, neither yet.

4. Sort six pieces of advice into the four labels. Each was written for this exercise, with an imagined description of the evidence behind it, so don't take any of them as a finding.

   a. Fit a lock to your garden shed. Imagine a trial that gave free locks to half the houses on an estate, at random, and shed break-ins fell where they were fitted.

   b. Never answer a call from a number you don't know. Imagine two studies finding that people who did this lost less to scams, and a third finding they lost just as much.

   c. Keep your car keys out of sight of your windows. Imagine that nobody has measured whether this prevents car theft, but that it follows from a well-established finding that thieves take what they can see.

   d. If a stranger stands close behind you at a cash machine, cancel and walk away. Imagine that security trainers teach this from experience and no study has looked at it.

   e. Keep the numbers for cancelling your bank cards at home, not in your wallet. Imagine that nobody has measured whether this cuts losses, but that losses are well known to grow the longer a stolen card goes unreported.

   f. Take a two-hour course in refusing requests calmly, and fewer people will threaten you. Imagine a trial finding that it cut threats against hotel reception staff.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your sort for question 4.

Tested: a, for households like those on the estate.

Contested: b. The studies point in more than one direction.

Sensible, untested: c, e and f. Each follows from something established, and none has been measured for the outcome that matters. If you put f under tested, that's the trap this course names: the trial measured hotel reception staff, so for you it is an inference.

Practitioner opinion: d. It is taught from experience, with no study behind it.
:::
