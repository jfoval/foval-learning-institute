In October 2025, 49% of Americans told [Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/697124/crime-seen-less-serious-second-straight-year.aspx) that crime in the country had risen over the past
year. That was the lowest share since at least 2018, and it was still half the country. Nearly a
third, 31%, said they'd be afraid to walk alone at night within a mile of their own home.[1] In
England and Wales, the crime survey for the year to March 2026 estimated that violence was 78% below
its 1995 peak, and that of the 9.6 million crime incidents it counted, 4.5 million were fraud.[2] Most harm
people fear is rarer, and most harm they actually run into is more ordinary, than the headlines
suggest.

That is not the same as saying the fear is wrong, and this lesson will not say it. It asks what fear
of crime is tracking, what it costs you and what it buys you, how to set a fear beside an ordinary
risk honestly, and what the rest of this course can and cannot do for you.

## What makes a crime frightening

In a study published in 1983, the criminologists Warr and Stafford asked residents
of Seattle how much they feared each of a list of specific crimes in everyday life, from homicide
and robbery to minor property and public order offences.[3]

:::predict Before you read on: which offence do you think came top of their fear list? And where do you think murder came?

Residential burglary outranked every other offence. Murder was low on the list. Warr, writing about
the study in 2000, says the ranking still startles people, and that the later offence-by-offence
surveys have generally found the same order, where they asked about comparable offences.[3]
:::

The explanation is the most useful idea in this lesson. Fear is not set by how serious a crime is.
In Warr's words, in a [free 2000 chapter for the National Institute of Justice](https://www.ojp.gov/criminal_justice2000/vol_4/04i.pdf), "the degree of fear attached to particular crimes is a multiplicative function of
the perceived seriousness and perceived risk of the offenses. To generate strong fear, an offense
must be perceived as both serious and likely to occur."[3] Burglary is seen as fairly serious and
fairly likely. Murder is seen as very serious and very unlikely.

Multiplicative is the word doing the work. Add two factors and a large one can carry a small one.
Multiply them and a factor near zero drags the whole product towards zero, however large the other
is. That is why the most terrible crime of all does not dominate most people's everyday fear, and why
a crime you'd rate as middling can.

Both factors are perceptions. People are not good at the sizes: Warr reports that they "tended to
systematically overestimate the frequency of rare offenses while underestimating the frequency of
common ones", while being accurate about which crimes are commoner than which.[3] He also points to
where most people's pictures of crime come from. News is chosen for seriousness, so "the media are most likely
to report precisely those crimes that are least likely to occur".[3] If what you know about crime
comes mostly from reports of it, you are looking at the list upside down.

:::checkpoint Try the idea on two cases this lesson has not used. A man dreads the thought of being kidnapped for ransom on holiday, though he has never heard of it happening to anyone he knows. He barely thinks about his car being broken into, which happens on his street every few weeks. On Warr's account, which fear is likely to be the stronger in his everyday life, and what would change the car one?

On the account, the car could easily win. Kidnap is dreadful and remote, a large factor times a tiny one, while a break-in is middling and likely. What would change his fear of car crime is a change in either factor: a break-in to his own car raises the likelihood he perceives, and caring less about what he leaves in it lowers the seriousness. His dread of kidnap is real too; the account only says that dread alone, with a likelihood near zero, doesn't make for much everyday fear.
:::

## Fear is information, not a mistake

A lot of writing about fear of crime asks whether it is rational. Warr thinks that's the wrong
question: "When applied to fear of crime, however, the concept of rationality is an ill-advised
notion."[3]

His reason is the seriousness factor. You can check perceived likelihood against data, because risk
has an objective side: there's a rate, and you can ask whether someone's guess is near it. But
seriousness depends on what a person values, and Warr's examples are a lost wedding album, a
recording of a dead parent, the life of a favourite uncle.[3] Nobody can check those valuations
against a table. Social scientists, he writes, can judge the perceptions and information behind
people's reactions to crime and measure the gap between perception and reality. "They are in no
position, however, to tell people the value they should place on the elements of their lives and
how much they should fear their loss or destruction."[3]

So fear itself is not the error. Warr is blunt: "Fear, then, is not intrinsically bad. It is when
fear is out of proportion to objective risk that it becomes dysfunctional."[3] The premise of his
chapter is that fear is justified when the risk you perceive matches the risk you run.[3]

This gives the course its stance on numbers. When it tells you how common a crime is, it's giving
you information about the likelihood half of your fear, the half that can be checked. It isn't
giving you a verdict on the seriousness half, which is yours. That division is this course's reading
of Warr, and every data lesson from here on is written to it.

## The gap between what people believe and what the data show

The likelihood half is where people's beliefs drift, and they drift in a particular direction.

Gallup has asked Americans about crime since 2000. In every one of those polls, people rated crime
nationally as far more serious than crime where they live. In the words of [a Gallup editor](https://news.gallup.com/opinion/polling-matters/698624/exploring-local-positivity-bias-crime-perceptions.aspx), "Americans’
views of the seriousness of crime nationwide over this 25-year period have averaged 43 percentage
points higher than their views of local crime."[4] The editor's explanation is that local views rest
on direct experience and national views on "dramatic events and broader narratives".[4] That is one
writer's account in an opinion piece, not a finding, but it fits Warr's point about the news.

Politics moves the national number too, in both directions. Gallup reports that people whose party
holds the White House are "generally less likely to report an increase in crime", and that pattern
has held for supporters of both parties as the presidency changed hands.[1]

England and Wales show the same drift. In the year to March 2016, 60% of people asked thought crime had
gone up nationally, down from 84% in 2008 to 2009, while the survey itself showed falls.[5] The
[Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/articles/publicperceptionsofcrimeinenglandandwales/yearendingmarch2016) (ONS) concluded that "individuals’ perceptions of crime on a national
level do not typically match well to reality", and that when people are asked about their immediate
local area, "perceptions seem to match reality more closely".[5] ONS added a fair caveat: its survey
doesn't cover every crime people may have in mind when they answer.[5]

:::predict Gallup's October 2025 poll also asked Americans how often they worry about a list of specific crimes. Only two of them were worried about frequently or occasionally by a majority. Which two do you think they were?

Identity theft and scams. "Overall, Americans worry most about being the victim of identity theft (69%) and being tricked into providing financial information to scammers (53%). These are the only crimes included in the survey about which majorities say they worry frequently or occasionally."[1]
:::

Here fear and risk agree. Fraud is the commonest crime the England and Wales survey measures. It
counted 4.5 million fraud incidents in the year to March 2026, nearly half of all the crime it
measured, with 7.8% of adults a victim.[2] In the US, the Federal Trade Commission [told Congress](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/ftc-testimony-jec-hearing-on-the-rising-scam-economy.pdf)
that "In 2025, consumers submitted 3 million fraud reports to CSN and reported $15.9 billion in
losses", and it counts only what gets reported.[6] So the gap is not that people fear the wrong
things across the board. It is widest for violence, and narrowest for the crime that reaches most
people. Lesson 3 gives the full picture of who is harmed, by whom, where and when.

## What fear costs, and what it buys

If fear is information, you might think less of it is always better. Warr asks you to imagine a dial
that sets a society's fear of crime. Turn it down and people stop taking precautions that were doing
them good: "by relieving fear, we stand the chance of increasing public injury."[3] Turn it up and
people take precautions they do not need and shrink their own lives. "At the extreme is a “fortress
society” in which citizens withdraw from public life altogether".[3]

The shrinking is the commonest response. Warr: "the single most common reaction to fear of crime in
the United States is spatial avoidance; that is, staying away from places that are perceived to be
dangerous".[3] In surveys he cites from Seattle and Dallas, 63% and 77% of respondents said they
avoided certain places in their city.[3] He also notes who profits from the far end of the dial:
"There are entire industries in the United States that rely on fear of crime to sell products and
services."[3] This course recommends no products, and that line is part of the reason.

The same shrinking shows up in Great Britain now. Between June 2021 and February to March 2022, the
share of men who said they'd stopped walking in quiet places such as parks after dark in the past
month, because they felt unsafe, rose from 18% to 24%, and the share of women from 32% to 37%.[11]

:::predict About a third of people in the 2003 to 2004 British Crime Survey, which covered England and Wales, said they were very or fairly worried about being robbed. New questions in one part of the survey then asked a different thing: how often, in the past year, had they actually worried about it? What share do you think said they hadn't worried about it once?

85%. Only 5% of all respondents had worried about robbery once a month or more.[7] Gray, Jackson and Farrall concluded that the standard "how worried are you?" question magnifies the everyday experience of fear, and that worry about crime is often better understood as a diffuse anxiety about risk than as a run of actual worrying moments.[7]
:::

That finding matters for you in a practical way. Asking yourself how worried you are about something
invites the diffuse version. Asking how many times you actually worried about it last month, and
what you were doing at the time, gets closer to what the worry is costing and when it bites.

### Two kinds of worry

Jackson and Gray drew the line this course uses. They separate a dysfunctional worry,
which erodes quality of life, from a functional worry, which motivates vigilance and routine
precaution.[8] In a London survey, about a quarter of the people who said they were worried about
crime described their worry as something close to problem-solving: they took precautions, the
precautions made them feel safer, and neither the precautions nor the worry reduced the quality of
their lives.[8]

That gives you a three-part test for any worry you carry. Does it lead to a precaution? Does the
precaution make you feel safer? And does the whole thing, worry and precaution together, leave your
life about the size it was? Three yeses is functional worry. A no on the third is the kind that
costs.

The cost isn't only lost evenings. Jackson and Stafford, working with the Whitehall II cohort study, suggested a feedback model in which worry about crime harms health, which in turn
heightens the worry.[9] A worry that keeps you in can make you less well, and being less well makes
you feel more exposed. A study in inner Sydney used the same functional and dysfunctional split on a
randomly selected sample of 409 residents and found that fewer than half worried about crime at all,
while a sizeable minority, 13%, had some worry about a kind of crime every week or more.[10]

Mental Fitness lesson 4 showed where a clinician's assessment of worry starts: worry that is
excessive and hard to control. If your worry about crime looks like that, that lesson's route is the
one to take, and this course is not a substitute for it.

## Derek and the Thursday club

Here's the first case, worked through. Derek is seventy and lives in a market town in England. For
years he went to a quiz night at a social club every Thursday evening, a ten-minute walk from his
house. Three weeks ago the local paper reported that a man had been mugged near the club's car park.
Derek hasn't been back since.

Start with Warr's two factors, and keep them separate.

**Seriousness.** For Derek this isn't only about losing a wallet. He's thinking about being knocked
down, a broken hip, and not coping on his own afterwards. That is his valuation, and on Warr's
argument nobody can tell him it's wrong. On Warr's account, that's why an ordinary street robbery can weigh
heavier for him than for someone who'd expect to get up and walk away.

**Likelihood.** Here the gap between local and national perception is useful. Gallup's editor thinks
local views hold up better because they rest on direct experience. Derek's sense of how likely a
mugging is now rests on one news report, which is not his experience even though it is local, and on
this course's reading it has pulled his local view towards the national kind. He knows his own
streets well. The
question worth asking him is what he'd have guessed about that walk a month ago, from his own years
of Thursdays. Lesson 3 gives the numbers for his age group, and the course will not guess at them here.

:::checkpoint Now the third step, before you read on. What has Derek's avoidance cost him, and what does this lesson's evidence suggest about that cost?

He has lost his main weekly evening out, and with it the people he saw there. On Jackson and Gray's test, his worry has produced a precaution (staying in) that may make him feel safer, but it fails the third question, because his life has got smaller. Jackson and Stafford's feedback model suggests that cost does not stay still: worry that harms health tends to heighten the worry. So the avoidance is not free, even if it lowers his risk of that one crime.
:::

What would a functional version look like? Jackson and Gray's worriers took precautions that made
them feel safer and did not shrink their lives. For Derek, that might be a lift home from another
member, or walking back with someone who lives his way, and a charged phone in his pocket. Each of
those is sensible, untested, in this course's labels: nobody has measured whether they prevent a
mugging, and the claim that they keep his Thursdays is this course's inference from Jackson and
Gray's test, not a finding.

The choice is Derek's. The course's job was to separate what he values from what he's guessing, and
to show him what the guess is costing. It is not to tell him to go back.

In 2024, 14.2 Americans per 100,000 died in a fall, 12.1 in road traffic, and 5.9 by homicide.[12][13]
The death rate from falls was about 2.4 times the homicide rate. And fall deaths weigh heaviest late
in life: CDC's rate for adults aged 65 and over in 2024 was 78.4 deaths per 100,000, age-adjusted
within that age group, against the 14.2 for everyone.[14] That is worth holding onto, because Warr
notes that the groups early researchers saw as unusually afraid of crime were women and the
elderly.[3]

Great Britain and England and Wales can't be set side by side as neatly, because the sources cover
different places. The Department for Transport counted 1,602 road deaths in Great Britain in
2024.[15] The Home Office Homicide Index, as ONS reports it, counted 522 homicide victims in England
and Wales in the year to March 2025.[16] Great Britain includes Scotland and the homicide figure
doesn't, and one is a calendar year while the other runs to March. Even allowing for all that,
these two counts give roughly three people dead on British roads for each person killed in
England and Wales. This course
gives no British falls figure, because it didn't read an official one.

Now the caution. Warr passes on advice from three risk-communication researchers, Vincent Covello,
Detlof von Winterfeldt and Paul Slovic: "Avoid comparisons of risk that may appear to the audience
to be noncomparable because of different qualitative characteristics".[3] Their example was the risk
of smoking set against the risk of living near a nuclear power plant. Warr adds that comparisons
across hazards can illustrate a risk well, but are hard to read if they're badly built.[3]

Violence is noncomparable with a fall in at least three ways, on this course's reading. Another
person chooses it. It carries a dread and a sense of being wronged that a slip on the stairs does not.
And some of it can be steered by what you do, which is the whole premise of lessons 4 to 7. So
"you're more likely to die in a fall, so stop worrying" is the careless version, and a reader who has
been attacked will rightly feel dismissed by it. The careful version says: here is the size of this
risk beside some others you accept every day, and here is why the size isn't the whole of it.

## Priya's walk home

The second case has a wrinkle. Priya is twenty-six and lives in a city in England. She says: "I know
the numbers say I'm more at risk from people I know, but I still feel unsafe walking home."

Start with what fails her. The falls comparison does nothing for Priya: the fall death rate is highest
far above her age, and in any case it's the noncomparable kind. Telling her that her fear is irrational
fails too, for Warr's reason.

Then what explains her. Warr describes offences that arrive together in the imagination. "What for
men is the perceived risk of robbery would for many women be the perceived risk of robbery, plus
rape, plus additional injury".[3] On the multiplicative account, that raises the seriousness factor
of every street crime she thinks about, so her fear can be strong even where the likelihood of any
one crime is low.

And her experience is data too. Harassment is common: in the year to March 2026 the crime survey
estimated that 10.0% of adults in England and Wales had experienced it,[2] and ONS's article on the
year to March 2024 put women's rate at 11.2% against men's 6.6%.[18] [Scott](https://www.westerncriminology.org/documents/WCR/v04n3/article_pdfs/scott.pdf), studying the 1993
Statistics Canada survey of violence against women, found women's fear best explained by "women
having had negative experiences with strangers. Negative experiences include being followed,
receiving unwanted attention, and having received obscene phone calls."[17] If Priya has been
followed or shouted at on her way home, that's information about her streets, even if nothing
else happened.

The course gives Priya the numbers for her age and sex in lesson 3, including the finding she
half-remembers about people she knows, and "Domestic abuse and stalking" is lesson 7. It will give her the evidence on routes, travel and the street, with each
piece of advice labelled, in lesson 6. It won't tell her to stay in, and it will not tell her the street
doesn't matter.

## Four readings of one gap

The groups who are harmed least by street crime, women and older people in the classic version,
often report fearing it most. Criminologists call
this the fear-victimisation paradox, and they don't agree about it. It is a contested empirical
question, and this course sets out four positions without choosing between them.

**The paradox is found.** The gap is real, and it has a structure. Warr's account of seriousness
explains much of the difference between men's and women's fear: the same perceived likelihood of
robbery multiplies against a larger seriousness when rape is part of what's feared.[3] On this
reading the fear is not mistaken; it's weighting a worse outcome.

**The paradox is an artefact.** The standard questions exaggerate fear. Gray, Jackson and Farrall's
robbery figures are the evidence: ask how often people worried, and most of the fear disappears.[7]
The Sydney study adds that "gender and age are largely unrelated to worry about crime, controlling
for perceptions of community disorder, perceptions of collective efficacy, direct victimisation
experience and indirect victimisation experience".[10] That was one city and 409 people.

**The paradox is an undercount.** Women's victimisation is hidden from the surveys that measure
risk, so the risk side is too low. Feminist criminologists, as Scott summarises them, argue that
"conventional criminology tends to look at street crime and not crimes happening behind closed doors
between non-strangers and thereby undermines the detection of crimes of violence against
women."[17] There is official support for part of this: ONS says the crime survey's
interviewer-administered questions underestimate domestic abuse, and prefers its self-completion
module.[2]

**The paradox is precaution.** Lower victimisation may partly be the result of the fear, because
people who fear a place stay out of it. Warr makes the general point that those most affected by a
fear, of flying for instance, may rarely feel it, because they've arranged their lives to avoid its
source.[3] Nobody this course read has tested the idea for crime. It is a hypothesis.

What would settle it? On this course's reading, repeated measures of actual worrying moments,
collected alongside confidential victimisation questions from the same people over time. Until then,
all four stand, and each tells Priya something different: that her fear is weighting a worse harm,
that her fear is smaller than a survey would record, that her risk is larger than a survey would
record, or that her caution is part of why her risk looks low.

## The course, and how it labels advice

This course is built on one value commitment, stated in the box at the top: responsibility for a
crime lies with the person who commits it, and precautions change the odds, not the blame. That is a
value, not a finding, and the institute makes it openly. It's shared by every position the course
describes. The team behind a randomised trial of a sexual assault resistance programme for university women,
which lesson 12 teaches, wrote that their programme "makes clear that perpetrators are entirely responsible
for the crimes they commit".[19]

Some researchers argue that safety advice can shade into blaming victims anyway. Vera-Gray and Kelly
describe the "safety work" asked of women and girls in public as limiting women's space for action
and making them responsible for preventing violence.[20] That critique is mainstream
research, not a fringe view, and lesson 6 gives it in full beside the case for teaching skills.

### The ladder

The course follows an order of options that runs through the whole of it: avoid, leave, talk,
comply or resist, get away, with leaving available at every rung. Lessons 4 to 7 are about avoiding,
at home, on the street and with people you know. Lessons 8 and 9 are about talking a situation down,
and stepping in for someone else. Lessons 10 to 12 are about what fear does to you in the moment and
the decision to hand something over or resist. Lessons 13 and 14 are the law. Lesson 15 is
afterwards.

### Four labels

Every piece of practical advice in the course carries one of four labels, in the sentence, not as a
badge:

- **Tested**: a trial, or a strong comparison, measured the outcome that matters. Lesson 5's
  comparison of home security devices in England and Wales is one; a trial that cut police use of
  force, in lesson 8, is another, for police.
- **Sensible, untested**: it follows from established theory or evidence nearby, and nobody has
  measured the outcome. Plan your route; tell someone where you're going.
- **Contested**: the evidence points in more than one direction. Whether a burglar alarm protects a
  home is one.
- **Practitioner opinion**: experienced people teach it and no study stands behind it.

A value question, such as who should carry the burden of preventing a crime, does not get a label.
The course describes each side in its own terms and doesn't resolve it.

The labels answer the question this course asks of every claim: **measured on whom, and measured as
what?** Personal safety advice fails in one way more than any other. It outruns what was measured. A
trial on police officers is tested for police, and for you it is an inference. A study of how raters
judge people walking isn't a study of who gets attacked. A crime figure is always a police count or a
survey estimate, of some population, over some period, and lesson 2 teaches you to read one.

### Where the law and the data apply

Crime figures in this course are for England and Wales, from ONS, and for the US, from the Bureau of
Justice Statistics and the FBI. Great Britain appears only where a source covers it, as the road
deaths above do. Scotland and Northern Ireland count their crime separately, and the course did not
read their figures.

The law is set out for England and Wales, for Scotland, and for named US states, side by side and
never ranked. Every time the course states a legal rule, it says: This is general information, not
legal advice. The law differs by state, and between England and Wales, Scotland and Northern
Ireland. It never tells you what you may lawfully do in your own situation.

### What the course leaves out

So you know before you go looking:

- **Technique.** No strike, release, hold or escape is taught or drawn. A page cannot teach one, and
  lesson 12 is about choosing a class that can.
- **Anything about choosing, carrying, using or storing a firearm, and any product**: no sprays,
  alarms, apps or locks by brand.
- **Medical care after an assault**, which First Aid and CPR covers, and **mental health after a
  crime**, which Mental Fitness lesson 7 and First Aid lesson 16 cover.
- **Online security and the con in depth**, which Digital Literacy lessons 10 and 11 and Personal
  Finance lesson 10 teach. This course keeps the doorstep and meeting people from online.
- **Child protection.** Lesson 6 has one section on talking to children.
- **Ordinary conflict**, which belongs to Conflict Resolution, and relationships, which belong to
  Relationships and Family.
- **Topics this course didn't research**: terrorism and responses to mass violence, drink spiking,
  hate crime, online harassment beyond tracking and stalkerware, human trafficking, and road safety
  beyond the comparison above. Nothing in the course should be read as covering them.

## What people get wrong

**"Crime is rising."** Half of Americans said so in 2025, the lowest share since at least 2018,[1]
and national perceptions drift furthest from the data. ONS: national perceptions "do not typically match well to reality".[5] Lesson 2
shows how to check.

**"People who are afraid are being irrational."** Warr calls rationality "an ill-advised notion"
here, because fear depends on what a person values.[3]

**"Less fear is always better."** Turn fear down too far and people drop precautions that were
helping them. Warr's dial has two bad ends.

**"You're more likely to die falling down the stairs, so stop worrying."** The numbers may be right
and the conclusion still careless: violence differs in kind, and the risk-communication advice Warr
passes on is to avoid comparisons that look noncomparable.

**"Personal safety advice is about making victims responsible."** Critics argue that some of it
works that way, and lesson 6 sets out their case. This course's commitment runs the other way: precautions change the
odds, and responsibility stays with the person who commits the crime.

## Practice

:::exercise A fear inventory, and six pieces of advice

Take 20 minutes over these, on paper, for yourself. Nothing here asks you to test anything in the world, and you shouldn't: no walking a route to see how it feels, no calling anyone to try it.

If one of your worries is about a partner, ex-partner or family member, leave it off the paper, and do not write it anywhere they could find it. The lesson "Domestic abuse and stalking" starts with the helplines and is the place for it.

1. List three harms you worry about, for yourself or your household.

2. For each, score seriousness and likelihood separately, from 1 to 5. Don't combine them yet. Then multiply them, and see whether the ranking matches the order you'd have put them in by feel.

3. For each, write what you do about it now, and what that costs you in time, money and freedom. Be specific: "I don't go to X after Y o'clock" is more useful than "I'm careful".

4. For each, ask Jackson and Gray's three questions: does it lead to a precaution; does the precaution make me feel safer; does the whole thing leave my life about the size it was? Mark it functional or dysfunctional.

5. Now sort these six pieces of advice into the four labels. You can decide each one from what this lesson has told you about the labels and the evidence behind each piece.

   a. Fit window locks and indoor lights on a timer, together with double locks or deadlocks and outside lights on a sensor. A large survey comparison of households in England and Wales measured burglary with entry against these devices.

   b. Fit a burglar alarm. Survey data from England and Wales and studies from the US point in different directions.

   c. Tell someone your route and when you expect to be home.

   d. Don't hold a phone conversation while walking somewhere unfamiliar. A study found phone conversations cut what walkers noticed; nobody has measured whether avoiding them prevents crime.

   e. De-escalation works on someone trying to win an argument, not on someone who has already decided to rob you. Self-defence teachers teach this distinction; no study has tested it.

   f. Read a guide to de-escalation and you'll use less force in a confrontation. A trial found that de-escalation training cut use of force by police officers.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your sort for question 5.

Tested: a. It's a comparison between households rather than a trial, and lesson 5 shows how strong it is and what it can't show.

Contested: b. Lesson 5 sets out both sides, including who funded which studies.

Sensible, untested: c, d and f. Each follows from something established (someone who'd notice; a finding about attention; a trial on police), and none has been measured for you. If you put f under tested, that's the trap this course names: the trial measured police officers, so for a reader of a guide it is an inference.

Practitioner opinion: e. It's a model taught from experience, and lesson 8 teaches it as one.
:::
