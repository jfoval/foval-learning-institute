Suppose your local paper runs the headline *Violent crime jumps 20% in our area*. You walk home from
work after dark, or your teenage son does. Should either of you change anything?

You can't answer that yet, and neither can the paper, until you know what was counted. Lesson 1 left
you with half of Americans telling a pollster that crime had risen, and a promise that this lesson
would show you how to check. A crime number is never a plain count of crimes. It's what survives a
series of filters: whether the victim told anyone, whether the police wrote it down, who the source could reach, how the offence was defined,
which months it covers and what it was divided by. Change any one of those and the number moves,
with no change in how many people were hurt. This lesson follows those filters one at a time and ends
with five questions you can put to any crime figure, starting with that headline.

The law differs between England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.

## Two ways to count

There are two main ways to find out how much crime there is. You can count what the police recorded,
or you can pick a sample of people and ask them what happened to them. Both the US and England and
Wales do both, and nearly every crime figure you'll read comes from one side or the other.

:::predict You want to know whether killings in England and Wales went up or down last year. Which source do you reach for, and why not the other?

The police count. A survey that works by interviewing victims cannot count people who were killed, and homicide is exactly the kind of rarer, more serious offence that ONS, as you're about to see, says police data handle better. In England and Wales there's also a third source, the Home Office Homicide Index, which lesson 1 cited and lesson 3 uses.
:::

**In the US**, the survey is the [National Crime Victimization Survey](https://bjs.ojp.gov/document/cv24.pdf)
(NCVS), run by the Bureau of Justice Statistics. In a typical year it interviews "about 240,000
persons in approximately 150,000 households", people aged 12 and over, and asks about nonfatal
violence (rape or sexual assault, robbery, aggravated and simple assault) and household property
crime, whether or not anyone told the police.[1] Because it works by interviewing victims, there is
one crime it can never count. Its tables carry the footnote: "Excludes homicide because the National
Crime Victimization Survey (NCVS) is based on interviews with victims."[1]

The police side is the FBI's [Uniform Crime Reporting programme](https://cde.ucr.cjis.gov/LATEST/resources/reports/UCR%20Summary%20of%20Reported%20Crimes%20in%20the%20Nation%202024.pdf), now built on the National
Incident-Based Reporting System (NIBRS). For 2024 it drew on 16,675 police agencies covering 95.6% of
the population, with estimation filling in the rest.[2] It counts offences known to the police, so
it includes homicide, crimes against businesses and crimes against children under 12, none of which
the survey reaches. The older Summary Reporting System (SRS) also had a counting rule that still
trips people up: "The SRS Hierarchy Rule states only the one most serious offense within a criminal incident is
counted. For example, if a robbery and a homicide occur in the same incident, SRS will only count the
homicide." NIBRS records up to ten offences per incident instead.[2]

**In England and Wales**, the survey is the Crime Survey for England and Wales (CSEW), which asks
people aged 16 and over, and the police side is police recorded crime. The Office for National
Statistics (ONS) publishes both in one [bulletin](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/bulletins/crimeinenglandandwales/yearendingmarch2026)
and says plainly what each is for. ONS says the CSEW is better for tracking long-term trends in
violence, theft, criminal damage, fraud and computer misuse. For the rest, it writes: "Police
recorded crime is not always a good indicator of general trends in crime, because of
changes in how it is recorded over time. However, it can give more insight into lower-volume,
higher-harm offences that are reported to the police".[7] The crimes it names for police data are
homicide, offences involving weapons, and robbery.

The survey has its own blind spots, and ONS lists them: "The survey excludes crimes against
commercial or public sector bodies, tourists, or those living in communal establishments like care
homes, student halls of residence, and prisons."[7]

As lesson 1 said, Scotland and Northern Ireland count their crime separately and this course did not
read their figures, so every crime figure in this course from outside the US is for England and
Wales only.

Side by side, the four look like this. Each is correct about what it counts and silent about what it
does not.

| In the US | Survey (NCVS) | Police (FBI) |
|---|---|---|
| Counts | People 12+ in households | Offences known to police |
| Unreported crime | Yes | No |
| Homicide | No | Yes |
| Businesses | No | Yes |

| In England and Wales | Survey (CSEW) | Police recorded crime |
|---|---|---|
| Counts | People 16+ in households | Crimes recorded by police |
| Unreported crime | Yes | No |
| Homicide | No | Yes |
| Businesses | No | Yes |

## What kind of number is it?

Once you know who counted, the next question is what kind of number they produced. The NCVS
bulletin defines three, and the difference between them is where most misreadings start.[1]

- **Victimizations**: "the total number of times that persons or households were victims". Someone
  robbed twice is two victimizations.
- **Incidents**: "specific criminal acts involving one or more victims". One robbery of two friends is
  one incident and two victimizations.
- **Prevalence**: "the number or percentage of unique persons who were crime victims". Someone robbed
  twice counts once.

Watch what that does to one year's figures. For 2024 the NCVS put the rate of violent victimization at
23.3 per 1,000 people aged 12 and over. It also gave a prevalence: "In 2024, 1.45% of persons age 12
or older experienced at least one violent victimization".[1] Turn both into what statisticians call
natural frequencies, meaning counts of people out of a round number, and the picture is plain. Out
of every 1,000 people aged 12 and over, about 15 were victims of violence at least once that year,
and between them they had about 23 victimizations. The gap is people who were victims more than
once, and lesson 3 comes back to them.

Then there is the denominator, the "per how many". Each source picks its own:

- the NCVS: per 1,000 persons aged 12 or older (or per 1,000 households for property crime);
- the FBI: per 100,000 inhabitants;
- ONS homicide figures: per million people;
- CSEW prevalence: a percentage of adults aged 16 and over.

None of these is wrong. They just can't be laid side by side until you convert them, and even then
only if they count the same thing.

The last label is the period, and it's slipperier than it looks. The NCVS for 2024 does not cover
calendar 2024. People interviewed during 2024 were asked about the six months before the month of
their interview, so the 2024 survey covers crimes from 1 July 2023 to 30 November 2024, and BJS files
each crime under the year of the survey, not the year it happened.[1] The CSEW works in years ending
in March, and it too asks about the 12 months before each interview.[7] So "2024" from one source and "the year ending March 2024" from another are not the same stretch of time.

:::checkpoint The FBI's 2024 figure was "An estimated 1,221,345 violent crime offenses were committed in 2024, indicating a rate of 359.1 violent crimes per 100,000 inhabitants". Restate the rate per 1,000 people, then as a natural frequency. It's a rate, but a rate of what: offences, victims or people?

Divide by 100: about 3.6 per 1,000 inhabitants, so roughly 4 violent offences recorded for every 1,000 people in the country that year. It is a rate of offences, not a prevalence. It says nothing about how many different people were victims, because one person can be the victim of several offences and one offence can have several victims.[2]
:::

## Three correct numbers for one year

The clearest example comes from a short brief BJS published in March
2026, [*The Nation's Two Crime Measures, 2015–2024*](https://bjs.ojp.gov/document/ntcm1524.pdf),
which sets the survey and the police data side by side.[3] To line the two up, the brief leaves simple assault out of the survey's
violent crime and uses three offences, rape or sexual assault, robbery and aggravated assault, while
the police figure covers homicide, rape, robbery and aggravated assault.

:::predict For the US in 2024, the brief gives three rates of violent crime: the survey's rate for those three offences, the survey's rate for the part victims said was reported to police, and the police-data rate. Before you look, put them in order from largest to smallest, and guess how far apart the largest and smallest are.

The survey's rate for the three offences was 8.9 per 1,000, the reported part 5.3 per 1,000, and the police rate 3.7 per 1,000. The largest is well over twice the smallest, and all three are correct.[3]
:::

The police rate here is 3.7 per 1,000, where the FBI figure you converted earlier came to about 3.6.
The brief uses the NIBRS Estimation Program's summary estimate, a different estimate from the FBI's
headline, built from the same police records.[3] The FBI publishes more than one estimate from its
own data, and says of them: "Both collections are estimates and should be viewed as analogous
measures, rather than as one being more accurate than the other."[2]

Take them apart one filter at a time.

*The three US rates of violent crime excluding simple assault, 2024, on one scale. The red lines are
the 95% confidence intervals BJS publishes with each figure, the kind you met in Habits and

**From 8.9 to 5.3: the reporting filter.** Both numbers come from the same interviews, the same
people and the same offences. The only difference is that 5.3 keeps just the crimes the victim said
had reached the police. That's about 60% of 8.9, and the NCVS bulletin gives the same share for
this group of crimes in 2024: 60.2% of violent crime excluding simple assault was reported.[1] So the
first filter is people deciding, for all sorts of reasons, whether to tell the police.

**From 5.3 to 3.7: the source filter.** Now you're comparing a survey with police data, and several
things change at once. Start with the denominator. The survey divides by people aged 12 and over.
The police rate divides by all US residents.

:::checkpoint Which way does dividing by all residents, rather than by people aged 12 and over, push the police rate?

Down. All residents is a bigger group than people aged 12 and over, and the same count divided by more people gives a smaller rate. So part of the gap between 5.3 and 3.7 is only arithmetic.
:::

That's not the whole story, because the scope pushes the other way. BJS notes that the police figure
includes crimes against businesses and against people aged 11 or younger, homeless people and people
in institutions, none of which the survey covers, and it includes homicide.[3] Those push the police
rate up. So the denominator accounts for some of the gap, the wider scope works against it, and the
brief doesn't say how the two net out.

The rest of the gap could come from several places. BJS itself says the two collections use different
methods and measure offences that are similar but not identical.[3] A crime a victim says was
reported still has to be recorded by the police before it's counted, and in England and Wales an
inspection found that often it was not (two sections on); the sources here do not say how often that
happens in the US. And the survey's figure is an estimate from a sample: the interval around 5.3 runs from about 4.1 to 6.6.[3]

The survey's intervals in the chart are wide because it interviews a sample of people. The police
figure's is tiny; it is an estimate too, but built from agencies' records rather than a sample. Narrow
does not mean closer to the truth about crime: it means a precise count of what reached the police
and was written down. The FBI says of the survey and its own data that
"each program has unique strengths. Together, they can provide a greater understanding of crime
trends in the United States."[2]

## Most crime never reaches the police

The first filter is the biggest, and it's the one people forget. In the US survey for 2024,
"Approximately 48% of violent victimizations were reported to police in 2024", and for property
crime the share was 30%.[1] As natural frequencies: of every ten violent crimes the survey found,
about five reached the police; of every ten property crimes, three.

England and Wales measure it differently: across what ONS calls CSEW headline crime, its name for
the main set of crimes the survey counts, rather than violence alone. ONS says "only an estimated 36%
of CSEW headline crime incidents (excluding fraud and computer misuse) were reported to the police in
the CSEW YE March 2026."[7] Roughly one in three. The two figures cover different crimes, so they do not
compare the countries; what they share is that in both, much crime never reaches the police.

Fraud is further out still. Lesson 1 met the Federal Trade Commission's 3 million US fraud reports
for 2025. Reports are the small part: the FTC's [own testimony](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/ftc-testimony-jec-hearing-on-the-rising-scam-economy.pdf)
cites a study finding "only 4.8 percent of victims of mass-market consumer fraud complained to a
Better Business Bureau or a government entity".[10] A Better Business Bureau is a private US
complaints body. So about one victim in twenty told anyone official. In England and Wales, the
survey estimated that 7.8% of adults were victims of fraud in the year to March 2026, and fraud is
one of the crimes for which ONS calls the survey its preferred source.[7]

The US bulletin names some of the reasons victims may not report, among them fear of reprisal, not
wanting to get the offender in trouble, believing the police could not or would not help, and thinking
the matter too personal or too trivial.[1] None of those is a mistake by the victim. They are the
reason a police count cannot tell you how much crime there is, only how much reached the police.
So if your local headline is a police count, part of what it measures is how many people in your
area chose to tell the police.

## When a count rises and crime does not

The second filter is recording. A crime reported to the police still has to be written down as a
crime before it appears in any figure, and whether it is depends on rules and on how closely forces
follow them.

In 2014 [Her Majesty's Inspectorate of Constabulary](https://assets-hmicfrs.justiceinspectorates.gov.uk/uploads/crime-recording-making-the-victim-count.pdf)
(HMIC, as it was then called) audited police recording in England and Wales and found this: "Over
800,000 crimes reported to the police have gone unrecorded each year. This represents an under-recording of 19 percent. The problem is greatest for victims of violence against
the person and sexual offences, where the under-recording rates are 33 percent and 26 percent
respectively."[8] As natural frequencies, about one reported violent crime in three was not making it
into the count. Earlier that year, before the report came out, police recorded crime had already
lost its accreditation as official statistics. ONS writes: "Their accreditation was removed by the
Office for Statistics Regulation (OSR) in January 2014 following an assessment that found evidence
that the quality and consistency of the underlying data may not be reliable." It has not been
restored; the figures are still published as official statistics, without that mark of quality.[7]

Now put that finding to work.

:::predict Suppose that at some point in the decade after that inspection you read the headline *Violent crime soars again, police figures show*, while the crime survey for the same years shows violence falling. The headline is made up for this lesson; the two sources are real. Which do you believe, and for what?

For the trend in violence against adults in households, the survey. ONS prefers it for violence, and its bulletin now says that changes in recording practices have affected the reliability of the police figures, particularly for violent crime, making them better indicators of police activity than of crime trends.[7] The survey's estimate of violence was 33% lower in the year to March 2026 than a decade earlier (989,000 incidents against 1.5 million).[7] The police count is still the better source for the rarer, more serious offences such as homicide, knife and firearm crime and robbery.
:::

Walk through why the two can move apart. If forces start recording more of what's reported to them,
the police count rises even if the number of attacks stays the same, because the filter has opened.
ONS says this of the police count directly: "Increases in volume over the last 10 years have been
largely influenced by improvements in recording standards."[7] A survey that asks people directly is
not touched by what the police write down. So the honest version of the headline is something like: *Police recorded more violent
crime this year. The crime survey, which the statistics office prefers for violence, shows no rise.
The statistics office says changes in recording have made police figures a better measure of police
activity than of crime, and a 2014 inspection found a third of reported violence going unrecorded.*
Your local 20% could be this: the same attacks, more of them written down.

The survey is not beyond question either, and its weaknesses are public. The [regulator](https://osr.statisticsauthority.gov.uk/guidance/osr-statement-on-statistics-from-the-crime-survey-for-england-and-wales/)
suspended the CSEW's accredited status in July 2022, citing quality concerns "including a drop in the
survey response rate", and after a review wrote: "In 2024, we reinstated the accredited official
statistics status of the CSEW estimates."[9] In April 2026 it answered claims it had seen about the
survey. As OSR puts them, critics had said the national estimates don't match what people experience
in their own lives, that the sample undercounts cities, and that "Police recorded crime figures are
inherently more accurate than CSEW estimates". OSR answered that a national estimate cannot mirror
every person's experience, which does not make it wrong; that the sample is drawn at random from
households across England and Wales; and that the third claim is a misunderstanding, because the two
measure different things.[9] Each source is good at something, and neither is the real number for
everything.

The questions under a series can change too. In the year to March 2026 the survey found 40.9% of
adults had experienced or witnessed anti-social behaviour, a significant rise that ONS says was
partly real but "inflated because of changes to how questions in the CSEW were asked from April
2025."[7] In the US, the NCVS ran its old and new questionnaires side by side in 2024, each on about
half the sample, and the 2024 bulletin reports only the old half, so its estimates are less precise
than usual. BJS says the split lets it keep comparing years across the change. Even so, if a US
figure for 2025 or later looks like a sudden jump, check which questionnaire it came from and
whether BJS itself calls the change significant.[1]

## The hardest numbers to count

Rarer and more private harms have the figures most often misread, in two ways.

**Rough numbers for rare crimes.** Two rows from the NCVS bulletin's table of crimes reported to
police show the first trap. For rape or sexual assault, the share reported was 46% in 2023 and 24% in
2024. For robbery it went the other way, from 42% to 73%.[1] BJS marks both changes as statistically
significant, the kind of test First Aid and CPR lesson 3 described: each change is bigger than the
survey's sampling error alone would usually produce, so neither is just noise. But each year's figure
rests on a small number of interviews, and BJS's own margin around the 2024 rape figure runs many
points either way.[1] So the fall is BJS's finding, how big it really was is very uncertain, and 24%
on its own is not a reporting rate anyone should quote.

A panel of the [National Research Council](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/18605/chapter/2),
reporting on the NCVS in 2014, explained why rape figures are especially hard for this survey. It
concluded that the survey "does not measure the low incidence events of rape and
sexual assault with the precision needed for policy and research purposes. Comparisons across
subgroups and years are particularly problematic."[5] It also found that the way the survey handles
people who report many similar incidents lets a few such reports move the national estimates of rape
a long way from one year to the next.[5] The panel was writing about how much rape occurs, not the
share reported, but its warning about comparing years carries across.

So the figure to hold on to is the one across all violence: in the 2024 survey, about half of US
violent victimizations reached the police, and under a third of property crime.[1]

:::checkpoint A friend says, *Rape reporting in the US halved last year.* Using what you've just read, what's the sentence the evidence supports, and what's wrong with hers?

Something like: *In the US crime survey, the estimated share of rapes and sexual assaults that victims said were reported to police fell from 46% in 2023 to 24% in 2024. BJS counts the change as statistically significant, but each year's figure is uncertain by many points, and a national panel has warned that year-to-year comparisons of these crimes in this survey are particularly problematic.* Hers drops the source, turns two imprecise estimates into a settled fact about the world, and invites the next person to quote 24% as the share of rapes reported in general, when it is one year's imprecise estimate.
:::

**Lifetime against annual.** The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) publishes this
on its [intimate partner violence page](https://www.cdc.gov/intimate-partner-violence/about/index.html):
"More than 1 in 3 women (nearly 43.5 million) and more than 1 in 6 men (20.7 million) experienced
contact sexual violence, physical violence, and/or stalking by an intimate partner during their
lifetimes."[6] The NCVS, for 2024, put intimate partner violence at 2.7 victimizations per 1,000
people aged 12 and over.[1]

Those can look like a contradiction, and they are not. The CDC figure is a lifetime prevalence: the
share of people who have ever experienced any of a broad set of harms, including stalking, over
their whole lives so far. The NCVS figure is a one-year rate of victimizations, for a narrower set of
crimes, from a survey that asks about crime. You cannot subtract one from the other, and you can't use
the pair to say whether anything is rising or falling.

## The US argument since 2020

Whether US crime rose or fell after 2020 became a political argument, and the two measures you've met
are at the centre of it. This course teaches how to read them and takes no side on whose account
was right.

The two BJS sources show this. The NCVS rate of all violent victimization was 16.4 per 1,000
in 2020 and 23.3 in 2024, higher than 2020 and 2021 and similar to 2022 and 2023.[1] In the Two
Crime Measures brief, the survey's narrower measure rose from 5.6 per 1,000 in 2021 to 9.8 in 2022
and has not changed significantly since, while the police-data rate stayed between 3.7 and 4.0 per
1,000 across the whole decade.[3] The FBI's figures also showed murder falling an estimated 14.9%
from 2023 to 2024.[2]

The Council on Criminal Justice, which describes itself as independent and nonpartisan, examined the
gap in its report [*When Crime Statistics Diverge*](https://counciloncj.org/when-crime-statistics-diverge/). Its
key finding is about the long run: "The nation’s two major tools for measuring crime, the UCR and
the NCVS, show similar long-term trends for rates of serious violent crime, motor vehicle theft, and
burglary." It adds that "Much of the difference between the two sources stems from variations in
methodology, rather than inaccuracies in the data." It also records that the gap between the two
sources' estimates for 2021 to 2022, a 2% fall in the FBI's figures against a 75% rise in the
survey's, "became a heated point of political contention", and that the argument intensified in
autumn 2024, when the FBI revised earlier figures "without explanation".[4]

The honest summary: over the long run the two sources agree; over a few recent years they have pointed different ways, and that
gap became a political argument; and both agencies describe the two as different tools with
different strengths, not as a true figure and a false one.[2][3][4] As lesson 1 showed, how people
read the national trend also follows their politics, for supporters of both parties.[11]

So if one measure next year shows violence up and the other flat, and someone says the flat one is
rigged, rule out the ordinary things first: different offences, periods or denominators, a change
that isn't significant, a new questionnaire, or a change in recording. That shows what evidence
would settle the claim, not that it is wrong.

## Checking a headline yourself

Everything in this lesson comes down to five questions. Ask them of any crime figure before you let it
change what you do.

1. **Who counted?** A police count or a victim survey, and which one.
2. **What did they count?** Which offences, defined how, and whether it's victimizations, incidents,
   offences or people.
3. **Whose harm, and where?** Which population and which geography: people 12 and over in US
   households, adults 16 and over in England and Wales, all residents, a named city.
4. **When?** Which period, and whether it is one year, a lifetime or a comparison between years.
5. **Per how many?** The denominator, and whether the figure is a count, a rate or a percentage.

If a figure cannot answer all five, it can't carry much weight. If it compares two years, ask the
extra question from the last two sections: did the recording, the questions or the survey change in
between?

You can check a claim yourself with the sources' own tools. For the US, BJS runs the
[NCVS Dashboard](https://ncvs.bjs.ojp.gov), which gives rates by crime, year and group, and the FBI
runs the [Crime Data Explorer](https://cde.ucr.cjis.gov). For England and Wales, ONS publishes *Crime
in England and Wales* every quarter. BJS and the FBI publish each autumn, so a number in this lesson
may already have a newer edition.

Now go back to the headline you started with, *Violent crime jumps 20% in our area*, and put the
five questions to it. Who counted? If it's a police count, did the force change how it records
anything? What was counted: which offences, and are they the ones you'd meet on that walk home?
Whose harm, and where: the whole area, or the streets you use? When: one year against the last, or a
run of years? Per how many: is 20% a rise from a small number, say from 50 recorded offences to 60,
where one busy weekend could do it?

If the headline can't answer those, it isn't on its own a reason to change your walk home, or your
son's. If it's a police count rising over several years in the offences you'd actually meet on that
walk, it's worth reading lessons 4 to 7 with it in mind. That reading is sensible, untested, in this
course's labels: it tells you how much weight a number can bear, not what to do. What you might
sensibly change, and what the evidence says about each option, is the subject of those lessons.

## What people get wrong

**"Police figures are the real numbers."** They are a precise count of crime that was reported and
recorded, which misses most crime. The UK's statistics regulator lists a version of this as a
misunderstanding.[9]

**"Survey figures are just opinion."** A victim survey asks people what happened to them, not what
they think about crime, and it catches crime nobody reported. Its weaknesses are sampling error and
response rates, which are measured and published.[1][9]

**"Recorded crime went up, so crime went up."** A change in recording can move the count with no
change in crime. HMIC's 2014 audit found a third of reported violence going unrecorded, and ONS now
calls police figures a better indicator of police activity than of crime trends.[7][8]

**"One year's figure is the trend."** A single year can mislead. A change can come from a new
question or questionnaire (the anti-social behaviour figure, the NCVS redesign), and even a change
that passes a significance test can rest on so few interviews that its size is very uncertain (the
rape and robbery reporting rows).[1][5][7]

**"A lifetime figure and an annual figure are the same kind of number."** One is the share of people
who have ever experienced something; the other is how often it happened in one year. They cannot be
compared to find a trend.[1][6]

**"The two US measures contradict each other, so neither can be trusted."** They measure different
things, agree over the long run, and each is the better source for some questions.[2][3][4]
