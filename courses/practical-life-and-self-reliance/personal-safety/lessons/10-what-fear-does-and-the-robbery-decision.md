Most people have wondered, at least once, what they would do if someone stepped out of a doorway
and demanded their bag. The honest answer is that nobody knows in advance, and part of the reason is
that the person who meets that moment isn't quite the person who wondered about it. A sudden threat
changes what the body does, what the eyes take in and what memory keeps, before any decision is
made. This lesson takes those two things in order: first what fear does to you, as far as the
research can say, and then the decision people argue about most, whether to hand property over or
to resist.

On a 999 call from a mobile, if you can't speak, cough or tap if you can and press 55 when you're prompted; in the US you can text 911 where that's available, but call if you can. Responsibility for a crime lies with the person who commits it: precautions change the odds, not the blame. The law differs between England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.

That responsibility sentence carries more weight here than anywhere else in the course. A person who
was robbed, and froze, or handed everything over, or fought, didn't cause what happened to them, and
none of those responses was the wrong one. This lesson describes what the evidence says happens on
average across thousands of incidents. It never grades what one person did in one moment, and if
you've been in that moment, nothing below is a verdict on it.

## What a threat does to the body

The fullest single account this course found is a 2015 review in the *Harvard Review of Psychiatry*
by Kozlowska and colleagues, [*Fear and the Defense Cascade*](https://doi.org/10.1097/HRP.0000000000000065).
This course read its abstract. It describes a set of defence responses that it calls innate and
automatically activated, and it puts them in an order: "Arousal is the first step ...
flight or fight is an active defense response ... freezing is a flight-or-fight response put on
hold; tonic immobility and collapsed immobility are responses of last resort to inescapable
threat".[1]

So on this account there are two very different kinds of stillness. Freezing comes early, while
fight or flight is still possible, and is held back from them. Tonic immobility, a state in which the
body is unable to move for a while, comes last, when there is no escape from the threat. Lesson 11, "Sexual
assault: the freeze, resistance and the law", comes back to tonic immobility, because the research on it
was done in that setting.

The idea people most often get wrong is that freezing is a failure: the body giving up, or the
person being weak. Roelofs, reviewing the animal and human research in the
[*Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2016.0206) in
2017, puts it the other way round.

:::predict When an animal or a person freezes in the face of a threat, do you think the heart speeds up, as it does when getting ready to run or fight, or slows down?

In the animal research Roelofs reviews, it slows. The abstract describes freezing in animals as behavioural inhibition that goes with a slowing of the heart, driven by the parasympathetic nervous system, where fight or flight goes with a faster heart. And: "Freezing is not a passive state but rather a parasympathetic brake on the motor system, relevant to perception and action preparation."[2] A brake, on this account, not a breakdown: the body holds still while it takes in the situation and gets ready to act.
:::

Roelofs adds a caution that belongs beside that sentence whenever it is quoted. Most of the work is
on animals, and in humans freezing's "phenomenology and neurobiological underpinnings remain
largely unexplored".[2] The abstract reports that brain imaging suggests similar brain regions may be
involved in people, and that being able to shift between freezing and active defence matters for
coping with stress. Nor does it claim that human freezing works exactly as a rat's does. So the fair
summary is: freezing is automatic, it has a job, and how it works in people is still
being worked out.

### Names that are proposals

You may have met longer lists. Bracha, in *CNS Spectrums* in 2004, argued that freeze, flight,
fight, fright, faint gives "a more complete description of the human acute stress response
sequence" than the accounts before it.[3] That's a proposal about the order and the names, made in an
article whose main argument is about fainting at the sight of blood, and this course read only its
abstract. Taylor and colleagues, in *Psychological Review* in 2000, proposed that "behaviorally,
females' responses are more marked by a pattern of 'tend-and-befriend'", while fight or flight may
describe the physiological response in both sexes.[4] That's a theory too, and the journal later
published a critical comment on it. "Fawn", often added to fight, flight and freeze,
comes from clinical writing about trauma, not from experiments, as the research behind this course
found; this course doesn't teach it as a finding.

None of this makes the proposals wrong. It means they are still proposals. Put side by side, the sources
this course read support three things well: going still under sudden threat is automatic, not
chosen; threat changes what people see, hear and remember; and it makes practised skills less
reliable. The exact sequence, the names for each stage, and whether women and men differ, are still
argued over.

One thing the sources here leave out is how often people freeze in a robbery or a street attack.
Nobody this course read has counted it. The one frequency figure the course has is for tonic
immobility, from a study of women seen at one clinic after rape, and lesson 11 gives it there, in
its own setting, rather than stretching it to every kind of threat.

:::checkpoint A colleague on a first-aid refresher says: "The science is clear. The stress response is freeze, flight, fight, fright, faint, in that order." How would you answer, in two sentences?

Something like: that sequence is one researcher's proposal, from a 2004 article that argued it describes the response more completely than older accounts; nobody has established it as the order everyone follows. What is well supported is simpler: freezing is automatic rather than chosen, and senses, memory and skill all behave differently under threat.

If your answer said the five Fs were wrong, that goes too far the other way. A proposal can turn out right. What it cannot yet do is carry the words "the science is clear".
:::

## What happens to the senses and to memory

The best-known figures on this come from police officers, because shootings are investigated in
detail and officers can be asked afterwards. Artwohl, a police psychologist, gave a written survey to
157 officers who had been involved in shootings at several agencies, and reported the results in an
article first published in the FBI's *Law Enforcement Bulletin* in 2002 and
[updated in 2008](https://www.aele.org/law/2008FPJUN/wb-19.pdf).[5]

:::predict Three of the things officers were asked about were hearing sounds less than normal, seeing a narrower field than normal, and losing memory of part of the event. Which do you think the most officers reported, and roughly what share?

Hearing less. Artwohl's results include "diminished sound 84%", "tunnel vision 79%" and "memory loss for part of the event 52%".[5] "Diminished sound" in her definition runs from not hearing gunshots and sirens at all to hearing them muffled and distant.
:::

Those numbers need their caveats, and Artwohl supplies most of them herself. It was a voluntary
written survey, not an experiment. The officers she surveyed herself, about two thirds of the total, were asked to fill it in only
after a group debriefing, where each officer's version of events was set against the others and
against the evidence, so they may have learned there that their memories were incomplete. The same survey found that 21% said they
had seen, heard or experienced something that didn't really happen. And after comparing her figures
with earlier studies, she wrote that "the exact percentages don't matter": what matters, in her
account, is that studies using different methods keep finding that perception and memory are
distorted in these events.[5]

The step from armed officers to a person at a cash machine is this course's, not Artwohl's. The
officers were trained, armed and in a situation their job had prepared them for. What carries over,
on this course's reading, is the direction rather than the numbers: under sudden threat, expect to
see less of your surroundings, to hear less, and to remember the event in pieces.

Lesson 9 made a promise about this. Theo, in the queue in Albany, was told that nobody should expect
a perfect description of themselves. This is why. If you once gave an account of something
frightening and later found it differed from a recording, or remembered a detail differently a week
on, the research describes the same thing happening to trained professionals. It is not lying, and
it is no sign that anything else is wrong with you.

## What happens to skill and judgement

Two small experiments with Dutch police officers separate two things that often get lumped together:
doing a practised movement, and deciding what to do.

In the first, [published in 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3142543/), Nieuwenhuys
and Oudejans had 27 officers shoot at an opponent who either shot back with coloured soap cartridges
or did not. Under that threat, accuracy fell for everyone at first. One group then trained with the
opponent shooting back; the other trained without. Afterwards, "shot accuracy of the experimental
group no longer deteriorated under anxiety", and the gain was still there four months later. The
authors' own conclusion is hedged: they say training with anxiety may have positive effects in
both the short and the long term.[6]

In the second, [published in 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2014.12.006), Nieuwenhuys and
colleagues tested 57 officers on a video test of whether to shoot at a suspect who appeared with or
without a gun, with a device that could fire small plastic bullets at them switched on or off. Under
threat, officers made more mistakes of shooting at a suspect who held no gun. After three weeks of
practice the mistakes "persisted", and the authors concluded that the effect of threat on these
decisions "may be hard to prevent within the limits of available practice".[7] This course read both
abstracts only.

Put together, and it is the research behind this course that puts them together, not the authors:
practice under realistic pressure helped a practised movement hold up, and did much less for a
split-second decision. Neither result came from reading about it. Both groups were police officers
with firearms, which is a long way from anyone reading this lesson, so treat it as a pointer rather
than a measurement of you. Lesson 12, "Resistance training, and choosing a class", takes up what
that means for a class.

One more thing is missing on purpose. Self-defence and police training often teach that above a
particular heart rate, fine movements or clear thinking fail at a set point. The research behind this
course found no peer-reviewed study that checks those particular thresholds, so this course leaves
them out.

### Deciding before the moment

If judgement is the part that threat damages most and practice repairs least, one response is to
make some decisions in advance, so that fewer are left for the moment itself. That's this course's
inference from the 2015 result, not something any study here tested: nobody has measured whether a
person who decides ahead of time what they would do if someone demanded their phone does better than
one who has not. In this course's labels, sensible, untested. The exercise at the end asks you to
write one such decision, for property, with its source.

## Hand it over, or resist?

Lesson 8 ended with four signals that talking has stopped being the right tool, and the fourth was a
demand for your property, or for you. This is the rung after that. It's also a question on which
careful researchers disagree, so in this course's labels it is contested, and this section gives each
side in its own words before saying what they share.

One thing first, which neither side disputes. This is general information, not legal advice. The law
differs by state, and between England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. The research behind
this course found that the law in neither country requires a victim of robbery to hand property
over, or to resist. Lesson 13, "The law on using force", sets out what the law does say about force.

### The case for handing it over

The official advice this course read all points one way. The Metropolitan Police's page on
[street robbery](https://web.archive.org/web/20250812180728/https://www.met.police.uk/cp/crime-prevention/personal-safety-how-to-stay-safe/mugging-street-robbery/)
ends: "if you're threatened with violence, don't risk your personal safety. Property can be replaced,
you can't."[9] The Metropolitan Police Department in Washington, DC, in its brochure
[*Guarding Against Robbery and Assault*](https://mpdc.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/mpdc/publication/attachments/robbery_assault.pdf),
says, under a heading about someone trying to rob you: "Don't resist." Its next words tell you to give
up your property, not your life. On carjacking the brochure is blunter still: "If the carjacker threatens you
with a gun or other weapon, GIVE UP YOUR CAR!"[10] And the US workplace regulator's
[guidance for late-night shops](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/osha3153.pdf),
which lesson 9 quoted, tells employers to train staff to hand over money without resistance.[11]

Three studies stand behind that advice.

**A convenience store cohort.** Faulkner, Landsittel and Hendricks followed 460 robberies of
convenience stores in the US over 1995 and 1996, using police reports, interviews with employees,
evaluations of the stores and census data, and published in the
[*American Journal of Industrial Medicine*](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajim.10011) in 2001. "Injury
risk was strongly associated with ... employee resistance". The abstract lists resistance alongside
several other things linked to injury, among them robberies without firearms, stores with limited
escape routes and older clerks, and it notes "Numerous intercorrelations between these
characteristics".[12] The abstract is silent on whether the injury came before or after the resistance.

**A Home Office study of police records.** Smith's
[*The nature of personal robbery*](https://library.college.police.uk/docs/hors/hors254.pdf), Home
Office Research Study 254, published in 2003, went through nearly 2,000 police crime reports and
witness statements from seven police areas in England. "One in four robberies involved resistance by
the victim in the form of a physical struggle". "Two out of every five (43%) personal robberies
resulted in injury of some sort". And: "Female victims were more likely to resist their attacker and,
probably as a consequence, more likely to sustain some physical injury".[13] Notice "probably": the
link from resisting to injury is the author's inference from the records, not a sequence the
records showed. Smith also found that "robberies involving the use of weapons were less likely to
result in physical injury to the victim", and he says himself that his sample may not be
representative.[13]

**A survey of stranger crimes.** Block and Skogan, reporting to the US National Institute of Justice in
1985, analysed rapes, robberies and assaults by strangers in the National Crime Survey from 1973 to
1979. This course read the [abstract](https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/dynamics-violence-between-strangers-victim-resistance-and-outcomes),
not the report. "Generally, nonforceful resistance was related to more favorable victim outcomes.
Victims who resisted by screaming, running away, reasoning with their attackers, or other nonforceful
means were less likely to be injured". The abstract names its own limits: the survey "excludes crimes
in which the victim dies, and it does not obtain data on the sequencing of victim and offender
actions".[14] This one sits in the middle, closer to "don't fight" than to "do nothing".

Put in its proponents' terms, the case runs like this. A robber wants property and wants it fast. A
victim who hands it over gives him both, and the encounter ends. The people most at risk of being
killed in a robbery may be those who resist, and that is the one outcome a survey of survivors can
never record. Property can be replaced. And the police in both countries, and the US workplace
regulator, all say so.

### The case that resistance often helps

The other side rests mainly on the US National Crime Victimization Survey, reanalysed with more care
about which came first.

Tark and Kleck, in *Criminology* in 2004, looked at 27,595 incidents of personal contact crime in the
survey from 1992 to 2001, and sixteen kinds of self-protection. This course read only
[their abstract](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-9125.2004.tb00539.x). Its central finding:
"self‐protection in general, both forceful and nonforceful, reduced the likelihood of property loss
and injury, compared to nonresistance." They explain why earlier research seemed to show the
opposite: "The appearance, in past research, of resistance contributing to injury was found to be
largely attributable to confusion concerning the sequence of SP actions and injury. In crimes where
both occurred, injury followed SP in only 10 percent of the incidents." Adding that injuries after
resistance were almost always relatively minor, they conclude: "victim resistance appears to be
generally a wise course of action."[15] (SP is their abbreviation for self-protection.) The abstract
also lists resistance with a gun among the tactics that appeared to cut the risk of injury most, and says "some of
the findings were unstable due to the small numbers of sample cases"; lesson 14 deals with guns.

Guerette and Santana, in [*Crime & Delinquency*](https://doi.org/10.1177/0011128707311644), used the
same survey from 1992 to 2004 for robbery and rape. "Greater levels of victim resistance increase the
effort needed by offenders, resulting in some cases in a 93% and 92% decrease in the odds of a
robbery and rape being completed, respectively, compared to when no resistance is used."[16] Two
things about that sentence. The outcome is whether the crime was completed, not whether anyone was
hurt. And it comes with a mechanism, which the authors draw from opportunity theory: resistance
raises the effort a crime takes. That's the same idea lesson 4 met in the situational prevention
grid, under Increase the Effort, applied in the moment rather than in advance.

A police guide adds one more figure. Monk, Heinonen and Eck's
[*Street Robbery*](https://popcenter.asu.edu/content/street-robbery-0), a problem-oriented policing
guide from 2010, says "using protective measures to resist robbery helped over half of U.S. victims
in 2006, while aggravating the incident in less than 8 percent of cases."[17] The figure comes from
a US government statistic that this course did not check, and "helped" is what victims said
afterwards, which is a weak measure.

In its proponents' terms: once you know whether injury came before or after resistance, resistance
rarely turns out to have caused it. Doing nothing doesn't buy safety when an attacker has already
used force. And resistance, especially running and shouting, often ends the crime.

### The flaw both sides share

Look down the last two columns and the problem is plain. Neither side has what would settle it.

**The dead aren't in the surveys.** Lesson 2 showed that the US crime survey cannot count homicide at
all, because, in BJS's words, the survey "is based on interviews with victims".[18] Every survey
figure above is about people who lived. If resisting occasionally gets someone killed, that outcome
is missing from all of them. The one record that does count killings in England and Wales, the
Home Office Homicide Index, found that "Furtherance of theft or gain accounted for 5% of homicides (27
offences)" in the year ending March 2025,[19] but that figure says nothing about what those victims
did, so it cannot settle the question either.

**People choose when to resist.** Nobody is randomly assigned to fight back. On this course's
reading, which follows the research behind it, people tend to resist where it looks survivable: an unarmed robber, open
space, a way out. If so, resisters would look better off partly because of where they resisted, not
because of resisting. And the pull runs the other way too: someone who is already being hit may
fight back because they're being hit, which makes resisters look worse off. Smith's records show
one form of this: in what he calls blitz robberies, the offender uses violence first, to control or
stun the victim, and only then takes the property.[13]

**The order is rarely known.** Only one study in the table knew it, and it is the one that disagrees
with the others.

**No trial is possible.** Nobody can randomise victims to comply or resist.

So both bodies of evidence are honest, both are partial, and the disagreement between them is, on
this course's reading, mostly about what each could see. That's this course's question again,
measured on whom and measured as what: surveys of survivors, police records, one chain of shops.

### Where they agree

There is one case where the argument stops. When a weapon is shown and all that is wanted is property,
every official source this course read says hand it over. There are three of them, the Met, the DC
police and OSHA's guidance to shops, and each goes further than that one case: the Met's condition is
being threatened with violence, and the DC brochure says not to resist a robbery at all.[9][10][11] And the resistance studies' abstracts don't argue against it. None of them
reports that case on its own, Guerette and Santana's measure is whether the property was lost, which
that advice has already accepted, and Smith's records found injury less likely when a weapon was
involved.[13][16] That the resistance evidence leaves this case alone is this course's reading of
the abstracts, not a claim any of the authors made.

In this course's labels, the comply-or-resist question is contested, and this narrow case is where
the contest stops.

## At the cash machine

The first case, worked through. Sanjay is using a cash machine set into a bank wall in Derby, in
England, at about eleven at night. Two men come up, one on each side. One holds a knife low, by his
leg, and says he wants Sanjay's wallet and phone. Sanjay's back is to the machine and the men are
either side of him, so walking away is not open to him.

Nothing about the time or the place makes this his doing, and the case is written forward: what is
this, and what does the evidence say?

**What kind of case it is.** A weapon is shown, and what is wanted is property. This is the narrow
case. The Met's advice fits it exactly: property can be replaced.[9] The resistance research,
read above, doesn't argue with it.[15][16]

**What his body is likely to do.** His heart may race. He may go completely still for a second or
two before he can move or speak. On Kozlowska's and Roelofs' accounts, that stillness is the body
holding fight or flight on hold and getting ready, not a failure.[1][2] He may see the knife in sharp
detail and almost nothing else, and the men's words may sound far away. That's the direction of
Artwohl's findings in officers, carried across by this course.[5]

**What happens next.** However he responds, the robbers are responsible for what happens. Afterwards
there will be a call to make, a report if he wants one, and support whether or not he reports:
lesson 15, "Afterwards", covers all of it. And what people often feel in the days after a
frightening event is Mental Fitness lesson 7's subject.

:::checkpoint Your turn. Suppose that in the same moment, Sanjay had pushed one man away and run instead. Write, in three short sentences: what the resistance research would say about that, what its limits are for his one case, and what this course says about his choice.

The resistance research, on Tark and Kleck's abstract, found that self-protection of both kinds reduced injury and property loss on average across thousands of survey incidents, and that injury followed resistance in only 10% of the incidents where both occurred.[15] Its limits: those are averages from survivors who chose when to resist, and nothing in them can say what would have happened to him, with a knife shown, that night.

And this course says what it says about handing things over: he didn't choose wrongly. Nobody who complied or resisted did. The men with the knife are responsible for what happened, whatever he did.

If your third sentence said he took a risk he shouldn't have, look again at the flaw both sides share. The evidence that would show that does not exist.
:::

## Property or the person

Everything so far is about a demand for things. When the demand is for you, to go somewhere with
someone, to get into a car, to be hurt or sexually assaulted, what the sources say changes.

The official robbery advice this course read is about property: give up the bag, the phone, the car.
None of those pages gives advice for being told to go with someone, and the DC brochure's line about
a person being forced into a car is about lowering the chance of it, not about what to do.[10] The
research that does bear on it is the resistance research. Block and Skogan's abstract found that
non-forceful resistance, screaming, running away or reasoning with the attacker, went with better
outcomes on average in stranger crimes, rape among them.[14] Tark and Kleck found that self-protection
in general reduced injury.[15] And lesson 11 sets out the evidence on resistance to rape, which, as
the research behind this course summarises it, points the same way, while its freeze research shows
how often the body holds still instead.

That is as far as any source goes. It's an average across thousands of incidents, drawn from survivors,
and it cannot tell one person in one moment what to do. What it does change is the starting point. In
the narrow case, the sources and the official advice agree on handing property over. When the
demand is for the person, they no longer do.

## In the parking garage

The second case has a wrinkle. Denise is on the fourth level of a parking garage in Wilmington,
Delaware, early one evening, unlocking her car. A man she has never seen steps between her and the
stairwell, says he has a gun in his pocket, and tells her to get into his car, which is parked beside
hers. The elevator is behind him too. She can't simply walk away.

The case is written forward, and it has no ending, on purpose.

**What kind of case it is.** Nothing he has asked for is property. The demand is for her. So the
narrow case, and the agreement that comes with it, does not apply.

**What the sources can say.** No official page this course read gives advice for this moment. The
resistance research, with the rape research lesson 11 sets out, points on average towards
resisting, with non-forceful resistance among the options it names: screaming, running,
talking.[14][15] It cannot say
which of those, if any, would work here, or whether his gun is real.

**What her body may do.** Exactly what this lesson's first half described. She may freeze. She may
find herself unable to speak, or to think past the next second. None of that is a choice, and none of it
would make what happens next her doing.

**Why no page can tell her what to do.** Every piece of evidence above is an average from people who
survived, and the moment in front of her is one case. Whatever she does, the man is responsible for
it. Lesson 12 is about the classes that practise options like these under pressure, and what the
evidence says they can and can't do.

## What people get wrong

**"Resisting a robber always gets you hurt"**, and its mirror, **"resisting is always safer"**. Each
side's own best studies stop short of either. Smith's "probably as a consequence" and Tark and
Kleck's 10% are both about averages, and both sides share the flaw that the dead and the order of
events are mostly missing.[13][15]

**"In an emergency you'll rise to the occasion."** Trained police officers' accuracy fell under
threat until they practised under threat, and their split-second decisions got worse under threat
and stayed worse after three weeks' practice.[6][7] Nobody should count on doing better than they
have practised, and a page isn't practice.

**"Freezing is cowardice."** Freezing is part of the defence responses Kozlowska and colleagues
describe as automatic, and Roelofs calls it a brake on movement that goes with taking in the scene
and preparing to act.[1][2] It is not chosen, and it is not giving up.

**"You'll remember exactly what happened."** In Artwohl's survey, 52% of officers lost memory for
part of the event and 21% recalled something that hadn't happened.[5] Memory after a threat comes in
pieces, and that is no sign of lying.

**"Above a certain heart rate, your fine motor skills fail at a set number."** The research behind
this course found no peer-reviewed validation of those thresholds, so the course prints none.
