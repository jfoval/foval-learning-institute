## You decide

Two sentences from the services set the rules for everything below. Refuge, which runs the
National Domestic Abuse Helpline, says on its
[helpline page](https://refuge.org.uk/i-need-help-now/how-we-can-help-you/national-domestic-abuse-helpline/):
"We will never tell you what to do."[4] The US Hotline's
[page on guns](https://www.thehotline.org/resources/safety-planning-around-guns-and-firearms/) says
that "you are the expert in your situation", and that you are the best person to decide what will
keep you safest.[14]

So this lesson describes; it doesn't direct. It does not tell anyone to stay or to go, and it never says "just leave". If you are
helping someone else, the same rule holds for you, and the last part of the lesson is about how.

Lessons 4 to 6 were about lowering the odds at home, on the street and when travelling. None of
that is a plan for living with someone who frightens you, and nothing here suggests the person
being harmed caused it or could have prevented it. Refuge says on
["What is domestic abuse?"](https://refuge.org.uk/what-is-domestic-abuse/) that abuse "is never the
fault of the person who is experiencing it, and it is a crime."[15]

One label, said once. Most of what the services advise is, in this course's labels, practitioner
opinion: guidance from their work that no study the course read has tested. The lesson marks it
where it matters rather than after every sentence.

## What the services call abuse

:::predict Before you read on: does abuse by a partner, as the domestic abuse services themselves define it, have to involve being hit or sexually assaulted?

No. Refuge says so in as many words: "A common myth is that it's only domestic abuse if it involves physical or sexual violence. In fact, many women experience domestic abuse without ever being physically harmed."[15] Both services, UK and US, define abuse by what it is for, which is control, not by any one act.
:::

Refuge's page opens with a definition: "Domestic abuse (sometimes called 'domestic violence' or
'intimate partner violence') is an incident or a pattern of behaviour that is used by someone to
control or obtain power over their partner or ex-partner."[15] The US Hotline's is close to it:
"Domestic violence (also referred to as intimate partner violence (IPV), dating abuse, or
relationship abuse) is a pattern of behaviors used by one partner to maintain power and control over
another partner in an intimate relationship."[1]

Two words carry both definitions. **Pattern** means the harm is in the repetition, so a single
incident can look small when the whole is not. **Control** means the question isn't whether any one
act was violent, but what the acts, taken together, are for.

Each service then lists the forms it sees, and the two lists are not the same, so here they are
separately, each in its own words. Refuge names six: "Emotional abuse, including gaslighting /
Coercive control / Physical abuse / Tech abuse / Economic abuse / Sexual abuse".[15] The
Hotline's [types of abuse](https://www.thehotline.org/resources/types-of-abuse/) page names nine:
physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, financial abuse, technology-facilitated abuse, sexual
coercion, reproductive coercion, stalking and spiritual abuse. It adds that "Multiple forms of abuse
are usually present at the same time in abusive situations".[22]

Emotional abuse, on the Hotline's page, is "non-physical behaviors that are meant to control,
isolate, or frighten someone", and its examples include "Monitoring your activities with or without
your knowledge, including demanding to know where you go, who you contact, and how you spend your
time."[22] Its list also covers cutting someone off from family and friends, threats against
children or pets, and blaming the person for the abuse.[22]

### Coercive control

Refuge gives coercive control its own [page](https://refuge.org.uk/i-need-help-now/coercive-control/).
It describes it as an act, or a pattern of acts, of assault, threats, humiliation and intimidation
that an abuser uses to harm, punish or frighten, and says it is a purposeful pattern rather than a
single incident.[23] Because it can be hard to see from inside, the page gives questions instead of
a definition to test against. Among them: does your partner isolate you from friends and family,
monitor how you spend your time, keep track of what you do online, control when you sleep, where
you go, who you see and what you wear, keep control of your money, or stop you getting to work or
using transport?[23]

Refuge's helpline site has a shorter test still: "Anyone forced to change their behaviour because
they are frightened of their partner or ex-partner's reaction is experiencing abuse."[4]

Refuge, an English charity whose helpline GOV.UK lists for England, also says on its coercive control
page that controlling or coercive behaviour in an intimate or family relationship is a crime and that
perpetrators can be prosecuted.[23][5] It does not say which law, and this course didn't read the
statutes in England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland or any US state, so it cannot tell you
whether or how that applies where you live. This is general information, not legal advice. The law
differs by state, and between England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland.

### Who it happens to

Refuge: "Domestic abuse can happen to anyone, regardless of age, background, gender identity, sex,
religion, sexual orientation or ethnicity. However, statistics show most domestic abuse is carried
out by men and experienced by women."[15] Its helpline is for women, and it says so, then
adds: "We recognise that men can experience domestic abuse too."[4] The Hotline's page
says that anyone can be a victim, or a perpetrator.[1] Both halves of Refuge's sentence are
true at once, and the figures below bear both out.

## How common it is

In England and Wales, the crime survey estimated that 7.8% of adults aged 16 and over had
experienced domestic abuse in the year ending March 2025, about 3.8 million people.[24] By sex, that
was 9.1% of women and 6.5% of men, about 2.2 million women and 1.5 million men, so roughly one man
in fifteen as well as one woman in eleven. Abuse by a partner or ex-partner (6.1% of adults) was more
common than abuse by a family member (2.7%).[34]

Those figures come from a part of the survey people fill in privately, and ONS explains why in its
[bulletin](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/bulletins/crimeinenglandandwales/yearendingmarch2026):
the face-to-face part of the survey, which it uses for other violence, "underestimates domestic
violence because victims may not disclose it to interviewers", so domestic abuse is "better captured
via a self-completion section".[24] The private questions give what ONS calls its preferred estimate.

One caution if you took lesson 3. Its domestic abuse figures, 6.6% of women and 3.0% of men for the
year ending March 2024, came from an older set of questions. ONS brought in new questions from that
year on and says the new and old estimates cannot be compared.[34] So lesson 3's numbers and these
are not the same measure, and the gap between them is not a rise.

:::checkpoint Lesson 1 gave 7.8% as the share of adults in England and Wales who were victims of fraud. A friend puts that beside this lesson's 7.8% and says, "So domestic abuse is exactly as common as fraud." Using lesson 2's labels, what's wrong with the sentence?

The two figures carry different labels. Fraud's 7.8% was for the year ending March 2026 and comes from the survey's main questions; domestic abuse's 7.8% was for the year ending March 2025 and comes from the private questions. They are different years and different measures, and the match is a coincidence. "About as common, on two different measures a year apart" is the most the pair supports.
:::

The police see a different slice. In the year ending March 2024, in data from 18 police forces in
England and Wales, 41% of suspects in violence against women were the women's intimate partners,
against 22% for violence against men.[25] That is police-recorded crime from part of the country, and
it is a share of suspects, not of people, so it does not line up with the survey's percentage of
adults. What it adds is the same direction from a second, independent source: for women, a partner
is a much larger share of the danger.

For killings, lesson 3's row from the Homicide Index is the one to remember: adult women killed in
England and Wales were most often killed by a partner or ex-partner. For the US, lesson 2 set the
CDC's lifetime figure (more than 1 in 3 women and more than 1 in 6 men, for a broad set of harms by
a partner, stalking included) beside the national crime survey's 2.7 victimizations per 1,000 people
aged 12 and over in 2024, and showed why the two measure different things.

## Stalking

:::predict The Stalking Prevention, Awareness, and Resource Center (SPARC) is a US project funded by the Justice Department's Office on Violence Against Women. If you ask someone being stalked, "Are you being stalked?", do you think they'll usually say yes?

Often not. SPARC writes that "victims are unlikely to use the words 'stalking' or 'harassment' when talking about their situation", and advises helpers to ask about specific behaviours instead.[26] That is worth knowing whichever side of the conversation you are on.
:::

The National Stalking Helpline is run by the Suzy Lamplugh Trust, and the trust's page on
[what stalking is](https://www.suzylamplugh.org/what-is-stalking) starts plainly: "Stalking is a
pattern of repeated, unwanted behaviour that causes you to feel distressed or scared. It can be
perpetrated by men or women."[27] Two things in it matter most. It does not need a threat:
"Stalking can happen with or without a fear of violence."[27] And it doesn't need anything
dramatic: "Stalking can consist of any type of behaviour such as regularly sending flowers or gifts,
making unwanted or malicious communication, damaging property and physical or sexual
assault."[27]

Who does it? The same page says that about 45% of the people who contact the helpline are being
stalked by ex-partners and a further third knew their stalker in some other way.[27] Read the
label on that: it is the helpline's callers, not everyone who is stalked, so it tells you who rings
this helpline and cannot be turned into a national share. The page adds that knowing the stalker,
now or in the past, doesn't make any of it the victim's fault.[27] Lesson 3 gave the survey's
figures for England and Wales in the year ending March 2024: 4.0% of women and 2.3% of men had been
stalked, and 16 to 19 year olds more than any other age group.

The Hotline counts stalking among its forms of abuse and defines it this way: "Stalking occurs when
someone watches, follows, or harasses you repeatedly, making you feel afraid or unsafe, and may
occur from someone you know, a past partner, or a stranger." Its examples include "Showing up at
your home or workplace unannounced or uninvited", "Using social media or technology to track your
activities" and "Waiting around at places you spend time."[22]

SPARC notes on its [page on stalking behaviours](https://www.stalkingawareness.org/stalking-behaviors/)
that "most stalkers engage in multiple tactics to scare their victims", and that "Stalkers' behaviors
can escalate from more indirect ways of making contact (like phone calls or texts) to more direct
contact (like delivering gifts or showing up where you are)."[26] Its page for victims suggests
keeping a record of each contact and saving messages and posts as evidence.[26] That is practitioner
opinion. And the warning at the top applies to it: if someone checks your phone or your things, a
record kept there could be found.

SPARC is also plain that it cannot help you directly: its page for victims says, in capitals, that it
does not provide direct services to victims of stalking. It asks people seeking help to reach out to
their local service provider first, and says domestic abuse and sexual assault services are often the
right people to contact about stalking even when it has nothing to do with a partner. For anyone who
doesn't know how to reach a local service, or wants more help, it points to VictimConnect, the
Hotline and RAINN.[26]

## Technology used to track and control

The Hotline defines technology-facilitated abuse as "the use of technology ... to bully, harass,
stalk, intimidate, or control a partner."[22] Refuge runs a separate site,
[Refuge Tech Safety](https://refugetechsafety.org/), which describes it as someone using technology
to harm or control you, and has step-by-step guides to securing phones, accounts and home
devices.[28]

Its page on [AirTags](https://refugetechsafety.org/airtag/) carries the line that matters most
before any of those steps: "depending on whether or not you are living with the person who is
harming you, you may choose to take different steps. Control and coercion make some of these steps
impossible or not safe."[28] The page does not say which steps, and this course will not guess.
Whether a step is safe depends on your situation, and the site leaves that choice with you. This
lesson links to the steps rather than copying them, because they change with each phone update;
that's this course's reason, not the services'.

Digital Literacy lesson 8 showed what a private window does: that browser does not keep the session's
history, cookies or form data on that device, and it reaches nothing beyond the device. That lesson
called it the right tool for one worry, someone later reading the history on a shared machine. When
the person who might look is the person frightening you, the Hotline's warning is wider: internet
use can be monitored and cannot be erased completely. If you're worried your internet use is being
monitored, the Hotline's advice is to call it instead.[1] Refuge's helpline site suggests leaving
and coming back on a safe device if you think someone might be monitoring yours.[4] The Men's Advice
Line says calls to it do not appear on itemised bills.[12] That is a statement about the bill; it says
nothing about what a phone itself shows.

## Kerry's question

Kerry lives in England. Her partner checks her phone most evenings, has started turning up at her
work at lunchtime without saying he is coming, and tells her which of her friends she can see. He has
never hit her. She isn't sure it "counts", and she has not told anyone.

**What the services describe.** Set her three facts beside their pages, one at a time. Checking her
phone and deciding who she sees are close to two of the Hotline's examples of emotional abuse:
monitoring someone's activities, including demanding to know where they go and who they contact,
and cutting them off from friends.[22] Turning up at her work uninvited is close to one of the
Hotline's examples of stalking, showing up at someone's workplace unannounced or uninvited. And
Refuge's coercive control questions ask whether a partner monitors how you spend your time and
controls who you see.[23]

**What the services do not do.** None of those pages gives a score, and none says "this counts" or
"this doesn't". Refuge's shorter test is a question only Kerry can answer: is she changing what she
does because she is frightened of his reaction? This course won't answer it for her either.

:::checkpoint Before reading on: suppose Kerry decides to ring Refuge's helpline. From what the service's own pages say, what is the call for, and what won't happen on it?

The page says an adviser will listen to whatever she wants to say, offer confidential information without judging her, and help her make a plan if she would like one. It also says the adviser will never tell her what to do. She does not have to be sure it "counts" before she rings: the page says she may not know yet what help she needs, and that calling is a place to start.[4]
:::

**The practical side.** Her partner checks her phone, so the warning at the top is about her.
Refuge's helpline site suggests a safe device if someone might be monitoring yours, and the Hotline
suggests calling rather than browsing if browsing may be watched.[1][4] Since he checks the phone
itself, this course reads Refuge's advice as covering a call or a chat just as much as a web page:
a phone or computer he cannot get at, at a time that's safe for her, whichever way she gets in
touch. The helpline is open all day and all night. Refuge's live chat runs weekdays
10am to 10pm and weekends 10am to 6pm, and lets her talk to an adviser in writing, without speaking
aloud. Refuge notes that when lines are busy, evenings, nights and weekends are often quieter.[4]

Every one of those is an option, not a step she ought to take. What she does next, including
nothing for now, is hers.

## Separation, and a study of killings

Lesson 3 found that separated people had the highest rate of violent victimisation in the US
survey's table, though it was also the least precise number there, and that neither source it read
explained why. It promised a study of killings by partners that comes at separation from another
direction. This is it.

In 2003 Campbell and colleagues published a
[study of femicide](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447915/), the killing of women, by
partners, in the *American Journal of Public Health*. In 11 US cities they interviewed people who
had known 220 women killed by a partner, the proxies, and compared their answers with interviews
with 343 abused women who had not been killed.[29] All were aged 18 to 50, and the women who were
killed had all been abused before by the man who killed them.[29] Notice who the comparison group
is. It is not women in general. It is women who were also being abused, so the study asks what
separates the most dangerous abusive relationships from other abusive ones.

:::predict The study compared abused women whose partner was highly controlling and who had separated from him after living together with abused women who had neither. Roughly how much higher were the odds of being killed for that combination: about 1.5 times, 3 times, 9 times or 30 times?

About 9 times, in the model where it first appeared. "The risk of intimate partner femicide was increased 9-fold by the combination of a highly controlling abuser and the couple's separation after living together (adjusted OR = 8.98; 95% CI = 3.25, 24.83)."[29] Each on its own went with a smaller rise: a highly controlling abuser without separation with an odds ratio of about 2.9, and separation after living together without a highly controlling abuser with about 3.1.[29] The next section shows why "about 9" is less settled than it sounds.
:::

The abstract lists the factors that stood up once the others were taken into account: the
"perpetrator's access to a gun and previous threat with a weapon, perpetrator's stepchild in the
home, and estrangement, especially from a controlling partner."[29] Notice whose
characteristics these are. Almost every one describes the abuser or the relationship. Two things
went the other way: "Never living together and prior domestic violence arrest were associated with
lowered risks."[29] Earlier threats mattered too: an abuser's previous threats to kill went with odds
about 2.6 times higher.[29]

Stalking, forced sex and abuse during pregnancy were each linked to higher risk when looked at one
at a time. Once the model included the abuser's earlier threats with a weapon and threats to kill,
stalking and abuse during pregnancy no longer stood out separately. The authors say those behaviours
tend to go with such threats, "factors that more closely predict intimate partner femicide
risks".[29] Forced sex came close to standing out even then (an odds ratio of 1.87, just short of
the usual test), and the authors say their way of handling "don't know" answers probably understated
factors like it.[29] None of that means stalking or forced sex did not matter. It means the threats carried
the signal in this study.

### What the numbers mean, and don't

An odds ratio of 8.98 isn't a nine-in-something chance of being killed. It compares how common a
factor was among the women who were killed with how common it was among the abused women who
were not, measured as odds; First Aid and CPR lesson 4 showed that odds are not percentages. The study
gives no absolute risk, and Logic and Argument lesson 9's warning applies: a relative figure without
its base tells you how much larger, not how large.

The interval matters as much as the headline. The 95% confidence interval runs from 3.25 to 24.83:
the data fit a multiplier as low as about three or as high as about twenty-five, and 8.98 is the
single best estimate inside that range. And the figure shrank when the study took more into account.
Once the abuser's earlier threats were added, the estimate for the combination fell to about 4, with
an interval from 1.33 to 12.4; in the authors' words the effect, "although substantially reduced,
remained strong".[29] The authors add a warning that runs the same way: the women in the most
dangerous situations may have been less likely to take part as comparison women, which would be
"inflating our estimates of increased risks".[29] The finding is strong that the combination goes
with higher risk, and weak on how much higher.

The paper names its own limits. "Perhaps the most important limitation of the study is its necessary
reliance on proxy respondents for data regarding hypothesized risk factors".[29] The women who
were killed could not be asked, so people who knew them answered, while the comparison women answered
for themselves. The study also left out women outside large cities, with one exception, and
comparison women without a telephone, so it's an urban sample, from the US, published more than
twenty years ago.[29]

And it is an association. Campbell's team measured which factors went with killings. The paper
does not say why separating from a controlling partner goes with danger, and this course will not guess.
Because it compares cases after the event, it also can't say when in a separation the danger is
greatest. The services say something close, from their own work rather than from this study. The
Hotline's page on guns says that ending an abusive relationship can be an extremely dangerous time,
especially if the partner has access to firearms.[14] The Men's Advice Line tells friends and family
that leaving is often a very dangerous time, when violence and abuse escalate, and that careful
planning is needed.[30]

:::checkpoint A friend reads about Campbell's study and says, "So the study shows that a woman who leaves a controlling partner has a nine-in-ten chance of being killed." Name the two things wrong with that sentence.

First, 8.98 is an odds ratio from a comparison with other abused women, not a probability. The study gives no absolute chance at all. Second, "leaves a controlling partner" drops half the finding: the combination was a highly controlling abuser and separation after living together, the interval runs from about three to about twenty-five, and the estimate fell to about four once earlier threats were counted. A sentence the study supports: among abused women aged 18 to 50 in 11 US cities, that combination went with much higher odds of being killed by the partner, by an amount the study could pin down only roughly.
:::

## Why "just leave" isn't advice

Three things are wrong with the phrase.

The first is Campbell's finding. Among the abused women in the study, separation after living
together went with higher odds of being killed, most of all with a highly controlling abuser. Having
ever left him or asked him to leave went with odds about three times higher (3.19) in one of the
study's earlier models, and where the worst incident of abuse (for the women who were killed, the killing
itself) was set off by her having left him, for a reason other than a new partner, the odds were
about four times higher (4.04).[29] Advice that treats leaving as the safe, simple answer leaves all of that out.

The second is the services' framing. The Hotline's definition of a safety plan, below, covers
safety "while experiencing abuse, preparing to leave an abusive situation, or after you leave"; it
does not assume any one of those.[31] Refuge's
[page for friends](https://refuge.org.uk/what-is-domestic-abuse/support-someone-i-know-who-is-being-abused/)
says not to tell a woman to leave or criticise her for staying, because she has to make that decision
in her own time, and calls leaving an abusive partner a process.[32] Among the reasons it gives why a
woman may not leave is that she "may be fearful of what the abuser will do to her and the children if
they leave or attempt to leave".[32] That is the service's account from its work, practitioner
opinion; the course read no research on why any particular person stays, and makes no claim about it.

The third is the one the lesson began with. "Just leave" is someone else deciding. Refuge's "We will
never tell you what to do" is a service's rule, and the Hotline gives the reason in its own words:
"you are the expert in your situation".[4][14]

None of that is an argument for staying, either. It is an argument for planning, with help, on the
person's own timing, which is what the services offer.

## What a safety plan is

The Hotline's [definition](https://www.thehotline.org/plan-for-safety/what-is-a-safety-plan/): "a
personalized, practical plan to improve your safety while experiencing abuse, preparing to leave an
abusive situation, or after you leave."[31] And its reason for making one in advance: "it can be hard
to think clearly or make logical decisions during moments of crisis."[31] That's the Hotline's
reason, and it is the only one the course has.

Both the Hotline and Refuge will make the plan with you. The Hotline's page invites you to contact it
at any hour to have an advocate help you prepare a personal plan, and Refuge's adviser will, in its
words on the page, help you make a plan if you would like.[31][4] There is a practical side to that, and
it's this course's point rather than the services': a plan made on the phone with an adviser
does not have to exist on paper or on a device where it could be found.

So this lesson does not describe a plan, and none of its exercises asks you to write one. The Hotline
also has an interactive planner on its site. This course did not open it, but the page that links to
it says the finished plan can be read online, printed, or sent as a link to your email that works for
24 hours.[31] A printout or an email is something another person could find, so the warning at the
top of this lesson applies to it too, and the Hotline will make a plan with you by phone instead. The
same page links to the Hotline's guides for staying, preparing to leave, leaving and afterwards.

Safety planning is practitioner opinion in this course's labels: the services teach it from their
work, and the course read no study that measured it.

### Guns, and one line on a protective order

Campbell's list includes the abuser's access to a gun. The Hotline's page on guns says that getting
a protective order, a kind of court order whose name and rules vary by state, can in many US states
require an abusive partner to turn over their guns.[14] This is general information, not legal
advice. The law differs by state, and between England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. The
separate question of a gun kept by the person being abused is one people disagree about, and this
lesson leaves it to lesson 14, "The home, property, and carrying something for protection", where the
course takes up guns for protection.

### Not a job for de-escalation

Lesson 8, "Talking it down", teaches ways to calm a tense encounter with a stranger or at work. None
of it is advice for an abusive relationship. Offered as a plan for living with a partner who
frightens you, it would put the work of controlling the abuse on the person being harmed. That's this
course's judgement from the difference between the two situations, not a finding, and it is no comment
on anything anyone does to get through a day with someone who frightens them.

## The right line, for you or someone else

The right line depends on the caller as much as the nation, so here is who each says it is for.

- **For women:** Refuge's helpline is for women experiencing any kind of abuse, and for friends,
  family and colleagues supporting someone.[4] GOV.UK's own list puts it under England.[5]
- **For men:** the Men's Advice Line is "for men including anyone who identifies as trans and
  non-binary, who are experiencing or have experienced domestic abuse, and for those supporting
  them". It adds: "No one has to prove that they are a victim to get a service."[12]
- **For anyone:** Live Fear Free in Wales serves "anyone experiencing domestic abuse or sexual
  violence", and people who know someone who needs help.[7] Scotland's helpline says it is "here to
  support anyone experiencing domestic abuse or forced marriage, as well as their family members,
  friends, colleagues and professionals who support them."[8] Northern Ireland's DSA Helpline
  serves anyone 18 or over.[10] In England and Wales, Victim Support's Supportline is for anyone
  affected by crime.[16]
- **Whether you've left or not:** Refuge's helpline site says it does not matter whether you are
  still with the person or have left, "You may even have left many years ago."[4]

### If you can't speak

In England and Wales, the Independent Office for Police Conduct, which oversees complaints about the
police there, runs a campaign about the
[Silent Solution](https://www.policeconduct.gov.uk/our-work/key-areas-of-work/silent-solution). It
began after the IOPC investigated police contact with a woman who was murdered by her partner: she
"had wrongly thought that police had her number and if she made a silent 999 call, they would know
to come."[2] They do not. On a 999 call from a mobile, "A recorded message will instruct callers who
can't talk to press 55 to be put through to police. Staying silent and not pressing 55 will lead to
the call being terminated."[2] The page adds that pressing 55 lets the operator know it's a genuine
emergency, and the guide says "Pressing 55 does not allow police to track your location."[2] Lesson 1
described what happens on a silent call from a landline, where the 55 step is not used. As lesson 1
noted, the guide does not say whether the same holds in Scotland and Northern Ireland.

For callers who are deaf, hard of hearing or have a speech impairment, the IOPC page points to the
Relay UK service, run by BT, and says you need to register your mobile number before using it.[2]
For support rather than emergencies in the UK, Refuge's live chat, Live Fear Free's text line and
Scotland's helpline's text and WhatsApp number all work without speaking.[4][7][8] Scotland's
helpline also takes calls through Relay UK and from British Sign Language users through Contact
Scotland BSL, and Refuge has a BSL video service on weekdays from 10am to 6pm.[8][4]

In the US there is no single equivalent. You can text 911 where that's available, though
[911.gov](https://www.911.gov/calling-911/frequently-asked-questions/) says "it is always best to
call 911 if you can, and text if you can't".[3] For support rather than emergencies, the Hotline
takes texts (START to 88788) and live chat.[1]

:::checkpoint Try it on a case the lesson hasn't used. A woman in Montana is being followed and messaged by a former coworker, not a partner. It's Saturday night, she's at home and safe for now, and she wants to talk to someone. Using only the lines this lesson gives, where can she turn, and what condition comes with one of them?

VictimConnect, 855-484-2846, by call or text, 24/7, which takes victims of any crime, with its condition: it can talk with her only if she is physically safe right now, which she is. If she stops being safe, 911.
:::

## Rhys and the lines he didn't think were for him

A second case, with a wrinkle. Rhys lives in Wales. He and his former partner, a woman, split up in
the spring. Since then she has messaged him most days, sometimes dozens of times, and twice he has
come home to find her waiting outside his building. He hasn't told anyone. He had assumed the
helplines were for women.

**Start with what it is.** On the Suzy Lamplugh Trust's definition, a pattern of repeated, unwanted
contact that distresses him is stalking, with or without any threat, and the trust says stalking
"can be perpetrated by men or women."[27] On the Hotline's list, "Waiting around at places you
spend time" is one of its examples.[22]

**Then the lines, and their hours.** Three lines serve him, and each says so on its own page.

- The **Men's Advice Line**, for men experiencing abuse from a partner or ex-partner, weekdays 10am
  to 5pm. He does not have to prove anything to use it.[12]
- The **National Stalking Helpline**, for England and Wales, weekdays only, with evening hours on
  Mondays and Wednesdays.[6] Its page says the adviser will ask whether he has been to the police,
  and that he can then decide what steps he wants to take.[6]
- **Live Fear Free**, the Welsh Government's line, 24 hours a day, for anyone experiencing domestic
  abuse, with calls welcome in Welsh. Its page notes that there are refuges, safe houses, for women
  and for men in Wales.[7]

So if it is eleven on a Saturday night when he decides to ring, Live Fear Free is the one that
answers. On a Monday afternoon all three are open; the stalking line stays open until 8pm that
evening, and Live Fear Free all night.

**What he does with it.** Why he told no one is not something this lesson can answer: nothing the
course read studies why men hesitate to call. The Men's Advice Line's page for friends and family
gives its own account, from its work: "Often people don't believe men when they first disclose
abuse."[30] What the lesson can give him is lesson 3's figure, that 2.3% of men in England and Wales
were stalked in a year, and the Silent Solution if he ever needs it. What he does with any of it is
his.

## Helping someone

Most of the lines in this lesson take calls from people worried about someone else: Refuge's, the
Men's Advice Line, Live Fear Free, Scotland's helpline and the National Stalking Helpline all say so
on their pages.[4][12][7][8][6] You do not have to be the person being harmed to ring.

The services' guidance for helpers is consistent, and it is the same rule they keep with callers.
Refuge's page for friends starts with a warning: "Your first instinct may be to protect her, but
intervening directly can be dangerous for you and her."[32] It says to speak in private and make it
clear you will not judge, to take her seriously and believe her, to tell her it's not her fault, and not
to ask why she has not left. It suggests being direct, and starting by telling her you are worried
about her and why, and it says not to tell her to leave or criticise her for staying.[32] The Men's
Advice Line's page for friends and family gives the same advice for men, and adds a list of don'ts:
don't tell the person what to do or give ultimatums, don't get angry when nothing changes, and don't
confront the abuser or the victim, since "Doing so could cause isolation, harm and contact to
end."[30] The Hotline puts the principle in a line: you cannot rescue someone, and decisions about
their life are theirs to make. It also asks helpers to keep supporting the person if they go back to
the relationship, and not to post anything online that could show who they are or where they spend
their time.[33]

Refuge adds some practical offers a friend can make, if she wants them: agreeing a code word she
can use to tell you she needs help, and keeping a spare set of keys or important documents for
her.[32] It also says that in an emergency, if you fear for your friend's safety, call 999.[32]
All of this is the services' practitioner opinion.

### Three sentences for a sister

Try this one yourself. Your sister rings one evening. After a while she says her husband has taken
her bank card "to help her budget", reads her messages, and went quiet for two days when she saw a
friend without asking him. Then she says, "I'm probably overreacting."

The first sentence, built from Refuge's advice to believe her and be direct: *"I don't think you're
overreacting, and I'm glad you told me."*

:::checkpoint Now write the second and third sentences yourself before you open this. One should take the blame off her; the other should leave every decision with her while making sure she knows help exists.

One version: *"None of this is your fault."* Then: *"Whatever you decide, now or later, I'm on your side, and if you ever want to talk it through with someone who knows about this, the helplines take calls from family too, so I could ring with you, or give you the number whenever you want it, in whatever way is safe for you."* Her husband reads her messages, so nothing about this goes in a text or a message unless she says it is safe; that's this course's application of the Hotline's warning, not a rule of Refuge's. Notice what is missing. There is no "you need to leave him", no "I'm going to say something to him", and no "why do you put up with it?". Each of those takes a decision away from her or, in the last case, asks the question Refuge tells helpers not to ask. If yours differs in wording but keeps those two jobs and avoids those three, it is right.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Just leave."** Campbell's study found that separation after living together, especially from a
highly controlling abuser, went with higher odds of being killed, and the services frame leaving as
a process to plan, on the person's own timing.[29][32]

**"If it were really abuse, she'd have left."** Whether someone has left says nothing about whether
what's happening to them is abuse. The services define abuse by the pattern of control, not by the
response to it, and Refuge names fear of what the abuser will do if she leaves as one reason a woman
may not.[32]

**"It's only abuse if he hits her."** Refuge calls that "a common myth", and both services' lists
include forms with no physical violence at all.[15][22]

**"Domestic abuse is rare."** The England and Wales survey put it at 7.8% of adults in the year
ending March 2025, 9.1% of women and 6.5% of men, and ONS measures it with private questions because
people may not tell an interviewer.[24][34]

**"Men aren't victims," and "the helplines are only for women."** Refuge recognises that men
experience abuse, the Suzy Lamplugh Trust says stalking can be perpetrated by men or women, and the
Men's Advice Line, Live Fear Free, Scotland's helpline, the DSA Helpline and the US Hotline all serve
men.[4][27][12][7][8][10][1]

**"If I dial 999 and can't speak, they'll know to come."** From a mobile, only if you press 55 when
the recorded message asks; staying silent ends the call, and pressing 55 does not give police your
location.[2]

**"A private window keeps me safe."** It stops one browser keeping a record on one device (Digital
Literacy lesson 8). The Hotline's warning is that internet use can be monitored and cannot be erased
completely.[1]
