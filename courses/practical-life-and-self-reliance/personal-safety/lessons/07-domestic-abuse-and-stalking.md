This lesson is about harm from a partner, an ex-partner or a family member, and about stalking.
Lesson 3 showed that this is where much of the most serious harm to women comes from, and men meet
it too, yet most everyday safety advice, about streets and strangers, barely reaches it. If you are
reading because someone close to you frightens you, the lines are in the second box below, and
nothing in this lesson will tell you what to do.

## You decide

Two sentences from the services set the rules for everything below. Refuge, which runs the
National Domestic Abuse Helpline, says on its helpline page: "We will never tell you what to do."[4]
The US Hotline writes that "you are the expert in your situation", and goes on to say you are the
best person to decide what will keep you safest.[14]

So this lesson describes. It says what the services call abuse and stalking, what one careful
study found about the most dangerous situations, what a safety plan is, and which line serves whom.
It doesn't tell anyone to stay or to go, and it never says "just leave". If you are helping someone
else, the same rule holds for you, and the last part of the lesson is about how.

It is also the one lesson in this course where no advice about avoiding danger in public applies.
Lessons 4 to 6 were about lowering the odds at home, on the street and when travelling. None of that
is a plan for living with someone who frightens you, and nothing in this lesson suggests the person
being harmed caused it or could have prevented it. Refuge says abuse "is never the fault of the
person who is experiencing it, and it is a crime."[15]

## What the services call abuse

:::predict Before you read on: does abuse by a partner, as the domestic abuse services themselves define it, have to involve being hit or sexually assaulted?

No. Refuge says so in as many words: "A common myth is that it's only domestic abuse if it involves physical or sexual violence. In fact, many women experience domestic abuse without ever being physically harmed."[15] Both the UK and the US services define abuse by what it is for, control, rather than by any one act.
:::

Refuge's page ["What is domestic abuse?"](https://refuge.org.uk/what-is-domestic-abuse/) opens
with a definition: "Domestic abuse (sometimes called 'domestic violence' or 'intimate partner
violence') is an incident or a pattern of behaviour that is used by someone to control or obtain
power over their partner or ex-partner."[15] The US Hotline's is close to it: "Domestic
violence (also referred to as intimate partner violence (IPV), dating abuse, or relationship abuse)
is a pattern of behaviors used by one partner to maintain power and control over another partner in
an intimate relationship."[1]

Two words carry both definitions. **Pattern** means the harm is in the repetition, so a single
incident can look small when the whole is not. **Control** means the question is not whether any one
act was violent, but what the acts, taken together, are for.

Each service then lists the forms it sees, and the two lists aren't the same, so here they are
separately, each in its own words. Refuge names six: "Emotional abuse, including gaslighting /
Coercive control / Physical abuse / Tech abuse / Economic abuse / Sexual abuse".[15] The
Hotline's [types of abuse](https://www.thehotline.org/resources/types-of-abuse/) page names nine:
physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, financial abuse, technology-facilitated abuse, sexual
coercion, reproductive coercion, stalking and spiritual abuse. It adds that "Multiple forms of abuse
are usually present at the same time in abusive situations".[22] There is no single agreed
list; each service's list is its own.

Emotional abuse, on the Hotline's page, is "non-physical behaviors that are meant to control,
isolate, or frighten someone", and its examples include "Monitoring your activities with or without
your knowledge, including demanding to know where you go, who you contact, and how you spend your
time."[22] Its list also covers name-calling and constant criticism, jealousy, cutting
someone off from family and friends, humiliation, threats against children or pets, damaging
belongings, and blaming the person for the abuse.[22]

### Coercive control

Refuge gives coercive control its own [page](https://refuge.org.uk/i-need-help-now/coercive-control/).
It describes it as an act, or a pattern of acts, of assault, threats, humiliation and intimidation
that an abuser uses to harm, punish or frighten, and says it is a purposeful pattern rather than a
single incident.[23] Because it can be hard to see from inside, the page gives questions instead of
a definition to test against. Among them: does your partner isolate you from friends and family,
monitor how you spend your time, keep track of what you do online, control when you sleep, where
you go, who you see and what you wear, keep control of your money, or stop you getting to work or
using transport?[23]

Refuge's helpline site has a shorter test still: "Anyone forced to change their behaviour because
they are frightened of their partner or ex-partner's reaction is experiencing abuse."[4]
And on its home page it asks: "Do you feel like you're walking on eggshells?"[4]

Refuge's coercive control page also says that controlling or coercive behaviour in an intimate or
family relationship is a crime, and that perpetrators can be prosecuted.[23] This is general
information, not legal advice. The law differs by state, and between England and Wales, Scotland and
Northern Ireland. This course did not read the statutes behind that sentence, so it goes no further
than Refuge does.

### Who it happens to

Refuge: "Domestic abuse can happen to anyone, regardless of age, background, gender identity, sex,
religion, sexual orientation or ethnicity. However, statistics show most domestic abuse is carried
out by men and experienced by women."[15] Its helpline is for women, and it says so, then
adds: "We recognise that men can experience domestic abuse too."[4] The Hotline's page
says that anyone can be a victim, or a perpetrator.[1] Both halves of Refuge's sentence are
true at once, and the figures below bear both out.

## How common it is

In England and Wales, the crime survey estimated that 7.8% of adults aged 16 and over had
experienced domestic abuse in the year ending March 2025, about 3.8 million people.[24]
ONS says plainly that this is likely to be too low where the questions are asked face to face: the
survey "underestimates domestic violence because victims may not disclose it to interviewers".[24]
Because of the sensitive nature of the subject, as ONS puts it, the survey asks about domestic abuse
in a section people fill in privately.[25] Lesson 3
gave the figures from those private questions by sex, for the year before: women were victims of
domestic abuse and of stalking at more than one and a half times men's rate, and men were victims
of both too.

:::checkpoint Lesson 1 gave 7.8% as the share of adults in England and Wales who were victims of fraud. A friend puts that beside this lesson's 7.8% and says, "So domestic abuse is exactly as common as fraud." Using lesson 2's labels, what is wrong with the sentence?

The two figures carry different labels. Fraud's 7.8% was for the year ending March 2026 and comes from the survey's main questions; domestic abuse's 7.8% was for the year ending March 2025 and comes from the private self-completion questions, which ONS says still undercount. They're different years and different measures, and the match is a coincidence. "About as common, on two different measures a year apart" is the most the pair supports, and even that leans on a figure its own publisher says is too low.
:::

The police see a different slice. In the year ending March 2024, in data from 18 police forces in
England and Wales, 41% of suspects in violence against women were the women's intimate partners,
against 22% for violence against men.[25] That is police-recorded crime from part of the
country, a share of suspects rather than of people, so it does not line up with the survey's
percentage of adults. What it adds is the same direction from a second, independent source: for
women, a partner is a much larger share of the danger.

For killings, lesson 3's row from the Homicide Index is the one to remember: adult women killed in
England and Wales were most often killed by a partner or ex-partner. For the US, lesson 2 set the
CDC's lifetime figure for partner violence beside the victim survey's one-year rate and showed why
the two cannot be compared.

## Stalking

:::predict The Stalking Prevention, Awareness, and Resource Center (SPARC) is a US project funded by the Justice Department's Office on Violence Against Women. If you ask someone being stalked, "Are you being stalked?", do you think they'll usually say yes?

Often not. SPARC writes that "victims are unlikely to use the words 'stalking' or 'harassment' when talking about their situation", and advises helpers to ask about specific behaviours instead.[26] That is worth knowing whichever side of the conversation you are on.
:::

The National Stalking Helpline is run by the Suzy Lamplugh Trust, and the trust's page on
[what stalking is](https://www.suzylamplugh.org/what-is-stalking) starts plainly: "Stalking is a
pattern of repeated, unwanted behaviour that causes you to feel distressed or scared. It can be
perpetrated by men or women."[27] Its fuller definition: "A pattern of fixated and obsessive
behaviour which is repeated, persistent, intrusive and causes fear of violence or engenders alarm
and distress in the victim."[27] Two things in it matter most. It does not need a threat:
"Stalking can happen with or without a fear of violence."[27] And it doesn't need anything
dramatic: "Stalking can consist of any type of behaviour such as regularly sending flowers or gifts,
making unwanted or malicious communication, damaging property and physical or sexual
assault."[27]

Who does it? The same page says that about 45% of the people who contact the helpline are being
stalked by ex-partners and a further third knew their stalker in some other way.[27] Read the
label on that: it is the helpline's callers, not everyone who is stalked, so it tells you who calls
this helpline and cannot be turned into a national share. The page adds that knowing, or having
known, the stalker does not make any of it the victim's fault.[27]

The Hotline counts stalking among its forms of abuse and defines it this way: "Stalking occurs when
someone watches, follows, or harasses you repeatedly, making you feel afraid or unsafe, and may
occur from someone you know, a past partner, or a stranger." Its examples include "Showing up at
your home or workplace unannounced or uninvited", "Using social media or technology to track your
activities" and "Waiting around at places you spend time."[22]

SPARC gives stalking no one-sentence definition on the pages this course read. It sorts the
behaviour instead: "SPARC categorizes stalking behaviors into SLII strategies: Surveillance, Life
Invasion, Intimidation, and Interference through sabotage or attack."[26] It notes that "most
stalkers engage in multiple tactics to scare their victims", and that "Stalkers' behaviors can
escalate from more indirect ways of making contact (like phone calls or texts) to more direct contact
(like delivering gifts or showing up where you are)."[26] Its page for victims also suggests
keeping a record of each contact and saving messages and posts as evidence.[26] That's the
service's guidance, practitioner opinion in this course's labels, since this course read no study
of it; and the rule that runs through this whole lesson applies to where such a record is kept.

SPARC is also clear about what it is not. Its page for victims says, in capitals, that it does not
provide direct services to victims of stalking. It points to VictimConnect, the Hotline and RAINN, and adds that
domestic abuse and sexual assault services are often the right people to contact about stalking even
when it has nothing to do with a partner.[26]

Lesson 3 gave the England and Wales figures: stalking fell on women more often than on men, and on
16 to 19 year olds more than on any other age group.

## Technology used to track and control

The Hotline defines technology-facilitated abuse as "the use of technology ... to bully, harass,
stalk, intimidate, or control a partner."[22] Refuge runs a separate site,
[Refuge Tech Safety](https://refugetechsafety.org/), which describes it as someone using technology
to harm or control you, and has step-by-step guides to securing phones, accounts and home
devices.[28]

Its page on [AirTags](https://refugetechsafety.org/airtag/) carries the line that matters most
before any of those steps: "depending on whether or not you are living with the person who is
harming you, you may choose to take different steps. Control and coercion make some of these steps
impossible or not safe."[28] The page does not say more than that about which steps, and this
course won't guess. Whether a step is safe is a judgement about your own situation, which is why the
site frames it as your choice and says it is there to support anyone who thinks technology is being
used against them.[28]

This lesson does not copy the steps themselves. They change with each phone update, so a page that
printed them would soon be out of date; that is this course's reason for linking rather than
teaching them, not the services'.

What this course can add is one piece of an earlier lesson. Digital Literacy lesson 8 showed what a
private browsing window does: it stops that browser keeping the session's history on that device,
and nothing more. The Hotline's warning at the top of this lesson is wider than that, and so is the
services' advice. The Hotline says that if you're worried your internet use is being monitored, call
it instead.[1] Refuge's helpline site says that if you think someone might be monitoring your
devices, leave the site and come back on a safe device.[4] The Men's Advice Line says calls
to it do not appear on itemised phone bills.[12] Each is the service's practical guidance: in this
course's labels, practitioner opinion.

## Kerry's question

Here is a case, worked through. Kerry lives in England. Her partner checks her phone most evenings,
has started turning up at her work at lunchtime without saying he is coming, and tells her which of
her friends she can see. He has never hit her. She isn't sure it "counts", and she has not told
anyone.

**What the services describe.** Set her three facts beside their pages, one at a time. Checking her
phone and deciding whom she sees are close to two of the Hotline's examples of emotional abuse:
monitoring someone's activities, including demanding to know where they go and whom they contact,
and cutting them off from friends.[22] Turning up at her work uninvited is close to one of
the Hotline's examples of stalking, showing up at someone's workplace unannounced or uninvited. And Refuge's coercive control questions ask whether a partner monitors how you spend
your time and controls who you see.[23]

**What the services do not do.** None of those pages gives a score, and none says "this counts" or
"this doesn't". Refuge's shorter test is a question only Kerry can answer: is she changing what she
does because she's frightened of his reaction? This course will not answer it for her either.

:::checkpoint Before reading on: suppose Kerry decides to ring Refuge's helpline. From what the service's own pages say, what is the call for, and what won't happen on it?

The page says an adviser will listen to whatever she wants to say, offer confidential information that does not judge her, and help her make a plan if she'd like one. It also says the adviser will never tell her what to do. She does not have to be sure it "counts" before she rings: the page says she may not know yet what help she needs, and that calling is a place to start.[4]
:::

**The practical side.** Her partner checks her phone, so the monitoring warning in the box at the
top is about her. The services' own suggestions are to use a device he cannot get at, or to call
rather than browse.[1][4] Refuge's live chat runs weekdays 10am to 10pm and
weekends 10am to 6pm, and lets her talk to an adviser in writing, without speaking aloud.[4]
The helpline itself is open all day and all night. Refuge notes that when lines are busy, evenings,
nights and weekends are often quieter.[4]

Every one of those is an option, not a step she ought to take. What she does next, including
nothing for now, is hers.

## Separation, and a study of killings

Lesson 3 found that separated people had the highest rate of violent victimisation in the US
survey's table, and that neither source it read explained why. It promised a study of killings by
partners that comes at separation from another direction. This is it.

In 2003 Campbell and colleagues published a study of femicide, the killing of women, by
partners, in the *American Journal of Public Health*. In 11 US cities they interviewed people who
had known 220 women killed by a partner, the proxies, and compared their answers with interviews
with 343 abused women who had not been killed.[29] Notice who the comparison group is. It
isn't women in general. It is women who were also being abused, so the study asks what separates the
most dangerous abusive relationships from other abusive ones.

:::predict The study looked at many possible risk factors, alone and in combination. Which do you think was linked to the largest increase in the risk of being killed: a highly controlling abuser, separation after living together, or both together?

Both together, and by a wide margin. "The risk of intimate partner femicide was increased 9-fold by the combination of a highly controlling abuser and the couple's separation after living together (adjusted OR = 8.98; 95% CI = 3.25, 24.83)."[29] Each on its own was linked to a smaller rise: a highly controlling abuser without separation to an odds ratio of about 2.9, and separation after living together without a highly controlling abuser to about 3.1.[29]
:::

The abstract lists the factors that stood up once the others were taken into account: the
"perpetrator's access to a gun and previous threat with a weapon, perpetrator's stepchild in the
home, and estrangement, especially from a controlling partner."[29] Notice whose
characteristics these are. Almost every one describes the abuser or the relationship. And two things
went the other way: "Never living together and prior domestic violence arrest were associated with
lowered risks."[29] Stalking, forced sex and abuse during pregnancy were linked to higher
risk when looked at one at a time, but not once the other factors were in the model.[29]

### What the numbers mean, and don't

An odds ratio of 8.98 is not a nine-in-something chance of being killed. It compares the odds of a
factor among the women who were killed with its odds among the abused women who were not. The study
gives no absolute risk, and Logic and Argument lesson 9's warning applies: a relative figure without
its base tells you how much larger, not how large.

The interval matters as much as the headline. Its 95% confidence interval runs from 3.25 to 24.83.
In plain words, the data fit a multiplier as low as about three or as high as about twenty-five, and
8.98 is the single best estimate inside that range. The finding is strong that the combination raises risk; it is weak on by how much.

The paper names its own limits. "Perhaps the most important limitation of the study is its necessary
reliance on proxy respondents for data regarding hypothesized risk factors".[29] The women who
were killed could not be asked, so people who knew them answered, while the comparison women answered
for themselves. The study also left out women outside large cities, with one exception, so it's an
urban sample, from the US, published more than twenty years ago.

And it is an association. Campbell's team measured which factors went with killings; the paper, as
this course read it, gives no mechanism for why separation from a controlling partner goes with
danger, and this course will not supply one. What the services say is their own framing, from their
work rather than from this study. The Hotline's page on guns says that ending an abusive relationship
can be an extremely dangerous time, especially if the partner has access to firearms.[14]
The Men's Advice Line tells friends and family that leaving is often a very dangerous time, when
violence and abuse escalate, and that careful planning is needed.[30]

:::checkpoint A friend reads about Campbell's study and says, "So the study shows that a woman who leaves a controlling partner has a nine-in-ten chance of being killed." Name the two things wrong with that sentence.

First, 8.98 is an odds ratio from a comparison with other abused women, not a probability. The study gives no absolute chance at all. Second, "leaves a controlling partner" drops half the finding: the combination was a highly controlling abuser and separation after living together, and the interval around it runs from about three to about twenty-five. A sentence the study supports: among abused women in 11 US cities, that combination was associated with much higher odds of being killed by the partner, by an amount the study could pin down only roughly.
:::

## Why "just leave" isn't advice

Put the two halves of this lesson together and the phrase falls apart.

The first reason is Campbell's finding. Separation after living together, especially from a highly
controlling abuser, went with the highest risk the study measured. Advice that treats leaving as the
safe, simple answer has the risk backwards at the very moment it is highest.

The second is the services' framing. The Hotline's definition of a safety plan, below, covers
safety "while experiencing abuse, preparing to leave an abusive situation, or after you leave"; it
doesn't assume any one of those.[31] Refuge's page for friends says not to tell a woman to
leave or criticise her for staying, because the decision is hers to make in her own time, and it
calls leaving a process that takes time rather than a single act.[32]

The third is the one the course began with. "Just leave" is someone else deciding. Refuge's "We will
never tell you what to do" is a service's rule, and on this course's reading it is also a statement
about who knows the situation best. This course read no research on why any particular person stays, and makes no claim
about it.

None of that is an argument for staying, either. It is an argument for planning, with help, on the
person's own timing, which is what the services offer.

## What a safety plan is

The Hotline's definition: "a personalized, practical plan to improve your safety while experiencing
abuse, preparing to leave an abusive situation, or after you leave."[31] And its reason for
making one in advance: "it can be hard to think clearly or make logical decisions during moments of
crisis."[31] That's the mechanism the service gives, and it is the only one this course
has.

Both the Hotline and Refuge will make the plan with you. The Hotline's page invites you to contact it at any
hour to have an advocate help you prepare a personal plan, and Refuge's adviser will, in its words
on the page, help you make a plan if you'd like.[31][4] That matters for a
practical reason, and it is this course's point rather than the services': a plan made on the phone
with an adviser does not have to exist on paper or on a device where it could be found.

So this lesson doesn't describe a plan, and none of its exercises asks you to write one. The Hotline
also has an interactive planner on its site; this course did not open it, so it cannot tell you what
it asks or how it stores what you enter. Its page ["What is a Safety
Plan?"](https://www.thehotline.org/plan-for-safety/what-is-a-safety-plan/) links to it and to the
Hotline's guides for staying, preparing to leave, leaving and afterwards.

In this course's labels, safety planning is practitioner opinion: the services teach it from long
experience, and this course read no study that measured it. That is an honest label, not a doubt.

### Guns, and one line on a protective order

Campbell's list includes the abuser's access to a gun. The Hotline's page on guns says that getting
a protective order in many US states can require an abusive partner to turn over their
guns.[14] This is general information, not legal advice. The law differs by state, and
between England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. The separate question of a gun kept by the
person being abused is one people disagree about, and this lesson leaves it to lesson 14, "The home,
property, and carrying something for protection", where the course takes up guns for protection.

### Not a job for de-escalation

Lesson 8, "Talking it down", teaches ways to calm a tense encounter with a stranger or at work. None
of it is advice for an abusive relationship. Calming techniques used on a partner who frightens you
can look like managing the abuser, and that's this course's judgement, from the gap between the two
kinds of situation, not a finding. The services' guidance for helpers points the same way: the Men's
Advice Line tells friends and family not to confront the abuser.[30]

### If you can't speak

In the UK, the Independent Office for Police Conduct runs a campaign about the **Silent Solution**.
It began after the IOPC investigated police contact with a woman who was murdered by her partner:
she "had wrongly thought that police had her number and if she made a silent 999 call, they would
know to come."[2] They do not. On a 999 call from a mobile, "A recorded message will instruct
callers who can't talk to press 55 to be put through to police. Staying silent and not pressing 55
will lead to the call being terminated."[2] And "Pressing 55 does not allow police to track your
location."[2] Lesson 1 described what happens on a silent call from a landline, where the 55
step isn't used.

In the US there is no single equivalent. You can text 911 where that is available, though 911.gov
says "it is always best to call 911 if you can, and text if you can't".[3] For support rather
than emergencies, the Hotline takes texts (START to 88788) and live chat, and Refuge's live chat
lets you write instead of speaking.[1][4]

## Rhys and the lines he didn't think were for him

A second case, with a wrinkle. Rhys lives in Wales. He and his former partner, a woman, split up in
the spring. Since then she has messaged him most days, sometimes dozens of times, and twice he's
come home to find her waiting outside his building. He hasn't told anyone. He'd assumed the
helplines were for women.

**Start with what it is.** On the Suzy Lamplugh Trust's definition, a pattern of repeated, unwanted
contact that distresses him is stalking, with or without any threat, and the trust says stalking
"can be perpetrated by men or women."[27] On the Hotline's list, "Waiting around at places you
spend time" is one of its examples.[22]

**Then the lines, and their hours.** Three lines serve him, and each says so on its own page.

- The **Men's Advice Line**, for men experiencing abuse from a partner or ex-partner, weekdays 10am
  to 5pm. He does not have to prove anything to use it.[12]
- The **National Stalking Helpline**, for England and Wales, weekdays only, with evening hours on
  Mondays and Wednesdays.[6] Its page says the adviser will ask whether he has been to the police,
  and that he can then decide what steps he wants to take.[6]
- **Live Fear Free**, the Welsh Government's line, 24 hours a day, for anyone experiencing domestic
  abuse, with calls welcome in Welsh. Its page notes that there are refuges for women and for men in
  Wales.[7]

So if it is eleven on a Saturday night when he decides to ring, Live Fear Free is the one that
answers. On a Monday afternoon all three are open; the stalking line stays open until 8pm that evening, and
Live Fear Free all night.

**What the lesson doesn't do.** It does not say why he told no one. Nothing this course read studies
why men hesitate to call, so it makes no claim. It gives him lesson 3's England and Wales figures
for men, which show that men are victims of stalking too, and the Silent Solution in case he ever needs it. What he
does with any of it is his.

## Helping someone

Most of the lines in this lesson take calls from people worried about someone else: Refuge's, the Men's
Advice Line, Live Fear Free, Scotland's helpline and the National Stalking Helpline all say so on
their pages.[4][12][7][8][6] You don't have to be the person being harmed to
ring.

The services' guidance for helpers is consistent, and it is the same rule they keep with callers.
Refuge's page for friends says to speak in private and make it clear you won't judge, to take her
seriously and believe her, to tell her it is not her fault, and not to ask why she has not left.
[32] It suggests being direct, and starting by telling her you are worried about her and why, and it says
not to tell her to leave or criticise her for staying.[32]
The Men's Advice Line's page for friends and family gives the same advice for men, and adds a list of
don'ts: don't tell the person what to do or give ultimatums, don't get angry when nothing changes, and
don't confront the abuser.[30] The Hotline puts the principle in a line: you cannot rescue
someone, and decisions about their life are theirs to make. It also asks helpers to keep supporting
the person if they go back to the relationship, and not to post anything online that could show who
they are or where they spend their time.[33]

Refuge adds some practical offers a friend can make, if she wants them: agreeing a code word she
can use to tell you she needs help, and keeping spare keys or copies of important documents for
her.[32] It also says that in an emergency, if you fear for your friend's safety, call
999.[32] In this course's labels all of this is practitioner opinion: the services'
experience, with no study this course read behind it.

### Three sentences for a sister

This case has a gap for you to fill. Your sister rings one evening. After a while she says her
husband has taken her bank card "to help her budget", reads her messages, and went quiet for two
days when she saw a friend without asking him. Then she says, "I'm probably overreacting."

The first sentence, built from Refuge's advice to believe her and be direct: *"I don't think you're
overreacting, and I'm glad you told me."*

:::checkpoint Now write the second and third sentences yourself before you open this. One should take the blame off her; the other should leave every decision with her while making sure she knows help exists.

One version: *"None of this is your fault."* Then: *"Whatever you decide, now or later, I'm on your side, and if you ever want to talk it through with someone who knows about this, the helplines take calls from family too, so I could ring with you, or just find you the number."* Notice what's missing. There is no "you need to leave him", no "I'm going to say something to him", and no "why do you put up with it?". Each of those takes a decision away from her or, in the last case, asks the question Refuge tells helpers not to ask. If yours differs in wording but keeps those two jobs and avoids those three, it is right.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Just leave."** Campbell's study found that separation after living together, especially from a
highly controlling abuser, went with the highest risk it measured, and the services frame leaving as
a process to plan, on the person's own timing.[29][32]

**"If it were really abuse, she'd have left."** Whether someone has left says nothing about whether
what is happening to them is abuse. The services define abuse by the pattern of control, not by the
response to it, and this course makes no claim about why anyone stays.

**"It's only abuse if he hits her."** Refuge calls that "a common myth", and both services' lists
include forms with no physical violence at all.[15][22]

**"Domestic abuse is rare."** The England and Wales survey put it at 7.8% of adults in the year
ending March 2025, and ONS says the face-to-face questions undercount it.[24]

**"Men aren't victims," and "the helplines are only for women."** Refuge recognises that men
experience abuse, the Suzy Lamplugh Trust says stalking can be perpetrated by men or women, and the
Men's Advice Line, Live Fear Free, Scotland's helpline, the DSA Helpline and the US Hotline all serve
men.[4][27][12][7][8][10][1]

**"Stalking is a stranger in the shadows."** Most of the National Stalking Helpline's callers are
being stalked by an ex-partner or someone they knew, as the trust reports for its own
callers.[27]

**"If I dial 999 and can't speak, they'll know to come."** From a mobile, only if you press 55 when
the recorded message asks; staying silent ends the call, and pressing 55 doesn't give police your
location.[2]

**"A private window keeps me safe."** It stops one browser keeping a history on one device (Digital
Literacy lesson 8). The Hotline's warning is that internet use can be monitored and cannot be erased
completely.[1]
