Burglary sits in an odd place among the crimes people fear. It is far from the worst harm this
course deals with, yet lesson 1 showed it topping a city's fear rankings above murder, because it
seems serious enough and likely enough at once. It is also where the strongest evidence in this whole
course is. A long run of survey data from England and Wales lets researchers compare households
that had particular locks and lights with households that had none, and the difference is large. This
lesson teaches that evidence and its limits, sets out the one piece of home security that is
really disputed, says what the data show about burglaries with someone at home, and ends at the
front door, where the con artist and the burglar turn out to use the same move.

:::callout Before you rely on this
This course is education, not legal advice, and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger now, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US. On a 999 call from a mobile, if you can't speak, cough or tap if you can and press 55 when you're prompted; in the US you can text 911 where that's available, but call if you can. If a partner, ex-partner or family member is frightening you, the lesson on domestic abuse and stalking starts with the helplines. Responsibility for a crime lies with the person who commits it: precautions change the odds, not the blame. The law differs between England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

That responsibility sentence matters more than usual here, because home security is the place where
"you should have locked it" comes most easily to people's lips. Nothing in this lesson says a
household that was burgled did something wrong. A lock changes how hard a house is to get into. It has
nothing to do with who chose to break in.

## What made burglary fall

In the 1990s burglary in England and Wales began a long fall, and criminologists argued about why.
Tseloni, Farrell, Tilley and colleagues tested one explanation, the security
hypothesis: that households fitted better locks and lights, and burglars found fewer houses they
could get into. [Their 2017 paper in *Crime Science*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7089640/)
used the Crime Survey for England and Wales from 1992 to 2011/12, which asks households both whether
they were burgled and which security devices they had.[1]

:::predict The survey sorts burglaries by how the burglar got in. Some were forced: a lock or window forced, glass broken, a door panel removed. Others were unforced: a door or window left open or unlocked. If better security caused the fall, which kind would you expect to have fallen, and which to have stayed about the same?
Forced entries should fall and unforced ones should stay flat, because a lock only makes a difference to a burglar who has to get past it. That's what the paper found. Burglaries with forced entry dropped from 31 per 1,000 households in 1993 to 7 per 1,000 in 2006/07, a fall of 77%, while unforced burglaries stayed at 7 per 1,000 over the same years.
:::

The paper draws the same conclusion itself: the decline was "solely in forced rather than unforced
entries to households", and the main drop came when the house was empty, which is when locks are
locked and timer lights are on.[1] Put with the rest of their evidence, the authors wrote: "The study
concludes that there is strong evidence that security caused the decline in burglary in England and
Wales in the 1990s."[1]

Notice what kind of evidence that is. Nobody randomly assigned locks to houses. The authors built a
case from several survey patterns pointing the same way: security spread just before burglary fell,
households with no security became far riskier than everyone else, and the fall came only where
security had to be overcome. It's a strong comparison rather than a trial, which is why lesson 1 used
it as the example of the label **tested**: a strong comparison that measured the outcome that matters.

## WIDE, and what "almost 50 times" means

The paper also reports which devices did the work, from the same team's analysis of the 2008/09 to
2011/12 surveys. The measure is the security protection factor (SPF). It compares the
burglary risk of households with a given device or set of devices against households with "no
security", meaning none of the devices the survey asked about. An SPF of 13 means households with
those devices had about a thirteenth of the risk of burglary with entry that no-security households
had.[1]

Single devices did less on their own. Window locks had an SPF of 7; indoor lights on a timer,
double or deadlocks on the doors, and external lights on a sensor had 3 each. Combinations did much
better:[1]

| Devices | SPF |
|---|---|
| Window locks and door double or deadlocks | 13 |
| Those two plus external lights on a sensor | 34 |
| All four: window locks, indoor lights on a timer, door double or deadlocks, external lights on a sensor | 49 |

The four together are what the team calls WIDE: "properties fitted with the four 'WIDE' devices:
window locks (W), indoor lights on a timer (I), door double or deadlocks (D) and external lights on a
sensor (E) have an SPF of 49."[1] The authors compare that with the four single SPFs added together,
16, and treat the difference as what combining them adds. In the discussion they put it plainly:
WIDE produces "a very substantial (almost 50 times) lower risk of burglary with entry than 'no
security'".[1]

That number gets quoted more than any other in this course, and it's easy to misread in three ways.

**It's against no security at all.** The comparison group is households with none of the listed
devices, and by 2008/09 to 2011/12 that was under one household in twenty (4.9%), down from about
one in six in 1992 to 1996 (on a shorter list of devices, which the authors say may partly explain
the higher earlier figure). Those households had become nearly eight times as likely as the average
household to be burgled.[1] Most homes already have some of the devices. So "almost 50 times" isn't
what adding WIDE to a typical home does, and "WIDE cuts your risk by 98%" is the wrong sentence: it
takes a comparison against the rarest and riskiest group and turns it into a promise about yours.

**It's a comparison between households, not a trial.** Households that fit four devices may differ
in other ways from households that fit none. The team's wider case is what makes them confident the
devices themselves matter, but no one put WIDE on some homes and not others and waited.

**It counts devices, not how good they are or whether they were used.** In the authors' words, the
survey "has asked only about the presence or absence of security devices. It has not been designed to
capture their quality, nor whether the devices were in use at the time of the victimisation."[1] A
window lock left unlocked every night is still a window lock in the survey. They add that the survey
could not test measures aimed at whole areas (planning standards for new housing, street lighting,
gates across the alleys behind houses), so security's full role may be "understated".[1]

:::checkpoint A neighbour reads a headline that says "four gadgets make you 50 times safer from burglars". Write the sentence the study actually supports.
Something like: "In England and Wales survey data from 2008/09 to 2011/12, households with all four WIDE devices had about a forty-ninth of the risk of burglary with entry of households with none of the devices the survey asked about; it's a comparison between households, and it counts whether devices were there, not how good they were or whether they were used." If your sentence kept "against no security" and "between households", it has the two parts headlines drop.
:::

### Callum and Sian ask the landlord

Callum and Sian are moving into a rented ground-floor flat in Lincoln. The front door has a latch but
no deadlock. The windows close with catches, not locks. There's no outside light, and neither of them
owns a plug-in timer. They want to know what to ask the landlord for and what to do themselves.

**Step 1: list what the flat has against WIDE.** Window locks: no. Indoor lights on a timer: no. Door
double or deadlock: no. External light on a sensor: no. It has none of the survey's other devices
either (no alarm, door chain or window bars), so by the survey's definition the flat as it stands is
a no-security household.

**Step 2: sort who does what.** The locks and the outside light are fixtures, so they are for the
landlord to agree to. A timer for an indoor lamp is a small thing the tenants can buy and plug in
themselves. The authors themselves suggest that minimum standards set for landlords are one way to
get these devices to the people most at risk, which gives the request some backing, though it is
their suggestion, not a rule anywhere this course read.[1]

**Step 3: price it.** They look up, for their own area, what a deadlock, window locks and an outside
sensor light would cost to fit. This course names no products and no prices; fitting a lock is Home
Repair and Maintenance's, the course after this one.

**Step 4: label it.** Each request is tested, in this course's labels, in the sense lesson 1 gave:
a strong comparison between households in England and Wales measured burglary with entry, against
households with no security at all.

:::checkpoint The landlord replies that he will fit window locks and a deadlock, but calls the lights "extras". Using the table, what can Callum and Sian fairly say about the lights, and what is beyond them?
What they can say: households with the two locks alone had an SPF of 13 and households with all four devices had 49, so the four-device households had roughly a quarter of the risk of the two-device ones (13 divided by 49 is about 0.27, this course's arithmetic on the paper's figures). What they can't say: that the two lights will cut their own risk by three-quarters. The figures compare different households, not one flat before and after, and count devices present, not used. Their honest line is that the combination did much better than locks alone in the national data, and the timer is theirs to supply anyway.
:::

## The alarm dispute

Look back at the table. There's no burglar alarm in it. In the same England and Wales data, the 2017
paper reports that "somewhat counterintuitively, alarms were not identified as contributory", and it
recommends caution: "It would be prudent to think twice before recommending an alarm as part of a
standard package of security devices to reduce the risk of domestic burglary."[1]
[A 2015 paper by Tilley, Tseloni and colleagues](http://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1456429/1/cpcs201417a.pdf)
went further. From its abstract: "It finds that alarms have become associated with increased rather
than decreased risk of burglary with entry. This counter-intuitive finding needs to be treated
cautiously."[2]

:::predict Households with alarms were, on average, more likely to have been burgled. Before reading on, think of an explanation for that which doesn't involve alarms attracting burglars.
One the authors call plausible is timing. Some households fit an alarm after a burglary, and a household burgled once is at higher risk of being burgled again (lesson 3's repeat victimisation). So an alarm may mark a home that was already a target rather than make it one. If you thought of that, you reasoned the way the authors did.
:::

Tilley and colleagues offered several explanations, and they tested none of them. Beside the timing
one, they call a second plausible, though they had no data to test it: that an alarm can flag that
something worth stealing is inside. The others include that offenders and police have adapted
(police no longer always attend an alarm) and that many alarms are poor-quality or do-it-yourself
systems.[2] Each is a hypothesis, and nobody has yet shown which, if any, is right. The authors
themselves warn that, given earlier research suggesting that alarms have been effective deterrents,
it "would be premature to conclude that domestic burglar alarms are (or have become)
counter-productive", and they call for more research.[2]

The other side of the question comes from the US, where one study asked burglars. A team at the
University of North Carolina at Charlotte surveyed 422 people imprisoned for burglary in three
states for a 2012 report, *Understanding Decisions to Burglarize from the Offender's Perspective*.
This course read [the university's release about
it](https://inside.charlotte.edu/news-features/2013-05-15/through-eyes-burglar-study-provides-insights-habits-and-motivations/),
not the report. From the release: "Approximately 83 percent said they would try to determine if an
alarm was present before attempting a burglary, and 60 percent said they would seek an alternative
target if there was an alarm on-site." And: "Among those who discovered the presence of an alarm
while attempting a burglary, half reported they would discontinue the attempt, while another 31
percent said they would sometimes retreat."[3] A Rutgers study of burglar alarms in Newark, New
Jersey, by S. Lee, looked at whole neighbourhoods instead of single homes. Again this course read
only [the university's
release](https://www.rutgers.edu/news/rutgers-study-finds-alarm-systems-are-valuable-crime-fighting-tool),
which reports that "Neighborhoods in which burglar alarms were densely installed have fewer
incidents of residential burglaries than the neighborhoods with fewer burglar alarms", and calls it
the first study of alarms "scientifically ruling out other factors that could have impacted the
crime rate".[4] Its advisory committee included Felson and Clarke, whose work lesson 4 was built on,
with Clarke as its chair.[4]

Who paid for each is worth knowing. The two England and Wales papers were funded by the Economic and
Social Research Council, a public research funder, and the 2017 paper says the funder had no role in
its design, analysis or writing.[1][2] Both US studies were funded by the Alarm Industry Research and
Educational Foundation, which the Charlotte release describes as working under the auspices of the
largest trade association for the electronic security industry.[3][4] None of that tells you a result
is wrong, and this course makes no such suggestion about either side: the way to judge a study is by
its design.

### Can both be right?

Yes, because they measured different things. Burglars asked in prison said what they would do on
meeting an alarm. The England and Wales survey measured which households, with and without alarms,
were actually burgled. On one of the explanations the 2015 paper calls plausible, an alarm might put
some burglars off a house while alarmed houses, on average, are still riskier because some of them
were targets before the alarm went in. The Newark study counted burglaries, but by neighbourhood
rather than by household, so it answers a different question again. The US studies also come from a
different country, with different alarms and police response.

So whether an alarm protects a home is a **contested** question in this course's labels. What would
settle it is a study that records when each alarm was fitted relative to any earlier burglary, and
follows households afterwards, or better, one that fits alarms to some homes at random and not to
others. The consensus on locks and lights is much firmer. If money is short, the evidence says spend
it on the combination in the table first. That's the course's reading of the England and Wales
evidence; an alarm on top is a reasonable choice, and the evidence points both ways and settles
neither.

## How burglars get in, and whether anyone's home

:::predict Think of burglaries in England and Wales where the burglar actually got inside. In roughly what share was somebody at home at the time?
More than half. In the Crime Survey for England and Wales, someone was at home in over half of burglaries with entry in every year's table from the year ending March 2015 to the year ending March 2025. If you guessed low, you share one of the commonest beliefs about burglary.
:::

The current figures are the
[Office for National Statistics' tables on the nature of burglary](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/datasets/natureofcrimeburglary),
which run from the year ending March 2015 to the year ending March 2025 (there are no survey data for
the two pandemic years).[5] They count household incidents of domestic burglary in a dwelling where
the burglar got in, and each year's figure rests on the incidents the survey's sample picked up,
between about 120 and 340 a year depending on the question. So look at the range across the years
rather than at any single year, which is lesson 2's rule about one year and a trend.

- **Someone was at home** in between 51% and 68% of burglaries with entry each year.
- **The way in** was a door in about two-thirds to four-fifths of them, and **the door was not
  locked** in between about a fifth and a third of them (18% to 35%).
- **The burglar pushed past whoever opened the door** in between 7% and 12%, and **got in by false
  pretences** in between about 3% and 8%.

The unlocked door is the row a household can act on. A lock only helps when it's locked, and the step
from that row to keeping the outside doors locked while you're in is this course's: sensible,
untested, in this course's labels, since nobody has trialled it.

The US picture comes from an older report.
[The Bureau of Justice Statistics](https://bjs.ojp.gov/content/pub/ascii/vdhb.txt) looked at the
National Crime Victimization Survey for 2003 to 2007 and found: "In about 28% of these burglaries, a
household member was present during the burglary. In 7% of all household burglaries, a household
member experienced some form of violent victimization."[6] Where nobody was home, unlocked ways in
were common there too: "In 40% of unlawful entries to unoccupied residences, offenders
gained access through an unlocked door or window".[6] Where someone was at home and the offender got
in without force, the report's table shows someone inside letting the offender in in 18% of those
burglaries, and someone opening the door and the offender pushing in in 12%.[6]

The two "someone at home" figures are different measures, and this course keeps them apart:

| | England and Wales | US |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Crime Survey for England and Wales (ONS) | National Crime Victimization Survey (BJS report by Catalano) |
| Years | Each year, 2014/15 to 2024/25 (year ending March) | 2003 to 2007, averaged |
| What's counted | Domestic burglaries in a dwelling where the burglar got in | Household burglaries, including attempted forcible entries |
| Someone at home | Over half in every year (51% to 68%) | About 28% |

The definitions and periods differ, so the gap between the columns isn't a finding about the two
countries. What each column says within itself is the finding.

### Two slogans, both too simple

Those figures answer two popular beliefs that point in opposite directions.

"Burglars strike when nobody's home." In England and Wales, more often than not, someone was. You
may meet a respectable version of this: lesson 1's Warr
[wrote in 2000](https://www.ojp.gov/criminal_justice2000/vol_4/04i.pdf) that "burglaries normally occur when no one is home".[7] That summarised older US research, and it fits
the US report above, where no one was home in most burglaries. It doesn't fit the current England
and Wales tables.

"Burglars attack people at home." Being home is common; violence isn't the usual outcome. In the US
data, a household member suffered violence in 7% of all household burglaries.[6] In England and
Wales the survey asks a narrower question. Where someone at home knew about the burglary and saw the
burglar (between about a fifth and a third of burglaries with entry each year), more than half of
those incidents involved some threat or use of force in every year's table. Each year rests on fewer
than 90 such incidents, and ONS says estimates based on fewer than 50 cases, as several of these
are, should be treated with caution.[5] Small as the counts are, every year points the same way:
where someone at home saw the burglar, threat or force was more common than not. The table doesn't
say who went to whom.

:::checkpoint Lesson 2 gave you five questions for any crime figure: who counted it, what it counts, whose harm, when, and per how many. Answer them for "the door was not locked in 18% to 35% of burglaries".
Who: the Office for National Statistics, from the Crime Survey for England and Wales. What: domestic burglaries in a dwelling where the burglar got in, sorted by how they got in, a survey estimate, so including burglaries never reported to police. Whose and where: households in England and Wales. When: each survey year from the year ending March 2015 to the year ending March 2025, a range across years, not an average. Per how many: a percentage of burglaries with entry, each year resting on a few hundred incidents at most. If your answer gave one year or one number, go back to the range.
:::

## If you think someone is inside

The two sources this course read on it agree: a UK charity, and a leaflet from the prosecutors and
police chiefs of England and Wales.

[The Suzy Lamplugh Trust](https://www.suzylamplugh.org/personal-safety-at-home), the UK personal
safety charity lesson 4 met, says that if you are in your home and think there is an intruder, "call
the police", and "don't go and investigate by yourself". And: "If you come home and suspect an
intruder has been inside, don't enter the property."[8] For that case, where you come home to it, its
page adds that you can use a mobile or a neighbour's phone for the call.
[The leaflet for householders](https://www.cps.gov.uk/sites/default/files/documents/publications/Householders-2018.pdf)
written by the Crown Prosecution Service and the National Police Chiefs' Council, which is mostly
about the law, says: "Wherever possible you should call the police."[9]

That's a crime in progress, the case
[GOV.UK's page on contacting the police](https://www.gov.uk/contact-police) gives for 999: "Call 999
if you are reporting a crime that is in progress or if someone is in immediate danger."[10] In the
US, the [Office for Victims of Crime](https://ovc.ojp.gov/help-for-victims) says to call 911 if you
feel in immediate danger.[10] This course read no US source that speaks to an intruder in the home
in particular: the Office for Victims of Crime's line is its general one, and the Lamplugh advice is
written for the UK. The callout at the top of this lesson has the silent-call steps for both.

How to label it: none of the sources read tested the advice, so it isn't tested. It's **sensible,
untested**. The survey figures above are the evidence nearby, and the step from them is this
course's: if seeing the burglar is when threat or force tends to come in, keeping your distance and
calling is the option that avoids that moment. People who did go and look, or who found the burglar
in front of them, did nothing wrong. The burglar made that moment, and this is about the options open
to you if it ever happens.

What the law says if it comes to force in your own home is lesson 14's subject, "The home and
property". This lesson states none of it.

## The weeks after a burglary

That "offenders take later advantage of opportunities which the first offence throws up".[11] So the way they got in comes first, and soon. That order is this course's step from Pease's finding, and it's sensible, untested, as lesson 3 labelled it.
:::

Tseloni's paper repeats, citing earlier research, that a previous burglary is the strongest single
predictor of the next one, and it suggests that the WIDE devices be targeted on the households most at
risk, which includes those burgled already.[1]

## The doorstep

Pushing past whoever opened the door and false pretences turn up in the England and Wales tables every
year. In the US report, among burglaries of occupied homes where no force was needed, nearly a fifth
began with someone inside letting the offender in; the table doesn't say whether those let in were
strangers with a story or people the household knew. Beside the forced entries whose fall opened this
lesson, Tseloni's paper puts entries by a key, pushing past, or false pretences in a third group,
"other", and its text gives no trend for it. The point that follows is this course's, not the paper's:
**a lock only works on someone who has to get past it, and a con at the door gets you to open it.**

So the door itself is where the second half of home security lives. The Suzy Lamplugh Trust's advice
on callers is plain, and it's practitioner opinion, in this course's labels.[8] Use the spy hole,
chain, intercom or outside light if you have them, to see who is there. If someone says they
represent a company or organisation, ask for ID. And never let the ID check itself: "Use the
telephone number given in the phone book or on your utility bill, rather than the one on the identity
card." The same page explains why the back of the house matters too: "a bogus caller will try and
distract you whilst an accomplice robs you, often by entering through a back door." That's what's
called a distraction burglary: one person keeps you at the front while another gets in. So lock the
other doors before you answer.

[The US National Crime Prevention Council's page on home and neighbourhood safety](https://www.ncpc.org/resources/home-neighborhood-safety/)
adds two habits that fit the same picture, also practitioner opinion: keep a spare key with a
trusted neighbour, "not under a doormat or planter", and "Illuminate or eliminate places an intruder
might hide".[12]

If a caller has ever talked their way past you, the con worked as it was built to. The person who ran
it is responsible, and what follows is about the options open next time.

### Harold and the man from the water company

Harold is 79 and lives alone. Mid-morning, a man rings the bell. He says he's from the water company,
that there has been a problem with the mains in the street, and he needs to check the kitchen tap
straight away. Harold can see a second man standing by his back gate.

Nothing has gone wrong yet. Here's how it can go, using the Lamplugh steps, in words he could say.

**First, the door stays between them.** If Harold has a chain, he puts it on. He can talk through the
gap or through the door. The Lamplugh advice is to see who is there before opening at all.

**Second, the back.** A second man by the back gate is the pattern the Lamplugh page warns about: one
at the front to distract, one at the back. So before anything else, the back door gets locked. Harold
can say, "Wait there a moment," and do that first.

**Third, the ID, and the call-back.** "Can I see your ID? Thank you. I'm going to ring the water
company on the number on my bill. Please wait outside." Then he closes the door and phones the number
printed on his own bill, not any number on the card or any number the man gives him.

**Fourth, what the answer tells him.** If the company confirms a visit, the man has waited and lost a
few minutes. If it doesn't, the door stays shut. If either man tries to get in, that's a crime in
progress and a 999 call. If they simply leave, GOV.UK's route for a crime that is not an emergency is
to report it online or on 101.[10]

:::checkpoint The man says, "I haven't got time for you to ring the office, the whole street's supply is at risk." What does that sentence tell Harold, and what does he do?
It's pressure, the FTC's third sign in the next section. A genuine caller can wait a few minutes on a doorstep while he checks; one who can't wait for a check is telling Harold something. Harold keeps the door closed and makes the call anyway.
:::

How to label the steps: the call-back rule is **sensible, untested**. The Lamplugh Trust gives it for
the door and the FTC for messages, but nobody has run a trial of it. The accomplice warning and the ID
steps are practitioner opinion.

## One pattern: the door and the phone

The man at Harold's door and the voice on the phone saying your bank account is compromised are
running the same play, and two official bodies describe it in nearly the same terms.

[The US Federal Trade Commission's page on avoiding scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-avoid-scam)
names four signs: "Scammers PRETEND to be from an organization you know"; "Scammers say there's a
PROBLEM or a PRIZE"; "Scammers PRESSURE you to act immediately"; "Scammers tell you to PAY in a
specific way".[13] Its advice: "Resist the pressure to act immediately. Honest businesses will give
you time to make a decision." And for a message that seems to come from a company you deal with, it
says to look up their number yourself rather than call a number they gave you or the one on your
caller ID.
[Take Five](https://web.archive.org/web/20260902060025/https://www.takefive-stopfraud.org.uk/), a
UK campaign led by UK Finance, the banking industry's trade body, says it in two sentences: "It's ok
to reject, refuse or ignore any requests. Only criminals will try to rush or panic you."[14]

Run Harold's caller through the FTC's four. He pretended to be from an organisation Harold knows. He
brought a problem. He pressed for now. The FTC's fourth sign is about how you pay; at the door, on
this course's reading, the payment is getting inside. **Urgency is the tell, and the call-back rule
is the answer**, at the door exactly as on the phone: end the contact, and reach the organisation by
a number you already had.

Two courses before this one teach the con itself in depth, and this lesson leaves them there:
Personal Finance Fundamentals lesson 10, "Defending it", on what nearly every scam has to do, and
Digital Literacy lesson 11, "Recognising a con, and the ten minutes after you clicked", on doing the
thing by your own route.

### Where to report a con

Attempted or successful, a con is worth reporting. In the UK, Take Five advises contacting your bank
at once if you think you've been scammed, which is practitioner opinion, in this course's labels.[14]

- **In England, Wales and Northern Ireland,** the police's reporting service is
  [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/), online or on 0300 123 2040.[15]
- **In Scotland,** Report Fraud's own site says to report via 101.[15]
- **In the US,** the FTC takes reports at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/), and
  says it can't resolve individual reports but shares them with law enforcement.[15]

A distraction burglary, where someone gets in, is a burglary, and a crime in progress is a 999 or 911
call. Lesson 16, "Afterwards", covers what reporting does and does not get you.

## Ruth and Imran, a month later

Now a case with every thread in it. Ruth and Imran came home from work five weeks ago to find the
lock on their back door forced and their laptops gone. The back door still has the old lock, pushed
back into place. One evening a salesman knocks. He is selling alarm systems, he has heard about their
burglary, and he tells them it proves they need an alarm. The price is lower if they sign tonight.

Nothing in this case says they caused the first burglary. The question is only what the evidence
says about the choices in front of them now.

**Thread 1: the risk is real, and it is now.** Pease's review, and the earlier research Tseloni's
paper cites, say that a household burgled once is at higher risk, and soon. The salesman is right
about that much.

**Thread 2: what closes the opportunity.** The first burglary showed someone a back door that could be
forced. On Pease's mechanism, that's the change to make first, and it's a lock, not an alarm: this
course's step, sensible, untested. Then the rest of WIDE: window locks, a timer for an indoor light,
an outside sensor light.

:::checkpoint Thread 3 is the alarm. Before reading on, work it out: what can the evidence in this lesson tell Ruth and Imran about whether an alarm will protect their home, and why is their situation the hard case?
Whether an alarm protects a home is contested, and their situation is the exact one that makes it hard to settle: a household fitting an alarm right after a burglary is the kind the timing explanation describes. An alarm might help them; the England and Wales data can't say that it will, and the US studies, which measured something else, can't settle it for one house either. Either choice is reasonable, as long as the locks come first. If your answer said the alarm will or won't work, go back to "Can both be right?".
:::

**Thread 4: the sale.** Tonight-only pricing is pressure, the FTC's third sign, and the FTC's answer
applies: an honest business will give them time to decide. They can take a leaflet, close the door,
and look up the company themselves, or other companies, whenever they like.

So the evidence gives them a clear first step, a real choice about the second, and one thing to
refuse, which is the deadline.

## What people get wrong

**"An alarm is the best protection."** In England and Wales survey data, alarms were not among the
devices associated with lower risk, and one study found them associated with higher risk. Two US
studies, one asking burglars and one comparing neighbourhoods, point the other way. It's contested.
Locks and lights in combination are not.

**"Alarms attract burglars."** The England and Wales finding is an association that its authors call
counter-intuitive and warn against over-reading, and the idea that an alarm flags something worth
stealing is one of several explanations they give, none tested.

**"Burglars attack people at home."** Violence is the minority case (7% of US household burglaries in
2003 to 2007), though in England and Wales threat or force is common when someone at home sees the
burglar.

**"An ID card proves who they are."** The Lamplugh advice is to check the card, not trust it: a
call to the organisation on a number you already had, from your bill, not the one on the card.

**"If I hear someone downstairs I should go and check."** The sources read say call the police
instead, and don't go in if you think someone has been inside. That's advice for next time, not a
judgement on anyone who went to look.

## Practice

:::exercise Audit, script, numbers
Take 25 minutes over these, on paper.

1. **A WIDE audit of your own home.** Walk round and look. For each of the four devices, write yes, no or partly: window locks (on which windows?), an indoor light on a timer, double or deadlocks on each outside door, external lights on a sensor. Look, list and price. Don't test any lock, window or door by forcing it or trying to get in. If you rent, turn the list into a short written request to your landlord, with the reason for each item in one line. Add two lines: which door or window you'd fix first if you were burgled tomorrow, and why, and where your spare key is.

2. **A doorstep script.** Write what you would say, word for word, to a caller who claims to be from a company and needs to come in, ending with the call-back. Then say it aloud, to yourself or to a willing friend. Don't phone any company, police force or helpline to practise it.

3. **Your call-back numbers.** From a bill, card or letter you already hold, write down the numbers for your water, gas or electricity supplier, your bank, and your council. Not from a search, and never from a card someone hands you at the door.

If you live with someone who frightens you, a list of locks does not cover the risk that matters, and it's better not written where they could find it. The lesson on domestic abuse and stalking starts with the helplines.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your audit and script against these.
The audit: if you have window and door locks but no lights, you're in the survey's SPF 13 group, not the WIDE group. The next step the survey figures point to is an external light on a sensor (SPF 34 with the two locks), and a timer for an indoor light is the one you can add without a landlord. For the door or window you'd fix first, the answer the evidence supports is the way a burglar would have got in, fixed first, and a spare key under a mat or a planter is the one the US crime prevention council names.

A model script: "Hello. Can you show me your ID through the window, please? Thank you. I'm going to ring the company on the number on my bill. Please wait outside." Then close the door, lock the back if it is open, and call. If your script had you checking the number on their card, or letting them in while you checked, move the call to before the door opens.
:::
