Burglary sits in an odd place among the crimes people fear. It is far from the worst harm this
course deals with, yet lesson 1 showed it topping a city's fear rankings above murder, because it
seems serious enough and likely enough at once. It is also where the strongest evidence in this whole
course is. A long run of survey data from England and Wales lets researchers compare households
that had particular locks and lights with households that had none, and the difference is large. This
lesson teaches that evidence and its limits, sets out the one piece of home security that is
genuinely disputed, says what the data show about burglaries with someone at home, and ends at the
front door, where the con artist and the burglar turn out to use the same trick.

:::callout Before you rely on this
This course is education, not legal advice, and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger now, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US. On a 999 call from a mobile, if you can't speak, cough or tap if you can and press 55 when you're prompted; in the US you can text 911 where that's available, but call if you can. If a partner, ex-partner or family member is frightening you, the lesson on domestic abuse and stalking starts with the helplines. Responsibility for a crime lies with the person who commits it: precautions change the odds, not the blame. The law differs between England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

That responsibility sentence matters more than usual here, because home security is the place where
"you should have locked it" comes most easily to people's lips. Nothing in this lesson says a
household that was burgled did something wrong. A lock changes how hard a house is to get into. It has
nothing to do with who chose to break in.

## What made burglary fall

In the 1990s burglary in England and Wales began a long fall, and criminologists argued about why.
Tseloni, Farrell, Tilley and colleagues tested one explanation, the security
hypothesis: that households fitted better locks and lights, and burglars found fewer houses they
could get into. Their 2017 paper in *Crime Science* used the Crime Survey for England and Wales from
1992 to 2011/12, which asks households both whether they were burgled and which security devices
they had.[1]

:::predict The survey sorts burglaries by how the burglar got in. Some were forced: a lock or window forced, glass broken, a door panel removed. Others were unforced: a door or window left open or unlocked. If better security caused the fall, which kind would you expect to have fallen, and which to have stayed about the same?
Forced entries should fall and unforced ones should stay flat, because a lock only makes a difference to a burglar who has to get past it. That's what the paper found. Burglaries with forced entry dropped from 31 per 1,000 households in 1993 to 7 per 1,000 in 2006/07, a fall of 77%, while unforced burglaries stayed at 7 per 1,000 over the same years.
:::

That pattern is the mechanism, and the paper gives it itself: the decline was "solely in forced rather
than unforced entries to households", and the main drop came when the house was empty, which is when
locks are locked and timer lights are on.[1] Put with the rest of their evidence, the authors wrote:
"The study concludes that there is strong evidence that security caused the decline in burglary in
England and Wales in the 1990s."[1]

Notice what kind of evidence that is. Nobody randomly assigned locks to houses. The authors built a
case from several patterns in survey data that all point the same way: security spread just before
burglary fell, the households with no security became far riskier relative to everyone else, and
the fall happened only where security had to be overcome. This is a strong comparison rather than a
trial, which is why lesson 1 used it as the example of the label **tested**: a strong comparison
that measured the outcome that matters.

## WIDE, and what "almost 50 times" means

The paper also reports which devices did the work, using figures from the same team's analysis of the
2008/09 to 2011/12 surveys. The measure is the security protection factor (SPF). It compares the
burglary risk of households with a given device or set of devices against households with "no
security", meaning none of the devices the survey asked about. An SPF of 13 means households with
those devices had about a thirteenth of the risk of burglary with entry that no-security households
had.[1]

Single devices did little on their own. Window locks had an SPF of 7; indoor lights on a timer,
double or deadlocks on the doors, and external lights on a sensor had 3 each. Combinations did far
more than their parts added up to:[1]

| Devices | SPF |
|---|---|
| Window locks and door double or deadlocks | 13 |
| Those two plus external lights on a sensor | 34 |
| All four: window locks, indoor lights on a timer, door double or deadlocks, external lights on a sensor | 49 |

The four together are what the team calls WIDE: "properties fitted with the four 'WIDE' devices:
window locks (W), indoor lights on a timer (I), door double or deadlocks (D) and external lights on a
sensor (E) have an SPF of 49."[1] The four single SPFs add up to 16, and the combination is 49. In
the discussion the authors put it plainly: WIDE produces "a very substantial (almost 50 times) lower
risk of burglary with entry than 'no security'".[1]

This is the most useful number in the course, and it's easy to misread in three ways.

**It's against no security at all.** The comparison group is households with none of the listed
devices, and by 2008/09 to 2011/12 that was under one household in twenty (4.9%), down from about
one in six in 1992 to 1996. Those households had become nearly eight times as likely as the average
household to be burgled.[1] Most homes already have some of the devices. So "almost 50 times" isn't
what adding WIDE to a typical home does, and "WIDE cuts your risk by 98%" is the wrong sentence: it
takes a comparison against the rarest and riskiest group and turns it into a promise about yours.

**It's a comparison between households, not a trial.** Households that fit four devices may differ
in other ways from households that fit none. The team's wider case is what makes them confident the
devices themselves matter, but no one put WIDE on some homes and not others and waited.

**It counts devices, not how good they are or whether they were used.** In the authors' words, the
survey "has asked only about the presence or absence of security devices. It has not been designed to
capture their quality, nor whether the devices were in use at the time of the victimisation."[1] A
window lock left unlocked every night is still a window lock in the survey. They add that the survey could not test
measures aimed at whole areas (planning standards for new housing, street lighting, gates across the
alleys behind houses), so security's full role may be "understated".[1]

:::checkpoint A neighbour reads a headline that says "four gadgets make you 50 times safer from burglars". Write the sentence the study actually supports.
Something like: "In England and Wales survey data from 2008/09 to 2011/12, households with window locks, indoor lights on a timer, door double or deadlocks and external lights on a sensor had about a forty-ninth of the risk of burglary with entry of households with none of the security devices the survey asked about; it's a comparison between households, it counts whether devices were there rather than how good they were or whether they were used, and it's about burglary with entry, not about your safety in general." If your sentence kept "against no security" and "between households", it has the two parts headlines drop.
:::

### Callum and Sian ask the landlord

Callum and Sian are moving into a rented ground-floor flat in Lincoln. The front door has a latch but
no deadlock. The windows close with catches, not locks. There's no outside light, and neither of them
owns a plug-in timer. They want to know what to ask the landlord for and what to do themselves.

**Step 1: list what the flat has against WIDE.** Window locks: no. Indoor lights on a timer: no. Door
double or deadlock: no. External light on a sensor: no. By the survey's definition, the flat as it
stands is a no-security household.

**Step 2: sort who does what.** The locks and the outside light are fixtures, so they are for the
landlord to agree to. A timer for an indoor lamp is a small thing the tenants can buy and plug in
themselves. The authors themselves suggest that minimum standards set for landlords are one way to
get these devices to the people most at risk, which gives the request some backing, though it is
their suggestion, not a rule anywhere this course read.[1]

**Step 3: price it.** They look up, for their own area, what a locksmith or the landlord's usual
contractor would charge for a deadlock and window locks, and what an outside sensor light costs to
fit. This course names no products and no prices; fitting a lock is Home Repair and Maintenance's,
the course after this one.

**Step 4: label it.** Each request is tested, in this course's labels, in the sense lesson 1 gave:
a strong comparison between households in England and Wales measured burglary with entry, against
households with no security at all.

:::checkpoint The landlord replies that he will fit window locks and a deadlock, but calls the lights "extras". Using the table, what can Callum and Sian fairly say about the lights, and what is beyond them?
What they can say: in the survey data, households with window and door locks alone had an SPF of 13, and households with all four devices had 49. So the four-device households had roughly a quarter of the risk of the two-device households (13 divided by 49 is about 0.27). That arithmetic is this course's, done on the paper's figures. What they can't say: that adding the two lights will cut their own risk by three-quarters. The figures compare different households, not the same flat before and after, and they count devices that were present, not used. Their honest line to the landlord is that the combination did much better than locks alone in the national data, and that the timer is theirs to supply anyway.
:::

## The alarm dispute

Look back at the table. There's no burglar alarm in it. That's not an oversight.

In the same England and Wales data, the 2017 paper reports that "somewhat counterintuitively, alarms
were not identified as contributory", and it recommends caution: "It would be prudent to think twice
before recommending an alarm as part of a standard package of security devices to reduce the risk of
domestic burglary."[1] A 2015 paper by Tilley, Tseloni and colleagues went further. From its
abstract: "It finds that alarms have become associated with increased rather than decreased risk of
burglary with entry. This counter-intuitive finding needs to be treated cautiously."[2]

:::predict Households with alarms were, on average, more likely to have been burgled. Before reading on, think of an explanation for that which doesn't involve alarms attracting burglars.
The one the authors call plausible is timing. Many households fit an alarm after a burglary, and a household burgled once is at higher risk of being burgled again (lesson 3's repeat victimisation). So an alarm may mark a home that was already a target rather than make it one. If you thought of that, you reasoned the way the authors did.
:::

Tilley and colleagues offered several explanations, and they tested none of them. The one they call
plausible is the latent repeat victim: the alarm goes in after a burglary, so alarmed homes
include many already-burgled homes. The others are that offenders and police have adapted (police no
longer always attend an alarm), that an alarm can signal something worth stealing inside, and that
many alarms are cheap or unmonitored.[2] Each is a hypothesis. Nobody has yet shown which, if any,
is right.

The other side of the question comes from the US, and it asks burglars. A team at the University of
North Carolina at Charlotte surveyed people convicted of burglary for a 2012 report, *Understanding
Decisions to Burglarize from the Offender's Perspective*. This course read [the university's release
about it](https://inside.charlotte.edu/news-features/2013-05-15/through-eyes-burglar-study-provides-insights-habits-and-motivations/),
not the report. From the release: "Approximately 83 percent said they would try to determine if an
alarm was present before attempting a burglary, and 60 percent said they would seek an alternative
target if there was an alarm on-site."[3] A Rutgers study of burglar alarms in Newark, New Jersey,
by S. Lee, looked at whole neighbourhoods instead of single homes. Again this course read only
[the university's release](https://www.rutgers.edu/news/rutgers-study-finds-alarm-systems-are-valuable-crime-fighting-tool),
which reports that "Neighborhoods in which burglar alarms were densely installed have fewer incidents
of residential burglaries than the neighborhoods with fewer burglar alarms".[4] Its
advisory committee included Felson and Clarke, whose work lesson 4 was built on, with Clarke as its chair.[4]

Both US studies were funded by the Alarm Industry Research and Educational Foundation, which the
Charlotte release describes as working under the auspices of the largest trade association for the
electronic security industry.[3][4] You should know that, as you should know who paid for any
study. It doesn't tell you the results are wrong, and this course makes no such suggestion. Industry
often pays for research into its own products, and the way to judge a study is by its design.

### Can both be right?

Yes, and seeing how is the useful part. The two sides measured different things. Burglars asked in
prison said what they would do on meeting an alarm. The England and Wales survey measured which
households, with and without alarms, were actually burgled. An alarm might put some burglars off a
house while alarmed houses, on average, are still riskier because so many of them were targets before
the alarm went in. The US studies are also from a different country, with different alarms and
police response, from the England and Wales data.

So whether an alarm protects a home is a **contested** question in this course's labels. What would
settle it is a study that records when each alarm was fitted relative to any earlier burglary, and
follows households afterwards, or better, one that fits alarms to some homes at random and not to
others. The consensus on locks and lights is much firmer. If money is short, the evidence says spend
it on the combination in the table first. That's the course's reading of the England and Wales
evidence; an alarm on top is a reasonable choice that the evidence neither supports nor rules out.

## How burglars get in, and whether anyone's home

:::predict Think of burglaries in England and Wales where the burglar actually got inside. In roughly what share was somebody at home at the time?
More than half. In the Crime Survey for England and Wales, someone was at home in over half of burglaries with entry in every year's table from the year ending March 2015 to the year ending March 2025. If you guessed low, you're in good company: "burglars wait until the house is empty" is one of the commonest beliefs about burglary.
:::

The current figures come from the Office for National Statistics' tables on the nature of burglary,
which run from the year ending March 2015 to the year ending March 2025 (there are no survey data for
the two pandemic years).[5] They count household incidents of domestic burglary in a dwelling where the
burglar got in, and each year's figure rests on the incidents the survey's sample picked up, between
about 130 and 330 a year. So look at the range across the years rather than at any single year,
which is lesson 2's rule about one year and a trend. ONS's 2017 overview, for the year to March
2017, gave the same picture in words, including that the offender was "almost equally likely" to be
a stranger or someone the victim knew.[6]

- **Someone was at home** in between 51% and 68% of burglaries with entry each year.
- **The way in** was a door in about two-thirds to four-fifths of them, and a window in about a
  fifth to three in ten.
- **The door was not locked** in between about a fifth and a third of them (18% to 35%).
- **The burglar pushed past whoever opened the door** in between 7% and 12%, and **got in by false
  pretences** in between 2% and 8%.
- **Stranger or known**: among incidents where the victim could say anything about the offender,
  about half of them, the offender was a stranger in between about two-fifths and a little over half,
  and known, by sight or well, in the rest.

The US picture comes from an older report. The Bureau of Justice Statistics looked at the National
Crime Victimization Survey for 2003 to 2007 and found: "In about 28% of these burglaries, a household
member was present during the burglary. In 7% of all household burglaries, a household member
experienced some form of violent victimization."[7] "In 40% of unlawful entries to unoccupied
residences, offenders gained access through an unlocked door or window". When someone was at home,
18% of respondents said someone inside let the offender in, and 12% said someone opened the door and
the offender pushed their way in. "Offenders were known to their victims in 65% of violent
burglaries; offenders were strangers in 28%."[7]

Here are the two "someone at home" figures side by side. They are different measures, and this course
keeps them apart:

| | England and Wales | US |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Crime Survey for England and Wales (ONS) | National Crime Victimization Survey (BJS report by Catalano) |
| Years | Year ending March 2015 to year ending March 2025 | 2003 to 2007, average |
| What's counted | Domestic burglaries in a dwelling where the burglar got in | Household burglaries, including those where a household member suffered violence |
| Someone at home | Over half in every year (51% to 68%) | About 28% |

The definitions and periods differ, so the gap between the two columns isn't a finding about the two
countries. What each column says within itself is the finding: in England and Wales, someone's at home
more often than not; in the US data, in more than a quarter of cases.

### Two slogans, both wrong

That settles two popular beliefs that point in opposite directions.

"Burglars strike when nobody's home." In England and Wales, more often than not, someone was. You may
meet a respectable version of this: lesson 1's Warr wrote in 2000 that "burglaries normally occur when
no one is home".[8] That was a summary of older US research, and it is out of line with the current
England and Wales tables and with the US report above.

"Burglars attack people at home." Being home is common; violence isn't the usual outcome. In the US
data, a household member suffered violence in 7% of all household burglaries.[7] In England and
Wales the survey asks a narrower question. Where someone at home knew about the burglary and saw the
burglar (between about a fifth and a third of burglaries with entry each year), more than half of
those incidents involved some threat or use of force in every year's table. Each year rests on fewer
than 90 such incidents, and ONS says estimates based on fewer than 50 cases, as several of these
are, should be treated with caution.[5] Read carefully, it is still a clear signal about the moment
it matters: meeting a burglar face to face is when threat and force come in.

:::checkpoint Lesson 2 gave you five questions for any crime figure: who counted it, what it counts, whose harm, when, and per how many. Answer them for "someone was at home in about 28% of burglaries".
Who: the US Bureau of Justice Statistics, from the National Crime Victimization Survey, in a 2010 report. What: household burglaries, a survey estimate, so including burglaries never reported to police, and including those where a household member suffered violence. Whose: US households. When: 2003 to 2007, averaged. Per how many: about 28 in every 100 such burglaries. Put the same questions to the England and Wales figure and every answer differs, which is why the table above keeps them in separate columns.

## If you think someone is inside

This is the one place in the lesson where the advice is short, and the two UK sources read on it
agree.

The Suzy Lamplugh Trust, the UK personal safety charity lesson 4 met: "If you are in your home and
think there is an intruder, call the police – don't go and investigate by yourself." And: "If you
come home and suspect an intruder has been inside, don't enter the property."[9] Its page adds that
you can use a mobile or a neighbour's phone for the call. The leaflet for householders written by the
Crown Prosecution Service and the National Police Chiefs' Council, which is mostly about the law,
says: "Wherever possible you should call the police."[10]

That's a crime in progress, which is exactly the case GOV.UK's page on contacting the police gives
for 999: "Call 999 if you are reporting a crime that is in progress or if someone is in immediate
danger."[11] In the US, the Office for Victims of Crime says to call 911 if you feel in immediate
danger.[11] The callout at the top of this lesson has the silent-call steps for both.

How to label it: none of the sources read tested the advice, so it isn't tested. It's **sensible,
untested**, and the survey figures above are the evidence nearby: when a person at home sees a
burglar, threat or force is common, and nothing is gained by walking into that room to find out.

What the law says if it comes to force in your own home is lesson 14's subject, "The home, property,
and carrying something for protection". This lesson states none of it.

## The weeks after a burglary

Lesson 3 gave you Ken Pease's finding that repeat victimisation comes quickly, and his mechanism:
"offenders take later advantage of opportunities which the first offence throws up".[12] Tseloni's
paper repeats, citing earlier research, that a previous burglary is the strongest single predictor
of the next one, and it suggests that the WIDE devices be targeted on exactly the households that are
most at risk, which includes those burgled already.[1]

Put the two together and the practical order is clear. **Fix the way they got in first.** If the back
door's lock was forced, that lock comes before anything else, because it is the opportunity the first
burglary showed someone. Then the rest of WIDE. Label it sensible, untested, as lesson 3 did: nobody
has compared households that fixed the lock that week with households that waited, but the risk is
highest soon after, and the sources say why.

The belief to drop is "it won't happen again". Lightning is the wrong model. A burglary tells the person
who did it something about a house that stays true until somebody changes it.

## The doorstep

Look back at the England and Wales list. Pushing past whoever opened the door and getting in by
false pretences together turn up in a noticeable share of burglaries every year, and in the US report
nearly a fifth of burglaries of occupied homes involved someone inside letting the offender in. And
remember Tseloni's forced-entry figures: locks cut the burglaries where something had to be
forced. The paper puts entries by a key, pushing past, or false pretences in a third group, "other",
and its text gives no trend for it. The point that follows is this course's, not the paper's: **a
lock only works on someone who has to get past it, and a con at the door gets you to open it.**

So the door itself is where the second half of home security lives. The Suzy Lamplugh Trust's advice
on callers is plain.[9] Use the spy hole, chain, intercom or outside light if you have them, to see who is
there. If someone says they represent a company or organisation, ask for ID. And never let the ID
check itself: "Use the telephone number given in the phone book or on your utility bill, rather than
the one on the identity card." The same page explains why the back of the house matters too: "a bogus
caller will try and distract you whilst an accomplice robs you, often by entering through a back
door." So lock the other doors before you answer.

The US National Crime Prevention Council's home page adds two habits that fit the same picture: keep
a spare key with a trusted neighbour, "not under a doormat or planter", and "Illuminate or eliminate
places an intruder might hide".[13]

### Harold and the man from the water company

Harold is 79 and lives alone. Mid-morning, a man rings the bell. He says he's from the water company,
that there has been a problem with the mains in the street, and he needs to check the kitchen tap straight
away. Harold can see a second man standing by his back gate.

Nothing has gone wrong yet, and Harold has several options now. Walk it forward with him, using the
sources, and say it as a script he could actually use.

**First, the door stays between them.** If Harold has a chain, he puts it on. He can talk through the gap
or through the door. The Lamplugh advice is to see who is there before opening at all.

**Second, the back.** A second man by the back gate is the pattern the Lamplugh page warns about: one
at the front to distract, one at the back. So before anything else, the back door gets locked. Harold
can say, "Wait there a moment," and do that first.

**Third, the ID, and the call-back.** "Can I see your ID? Thank you. I'm going to ring the water
company on the number on my bill. Please wait outside." Then he closes the door and phones the number
printed on his own bill, not any number on the card or any number the man gives him.

**Fourth, what the answer tells him.** If the company confirms a visit, the man has waited and lost a
few minutes. If it doesn't, the door stays shut. If either man tries to get in, that's a crime in
progress and a 999 call. If they simply leave, GOV.UK's route for a crime that is not an emergency is
to report it online or on 101.[11]

:::checkpoint The man says, "I haven't got time for you to ring the office, the whole street's supply is at risk." What does that sentence tell Harold, and what does he do?
It's pressure, and pressure is the one thing a con can't do without. A real worker can wait five minutes on a doorstep; the only caller who cannot afford a check is one who would fail it. Harold keeps the door closed and makes the call anyway. The urgency is a reason to check, not a reason to skip checking.
:::

How to label the steps: the call-back rule is **sensible, untested**. The Lamplugh Trust gives it for
the door and the FTC for messages, and it follows directly from how a con works, which is the next section, but nobody has run a
trial of it. The accomplice warning and the ID steps are the Lamplugh Trust's practitioner advice.

## One pattern: the door and the phone

The man at Harold's door and the voice on the phone saying your bank account is compromised are
running the same play, and two official bodies describe it in nearly the same terms.

The US Federal Trade Commission's page on avoiding scams names four signs: "Scammers PRETEND to be from
an organization you know"; "Scammers say there's a PROBLEM or a PRIZE"; "Scammers PRESSURE you to act
immediately"; "Scammers tell you to PAY in a specific way".[14] Its advice: "Resist the pressure to act
immediately. Honest businesses will give you time to make a decision." And for a message that seems to come from a company you deal with, it says to
look up their number yourself rather than call a number they gave you or the one on your caller
ID. Take Five, a UK campaign led by UK Finance, says it in two sentences: "It's ok to
reject, refuse or ignore any requests. Only criminals will try to rush or panic you."[15]

Run Harold's caller through the FTC's four. He pretended to be from an organisation Harold knows. He
brought a problem. He pressed for now. The "payment" at the door is getting inside. **Urgency is the
tell, and the call-back rule is the answer**, at the door exactly as on the phone: end the contact,
and reach the organisation by a number you already had.

Two courses before this one teach the con itself in depth, and this lesson leaves them there.
Personal Finance Fundamentals lesson 10, "Defending it", teaches the three things nearly every scam
has to do, why the "safe account" is always fake, and why a caller who knows your details or shows
your bank's number proves nothing. Digital Literacy lesson 11, "Recognising a con, and the ten
minutes after you clicked", teaches the con's structure and the move of doing the thing by your own
route. The doorstep is the same con with a face on it.

**Where to report a con**, attempted or successful. Take Five's advice is to contact your bank at
once if you think you've been scammed.[15] In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, the police's
reporting service is Report Fraud, at reportfraud.police.uk or on 0300 123 2040; its own site says
people in Scotland should report via 101.[16] In the US, the FTC takes reports at ReportFraud.ftc.gov,
and says it can't resolve individual reports but shares them with law enforcement.[16] A distraction
burglary, where someone gets in, is a burglary, and a crime in progress is a 999 or 911 call. Lesson
15, "Afterwards", covers what reporting does and does not get you.

## Ruth and Imran, a month later

Now a case with every thread in it. Ruth and Imran came home from work five weeks ago to find the
lock on their back door forced and their laptops gone. The back door still has the old lock, pushed
back into place. One evening a salesman knocks. He is selling alarm systems, he has heard about their
burglary, and he tells them it proves they need an alarm. The price is lower if they sign tonight.

Nothing in this case says they caused the first burglary. The question is only what the evidence
says about the choices in front of them now.

**Thread 1: the risk is real, and it is now.** Pease's review, and the earlier research Tseloni's
paper cites, say that a household burgled once is at higher risk, and soon. The salesman is right about that much.

**Thread 2: what closes the opportunity.** The first burglary showed someone a back door that could be
forced. On Pease's mechanism, that's the change to make first, and it's a lock, not an alarm. Then
the rest of WIDE: window locks, a timer for an indoor light, an outside sensor light.

**Thread 3: the alarm.** Whether an alarm protects a home is contested, and their situation is the
exact one that makes it hard to settle: a household fitting an alarm right after a burglary is the
kind Tilley and colleagues had in mind with the latent repeat victim explanation. An alarm might help them; the England and
Wales data can't say that it will. Either choice is reasonable.

**Thread 4: the sale.** Tonight-only pricing is pressure, the FTC's third sign, and the FTC's answer
applies: an honest business will give them time to decide. They can take a leaflet, close the door,
and look up the company themselves, or other companies, whenever they like.

So the evidence gives them a clear first step, a genuine choice about the second, and one thing to
refuse, which is the deadline.

## What people get wrong

**"An alarm is the best protection."** In England and Wales survey data, alarms were not among the
devices associated with lower risk, and one study found them associated with higher risk. US studies
funded by the alarm industry point the other way. It's contested. Locks and lights in combination are
not.

**"Burglars strike when nobody's home."** In England and Wales, someone was at home in more than half
of burglaries with entry, in every year's table from 2015 to 2025.

**"Burglars attack people at home."** Violence is the minority case (7% of US household burglaries in
2003 to 2007). But when someone at home meets the burglar, threat or force is common, which is the
reason not to go and look.

**"An ID card proves who they are."** A card is the easiest thing in the con to fake. The check is a
call to a number you already had, from your bill.

**"If I hear someone downstairs I should go and check."** The UK sources read say call the police
instead, and don't go in if you think someone has been inside.

**"It won't happen again."** A first burglary is the best single predictor of a second, and the risk
is highest soon after.

## Practice

:::exercise Audit, script, numbers
Take 30 minutes over these, on paper.

1. **A WIDE audit of your own home.** Walk round and look. For each of the four devices, write yes, no or partly: window locks (on which windows?), an indoor light on a timer, double or deadlocks on each outside door, external lights on a sensor. Look, list and price. Don't test any lock, window or door by forcing it or trying to get in. If you rent, turn the list into a short written request to your landlord, with the reason for each item in one line.

2. **Two more lines on the list.** Which door or window would you fix first if you were burgled tomorrow, and why? And where's your spare key?

3. **A doorstep script.** Write what you would say, word for word, to a caller who claims to be from a company and needs to come in, ending with the call-back. Then say it aloud, to yourself or to a willing friend. Don't phone any company, police force or helpline to practise it.

4. **Your call-back numbers.** From a bill, card or letter you already hold, write down the numbers for your water, gas or electricity supplier, your bank, and your council. Not from a search, and never from a card someone hands you at the door.

If you live with someone who frightens you, a list of locks does not cover the risk that matters, and it's better not written where they could find it. The lesson on domestic abuse and stalking starts with the helplines.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your audit and script against these.
The audit: if you have window and door locks but no lights, you're in the survey's SPF 13 group, not the WIDE group; the cheapest step is usually a plug-in timer, which needs no landlord. For question 2, the answer the evidence supports is the way a burglar would have got in, fixed first, and a spare key under a mat or a planter is the one the US crime prevention council names.

A model script: "Hello. Can you show me your ID through the window, please? Thank you. I'm going to ring the company on the number on my bill. Please wait outside." Then close the door, lock the back if it is open, and call. If your script had you checking the number on their card, or letting them in while you checked, move the call to before the door opens.
:::
