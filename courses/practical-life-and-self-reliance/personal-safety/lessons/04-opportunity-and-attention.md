You can't change who's out tonight. You can change some of the circumstances you walk into: where
you are, when, who's with you, what you carry, and how much of your attention is on the
street rather than a screen. Criminologists have a theory built on exactly that, and it's the most
useful tool this course has for your own risk. Alongside it comes advice sold with more
confidence than the research can carry: "be aware", "walk with purpose", "trust your gut". Each of
those gets taken apart here, with the strength of the evidence under it.

:::callout Before you rely on this
This course is education, not legal advice, and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger now, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US. On a 999 call from a mobile, if you can't speak, cough or tap if you can and press 55 when you're prompted; in the US you can text 911 where that's available, but call if you can. If a partner, ex-partner or family member is frightening you, the lesson on domestic abuse and stalking starts with the helplines. Responsibility for a crime lies with the person who commits it: precautions change the odds, not the blame. The law differs between England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

Lesson 3, "Who is harmed, by whom, where and when", gave you the measured risks. This lesson asks
what part of a risk is yours to change. A frame for lowering your own odds is not a verdict on
anyone who was harmed. The research below is easy to turn into one, and wherever it could be, the
lesson says why the evidence can't carry it.

## Three things that have to meet

:::predict In 1979 two sociologists set out to explain why crime in the US rose so much between 1947 and 1974. They didn't look for more criminals. What would you look for instead?
A change in how ordinary people lived. They pointed to the spread of daily activity away from home and family: on their account, changes such as more adults in paid work and more people living alone put more people and property out in the world and left fewer people at home to watch over it. The data they gave included changes in labour force participation and in single-adult households. If you looked at daily life rather than at who offends, you already made the theory's main move.
:::

Lawrence Cohen and Marcus Felson called it a routine activity approach, and their [1979
paper](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2094589) in the *American Sociological Review* states the idea
in one sentence: "Most criminal acts require convergence in space and time of likely offenders,
suitable targets and the absence of capable guardians against crime."[1] Three things have to meet
at one place and one time. For what they called direct-contact predatory crime, they argued, take
any one of the three away and that crime, there and then, can't be completed.[1]

The paper also made a deliberate turn, which is the reason this course uses the theory: "Rather than
emphasizing the characteristics of offenders, with this approach we concentrate upon the
circumstances in which they carry out predatory criminal acts."[1] The theory has no need to know
who is a criminal, why they offend, or what they look like, only what the situation is.

Their mechanism is convergence. They hypothesised that "the dispersion of activities away from
households and families increases the opportunity for crime and thus generates higher crime
rates".[1] Nobody had to become more criminal for crime to rise. The everyday pattern of life put
more targets in more places with fewer guardians, and offenders met them more often.

That gives you three levers, one for each element:

- **Be a less suitable target.** Less on show, less within reach, a locked door.
- **Add guardianship.** A person who is with you, or who knows your plan and will act if it breaks;
  neighbours; staff; light and visibility.
- **Change the convergence.** A different route, time or routine, so the things that have to meet
  meet less often.

Notice that the first lever is about what's available, not about how you look or walk. A suitable
target in Cohen and Felson's sense is a thing or a person in a situation, and the situation is what
you can alter.

Digital Literacy lesson 1 did something similar for a computer. It built a security plan from five
questions: what you have, who might want it, how likely they are to try, how bad it would be, and
how much trouble you will go to. It also met the folk picture of attackers as burglars, opportunists
after identity information, and said that picture produces sensible avoidance. The triangle asks the
same questions about your evening: the burglar picture, with a research literature behind it.

## Situational prevention: changing the situation on purpose

Situational crime prevention is the practice. It grew out of British government research in the
1960s and 1970s, and it draws on routine activity theory among other ideas.[2] R. V. Clarke, one of
its founders, defined it in his
[introduction](https://popcenter.asu.edu/sites/g/files/litvpz3631/files/scp2_intro_0_0.pdf) to
*Situational Crime Prevention: Successful Case Studies* (1997): "Situational prevention comprises
opportunity-reducing measures that (1) are directed at highly specific forms of crime, (2) involve
the management, design or manipulation of the immediate environment in as systematic and permanent
way as possible, (3) make crime more difficult and risky, or less rewarding and excusable as judged
by a wide range of offenders."[2]

Two parts of that do the work. "Specific": not crime in general but this crime, here. And the last
clause, which gives the ways to change a situation. Cornish and Clarke laid those out in 2003 as a
[grid of twenty-five
techniques](https://popcenter.asu.edu/sites/g/files/litvpz3631/files/twenty_five_techniques_of_situational_prevention.pdf)
under five headings: Increase the Effort, Increase the Risks, Reduce the Rewards, Reduce
Provocations, Remove Excuses.[3]

Most of the grid is written for people who design places and run organisations: steering locks,
entry phones, street closures, two clerks in a convenience store. A handful of entries are on a
personal scale. Under "Extend guardianship" the grid lists "Take routine precautions: go out in
group at night, leave signs of occupancy, carry phone".[3] Under Reduce the Rewards, it has
concealing targets and removing them. And a whole column, Reduce Provocations, is about lowering
frustration and avoiding disputes (efficient queues, polite service, less crowding in pubs), which
lesson 8, "Talking it down", builds on.[3]

Two cautions before you use it. First, applying a grid written for planners and managers to your own
walk home is this course's step, not Clarke's; the grid tells you where to look, not what works for
you. Second, not everything in it is settled. Burglar alarms sit in the grid under "Strengthen
formal surveillance", and lesson 5, "Home, the door and the doorstep", sets out the dispute over
whether an alarm protects a home at all.[3]

Lesson 3 connects here too. Its findings that repeats tend to come quickly, and that a major reason
for them is offenders taking later advantage of what the first offence revealed, are the grid's
"remove targets" with a date on it: after something happens, close the opening the offender used,
and do it soon. As lesson 3 said, the advice is sensible, untested.

### Doesn't crime just move next door?

The commonest objection to all this is displacement: block one opportunity and the offender uses
another nearby, so nothing is gained. Clarke's answer was that the assumption "is not supported by
empirical research which has generally found rather little displacement."[2]

This is a contested empirical question with a strong lean, and it pays to be exact about what this
course has. It read Clarke's summary of the research as it stood in 1997, not the evaluations behind
it, and it read no later review of them in full, so it gives no figure for how often displacement
happens. What the summary supports is narrower than "precautions always work": when researchers
looked, crime seldom reappeared next door in full.

## What the frame claims, and what it doesn't

Situational prevention has critics from every direction, and Clarke, one of its main proponents, set
them out himself. From the Right, he wrote, it looks like an "irrelevant response to crime because
it neglects issues of moral culpability and punishment", and as infringing the freedom and privacy
of people who have done nothing wrong. From the Left, it is called "politically and socially naive
in its neglect of the role of social and economic inequities in causation", and of who has the power
to define what counts as crime. Liberals say that by "tinkering" with symptoms it diverts attention
from the need to tackle the "root causes" of crime, such as, in his summary of their view,
unemployment, racial discrimination, poor housing and schooling, and inconsistent parenting.[2]

He also recorded worries about how the measures are used. One is a "fortress society", the
phrase lesson 1 met in Warr: people so afraid they barricade themselves in. Clarke thought
experience had dispelled some of that fear, though not all. Another is that the measures can be
used by the powerful to keep out people they see as undesirable, and he names the poor, minorities
and young people, kept out of malls, parks and town centres. He replied that many such measures
serve people who are far from rich, and gave examples from public housing and a low-income
neighbourhood.[2]

Then comes blame. Clarke first raises "blaming the victim" about businesses: asking shops to change
practices that invite theft risks being dismissed on those grounds. He then turns to victim
advocates, who in his account resist any suggestion that victims are responsible because it could
set back better rights and treatment for victims. His reply, quoting a piece he wrote with Felson,
is that routine precautions let people take responsibility without accepting criminal blame or even
civil liability, and do not exempt offenders from criminal responsibility: a person who locks her
car still has a right to expect nobody steals it, locked or not.[2]

This is a proponent describing his critics, not the critics' case in their own words. Lesson
6, "Out and away", gives the strongest version of it, from researchers who argue that safety advice
aimed at women restricts them and makes them responsible for preventing violence, and sets it beside
the case for teaching skills.

What this course takes from all of it is the limit of the frame. Routine activity theory and
situational prevention are tools for lowering your own odds. They offer no theory of why people
offend, they say nothing about anyone harmed failing to take a precaution, and nothing in them moves
responsibility for a crime away from the person who committed it. That last point is the course's
one value commitment, and it holds whatever the frame says about odds.

:::checkpoint A woman who works from home keeps a parcel delivery box on her front step. Three times this month parcels have gone from it within an hour of delivery. Using the triangle, name one change for each lever, and say which one says anything about her.
A less suitable target: have parcels held at a collection point, or delivered when someone is home. Guardianship: ask a neighbour who is home in the day to take them in, or have the box moved where it can be seen from inside. Convergence: change delivery times so parcels no longer sit out through the hours when they've been taken. None of the three says anything about her. All three are about the parcels, the step and the hour, which is the point of the frame. And because repeats tend to come quickly, the change is most worth making now, not after a fourth. Each is sensible, untested: no study this course read trialled any of them.
:::

## Martin's walk to the bus

Martin is a hospital porter. Most nights his shift ends at eleven, and he walks ten minutes from the
staff entrance to a bus stop on the main road. The shortest way is diagonally across the staff car
park and down a service lane; the longer way follows the lit road past the hospital's front
entrance, and adds about three minutes. He usually takes the short cut, on the phone to his partner,
because it's the only time in the day they get to talk. He has had no trouble so far, and he'd like
to keep it that way without giving up the call.

Nothing here says Martin is at fault for anything, now or if something ever happened. The question
is only what in this situation he can change, and what each change is worth.

**Step 1: the target.** What does Martin have on show? A phone, in his hand, which is worth taking.
And with his attention on the call he notices less of his surroundings: the next section has the
evidence for that much, and it measured noticing, not crime. The change: make the call from the bus
stop or the bus, and walk with the phone in a pocket. Label it sensible, untested.

**Step 2: guardianship.** Who is with Martin, or knows where he is? Nobody on the walk, though his
partner is on the line, and how much a person on the phone could do is a fair question. The
changes: walk with a colleague who finishes at the same time; ask whether the hospital's security
staff will walk people out; if the call is the point of the walk, agree to message from the bus
instead. Each adds someone who notices, or someone who knows the plan and will act if it breaks.
Label them sensible, untested. The grid's "go out in group at night" is the same idea.

:::checkpoint Step 3 is yours before you read on. What does the third lever, convergence, say about Martin's route, and which label fits the change?
It says the route is a choice, and so is the time. The short cut takes him across a car park and down a service lane where there are few people and, at eleven at night, probably nobody else. The longer way keeps him on a lit road past the front entrance, where there are more people and staff, which is more potential guardianship on the way. Three minutes is the price. The label is sensible, untested: it follows from the theory, and nobody has measured it for one walker on one road. Lesson 6, "Out and away", looks at what the evidence on lighting does measure.
:::

**Step 4: the cost.** Lesson 1 gave you Jackson and Gray's test for whether a worry is working for
you: it leads to a precaution, the precaution makes you feel safer, and neither shrinks your life.
Martin's changes cost him a pocketed phone for ten minutes, a message instead of the walk-time call
some nights, and three minutes of walking. That passes. A version that had him stop taking the late
shift, or dread every walk, would fail it, and nothing in the evidence asks for that.

## Awareness is attention

"Be aware of your surroundings" is one of the commonest pieces of safety advice, and one of the
least defined. The clearest evidence this course found on what awareness is, and what degrades it,
comes from outside criminology altogether: a psychology study with a clown in it.

:::predict In a campus study published in 2010, a clown rode a unicycle around a sculpture in a square people walked across, and researchers asked walkers in four groups whether they had seen it: people walking alone, people walking in pairs, people listening to music players, and people talking on a phone. Phone users noticed least, as Martin's step 1 suggested. Where would you put the music listeners and the pairs, compared with people walking alone?
Both did at least as well as people walking alone. Pairs noticed most (71%), then music-player users (61%), then people walking alone (51%), and people on a phone least of all (25%). The music gap is small, the groups were small, and the paper runs no test on that one difference, so read it as "no less", not "more". If you guessed that music would cut noticing, you guessed what the common earphone rule assumes.
:::

Hyman and colleagues [reported two studies](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1638) in 2010 in *Applied
Cognitive Psychology*.[4] In the first they found that "cell phone users walked more slowly, changed
directions more frequently, and were less likely to acknowledge other people than individuals in the
other conditions." In the second came the clown, and phone users "were less likely to notice an
unusual activity along their walking route (a unicycling clown)."[4]

*Percentage of walkers in each condition who had noticed the unicycling clown, from Hyman and
colleagues (2010): 151 walkers, 24 of them on a phone, on one campus in a single hour one spring
afternoon. All bars drawn to one scale. It measures noticing an unusual event, not crime.*

The authors' name for what happened to the phone users is inattentional blindness: failing to see
something plainly in view because attention is taken up elsewhere. Their account is divided
attention. A conversation takes a share of attention that walking alone, or walking to music,
leaves free, and they suggest phone use may produce this even during something as undemanding as
walking.[4] This is their account of their result, and this course adds no cognitive science to it.

Now the limits, which matter as much as the result. It's one campus, one spring afternoon, 151
walkers of whom 24 were on a phone, with phones as they were then. The researchers watched whoever
came by rather than assigning anyone to a condition, and they say themselves that something about
people who choose to phone while walking may be the cause rather than the call. And it measured
whether people noticed a clown, not whether anyone was approached, followed or robbed. So the study
is good evidence that people on a phone call noticed less, and nearby evidence, not proof, for the
advice built on it. "Put the phone away while you walk somewhere unfamiliar or late" is sensible,
untested: nobody has measured whether it prevents a crime.

Two other things in the chart are worth a sentence. People with music players noticed the clown at
least as often as people walking alone, and the authors conclude that using a device was not by
itself what distracted people. So this study gives no support to the common rule that earphones make
you a target, and no refutation of it either: it measured noticing, not whether anyone was targeted,
and nothing this course read measured whether earphones change anyone's risk. And pairs noticed most
of all, about what you'd expect from combining two people's chances, the authors say. Hyman's team
studied noticing, not crime, so the link is this course's, not theirs, but it makes a neat picture
of Cohen and Felson's guardian: two people notice more than one.

### What awareness isn't

Put those together and awareness, as this course uses the word, is noticing what is around you and
thinking a step ahead about what might happen next. It is attention, and attention is limited and
easy to spend elsewhere.

It isn't scanning every face for danger, or staying frightened. Lesson 1 drew the line: worry that
leads to a precaution, which makes you feel safer and leaves your life about the size it was, is
functional; worry that shrinks a life is the kind that costs. Awareness that works looks like a
habit you barely notice, such as the phone in the pocket for ten minutes, not a state of alarm you
carry everywhere.

Nor does it need a theory of who is dangerous. Martin's changes were about where his own attention
was and who was with him, not about watching anyone in particular.

## What the walking studies measured

"Predators pick victims by how they walk, so walk confidently and you won't be picked" is a common
line in self-defence writing. It rests on a real line of research. What that research measured is
narrower than the claim, and the gap between the two is this section.

### Where it started

In 1981 Betty Grayson and Morris Stein published "Attracting assault: victims' nonverbal cues" in
the *Journal of Communication*. This course read only its [ERIC
abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ238367) and later researchers' accounts of it, not the paper.
The abstract describes "a study in which prison inmates convicted of assault identified potential
victims from videotapes", and a coding system used "to determine which nonverbal body movement
categories differentiated victims and nonvictims."[5] As quoted by a later team, the paper argued
that "potential victims may be signaling their vulnerability to would-be assailants through
gestures, posture and exaggerated movement".[6] Numbers for how many people were filmed and how many
inmates rated them circulate widely; since this course never read the paper, it gives none.

A 2010 master's thesis by Sarah Wheeler at Brock University, whose literature review is a short
account of this whole line of work, summarises the cues that study found: the people judged most vulnerable "displayed longer or
shorter strides, and weight that shifted up/down, forward/back, or side-to-side. They also tended to
use only one side or part of their body when moving, and tended to lift their feet."[7]

### Dots of light

:::predict A 2002 study showed raters people walking as nothing but moving points of light at the joints: no clothes, no face, no body shape. Could raters still sort the walkers by how easy they would be to attack? And would changing someone's shoes change their rating?
Yes to both. From the movement alone, raters sorted both female and male walkers by ease of attack, and particular features of the walk predicted their ratings. The changes in walking that different clothing and footwear produced went with different ratings. Its limits come two paragraphs down.
:::

Gunns and colleagues' [point-light
study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1020744915533) found that "Both female
(Experiment 1) and male (Experiment 2) walkers could be differentiated according to ease-of-attack
based solely on the kinematic information provided whilst walking."[6] Specific walking features
predicted the ratings, and in a third experiment, variations in walking caused by different
clothing and footwear predicted differences in ratings.[6] That is about how raters judged moving
points of light. It says nothing about why anyone is attacked, and on this course's one value
commitment, what someone wore never puts the blame on them.

The authors were careful about what that shows, and their limits are the most important sentences
in this line of research. Their raters made judgements of how easy someone would be to attack; they
didn't attempt any assaults. To say the raters were accurate, the authors wrote, you would have to
show that the people rated easiest to attack actually were easiest to attack, which the study did
not do. And the raters were told to judge ease of attack; whether anyone would pay attention to such
cues without being asked, the authors said, is unknown.[6]

Wheeler's thesis makes the design point about the whole early literature. Grayson and Stein, she
notes, filmed real people walking, but "their study design did not include a comparison of ratings
with target demographics either", and later point-light studies used the same design.[7] In plain
terms, most of these studies measure whether people look easy to attack to someone asked to rate
them. They don't check the ratings against whether the people rated had in fact been victimised.

### Checking the ratings against real lives

Two later studies from a Canadian group went further. This course read the abstract of each, and
nothing more.

[Book and colleagues (2013)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23422847/) showed short clips of people
walking to 47 inmates of a maximum-security penitentiary in Ontario, who judged how vulnerable each
was. The inmates who scored higher on the interpersonal and emotional side of psychopathy, measured
with a standard assessment called the PCL-R, were more accurate, and more likely to explain their
judgements by mentioning the walk.[8] The abstract doesn't say what "accuracy" was measured against,
and the abstract is all this course read.

[Fulham and colleagues (2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29294783/) coded people's walking for
vulnerability cues, with some walkers knowing they were being filmed and some not. Walking style was
associated with a history of victimisation, "but only in the Unaware condition": that is, only among
people unaware they were being filmed. In a second study, of female university students,
the authors found that hypervigilance, a constant watchfulness for threat, changed the link between
sexual victimisation and walking style, and concluded that "hypervigilance may be an adaptive
response that reduces perceived vulnerability in sexually victimized women."[9] That is a finding
about how people's walking looked, not advice to live on guard. The authors' analysis treats the
harm as coming first and the walk as changing afterwards; one analysis can't prove that order, and
the abstract claims only the association.[9]

### What that adds up to

| Claim | What the studies read here support |
|---|---|
| Raters sort walkers | Well supported. Raters asked to judge ease of attack sorted walkers from movement alone (Gunns), and prison inmates convicted of assault picked out potential victims from videotapes (Grayson and Stein, abstract) |
| The cues track real lives | Partly supported. In one study's samples, walking style went with a history of victimisation (Fulham); another found raters higher on one psychopathy factor more "accurate", without the abstract saying against what (Book) |
| Changing your walk lowers your risk | Not shown. No study this course read measured later victimisation after anyone changed how they walked |
| Offenders choose like this in life | Not shown. A rating task, in which people are told what to judge, is not a street |

So "does a confident walk prevent attack?" is a contested question, and "not shown" is the honest
answer on prevention. What would settle it is a study that followed people over time, trained some
of them and not others, and counted what happened to each group. This course found no published one.

## Two sentences the evidence cannot carry

Martin's walk was the simple case. This one has a wrinkle, and it comes in two halves.

Nadia, a friend of yours, says over coffee: "I walk with purpose, so I'm never picked." Sort it the
way the table does. The first half, walking with purpose, may well make her look harder to attack to
a rater; the studies support that much. The second half, "never picked", is a claim about attacks,
and no study here measured attacks. And "never picked" is missing a comparison: most people aren't
picked on most walks, however they walk, so her own record can't tell her whether the walk is doing
anything. Her habit costs nothing, which is fine: sensible, untested, in this course's labels. The
sentence is what outruns the evidence.

Now the inverse, which is where this literature gets misused. Suppose someone you know was attacked,
and a third person says: "She was picked because of how she walked." Take it apart on the evidence.

The studies measured how raters judged walkers. None showed that any attacker, in a real street,
chose anyone because of their walk, and the Gunns team said plainly that their design couldn't show
raters were even accurate. Where a link with victimisation was found, as in Fulham's first study, it
was with a history of harm that had already happened, and the authors modelled the harm as coming
first. Lesson 3 showed that in both countries most women who are killed are killed by someone they
know, and that in England and Wales, of victims of rape or assault by penetration since age 16,
fewer than one in five were attacked by a stranger and fewer than one in ten on a street, in a car
park, a park or another open public space. How someone walks down a street
has no obvious bearing on harm from a partner at home. That point is this course's reasoning, not a
finding of the walking studies. And under all of it is the course's commitment: responsibility for
an attack lies with the person who made it. Precautions change the odds. They don't move the blame.

:::checkpoint A self-defence website says: "Research proves attackers pick their victims by the way they walk." Using this lesson, write the sentence the research actually supports.
Something like: "In rating studies, people asked to judge walkers, including prison inmates, picked out who looked easy to attack, and in one study walking style went with a history of victimisation; no study read here showed that attackers choose this way in real life, or that changing your walk lowers your risk."
:::

## Warning signs are things people do

One piece of advice this course read ends in the gut. The [Suzy Lamplugh
Trust](https://www.suzylamplugh.org/personal-safety-out-and-about), the UK's personal safety
charity, tells people who think they're being followed to trust their instincts and act, and its
festival advice says to watch for aggressive behaviour from others and to get away from it.[10] The
US State Department's [page for women
travellers](https://web.archive.org/web/20251226183516/https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/planning/personal-needs/women.html)
says something close without naming instinct: in an unsafe situation it can help to speak loudly,
and "Being safe is more important than being polite."[11]

Look at what sets each of these off: being followed; aggressive behaviour; an unsafe situation. Each
is something happening, not the look of a person. This course takes that and makes it a rule. **A
warning sign is something a person does, never how they look.** Here are examples, and the list is
this course's, not any one source's:

- someone who keeps coming closer after you've stepped back;
- someone who carries on after you've said no, or who treats "no" as the start of a negotiation;
- someone who follows you across a road and back again;
- someone who places themselves between you and the only way out;
- someone who tries to move you somewhere less visible, a side street, a car, a quieter room.

Not on the list, ever: race, ethnicity, religion, class, clothes, accent, build, tattoos, or looking
"out of place". Those are appearance.

Why the rule, when "trust your gut" sounds so natural? This is the course's own argument, not a
finding, built on Cohen and Felson. Their frame deliberately turned away from "the
characteristics of offenders" to "the circumstances in which they carry out predatory criminal
acts".[1] Everything useful in this lesson has come from circumstances: what's on show, who's
around, where and when. None of it needed a picture of what an offender looks like. And a feeling
set off by how someone looks can carry prejudice as easily as it carries pattern, which puts the
cost of a false alarm on people who have done nothing, the same worry Clarke recorded about measures
used to keep "undesirables" out of public places.[2] A feeling set off by what someone is doing is
about the situation you're in.

How to label it: "trust your instincts" is advice from an experienced charity, and no study this
course read tested it, so the label is practitioner opinion. The course's rule that a cue is
behaviour is sensible, untested: it follows from the theory, and nobody has measured it.

None of this is a test that anyone who was harmed failed. Plenty of harm comes with no warning a
person could read, much of it from someone the person had every reason to trust, and a feeling you
didn't act on, or a moment you froze, was never the cause. The person who chose to do harm was.

:::checkpoint Sort these into behaviour, which the course treats as a warning sign, and appearance, which it does not. (a) A man at a bus stop asks you the time, then asks where you're going, then moves to stand beside you after you've turned away. (b) A group of teenagers in hooded tops on a corner. (c) A driver who pulls up, asks for directions, and when you step back, gets out of the car. (d) Someone who "doesn't look like they belong" on your street. (e) A colleague who keeps finding reasons to be alone with you after you've said no.
Behaviour: (a), (c) and (e). Each persists or closes distance after you've set a limit: moving in after you turned away, getting out after you stepped back, continuing after a clear no. Appearance: (b) and (d). Hooded tops and "not belonging" describe how people look to you, not anything they did. Notice that (e) is someone you know. Lesson 3 showed how much harm comes from people the victim knows, and the lesson "Domestic abuse and stalking" takes that further.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Walk confidently and you won't be picked."** Rating studies find that raters, inmates among them,
pick out who looks easy to attack. No study this course read measured whether changing your walk
lowers your risk, or whether real attackers choose that way.

**"Earphones make you a target."** In Hyman's study, people with music players noticed the clown no
less often than people walking alone; phone callers were the ones who noticed least. It measured
noticing, not being targeted, and nothing this course read measured earphones against risk.

**"Awareness means scanning everyone for danger."** Awareness is noticing and thinking a step
ahead. Phone callers had least of it in Hyman's study and pairs had most, and it needs no theory of
who looks dangerous.

**"Precautions just move crime next door."** Clarke reported that research had generally found
rather little displacement. Contested, then, with a strong lean against the objection.

**"Trust your gut" as a complete rule.** This is practitioner opinion, and a gut feeling about how
someone looks can be prejudice. A gut feeling about what someone is doing is worth taking seriously:
sensible, untested.

**"Safety is about spotting the kind of person who looks dangerous."** The theory this course uses
works without knowing anything about offenders, and the course uses behaviour, never appearance.

## Practice

:::exercise Map a route and sort six claims
Take 20 minutes over these, on paper. If you live with someone who frightens you, don't write any of this where they could find it, and leave out any route they'd recognise. The lesson "Domestic abuse and stalking", which starts with the helplines, is the better place to start.

1. Pick one routine trip or evening you actually make: a commute, a late shift, a night out, the walk from a car park. Write it out as Martin's was written: where, when, with whom, what you carry, where your attention usually is.

2. Map it onto the triangle. Under "target", write what you have on show and where your attention goes. Under "guardian", write who is with you or knows your plan. Under "convergence", write the route and the time, and one alternative for each.

3. List three changes that cost little. For each, write which lever it pulls, what it costs you in time, money and freedom, and its label from the four the course uses. If a change follows from the triangle and nobody has measured it, its label is sensible, untested, and most of yours will be.

4. Sort these six claims into the four labels (tested; sensible, untested; contested; practitioner opinion). Write your answers before you open the checkpoint.

   a. Change the way you walk and you'll be less likely to be attacked.

   b. Go out in a group at night.

   c. If you think someone is following you, trust your instincts and act.

   d. Put anything valuable out of sight before you leave your car.

   e. Don't bother with precautions, because offenders will just go somewhere else.

   f. People talking on the phone while they walk notice less around them.

Next time you're somewhere public and busy in daylight, somewhere you already feel comfortable, try a noticing exercise with its limits written in. With your phone put away, note for five minutes what you see of the place, not the people in it: the ways in and out, where the staff are, where it's lit and where it isn't, which way people move through it. Do not watch, follow, photograph or describe any individual, and don't do this anywhere you feel uneasy. Then spend five minutes reading your phone, put it away, and note anything new. Compare the two lists.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your sort for question 4.
Tested, in the narrow sense: f, for noticing. One comparison of four groups of walkers on one campus found phone users noticed least; the authors note it may be the people who choose to phone, not the call. For crime nothing was tested, and the advice built on it is sensible, untested.

Sensible, untested: b and d. Both follow from routine activity theory (add a guardian; conceal the target), and both appear in the situational prevention grid, which was written for places and organisations; nobody has trialled either for one person.

Contested: a and e. For a, raters sort walkers and one study found walking style went with past harm, but nobody has measured whether changing a walk prevents attack. For e, the research Clarke summarised generally found rather little displacement, so the claim runs against the lean of the evidence; the course still calls the question contested because it read the summary, not the studies.

Practitioner opinion: c. An experienced charity advises it and no study this course read tested it. If you put c under sensible, untested, notice what makes the difference: the course's rule that a cue is behaviour follows from a theory; "trust your instincts" on its own does not.
:::
