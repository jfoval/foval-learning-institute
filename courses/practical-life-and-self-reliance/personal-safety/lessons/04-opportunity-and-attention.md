You cannot change who is out tonight. You can change a surprising amount about the circumstances you
walk into: where you are, when, who's with you, what you are carrying, and how much of your attention
is on the street rather than a screen. Criminologists have a theory built on exactly that, and it is
the most useful tool this course has for your own risk. This lesson teaches it, then turns to the
advice that's sold alongside it with more confidence than the research can carry: "be aware", "walk
with purpose", "trust your gut". By the end you will be able to take a walk, a shift or an evening
apart into the few things you can actually change, and say for each how strong the evidence is.

Lesson 3, "Who is harmed, by whom, where and when", gave you the measured risks. This lesson asks
what part of a risk is yours to change, and it is careful about one thing throughout. A frame for
lowering your own odds is not a verdict on anyone who was harmed. That sentence comes back several
times below, because the research in this lesson is easy to turn into blame, and each time the
lesson says why the evidence can't carry it.

## Three things that have to meet

:::predict In 1979 two sociologists set out to explain why crime in the US rose so much between 1947 and 1974. They did not look for more criminals. They looked for a change in how ordinary people lived that would make crime easier. What change in daily life would you guess they pointed to?
The spread of daily activity away from home and family. On their account, changes such as more adults in paid work and more people living alone put more people and property out in the world and left fewer people at home to watch over it. The data they gave included changes in labour force participation and in single-adult households. If you guessed something about how people live rather than about who offends, you have understood the theory before reading it.
:::

Lawrence Cohen and Marcus Felson called it a routine activity approach, and their 1979 paper in the
*American Sociological Review* opens with the idea in one sentence: "Most criminal acts require
convergence in space and time of likely offenders, suitable targets and the absence of capable
guardians against crime."[1] Three things have to meet at one place and one time. Take any one of
them away and that crime, there and then, doesn't happen.

The paper also made a deliberate turn, and it is the reason this course uses the theory: "Rather than
emphasizing the characteristics of offenders, with this approach we concentrate upon the
circumstances in which they carry out predatory criminal acts."[1] The theory does not need to know
who is a criminal, why they offend, or what they look like. It asks about the situation.

Their mechanism is convergence. They hypothesised that "the dispersion of activities away from
households and families increases the opportunity for crime and thus generates higher crime
rates".[1] Nobody had to become more criminal for crime to rise. The everyday pattern of life put
more targets in more places with fewer guardians, and offenders met them more often.

That gives you three levers, one for each element:

- **Be a less suitable target.** Less on show, less within reach, a door that's locked.
- **Add guardianship.** A person who is with you, or who knows your plan and will act if it breaks;
  neighbours; staff; light and visibility.
- **Change the convergence.** A different route, time or routine, so the things that have to meet
  meet less often.

Notice that the first lever is not about how you look or walk. It is about what's available. A
suitable target in Cohen and Felson's sense is a thing or a person in a situation, and the
situation is what you can alter.

If you took Digital Literacy, this should feel familiar. Lesson 1 there built a security plan from
five questions: what you have, who might want it, how likely they are to try, how bad it would be,
and how much trouble you will go to. It also met the folk picture of attackers as burglars,
opportunists who would take what was easy, and said that picture tends to produce sensible avoidance.
The triangle asks the same questions about your body and your evening, and it's the burglar picture
with a research literature behind it.

## Situational prevention: changing the situation on purpose

R. V. Clarke turned the theory into a practice. In his introduction to *Situational Crime
Prevention: Successful Case Studies* (1997) he defined it: "Situational prevention comprises
opportunity-reducing measures that (1) are directed at highly specific forms of crime, (2) involve
the management, design or manipulation of the immediate environment in as systematic and permanent
way as possible, (3) make crime more difficult and risky, or less rewarding and excusable as judged
by a wide range of offenders."[2]

Two parts of that do the work. "Specific": not crime in general but this crime, here. And the last
clause, which gives five ways to change a situation. Cornish and Clarke laid those out in 2003 as a
grid of twenty-five techniques under five headings: Increase the Effort, Increase the Risks, Reduce
the Rewards, Reduce Provocations, Remove Excuses.[3]

Most of the grid is written for people who design places and run organisations: steering locks,
entry phones, street closures, two clerks in a convenience store. A handful of entries are on a
personal scale. Under "Extend guardianship" the grid lists "Take routine precautions: go out in
group at night, leave signs of occupancy, carry phone".[3] Under Reduce the Rewards, it has
concealing targets and removing them. And a whole column, Reduce Provocations, is about lowering
frustration and avoiding disputes (efficient queues, polite service, less crowding in pubs), which
lesson 8, "Talking it down", builds on.[3]

Two cautions before you use it. First, applying a grid written for planners and managers to your own
walk home is this course's step, not Clarke's; the grid tells you where to look, not what works for
you. Second, not everything in it is settled. Burglar alarms sit in the grid under "Strengthen formal
surveillance", and lesson 5, "Home, the door and the doorstep", sets out the dispute over whether an alarm
protects a home at all.[3]

There is also a link back to lesson 3. Its finding that repeat victimisation comes quickly, because
offenders take later advantage of what the first offence revealed, is the grid's "remove targets"
with a date on it: after something happens, close the opportunity it showed someone, and do it
soon.

### Doesn't crime just move next door?

The commonest objection to all this is displacement: block one opportunity and the offender uses
another nearby, so nothing is gained. Clarke's answer was that the assumption "is not supported by
empirical research which has generally found rather little displacement."[2]

That's a contested empirical question with a strong lean, and it is worth being exact about what
this course has. It read Clarke's summary of the research as it stood in 1997, not the evaluations
behind it, and it read no later review of them in full, so it gives no figure for how often
displacement happens. What the summary supports is narrower than "precautions always work": when
researchers looked, crime usually did not simply reappear next door in full.

## What the frame claims, and what it doesn't

Situational prevention has critics from every direction, and Clarke, one of its main proponents,
set them out himself. From the Right, he wrote, it is seen as an "irrelevant response to crime
because it neglects issues of moral culpability and punishment", and as infringing the freedom and
privacy of people who have done nothing wrong. From the Left, it's "politically and socially naive in
its neglect of the role of social and economic inequities in causation", and of who has the power to
define what counts as crime. Liberals say that by "tinkering" with symptoms it diverts attention
from the need to tackle the "root causes" of crime, among which he lists unemployment, racial
discrimination, poor housing and schooling, and inconsistent parenting.[2]

He named two more fears. One is a "fortress society", the phrase lesson 1 met in Warr: people so
afraid they barricade themselves in. The other is that the measures can be used by the powerful to
keep out people they do not want, and he names the poor, minorities and young people, from malls,
parks and town centres. He treats both as real concerns about how the measures are used.[2]

Then there is blame. Clarke first raises "blaming the victim" about businesses, where asking shops
to change practices that invite theft risks being dismissed on those grounds. He then turns to
victim advocates, who in his account resist any suggestion that victims are responsible because it
could set back better rights and treatment for victims. His reply, written with Felson, is that
routine precautions let people take responsibility for precautions without taking any blame, and
don't excuse offenders at all: a person who locks her car still has every right to expect nobody
steals it, locked or not.[2]

That is a proponent describing his critics, so it is not the critics' case in their own words. Lesson
6, "Out and away", gives the strongest version of it, from researchers who argue that safety advice
aimed at women restricts them and makes them responsible for preventing violence, and sets it
beside the case for teaching skills.

What this course takes from all of it is the limit of the frame. **Routine activity theory and
situational prevention are tools for lowering your own odds.** They're not a theory of why people
offend, they do not say that anyone harmed failed to take a precaution, and nothing in them moves
responsibility for a crime away from the person who committed it. That last point is the course's
one value commitment, and it holds whatever the frame says about odds.

:::checkpoint A woman who works from home keeps a parcel delivery box on her front step. Three times this month parcels have gone from it within an hour of delivery. Using the triangle, name one change for each lever, and say which one says anything about her.
A less suitable target: have parcels held at a collection point, or delivered when someone's in. Guardianship: ask a neighbour who is home in the day to take them in, or have the box moved where it's visible from inside. Convergence: change delivery times so parcels do not sit out through the part of the day they have been going. None of the three says anything about her. They're all about the parcels, the step and the hour, which is the point of the frame. And because repeats come quickly, the change is most worth making now, not after a fourth. Each is sensible, untested: nobody has trialled parcel boxes.
:::

## Martin's walk to the bus

Martin is a hospital porter. Most nights his shift ends at eleven, and he walks ten minutes from the
staff entrance to a bus stop on the main road. The shortest way is diagonally across the staff car
park and down a service lane; the longer way follows the lit road past the hospital's front
entrance, and adds about three minutes. He usually takes the short cut, on the phone to his partner,
because it is the only time in the day they get to talk. He has never had any trouble, and he would like to
keep it that way without giving up the call.

Nothing here says Martin is at fault for anything, now or if something ever happened. The question
is only what in this situation he can change, and what each change is worth.

**Step 1: the target.** What does Martin have on show? A phone, in his hand, with his attention on
it. That makes him a more suitable target in two ways: the phone is worth taking, and his attention
is somewhere else. The change: make the call from the bus stop or the bus, and walk with the phone
in a pocket. Label it sensible, untested. The next section gives the evidence nearby, a study of
what phone calls do to what walkers notice, and it measured noticing, not crime.

**Step 2: guardianship.** Who is with Martin, or knows where he is? Nobody on the walk, though his
partner is on the line, and it's a fair question how much a person on the phone could do. The
changes: walk with a colleague who finishes at the same time; ask whether the hospital's security
staff will walk people out; if the call is the point of the walk, agree that he will
message when he is on the bus instead. Each adds someone who notices, or someone who knows the plan
and will act if it breaks. Label them sensible, untested. The grid's "go out in group at night" is
the same idea.

:::checkpoint Step 3 is yours before you read on. What does the third lever, convergence, say about Martin's route, and what's the honest label on the change?
It says the route is a choice, and so is the time. The short cut takes him across a car park and down a service lane where there are few people and, at eleven at night, probably nobody else. The longer way keeps him on a lit road past the front entrance, where there are more people and staff, which is more potential guardianship on the way. Three minutes is the price. The label is sensible, untested: it follows from the theory, and lesson 6 shows that the evidence on lighting is about crime across whole areas, not the risk to one walker on one road, so nobody has measured this change for Martin.
:::

**Step 4: the cost.** Lesson 1 gave you Jackson and Gray's test for whether a worry is working for
you: it leads to a precaution, the precaution makes you feel safer, and neither shrinks your life.
Martin's changes cost him a pocketed phone for ten minutes, a message instead of the walk-time call
some nights, and three minutes of walking. That passes. A version that had him stop taking the late
shift, or dread every walk, would not, and nothing in the evidence asks for it.

The worked example gives Martin three changes, each small, each labelled. None of them is a promise.
That is what the frame can do: tell you where to look, and let you choose changes whose cost you can
see.

## Awareness is attention

"Be aware of your surroundings" is the commonest piece of safety advice there is, and it's rarely
defined. The clearest evidence this course found on what awareness is, and what degrades it, is not
from criminology at all. It is a psychology study with a clown in it.

:::predict In a study on a campus in 2009, a unicycling clown was on a route people walked along, and researchers compared how many walkers in four groups had noticed it: people walking alone, people walking in pairs, people listening to music players, and people talking on a phone. Put the four in order, from most likely to have noticed the clown to least.
Pairs noticed most (71%), then music-player users (61%), then people walking alone (51%), and people on a phone least of all (25%). If you put the phone users last, you were right. The two results worth a second look are that music-player users noticed more than people with nothing in their ears, and that pairs came top.
:::

Hyman and colleagues reported two studies in 2010 in *Applied Cognitive Psychology*.[4] In the
first they found that "cell phone users walked more slowly, changed
directions more frequently, and were less likely to acknowledge other people than individuals in the
other conditions." In the second came the clown, and phone users "were less likely to notice an
unusual activity along their walking route (a unicycling clown)."[4]

The authors' name for what happened to the phone users is inattentional blindness: failing to see
something plainly in view because attention is taken up elsewhere. Their account is divided
attention. A conversation takes a share of attention that walking alone, or walking to music, didn't
take, and they suggest phone use may produce this even during something as undemanding as
walking.[4] That is their mechanism for their result, and this course does not add any cognitive
science to it.

Now the limits, which matter as much as the result. It's one campus, in 2009, with phones as they
were then. It measured whether people noticed a clown, not whether anyone was approached, followed
or robbed. So it is tested evidence that a phone conversation cuts what walkers notice, and it is
nearby evidence, not proof, for the advice built on it. "Put the phone away while you walk somewhere
unfamiliar or late" is sensible, untested: nobody has measured whether it prevents a crime.

Two other things in the chart are worth a sentence. People with music players noticed more than
people walking alone, so this study gives no support to the common rule that earphones make you a
target; it's the conversation, not the sound, that went with missing the clown. And pairs noticed
most of all. Hyman's team did not study crime, so the link is this course's, not theirs, but it is a
neat picture of Cohen and Felson's guardian: two people notice more than one.

### What awareness isn't

Put those together and awareness, as this course uses the word, is noticing what is around you and
thinking a step ahead about what might happen next. It is attention, and attention is limited and
easy to spend elsewhere.

It isn't scanning every face for danger, and it is not staying frightened. Lesson 1 drew the line:
worry that turns into a few habits and then stops is functional, and worry that shrinks a life costs
health. Awareness that works looks like a habit you barely notice, such as the phone in the pocket
for ten minutes, not a state of alarm you carry everywhere.

It also does not need a theory of who's dangerous. Martin did not have to watch anyone in particular
to make his walk safer; he changed where his own attention was and who was with him.

## What the walking studies measured

"Predators pick victims by how they walk, so walk confidently and you won't be picked" is repeated
in self-defence classes, magazines and safety leaflets. It rests on a real line of research. What
that research measured is narrower than the claim, and the gap between the two is this section.

### Where it started

In 1981 Betty Grayson and Morris Stein published "Attracting assault: victims' nonverbal cues" in
the *Journal of Communication*. This course read only its [ERIC abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ238367)
and later researchers' accounts of it, not the paper. The abstract describes "a study in which
prison inmates convicted of assault identified potential victims from videotapes", and a coding
system used "to determine which nonverbal body movement categories differentiated victims and
nonvictims."[5] As quoted by a later team, the paper argued that "potential victims may be signaling
their vulnerability to would-be assailants through gestures, posture and exaggerated movement".[6]
Numbers for how many people were filmed and how many inmates rated them circulate widely; since this
course hasn't read the paper, it gives none.

A 2010 master's thesis by S. Wheeler at Brock University summarises the cues the study found: the people
judged most vulnerable "displayed longer or shorter strides, and weight that shifted up/down,
forward/back, or side-to-side. They also tended to use only one side or part of their body when
moving, and tended to lift their feet."[7]

### Dots of light

:::predict A 2002 study showed raters people walking as nothing but moving points of light at the joints: no clothes, no face, no body shape. Could raters sort the walkers by how easy they would be to attack, and would they agree with each other?
Yes. From the movement alone, raters sorted both female and male walkers by ease of attack, and particular features of the walk predicted their ratings. Clothing and footwear changed the ratings too, by changing how people walked. What the study could not tell you is the next section.
:::

Gunns and colleagues' point-light study found that "Both female
(Experiment 1) and male (Experiment 2) walkers could be differentiated according to ease-of-attack
based solely on the kinematic information provided whilst walking."[6] Specific walking features
predicted the ratings, and in a third experiment, variations in walking caused by different
clothing and footwear predicted differences in ratings.[6]

The authors were careful about what that shows, and their limits are the most important sentences
in this line of research. Their raters made judgements of how easy someone would be to attack; they
didn't attempt any assaults. To say the raters were accurate, the authors wrote, you would have to show
that the people rated easiest to attack actually were easiest to attack, which the study did not do.
And the raters were told to judge ease of attack; whether anyone would pay attention to such cues
without being asked, the authors said, is unknown.[6]

Wheeler's thesis makes the design point about the whole early literature. Grayson and Stein, she
notes, filmed real people walking, but "their study design did not include a comparison of ratings
with target demographics either", and later point-light studies used the same design.[7] In plain
terms, most of these studies measure whether people look easy to attack to someone asked to rate
them. They don't check the ratings against what later happens to the people rated.

### Checking the ratings against real lives

Two later studies from a Canadian group did compare ratings or walking style with something about
the walkers. This course read the abstract of each, and nothing more.

Book and colleagues (2013) showed short clips of people walking to 47 inmates of a
maximum-security penitentiary in Ontario, who judged how vulnerable each was. "Higher Factor 1
psychopathy scores (as measured by the PCL-R; Hare 2003) were positively related to accuracy in
judging victim vulnerability."[8] Factor 1, the abstract explains, is the interpersonal and affective side of
psychopathy. The inmates higher on it were also more likely to explain their
judgements by mentioning the walk. The abstract does not say what "accuracy" was scored against, and
since this course read only the abstract, it cannot tell you.

Fulham and colleagues (2020) coded people's walking for vulnerability cues, with some
walkers knowing they were being filmed and some not. Walking style was associated with a history of
victimisation, "but only in the Unaware condition": that is, only among people who didn't know
they were being filmed. In a second study, of female university students, the authors found that
hypervigilance changed the link between sexual victimisation and walking style, and concluded that
"hypervigilance may be an adaptive response that reduces perceived vulnerability in sexually
victimized women."[9] Their analysis also modelled how much people felt the victimisation had
affected them as sitting between the harm and the walk: a model in which the harm comes first. One
analysis can't prove that order by itself, and the abstract claims no more than the association.[9]

### What that adds up to

| | What the studies read here support |
|---|---|
| Raters agree | Established. Raters, including people convicted of assault, agree with each other about who looks easy to attack, from movement alone |
| The cues track real lives | Partly supported. In some samples, ratings or walking style went with past victimisation |
| Changing your walk lowers your risk | Not shown. No study this course read measured later victimisation after anyone changed how they walked |
| Offenders choose like this in life | Not shown. A rating task, in which people are told what to judge, is not a street |

So "does a confident walk prevent attack?" is a contested question, and "not shown" is the honest
answer on prevention. What would settle it is a study that followed people over time, trained some
of them and not others, and counted what happened to each group. Nobody has published one that this
course found.

## Two sentences the evidence cannot carry

Martin's walk was the simple case. This one has a wrinkle, and it comes in two halves.

Nadia, a friend of yours, says over coffee: "I walk with purpose, so I'm never picked." Sort it the
way the table does. The first half, walking with purpose, may well make her look harder to attack to
a rater; the studies support that much. The second half, "never picked", is a claim about attacks,
and no study here measured attacks. And "never picked" is missing a comparison: most people are not picked on most walks, however they walk, so her own record can't tell
her whether the walk is doing anything. Her habit costs nothing and may help her notice more, which
is fine. The sentence outruns the evidence.

Now the inverse, which is where this literature does harm. Suppose someone you know was attacked,
and a third person says: "She was picked because of how she walked." Take it apart on the evidence.

The studies measured how raters judged walkers. None showed that any attacker, in a real street,
chose anyone because of their walk, and the Gunns team said plainly that their design could not show
raters were even accurate. Where a link with victimisation was found, as in Fulham's first study, it
was with a history of harm that had already happened, and the authors modelled the harm as coming
first. Lesson 3 showed that most killings of women and most rapes are by people the victim knows, and that
the street is a small share of where rape happens; how someone walks down a street has no obvious
bearing on harm from a partner at home. That is this course's reasoning, not a finding of the
walking studies. And under all of it is the course's commitment:
responsibility for an attack lies with the person who made it. Precautions change the odds. They
don't move the blame.

:::checkpoint A self-defence website says: "Research proves attackers pick their victims in seconds by the way they walk." Using this lesson, write the sentence the research actually supports.
Something like: "In rating studies, people asked to judge walkers, including prison inmates, agreed about who looked easy to attack, and in some samples walking style went with past victimisation; no study read here showed that attackers choose this way in real life, or that changing your walk lowers your risk." The "in seconds" has no source in anything this course read, and it is the kind of popular number the course does not repeat.
:::

## Warning signs are things people do

Two pieces of advice this course read end in the gut. The Suzy Lamplugh Trust, the UK's personal safety
charity, tells people who think they're being followed to trust their instincts and act, and its
festival advice says to watch for aggressive behaviour from others and to get away from it.[10] The
US State Department's page for women travellers says that in an unsafe situation it can help to
speak loudly, and adds: "Being safe is more important than being polite."[11]

Look at what sets off the instinct in each: being followed; aggressive behaviour; an unsafe
situation. Each is something happening, not the look of a person. This course takes that and makes it
a rule. **A warning sign is something a person does, never how they look.** Some examples of the
kind of thing the course means, which are this course's list rather than any one source's:

- someone who keeps coming closer after you have stepped back;
- someone who carries on after you have said no, or who treats "no" as the start of a negotiation;
- someone who follows you across a road and back again;
- someone who places themselves between you and the only way out;
- someone who tries to move you somewhere less visible, a side street, a car, a quieter room.

Not on the list, ever: race, ethnicity, religion, class, clothes, accent, build, tattoos, or looking
"out of place". Those are appearance, and the course doesn't use them.

Why the rule, when "trust your gut" sounds so natural? This is the course's own argument, not a
finding, and it is built on Cohen and Felson. Their frame deliberately turned away from "the
characteristics of offenders" to "the circumstances in which they carry out predatory criminal
acts".[1] Everything useful in this lesson has come from circumstances: what is on show, who's
around, where and when. None of it needed a picture of what an offender looks like. And a feeling
set off by how someone looks can carry prejudice as easily as it carries pattern, which puts the
cost of a false alarm on people who have done nothing, the same worry Clarke recorded about measures
used to keep "undesirables" out of public places. A feeling set off by what someone is doing is
about the situation you are actually in.

How to label it: "trust your instincts" is advice from experienced bodies, and no study this course
read tested it, so it's practitioner opinion. The course's rule that a cue is behaviour is sensible,
untested: it follows from the theory, and nobody has measured it.

:::checkpoint Sort these into behaviour, which the course treats as a warning sign, and appearance, which it does not. (a) A man at a bus stop asks you the time, then asks where you are going, then moves to stand beside you after you've turned away. (b) A group of teenagers in hooded tops on a corner. (c) A driver who pulls up, asks for directions, and when you step back, gets out of the car. (d) Someone who "doesn't look like they belong" on your street. (e) A colleague who keeps finding reasons to be alone with you after you have said you are not interested.
Behaviour: (a), (c) and (e). Each persists or closes distance after you've set a limit: moving in after you turned away, getting out after you stepped back, continuing after a clear no. Appearance: (b) and (d). Hooded tops and "not belonging" describe how people look to you, not anything they have done. Notice that (e) is someone you know. Lesson 3 showed that most killings of women and most rapes are by people the victim knows, and lesson 7 takes that further.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Walk confidently and you won't be picked."** Rating studies find that people agree about who
looks easy to attack. No study this course read measured whether changing your walk lowers your
risk, or whether real attackers choose that way.

**"Earphones make you a target."** In Hyman's study, people with music players noticed the clown
more often than people walking alone. Phone conversations were what cut noticing.

**"Awareness means scanning everyone for danger."** Awareness is noticing and thinking a step
ahead. It is spent by a phone call, it's shared by walking with someone, and it does not need a theory
of who looks dangerous.

**"Precautions just move crime next door."** Clarke reported that research had generally found
rather little displacement. It is contested, with a strong lean against the objection.

**"Trust your gut" as a complete rule.** It's practitioner opinion, and a gut feeling about how
someone looks can be prejudice. A gut feeling about what someone is doing is worth acting on.

**"Safety is about spotting the kind of person who looks dangerous."** The theory this course uses
works without knowing anything about offenders, and the course uses behaviour, never appearance.

## Practice

:::exercise Map a route, notice a place, sort six claims
Take 35 minutes over these, on paper.

1. Pick one routine trip or evening you actually make: a commute, a late shift, a night out, the walk from a car park. Write it out as Martin's was written: where, when, with whom, what you carry, where your attention usually is.

2. Map it onto the triangle. Under "target", write what you have on show and where your attention goes. Under "guardian", write who is with you or knows your plan. Under "convergence", write the route and the time, and one alternative for each.

3. List three changes that cost little. For each, write which lever it pulls, what it costs you in time, money and freedom, and its label from the four the course uses. If you cannot find evidence for one, the label is sensible, untested, and that is fine.

4. Then a noticing exercise, with limits written into it. Sit for five minutes somewhere public and busy, in daylight, where you already feel comfortable, with your phone put away. Write down what you notice about the place, not the people in it: the ways in and out, where the staff are, where it's lit and where it is not, which way people move through it. Do not watch, follow, photograph or describe any individual, and don't do this anywhere you feel uneasy. Then spend five minutes there reading your phone, put it away, and write down anything new. Compare the two lists.

5. Sort these six claims into the four labels (tested; sensible, untested; contested; practitioner opinion). Write your answers before you open the checkpoint.

   a. Change the way you walk and you will be less likely to be attacked.

   b. Go out in a group at night.

   c. If you think someone is following you, trust your instincts and act.

   d. Put anything valuable out of sight before you leave your car.

   e. Do not bother with precautions, because offenders will just go somewhere else.

   f. Talking on the phone while you walk cuts how much you notice around you.

If you live with someone who frightens you, don't write any of this where they could find it, and leave out any route they would recognise. The lesson on domestic abuse and stalking starts with the helplines, and it is the better place to start.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your sort for question 5.
Tested: f, for noticing. A comparison of four groups of walkers measured it on one campus in 2009. For crime it's untested, and the advice built on it is sensible, untested.

Sensible, untested: b and d. Both follow from routine activity theory (add a guardian; conceal the target), and both appear in the situational prevention grid, which was written for places and organisations; nobody has trialled either for one person.

Contested: a and e. For a, raters agree and some cues track past harm, but nobody has measured whether changing a walk prevents attack. For e, the research Clarke summarised generally found rather little displacement, so the claim runs against the lean of the evidence; the course still calls the question contested because it read the summary, not the studies.

Practitioner opinion: c. Experienced bodies advise it and no study this course read tested it. If you put c under sensible, untested, notice what makes the difference: the course's rule that a cue is behaviour follows from a theory; "trust your instincts" on its own does not.
