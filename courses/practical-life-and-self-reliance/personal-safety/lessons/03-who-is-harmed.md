Suppose you're trying to decide what to worry about for the people in your house. A son who's
twenty and goes into town most weekends. A mother in her fifties who works from home. An older
relative who's stopped going out after dark. Each of them has a fear, and so do you on their
behalf. This lesson gives you the numbers that say who is actually harmed, by whom, where and
when, in the US and in England and Wales, so that you can set each fear beside a measured risk
without dismissing either. You'll find that the most feared harm and the most likely harm are
usually different things, and that "who harms whom" has several right answers, each belonging to
one crime, one group of people and one source.

This course is education, not legal advice, and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger now, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US. On a 999 call from a mobile, if you can't speak, cough or tap if you can and press 55 when you're prompted; in the US you can text 911 where that's available, but call if you can. If a partner, ex-partner or family member is frightening you, the lesson on domestic abuse and stalking starts with the helplines. Responsibility for a crime lies with the person who commits it: precautions change the odds, not the blame. The law differs between England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

## Age, and the steepest line in the data

:::predict In the US victim survey for 2024, which age group had the highest rate of violent victimisation, and where did people 65 and over come?
The highest rate was among 18 to 24 year olds, at 34.8 violent victimisations per 1,000 people a year. People 65 and over had the lowest rate of any group, 7.5 per 1,000, less than a quarter of the peak. If you guessed the oldest group was near the top, you're in large company: older people are often the most afraid of crime in surveys, and the least often victimised.
:::

Its bulletin for 2024, [*Criminal Victimization, 2024*](https://bjs.ojp.gov/document/cv24.pdf),
gives rates per 1,000 people aged 12 or older for nonfatal violence: rape or sexual assault,
robbery, and aggravated and simple assault, whether or not anyone told the police.[1] Here they are
by age.

Two things about that chart before you use it. First, 2024 was the year the survey changed its
questionnaire, and the bulletin reports only the half of the sample that got the old one, so these
estimates are less precise than usual, and figures from the new questionnaire will not line up with
them.[1] Second, a rate per 1,000 is a population figure. It says that in a thousand people of that
age, that many violent victimisations were reported to the survey in a year. It does not say who in
the thousand, and one person can be victimised more than once, which becomes important later in
this lesson.

The line falls with age after the mid-twenties, and it falls a long way. The group with the most
fear in many surveys, older people, has the lowest measured rate. In England and Wales the pattern
points the same way. The Office for National Statistics' article on violent crime for the year
ending March 2024 found the highest rate among people aged 16 to 24: 2.2% had been a victim of
violence from a stranger in the year, and 1.3% of violence from an acquaintance.[2] This course
did not record that article's figures for older age groups, so I will not give you one.

Lesson 1's example of the man of seventy who stopped going to his evening club comes back here,
with a caution. The 7.5 per 1,000 is a US figure; if he lives in England, the evidence this course
read tells you only that his age group is not where the peak is. And a low rate does not make his
fear a mistake. It's one input to a decision that's his to make.

## How you live, and where

Age is not the only line. The same US bulletin gives rates by marital status and by the kind of
place people live, all for 2024 and all per 1,000 people aged 12 or older:[1]

| Group | Violent victimisations per 1,000, US, 2024 |
|---|---|
| Married | 12.5 |
| Never married | 32.5 |
| Separated | 74.7 |
| Rural | 16.7 |
| Suburban | 23.3 |
| Urban | 34.0 |

Separated people had the highest rate in the table, about six times the rate for married people.
The bulletin reports these rates without explaining them, and it does not say how much of each
difference age or anything else accounts for. This course will not supply a reason the source did not
give. Lesson 7, "Domestic abuse and stalking", comes back to separation from a different direction,
with a study of killings by partners.

In England and Wales, the ONS article for the year ending March 2024 names groups that a "who is at
risk" lesson must not leave out:[2]

- Disabled people: 1% had been a victim of violence from an acquaintance in the year, against 0.4%
  of people without a disability; 15.3% had experienced harassment, against 7.5%.
- Gay, lesbian and bisexual people: harassment was reported by 21% of gay and lesbian people and
  35% of bisexual people, against 8.4% of heterosexual people.
- The young, for stalking and harassment: stalking peaked at ages 16 to 19 (8.8% in the year), and
  so did harassment (19.8%).

The survey's figures for that year carry a label worth knowing. The regulator had suspended the
survey's accreditation in 2022 over quality concerns, including a falling response rate, and ONS
marks the years ending March 2023 and March 2024 as "not designated as accredited official
statistics". Accreditation was reinstated in 2024.[3] The numbers are still the best survey
estimates available for those years; the label tells you to hold them a little more loosely.

None of these figures is a statement about blame. A higher rate for young men, for people who've
separated, or for disabled people describes where harm has fallen. **Responsibility for each of
those crimes lies with the person who committed it**, and nothing a group does or is makes it
otherwise.

## Men, women, and who harms whom

### It depends on the crime

:::predict In England and Wales, who's more often a victim of violence from a stranger in a year, men or women? And who's more often a victim of domestic abuse, stalking and sexual assault?
Men, for violence from a stranger: 1% of men against 0.4% of women in the year ending March 2024. Women, for the other three: domestic abuse 6.6% against 3.0%, stalking 4.0% against 2.3%, sexual assault 3.4% against 0.8%. If you answered "women" to both, the first half is the one most people miss.
:::

The ONS article on violent crime for the year ending March 2024 puts the split in two sentences. For
violence from a stranger, "a higher proportion of men were a victim of violence with or without
injury where the perpetrator was a stranger (1%) than women (0.4%)". For the harms that mostly come
from people the victim knows, the direction reverses: in the same year, 6.6% of women and 3.0% of
men experienced domestic abuse, 4.0% and 2.3% stalking, 3.4% and 0.8% sexual assault, and 11.2% and
6.6% harassment.[2] Each of those is a percentage of adults aged 16 and over living in households
in England and Wales, in one year.

So "who is more at risk, men or women?" has no answer until you say which crime. In the US, the
victim survey for 2024 found rates of 24.7 per 1,000 for males and 21.9 for females, a difference
the bulletin says was not statistically significant that year.[1] That does not mean the sexes meet
the same harms; it means that, added together across all nonfatal violence, the totals came out
close.

### One question, several right answers

Here's the question people argue about most: is violence mostly by strangers, or mostly by people
you know? Each row below is a real answer from a source this course read. Read them one at a time,
with the measure, before you compare any two.

| Source, year, place | What it measures | What it found |
|---|---|---|
| US victim survey (NCVS), 2024 | Share of nonfatal violent victimizations, from interviews | Strangers about 46% (3,091,500 of 6,671,640, worked out from the bulletin's Table 1) |
| FBI police-recorded data (NIBRS), 2024 | Share of offences known to police, by relationship | Strangers 13.5% of assaults, 16.5% of homicide victims, 42.5% of robberies |
| CDC page on partner violence, US, killings | Share of homicide victims | About 1 in 5 killed by an intimate partner; over half of female victims by a current or former male partner |
| ONS Homicide Index, England and Wales, year to March 2025 | Adult homicide victims, by sex | Women: partner or ex-partner 40%, family 16%. Men: friend or acquaintance 20%, stranger 18% |
| ONS, rape since age 16, England and Wales, year to March 2025 | Share of victims | Partner or ex-partner 43.2%; stranger 16.3% (15.0% of female victims, 37.5% of male) |

Sources for the rows, in order: [1], [4], [5], [6], [7].

Now take the rows apart. The US survey's first row is a direct challenge to the slogan that
violence is mostly by people you know: nearly half of nonfatal violent victimizations in 2024 were
by strangers.[1] The FBI's police data, for the same country and year, show strangers in only 13.5%
of assaults.[4] Both are correct. They count different things. The survey counts what people tell
an interviewer, reported to police or not; the police data count offences known to police, where
the assault category includes intimidation, and where large shares of cases are filed as "known to
victim and other" or, for homicide, "all other". The FBI explains that last category: about a third
of homicide victims (32.7%) had relationships "categorized as “all other,” meaning the victim(s) did
not see the offender to determine whether they knew them, was a mutual combatant of the offender, or
had a combination of other undefined relationships with multiple offenders."[4] Those are reasons
the two can differ; which of them does the most work, nobody this course read has measured.

Robbery is the stranger crime in the police data: "the most common relationship type reported by
robbery victims in 2024 was stranger (42.5%)."[4] Killings of women are the opposite. The ONS
Homicide Index puts it plainly: "Adult female victims (defined as those aged 16 years and over)
were most commonly killed by a partner or ex-partner (40%) or a family member (16%) in year ending
(YE) March 2025. For adult males, the suspected killer was more commonly a friend or acquaintance
(20%) or a stranger (18%)".[6] The CDC's page on intimate partner violence gives the US counterpart
from its death reporting system: about one in five homicide victims killed by an intimate partner,
and over half of female homicide victims killed by a current or former male partner.[5]

There's one more England and Wales figure that looks as if it belongs in the table and does not. The
crime survey for the year ending March 2026 estimated that about 0.7% of people aged 16 and over had
been a victim of violence from a stranger in the year, and 0.4% from an acquaintance.[3] Those are
percentages of all adults, not shares of violence. Put 0.7% in the same column as the US survey's
46% and you've compared a slice of the population with a slice of the crimes: two different
measures, which is the kind of mix-up lesson 2 was about.

:::checkpoint A friend says, "The FBI's data show only 13.5% of assaults are by strangers, so the survey's 46% must be wrong." What would you tell them?
Both can be right, because they measure different things. The survey counts nonfatal violent victimizations people describe to an interviewer, whether or not police heard about them. The FBI's figure is a share of assault offences known to police, where assault includes intimidation and a large share of cases sit in "known to victim and other". Say which source you mean and what it counts, and the contradiction goes away. What does not go away is that "what share of violence is by strangers?" has no single answer.
:::

### Testing a leaflet's sentence

Here's the method at work. A charity leaflet says: "Most violence is committed by someone the
victim knows." Test it against each row in turn, and write down what you'd say for each.

For killings of women, it's right in both countries: 56% of adult female homicide victims in
England and Wales were killed by a partner, ex-partner or family member, before you add friends
and acquaintances,[6] and the CDC reports over half of female homicide victims in the US killed by a
current or former male partner.[5] For rape, it holds too, as far as the figures go: 16.3% of victims since age 16 in
England and Wales were raped by a stranger, fewer than one in five.[7]

For nonfatal violence in the US as a whole, the sentence hides more than it shows. Strangers
account for about 46% in the survey. Whether people the victim knew make up all of the rest, this
course can't say, because it did not record the bulletin's rows for unknown relationships; either
way, "most" is at best a narrow majority, and a reader would come away thinking strangers are rare.

:::checkpoint Now test the leaflet on men. Using the figures above, is "most violence is committed by someone the victim knows" a fair sentence to say to a young man in England and Wales?
No, not without splitting it. Violence from strangers falls more on men than on women (1% of men against 0.4% of women in the year ending March 2024), and it peaks at 16 to 24. Adult male homicide victims were killed by a stranger in 18% of cases and by a friend or acquaintance in 20%, not mostly by partners or family. And of male victims of rape since 16, 37.5% were raped by a stranger, far more than the 15.0% of female victims. A corrected leaflet names crime, sex and source: "In England and Wales, women who are killed are most often killed by a partner or ex-partner, and fewer than one in five rape victims were raped by a stranger (ONS); violence from strangers falls mostly on young men (ONS crime survey)."
:::

That corrected sentence is longer, and it's supposed to be. Every clause is a label, and without
the labels it would tell half its readers something false.

### Where and when

The place follows the relationship. For rape, the ONS article for the year ending March 2025 found
that of victims since age 16, "over one-third (38.2%) were victimised in their own home and 8.0% were
victimised on the street, in a car park, park, or another open public space"; the perpetrator's home
came next, at 28.5%.[7] The street is a real part of the picture and a small one. That does not make
advice about walking home wrong; it means most of the risk of rape sits somewhere a street-safety
tip does not reach, which is why this course gives domestic abuse a whole lesson.

For violence generally, the most recent ONS narrative on when and where is old, from the year
ending March 2018, so read it as a pattern rather than a current level. That year, "more than half of
violent incidents (54%) occurred in the evening or during the night", and victims believed the
perpetrator was under the influence of alcohol in 39% of violent incidents.[8] Location followed
the relationship: 78% of domestic violence happened around the home, while violence from strangers
happened most often around work (30%), in the street (21%) or in or near a pub or club (19%). The
survey's "around work" means work premises, inside or outside, and their car parks; the pub or
club category is separate.[8]

The US has an older special report on strangers, covering 2005 to 2010. In those years, "strangers
committed 62% of violent victimizations occurring in public places", and about 9% of violent
victimizations in the victim's home.[9] Men were victimised by strangers at about twice the rate
of women in 2010, 9.5 against 4.7 per 1,000.[9] The levels have changed since; the pattern is what
still teaches.

Put together, the two countries' data point the same way on place: strangers in public, and more
often against men; people known to the victim at home, for the harms that fall most on women. The
evening and alcohol figures above are for violent incidents of every kind, not for strangers alone.

### How killings start

The ONS Homicide Index for the year ending March 2025 counted 522 victims in England and Wales, 8.6
per million people, "the lowest rate since 1977".[6] Its account of how they happened is the most
useful fact in this lesson for lesson 8: "Around half (51%, 266 offences) of all homicide cases were
thought to have resulted from a quarrel, a revenge attack or a loss of temper". Only 5% were in the
furtherance of theft. A sharp instrument was the method in 39%, most often a kitchen knife. Women
were mostly killed in or around a house, men mostly in public.[6]

So killings rarely begin as a theft (5%), and about half begin as an argument. That's why lesson 8, "Talking it down", matters as much as anything in this course.

ONS attaches a caution to its figures on who is killed, and it applies to every table in this
lesson: "Demographic factors discussed in this section are not necessarily independently related to
homicide."[6] It points to socioeconomic factors, for individuals and for neighbourhoods. A rate
for a group tells you where harm fell. It does not tell you that the group's age or sex caused it.

### What the surveys undercount

Two of the harms in this section are the ones the surveys are least able to see, and both sources
say so themselves. ONS says the survey's interviewer questions underestimate domestic violence,
because victims may not want to disclose it with an interviewer present, and it prefers the
separate self-completion questions for domestic abuse.[2][3] In the US, a National Research Council
panel concluded that the victim survey "does not measure the low incidence events of rape and sexual
assault with the precision needed for policy and research purposes."[10] So the harms that fall most
on women are the ones most likely to be counted low. Keep that in mind when anyone uses the survey
to say women's fear is out of proportion to their risk.

## How often violence means serious injury

Most violence does not end in serious injury. The England and Wales crime survey, as reported in the
ONS article for the year ending March 2024, found: "The CSEW estimated that victims sustained a
physical injury in 42% of violent incidents in YE March 2023." The commonest injury was minor
bruising or a black eye, in 25% of violent incidents; cuts were 7% and broken bones 2%.[2] Notice
the year: the injury figure is for the year ending March 2023, though the article is the next
year's.

In the US report on strangers, for 2005 to 2010, "about 22% of victims of violence committed by
strangers were injured, compared to 31% of victims who knew the offender."[9] Violence from someone
you know was more likely to injure than violence from a stranger, in those years and in that
survey.

None of this makes violence minor to the person it happens to. The figure is useful for a narrower job: it corrects the
picture in which every incident ends in hospital, and it lets you see that most of the harm you're
planning against is the ordinary kind.

## The commonest crime is not violence

If you asked the England and Wales crime survey what crime a household is most likely to meet, the
answer for the year ending March 2026 was fraud. Of 9.6 million incidents the survey counted, 4.5
million were fraud, nearly half; violence with or without injury was 989,000, about a tenth. About
7.8% of adults were victims of fraud in the year.[3] In the US, lesson 2 met the Federal Trade Commission's 3 million fraud reports for 2025. The
same testimony to Congress gives the losses those reports described, $15.9 billion, and the FTC's
own estimate, allowing for the fraud nobody reports, is that the cost to consumers in 2024 "could
be as high as $195.9 billion".[11] Those are reports and an estimate, not a survey, so they don't
line up with the England and Wales figures; each tells you fraud is common in its own country.

This course keeps fraud short, because other courses already teach it well. Lesson 5, "Home, the
door and the doorstep", covers the con that turns up on your doorstep. Personal Finance
Fundamentals lesson 10, "Defending it", teaches the three things nearly every scam has to do and
the payment rail as the gate, and Digital Literacy lesson 11, "Recognising a con, and the ten
minutes after you clicked", covers the messages. The point here is only the weighting: a plan for a
household that covers the street and not the phone is planning for the rarer harm.

## Once is the best predictor of twice

:::predict Something has just happened to you: a burglary, say, or a theft from your car. Compared with before it happened, is your chance of another one in the next few weeks higher, lower or about the same?
Higher, and highest soon after. That's what the research on repeat victimisation found, and it runs against the common feeling that lightning does not strike twice.
:::

Ken Pease's review for the Home Office in 1998 pulled together what was known, and its conclusions
are four clauses long: "victimisation is the best single predictor of victimisation; that when
victimisation recurs it tends to do so quickly; that high crime rates and hot spots are as they are
substantially because of rates of repeat victimisation; that a major reason for repetition is that
offenders take later advantage of opportunities which the first offence throws up".[12] That last
clause is the mechanism, and it's Pease's, not mine: the first crime shows someone the unlocked
window, the route, the account, and it's still there next week.

You may have heard a number attached to this, that 4% of people suffer 44% of crime. Pease himself
warns against leaning on it: "The conclusion that 4% of people suffer 44% of crime is the sound-bite
from Farrell and Pease (1993) which seems to stick in people’s memory, but this kind of calculation
is dependent upon various counting conventions, and varies by place and time."[12] Quote the idea,
not the number.

The home security study that lesson 5 relies on found the same thing for burglary: "previous
burglary victimisation is the single highest predictive factor of current risk".[13]

So here is the one practical conclusion this lesson draws, and it's this course's step from those
findings rather than a finding itself. **After something happens, the weeks straight after are the
time to change the circumstance that allowed it**: mend the lock, change the route, secure the
account that was breached. Label it sensible, untested. This course found no trial comparing people who
changed the lock that week with people who waited, but the research on repeats says the risk is
highest then, and says why. It is not a judgement on what anyone did before. The first crime was the
offender's choice; the change afterwards is just the thing most likely to stop a second one.

:::checkpoint Pease gives a reason repeats happen. In his words, what is it, and what does it tell you about which change to make after a crime?
That "offenders take later advantage of opportunities which the first offence throws up". So the change most worth making is the one that closes the opportunity the first crime revealed: the particular window, route or account, not a general tightening of everything. And because "when victimisation recurs it tends to do so quickly", the time is soon.
:::

## One family, two sets of numbers

Go back to the household from the start, and suppose they live in England. The son is twenty and
goes into a city centre most weekends. His mother is 52 and works from home. She worries most about
him being stabbed on a night out. He does not worry much about anything.

Start with him. The measured risk that fits his life is violence from a stranger: in the year ending
March 2024, 2.2% of people aged 16 to 24 in England and Wales were victims of violence from a
stranger, the highest of any age group, and men more often than women (1% against 0.4% across all
ages).[2] The 2018 pattern puts that violence mostly in the evening and night, and around pubs,
clubs, the street and work.[8] If it happens, it most likely will not mean serious injury: 42% of violent
incidents caused any physical injury in the year ending March 2023, and broken bones 2%.[2]

Now the harm his mother fears. Homicide in England and Wales in the year ending March 2025 was 12.3
per million males, about one in 81,000 in a year, and that rate is for males of every age.[6] A
sharp instrument was used in 39% of all killings. The fear is serious because the harm is the worst
there is; the chance is small. His likelier risks are a stranger's assault, as above, and fraud:
7.8% of adults were victims of fraud in the year ending March 2026, though that figure is for all
adults, and this course did not read one for his age group.[3]

Then her. For women aged 16 and over in England and Wales, in the year ending March 2024, the survey
found violence from a stranger at 0.4%, harassment at 11.2%, and domestic abuse, stalking and sexual
assault at 6.6%, 4.0% and 3.4%.[2] Those are figures for all women, not for her, and they say
nothing about her household or her marriage. The example stops there, because that's where the
evidence about her stops. Her likeliest measured harms, as for her son, include fraud (7.8% of
adults) and harassment.

What the family learns is that their fears and their risks are shaped differently. The most feared
harm, a killing, is the rarest. The likeliest harms are ordinary: a scam for both of them, an
assault in town for him, harassment for her. That's not a reason for her to stop worrying about
him. It's a reason for the household's planning to cover the phone and the bank account as well as
the night out.

## What people get wrong

**"Most violence is by strangers,"** and its mirror, **"most violence is by people you know."** Each
is true for some crimes and victims and false for others. Killings of women and rape: mostly by
people known to the victim. Robbery: the stranger crime. Nonfatal violence in the US survey: nearly
half by strangers. Violence from strangers in England and Wales: more often against men than women. Say which
crime, which victims and which source.

**"The person most at risk on the street at night is a woman alone."** Measured by violence from
strangers, it's young men.[2] That does not make a woman's fear on the street irrational: harassment
is common, and 11.2% of women reported it in the year ending March 2024. It means the street
is not where most serious harm to women happens.

**"Older people are most at risk."** They're the least victimised age group in the US survey, at 7.5
per 1,000 in 2024 against 34.8 for 18 to 24 year olds.[1]

**"Violence usually means serious injury."** In England and Wales, fewer than half of violent
incidents caused any physical injury, and broken bones were 2%.[2]

**"Lightning does not strike twice."** Being victimised is the best single predictor of being
victimised again, and repeats come quickly.[12]
