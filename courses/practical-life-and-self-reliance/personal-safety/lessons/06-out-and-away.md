Most safety advice you'll ever be given is about this lesson's ground: the walk home, the late
train, the first night in a strange city, the coffee with someone you have only met through an app.
It comes from charities, governments and friends, it is usually sensible, and almost none of it has
been tested, so this lesson sorts it by what stands behind it and then asks where the street sits
among the risks people actually face.

:::callout Before you rely on this
This course is education, not legal advice, and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger now, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US. On a 999 call from a mobile, if you can't speak, cough or tap if you can and press 55 when you're prompted; in the US you can text 911 where that's available, but call if you can. If a partner, ex-partner or family member is frightening you, the lesson on domestic abuse and stalking starts with the helplines. Responsibility for a crime lies with the person who commits it: precautions change the odds, not the blame. The law differs between England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

The responsibility sentence in that box carries a lot of weight here. Advice about where to walk
and when is exactly the kind that turns, in the wrong mouth, into "she shouldn't have been out".
Nothing in this lesson means that. A route changes the odds of meeting someone who has decided to
hurt a person. It has nothing to do with who made that decision.

## The advice, as it's written

Start with what the bodies say, in their own words, before asking what stands behind it.

The [Suzy Lamplugh Trust](https://www.suzylamplugh.org/personal-safety-out-and-about), the UK
personal safety charity lessons 4 and 5 met, puts its street advice under "Plan ahead". It opens:
"Before you go out, consider how you are going to get home", and on foot, "Try to use well-lit, busy
streets and use the route you know best."[1] For running it suggests "Consider just having one
earphone in", and it tells you to let someone know where you are going and for how long.[1] Both
of its advice pages this course read carry the same disclaimer, and it is the most honest sentence in
any of the advice this lesson reads: the advice "is in no way intended to suggest that taking such
actions will eliminate incidents of aggression and violence. The guidance merely helps readers better
understand how to assess and mitigate risks to their personal safety."[1]

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) has a page on [solo and independent
travel](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/solo-and-independent-travel). "Leave an itinerary with someone
at home and agree how regularly you will be in contact". Consider "if walking alone, avoiding
poorly lit areas, particularly at night". And "never leaving your key where someone can note your
room number".[2] It says that travelling alone may bring an increased risk of being a victim of
crime, and its checklist for your room puts fire exits and carbon monoxide beside locks and
keys.[2]

In the US, the Washington DC police's [brochure on robbery and
assault](https://mpdc.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/mpdc/publication/attachments/robbery_assault.pdf),
from January 2011, gives the same kinds of advice for city streets: "If possible, don't walk alone
during late-night hours", and "Let a family member or friend know your destination and your
estimated time of arrival or return. That way, the police can be notified as quickly as possible if
there is a problem."[3]

The US State Department's page for [women
travelers](https://web.archive.org/web/20251226183516/https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/planning/personal-needs/women.html)
says: "Consider using app-based transportation companies, which offer a record of your ride";
"Think ahead and come up with a safety plan to deal with unsafe situations, in the event you end up
in one"; and "Consider bringing personal safety whistles or alarms. However, confirm legality before
traveling with items like pepper spray."[4] Its [traveler's
checklist](https://web.archive.org/web/20260103122443/https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/international-travel/before-you-go/travelers-checklist.html)
adds two practical steps: enrol in the Smart Traveler Enrollment Program (STEP), which "also helps
the U.S. embassy or consulate reach you or your emergency contact in an emergency", and copy your
documents: "Give a set of copies to a trusted friend or family member."[5]

Be clear about what the US half rests on. The US street advice here is one city police force's
brochure, Washington DC's from 2011; the State Department writes for Americans travelling abroad,
not for someone walking home in their own city. The National Crime Prevention Council has
street-safety material this course could not read. So the US half is thinner: one city's police, and
the State Department's advice for abroad.

Read all of that together and a pattern shows. Most of the advice is one of three kinds: plan before
you go, have someone who knows the plan, and stay where there are other people and light. Lesson 4
gave you the triangle those map onto. The question now is how strong the evidence under each one
is.

## What stands behind each piece

### Lights

:::predict Better street lighting is the one piece of this advice with a real research review behind it. A review pooled studies of areas that improved their lighting and compared them with similar areas that did not. If lighting works by letting people see who is about, what would you expect to happen to crime in the daytime, when the lights are off?
If seeing were the whole story, daytime crime should not change, because nothing about daytime visibility changed. The review found that when lighting was improved, "nighttime crimes did not decrease more than daytime crimes", which is why the review's summary points to a second explanation. The next paragraphs give it.
:::

Welsh and Farrington's review, published by the Campbell Collaboration in 2008, pooled 13 studies
from the UK and the US over four decades. In the words of the Collaboration's [plain-language
summary](https://web.archive.org/web/20260920211306/https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/2015/09/improved-street-lighting-can-reduce-crime/),
crime "decreased by 21% in areas that experienced street lighting improvements compared to similar
areas that did not". The UK studies found 38% and the US studies 7%.[6] The summary adds that
the American studies were older and several counted only night-time crime.[6]

The summary names two ways lighting is thought to work: by making people easier to see, which
deters offenders, and by signalling that someone has invested in a place, which raises pride in it.
Because night-time crime didn't fall more than daytime crime, the summary says, "community pride
may play a crucial role".[6] That is a suggestion from the pattern, not a tested mechanism,
and this course read the summary, not the full review. So "attackers avoid light because they can
be seen" is more than the evidence says. The honest version is that lit areas had less crime, and
the summary leaves the reason open.

Notice too what was measured: crime across an area, before and after. That is not the risk to you,
walking one street on one night. Choosing the lit road is sensible, untested, in this course's
labels, with area-level evidence nearby.

### Cameras

[Piza and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1111/1745-9133.12419) pooled forty years of evaluations of
closed-circuit television in 2019. Their summary: "The findings show that CCTV is associated with a
significant and modest decrease in crime. The largest and most consistent effects of CCTV were
observed in car parks."[7] They also found effects in residential areas, larger effects where
someone was actively watching the screens, and they advise that cameras be aimed at vehicle and
property crime and not used on their own.[7]

For your own safety a camera is at best a record. It is not someone who will step in, and in lesson
4's terms it is not a capable guardian unless a person is watching and able to act. The common advice
to walk where there are cameras is sensible, untested for personal safety, and the evidence under it
is about something else.

### The rest

Everything else on the lists above, from planning the way home to telling someone your plans,
follows from routine activity theory and the guardianship evidence lesson 4 gave you. It's also
lesson 4's grid taken onto the street: the grid's "Take routine precautions: go out in group at
night, leave signs of occupancy, carry phone" sits under "Extend guardianship".[8] No study this
course found has measured whether following any of it prevents harm to one person. That doesn't make
it bad advice. It makes it sensible, untested, and it's worth knowing which is which, because a
piece of advice you know to be untested is one you can weigh against its cost.

Ten common pieces of advice, most from the pages above, sorted:

| Advice | Label | What stands behind it |
|---|---|---|
| Plan how you will get home before you go out | Sensible, untested | Routine activity theory; no outcome measured |
| Use well-lit, busy streets | Sensible, untested | Area-level lighting review; nothing on one walker's risk |
| Walk where there is CCTV | Sensible, untested | Modest area-level fall, largest in car parks |
| Tell someone your plans and when to expect you | Sensible, untested | A guardian who can act; no outcome measured |
| Put the phone away when walking somewhere unfamiliar | Sensible, untested | People on a call noticed least (lesson 4); crime not measured |
| Keep one earphone in when running | Practitioner opinion | No study read; music users noticed no less in lesson 4's study |
| If followed, cross over, look, keep moving, get to people | Practitioner opinion | No study read |
| Set off a personal alarm and leave | Practitioner opinion | No study read |
| Book your first night's accommodation before you travel | Sensible, untested | The FCDO's reasoning; no outcome measured |
| Use an app-based ride that keeps a record | Sensible, untested | The State Department's reasoning; no outcome measured |

Not one row is tested. On the street, the course's question, measured on whom and measured as what,
keeps getting the same answer: the nearest measurements are of areas, or of noticing, not of whether
a person who followed the advice was harmed less.

## Why telling someone helps, when it does

If nothing here is tested, why does the course still pass it on? Because most of it rests on one
mechanism with solid theory behind it.

Lesson 4 gave you Cohen and Felson's [routine activity
theory](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2094589) and its capable guardian, and counted two kinds of
guardian: a person who is with you, and a person who is not there but knows your plan and will act if
it breaks.[9] Most of the advice above adds the second kind.

Think about what "tell someone where you're going" actually does. On its own, nothing. A friend
who knows you have gone out cannot act on that. What turns knowledge into guardianship is a plan that
can visibly break: you said you'd message by eleven, and at quarter past there is no message. Now
someone knows something's wrong, knows where you were meant to be, and knows when. That is the
course's reading of Cohen and Felson applied to a single evening, and the step from their theory
to your walk home is the course's, not theirs.

So the rule this lesson builds everything on is this: a plan is only as useful as **what happens
when it breaks**. A plan with no agreed check-in, and no agreement about what the other person does
if it is missed, is mostly a comfort.

:::checkpoint Two friends each "let someone know" before a night out. One texts her brother "out tonight, don't wait up". The other texts her flatmate "leaving the bar at 11, home by 11.30, I'll message when I'm in; if you haven't heard by midnight, ring me, and if I don't answer, ring the bar". Which one has added a guardian in the sense this lesson uses, and why?
The second. The first message tells her brother she's out, but there is no time, no place and nothing he is meant to do, so nothing would ever make him act. The second gives a time, a route and a place, a moment when silence means something, and a thing to do about it. Both are sensible, untested, but only the second can work the way the theory says a guardian works.
:::

## Owen's night out

Owen is twenty, a student, and he is going to a party across town on Saturday. He'll walk the
twenty minutes home afterwards, probably around one in the morning. He isn't worried. The figures
aren't on his side there: lesson 3 showed that in England and Wales, in the year to March 2024,
violence from strangers fell on 1% of men against 0.4% of women, and on 2.2% of people aged 16 to
24.[10] That is a reason for a plan, not for staying in.

Martin's walk in lesson 4 already dealt with the route and the phone. Owen's night adds what
Martin's did not need: a decision made before he goes out, and a check-in that cannot go off by
accident.

**1. Decide how he is getting home before he goes out.** The Lamplugh Trust's first line.[1] What
it is for: the decision gets made while he's sober and fresh, not at one in the morning. If the walk
home looks bad on reflection, a night bus or a shared taxi can be planned now. Sensible, untested.

**2. A friend who knows.** His flatmate knows the address of the party and that he expects to leave
around one. If she has not heard from him by two, she rings him; if he does not answer, she rings the
friend whose party it is. What it is for: a guardian who can act when the plan breaks, as above.
Sensible, untested.

**3 and 4. The high road, and the phone in his pocket.** These are Martin's steps: the lit high road
rather than the shorter cut through a park, for more people and the lit streets the lighting review
found had less crime at area level;[6] and his attention on the street, since in lesson 4's
walking study people on a phone call noticed least, though people who choose to phone may differ,
and it did not measure crime.[11] Both sensible, untested.

:::checkpoint Step 5 is yours before you read on. His flatmate has a time and an action. What does Owen himself still need to agree, so that the plan cannot go off by accident and send her ringing round at two in the morning for nothing?
An agreed "home" message, and a message if the plan changes. If he goes on somewhere else, or stays over, he says so, with the new time. Without that, the check-in either raises a false alarm or, worse, teaches his flatmate to ignore a missed message. The agreed message is what keeps the guardian's trigger meaningful.
:::

**5. An agreed "home" message, and a message if plans change.** What it is for: the check-in means
something only if a missed message is rare and real. Sensible, untested.

Like Martin's, the plan leaves Owen's life the size it was: a few messages and a few minutes. If
something happened to him on the high road anyway, the plan would not have failed him and he would not
have failed the plan. Responsibility would sit with whoever chose to hurt him.

## If you think you're being followed

The Lamplugh Trust's advice for this moment is a short sequence. If you think you're being
followed, it says, trust your instincts and act: as confidently as you can, "cross the road, turning
to see who is behind you. If you are still being followed, keep moving. Head to a busy area and
tell people what is happening". If you need to, call the police.[1]

Each step has a reason you can see. Crossing and turning tests the suspicion: someone who crosses
with you is different from someone who carries on. Keeping moving keeps options open. A busy area
brings other people in, and telling them turns bystanders into help. Those reasons are this
course's, drawn after the fact; the charity gives none, and no theory predicts the order of the
steps, so the sequence is practitioner opinion, from the UK's personal safety charity, and nobody
has tested it. Lesson 4 said the same about "trust your instincts", and gave you the rule to go with
it: the instinct worth acting on is set off by what someone is doing, here following you across a
road and back, not by how they look.

On asking for help, the Trust says that if you can attract passers-by you are more likely to get
help if you shout a specific instruction, such as "Call the police!"[1] First Aid and CPR lesson 1
reached the same place from a different direction: in an emergency, point at one person and give
them a job. A named task gets done; a general cry for help leaves everyone waiting for someone
else.

On personal alarms, the Trust's plainest line is the one this course keeps: "Don't wait to check
that your alarm has had the desired effect; just go."[1] An alarm is for setting off and leaving,
nothing more, and this course names no product.

This course read the UK sequence only. The one US police brochure it read says "Trust your
instincts. If something or someone makes you uneasy, avoid the person or leave", and gives no
sequence.[3]

## Travel

Travel is guardianship applied far from home, with one extra problem: you don't know the place.

The FCDO's reason for its most specific piece of advice is worth reading slowly. It recommends
booking accommodation before you go, "in particular your first night's accommodation. You are at
your most vulnerable when you first arrive in a foreign country as you are likely to be tired and
unsure of your surroundings".[2] That is lesson 4's triangle in one sentence. Tired means
attention is low. Unsure means you do not know which street is the lit one. Arriving late with
nowhere booked means walking around with luggage, looking at a phone, asking strangers for
directions: a suitable target, with no plan anyone else knows. The same page adds a line worth
keeping for any offer you would rather turn down: "remember that being safe is more important than
being polite".[2]

The rest of the travel advice works the same way. An itinerary left at home, with an agreed rhythm
of contact, is Owen's check-in stretched to a week.[2] Copies of your documents with someone you
trust, and STEP for US citizens, let someone at home, or an embassy, act if the plan breaks.[5]
An app-based ride, which the State Department says offers "a record of your ride", puts a record
where there wasn't one.[4] Each is sensible, untested.

And the FCDO's lines on fire exits and carbon monoxide are a useful correction: its own checklist
puts "poor ventilation can kill" beside the locks.[2] A plan that checks the smoke alarm is a
better plan than one that only thinks about strangers.

One piece of travel advice carries a legal warning. The State Department says to "confirm legality
before traveling with items like pepper spray".[4] In Great Britain the answer is that a spray
is a prohibited weapon under the Firearms Act 1968, which makes possessing one an offence, not only
carrying it,[12] and lesson 14, "The home, property, and carrying something for protection",
gives the law. This is general information, not legal advice. The law differs by state, and between
England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. Travel Skills, later on the Core, takes travel
further than this course does.

## Meeting someone from online

Meeting someone you have only talked to online is a special case of the same idea, and the Lamplugh
Trust's [Safety Online](https://www.suzylamplugh.org/safety-online) page has the clearest version.
"Tell someone where you're going, who you're meeting (name, website arranged through etc) and check
in with them before, during and after the date." "Agree that if you don't check in as agreed or
respond then your friend would call the place where you've met or even go there to check on you."
"Meet in a public space and plan an exit strategy if needed." And, while you are still chatting:
"consider talking about where you've been rather than where you're going."[13]

Read that against Owen's plan and the parts line up: a named person, a place, times, and an action
if the check-in is missed. It's guardianship, built for a first date. The last line is the target
lever: where you are going is something a stranger can use; where you have been mostly is not. The
page also advises keeping your home address and other personal details to yourself on a first
date.[13] All of it is sensible, untested.

One line on the same page this course doesn't pass on. It advises changing passwords regularly, and
the UK's National Cyber Security Centre has advised against forcing regular changes since its 2015
guidance.[14] Digital Literacy lesson 10 explains why, and it is where passwords belong.

### Graham's first date

Graham is forty-six, divorced two years, and he is meeting a woman from a dating app for coffee on
Saturday afternoon. He's read the Lamplugh steps and thinks they are for women. They aren't: the
page is written for anyone, and nothing in its reasoning depends on who is meeting whom. A man
meeting a stranger is still meeting a stranger, and Owen's figures above are a reminder that men
are more often the victims of violence from strangers than women are.

So Graham's plan uses the steps as written. His brother knows her first name, the app, the café
and the time. He'll message at the start, halfway and when he leaves, and if his brother hears
nothing by five, he rings, then rings the café. Graham drives himself there, so he can leave when
he likes.

The wrinkle comes after the coffee. Suppose it goes well, and they arrange to meet again, then
again. Most people's plans stop after the first date, because the stranger has become someone they
know. But the Lamplugh steps were built for a public place, and the first meetings somewhere
private, her home or his, are where that protection stops. That is the course's reading of the
steps, not a finding about dating, and it does not mean that anyone Graham meets is a risk.

:::checkpoint What does that point change about Graham's plan for the next few meetings, and what stays the same?
It changes how long the check-in lasts. The friend-knows-the-plan habit is worth keeping for the first meetings that move somewhere private, because that is where the public-place protection stops. It does not change how he treats her, and it is not suspicion: the check-in is invisible to her and costs almost nothing. If a partner ever starts to control, track or frighten him, the lesson "Domestic abuse and stalking" describes the help and safety planning the services offer, for men as well as women.
:::

## Where the street sits

Almost all of the advice in this lesson so far is about the street. Lesson 3 has figures from the
[ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/articles/natureofsexualassaultbyrapeorpenetrationenglandandwales/yearendingmarch2025)
on where rape happens.

:::predict Before you look back at lesson 3: of people in England and Wales who had been raped or assaulted by penetration since the age of 16, what share do you think said it happened in a street, car park, park or other open public space? And what share in their own home?
8.0% of victims said it happened in a street, car park, park or open space, and 38.2% in their own home (39.3% of women), with the perpetrator's home next at 28.5%. Those figures pool the survey years to March 2020 and March 2025.[23] If you guessed the street was the commonest place, you've guessed what most safety advice implies.
:::

Violence from strangers has a different shape: as Owen's figures showed, it falls more on men and
on the young.[10] So "don't walk alone at night" isn't wrong. It's the smaller part of the
picture, and taken as the core of women's safety it points attention away from where most serious
sexual violence happens: in homes, the victim's or the perpetrator's.

That doesn't make the street safe, or the fear of it mistaken. What many people meet on the street
is real, and it is harassment. In an ONS survey of adults in Great Britain in February and March 2022,
27% of women and 16% of men said they had experienced at least one form of harassment in the previous
twelve months.[24] In the same survey, 82% of women said they felt very or fairly unsafe walking
alone in a park or other open space after dark, against 42% of men.[24] Lesson 1 showed what that
feeling costs: in the same survey, the share of people who felt unsafe who had stopped walking in
parks after dark had risen for both men and women.[24] Harassment is information about a place, as
lesson 1 said, and the cost of avoiding a place is real too.

## The argument about advice aimed at women

This is a contested question, part empirical and part value, and the course describes it rather
than settling it.

### The critique

The strongest statement of the case against advice like this lesson's comes from UK research on
violence against women. [Vera-Gray and Kelly](https://repository.londonmet.ac.uk/5706/), writing in
2020, start from harassment in public space and ask what it costs women beyond their safety: their
freedom. Their abstract describes the "safety work" mandated for women and girls in public, meaning
the precautions women are expected to take there, and says it functions "to limit women's space for
action and responsiblise them for preventing violence" (their spelling).[25] This course read the
abstract only.

Put in its strongest form, in this course's words (the abstract gives the second and third points;
the first is the course's reading of lesson 3's figures): the advice is misdirected, because most of
the risk to women is from men they know, not strangers on the street; it's costly, because every
"don't go there" and "don't go alone" is a piece of freedom given up, and shrinks the lives of people
who have done nothing; and it shifts responsibility, because a list of precautions invites the
question of which one a victim skipped. That is a mainstream position in UK research on violence
against women, not a fringe one, and the figures in the last section are consistent with its first
point. Its target isn't a straw man either: the Washington DC police brochure quoted earlier says "If
possible, don't walk alone during late-night hours".[3] Lesson 1 met feminist criminologists'
related argument, as Scott summarises it, that street-crime framing hides violence behind closed
doors.

### The defence

The case for teaching precautions starts from the same evidence and draws a different conclusion.
Precautions change the odds: lesson 5 showed the strongest evidence in the course, that locks and
lights in combination went with far lower burglary risk. That evidence is about burglary at home;
carrying it to the street is the defence's step, and nothing this course read has tested the street
advice itself, as the table above showed. On blame, the reply lesson 4 met in Clarke, written with
Felson, is that routine precautions let people take responsibility without accepting criminal blame
or even civil liability, and do not exempt offenders from criminal responsibility.[26] And the
Lamplugh Trust's own disclaimer, quoted at the top of this lesson, refuses to promise that any
precaution will prevent violence.

The defence has one more piece, and it comes from a randomised trial. [A
programme](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26061837/) for first-year university women that teaches
them to "assess risk from acquaintances, overcome emotional barriers in acknowledging danger, and
engage in effective verbal and physical self-defense" lowered the one-year risk of completed rape,
compared with a group given brochures instead.[27] Lesson 12, "Resistance training, and choosing
a class", teaches the trial and its limits.

Critics of resistance education have a reply, which the trial's authors report themselves in their
[two-year follow-up](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5821219/): some feminist scholars
argue that it is misguided, because perpetrators "may simply move on to another woman". The authors
suspect instead that perpetrators "may learn important lessons" from being thwarted, and grant that
which explanation is right is an empirical question.[28] And training still asks women to do the
work, which brings back the critique's second and third points.

### How they fit

On one point the two sides are closer than they look, and on another they are as far apart as ever.
The critique's target is advice that tells women to restrict where they go and when. The trial
tested something else: training that helps people recognise and resist the risks they actually face,
and it was aimed at acquaintances, not strangers. Vera-Gray and Kelly's own abstract ends by
exploring feminist self-defence as a way of making safety work visible and measurable.[25] On the
empirical part, then, restriction and skills are different things, and the evidence speaks to them
differently.

What's left is a value question: who should carry the burden of prevention? One answer holds that
people can reasonably take cheap precautions for themselves while the larger work goes on, and that
refusing them useful information has costs too. Another puts the burden on the people who commit
violence and on the institutions around them, and sees advice to individuals, training included, as
a burden that falls on potential victims. The course does not choose. It holds one commitment that
every position here shares: responsibility for a crime lies with the person who commits it.

## Talking to children

This course is for adults, and child protection is not its subject. But many adults have children
in the house, and the rule many children are taught, "never talk to strangers", is worth checking
against the figures the same way.

:::predict How many children in the whole of the US do you think were victims of a stereotypical kidnapping, the kind by a stranger or slight acquaintance that most parents picture, in a year?
An estimated 105, in 2011, "virtually the same as the 1997 estimate".[29]
:::

The US Justice Department's [third national study of missing
children](https://ojjdp.ojp.gov/sites/g/files/xyckuh176/files/pubs/249249.pdf) defines a
stereotypical kidnapping narrowly: "abductions in which a slight acquaintance or stranger moves a
child at least 20 feet or holds the child at least 1 hour, and in which the child is detained
overnight, transported at least 50 miles, held for ransom, abducted with the intent to keep
permanently, or killed".[29] It found how most of these kidnappings began: the kidnappers "lured
almost 70 percent of victims through deception or nonthreatening pretexts".[29] Those victims
were most commonly aged 12 to 17, so the figures are mostly about teenagers.[29]

The National Center for Missing & Exploited Children (NCMEC), the US charity that handles missing
child reports, puts it this way: "only one percent of missing child cases reported to NCMEC are the
classic nonfamily abduction, or stereotypical kidnapping, that many parents fear."[30] Its
[blog](https://www.missingkids.org/blog/2023/staying-away-from-stranger-danger) pairs each old rule
with a script to use instead, for younger children: its safety programme, KidSmartz, is for
kindergarten to fifth grade in US schools.[30]

- Instead of "Never talk to strangers": "You should not approach just anyone. If you need help, look
  for a uniformed police officer, store clerk with a nametag or a parent with children."[30]
- Instead of warning a child to stay away from people they don't know: "It's important for you to
  get my permission before going anywhere with anyone."[30]
- Instead of warnings about "bad guys", or telling a child they can spot a bad person by looking:
  "Let me know right away if anyone asks you to keep a secret, makes you feel uncomfortable or tries
  to get you to go somewhere with them."[30]

Look at what each script does. The first admits that a lost child will have to talk to a stranger,
and says which one, chosen by role. The second covers people the child knows as well as strangers,
and NCMEC's own reason is that "most child abductions involve a relative or someone the child
knows, like a neighbor or online acquaintance";[30] in this course's reading it is also aimed at
the lure, which is how the kidnapping study says most stereotypical kidnappings began. The third
teaches the child to watch what someone does, which is lesson 4's rule for adults: behaviour, not
looks. All three are sensible, untested: NCMEC recommends them, and no study this course read
measured what they prevent.

### Joanna rewrites her rules

Joanna's son is seven. She's told him "never talk to strangers" since he could walk, and she is
starting to see the problems: he will not answer the shop assistant, and he'd happily go off with the
neighbour who gives him sweets. She rewrites her three rules into NCMEC's three scripts.

"Never talk to strangers" becomes: "You don't go up to just anyone. But if you need help, find a
police officer, someone at a till with a name badge, or a mum or dad with children." Next time
they are in a shop, she points out the woman at the till with a name badge.

"Stay away from people you don't know" becomes: "Always ask me before you go anywhere with anyone,
even people you know." The second script, with the part about people he knows said out loud,
because that's where the old rule failed.

"You can tell a bad person by looking" becomes: "If anyone tells you to keep a secret from me, or
makes you feel funny, or wants you to go somewhere with them, you tell me straight away. You won't
be in trouble." The third, with a promise added so that telling does not feel risky to him.

## What people get wrong

**"Stranger danger" is the core of child safety.** Stereotypical kidnappings were about a hundred
in the US in 2011, most began with a lure, and NCMEC recommends teaching permission and behaviour
instead.[29][30]

**"Don't walk alone at night" is the core of women's safety.** The figures in "Where the street
sits" put a small share of serious sexual violence on the street and most of it in homes. The advice
is not wrong; it's the smaller part of the picture.

**"CCTV means someone will come."** Cameras went with a modest fall in area crime, mostly in car
parks.[7] A camera is a record, not a guardian, unless someone is watching and can act.

**"Streetlights work because attackers can be seen."** Night-time crime did not fall more than daytime
crime where lighting improved, and the review's summary suggests community pride.[6] The
mechanism is not settled.

**"Earphones make you a target."** In lesson 4's walking study, people with music players noticed no
less than people walking alone; people on a phone call noticed least.[11] The Lamplugh Trust's one
earphone for running is practitioner opinion.

**"Carry pepper spray when you travel."** The State Department itself says to check the law
first;[4] lesson 14, "The home, property, and carrying something for protection", has the law.

**"Telling someone where you are is enough."** Only if they know when to expect you and what to do
when you don't turn up.

## Practice

:::exercise A plan and a check-in
Take 20 minutes over these, on paper. Limits first, and they matter. If you live with someone who frightens you, leave this plan off paper altogether, and do not agree a check-in that would tell them where you go; the lesson "Domestic abuse and stalking" describes the help and safety planning the services offer, starting with the helplines. For everyone: do not walk any route at night, or while on the phone, to test it, and do not follow or watch anyone. Plan on paper; the only real-world action here is a conversation with someone you trust.

1. Take the trip you mapped in lesson 4's practice, or a new one in the next month: a night out, a late shift, a trip, a first meeting with someone from online. Turn it into a plan of four to six steps, in the shape of Owen's. For each step write what it is for and its label from the four the course uses. If a step has no evidence you can point to, its label is sensible, untested or practitioner opinion, and that is fine.

2. Write the check-in as you would send it: who, what time, what the message will say, what they will do, in order, if it doesn't come, and what you will send if the plan changes. Then agree it with one real person. Tell them what the plan is for, so a missed message means something to them.

3. If you look after a child, write two rules you have heard given to children about strangers, and rewrite each into the NCMEC script it pairs with, in words that child would use.
:::

:::checkpoint A model plan, to compare with yours
Rachel is flying alone to a city she has not visited, landing at 10pm on a Friday.

1. Book the first night's room before she flies, with the address saved offline. For: she'll arrive tired and unsure of the place, which is when the FCDO says a traveller is most vulnerable. Sensible, untested.

2. Book the transfer from the airport, or use an app-based ride that keeps a record. For: no walking around with luggage at night, and a record exists. Sensible, untested.

3. Itinerary to her sister, with a check-in: a message on landing, one from the room, then one each evening at about nine. If nothing by eleven, her sister rings her, then the hotel. And a message if her plans change. For: a guardian who acts when the plan breaks. Sensible, untested.

4. Copies of her passport and insurance with her sister. For: someone at home can act if documents go. Sensible, untested.

5. On arrival, check the fire exit and that the room door locks, and keep the key where nobody can read the room number. For: crime is not the only risk the FCDO's checklist names. Sensible, untested.

What it cost her: a booking she would have made anyway, a few messages, and five minutes in the room. If yours has a check-in with a time and an action, and a label on every step, it is doing the same job.
:::
