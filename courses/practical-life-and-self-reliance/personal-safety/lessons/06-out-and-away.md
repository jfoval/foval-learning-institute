Most safety advice you will ever be given is about this lesson's territory: the walk home, the late
train, the first night in a strange city, the coffee with someone you've only met through an app.
It comes from charities, governments and friends, it is usually sensible, and almost none of it has
been tested. That combination is what this lesson is for. It takes the advice as the official
bodies actually wrote it, sorts each piece by what stands behind it, and shows you the one idea
that explains why most of it helps when it does: somebody who knows your plan and will act when it
breaks. Then it asks where the street sits among the risks people actually face, and gives the
strongest argument against this whole genre of advice, in its own terms.

:::callout Before you rely on this
This course is education, not legal advice, and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger now, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US. On a 999 call from a mobile, if you can't speak, cough or tap if you can and press 55 when you're prompted; in the US you can text 911 where that's available, but call if you can. If a partner, ex-partner or family member is frightening you, the lesson on domestic abuse and stalking starts with the helplines. Responsibility for a crime lies with the person who commits it: precautions change the odds, not the blame. The law differs between England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

The responsibility sentence in that box carries a lot of weight here. Advice about where to walk
and when is exactly the kind that turns, in the wrong mouth, into "she shouldn't have been out".
Nothing in this lesson means that. A route changes the odds of meeting someone who has decided to
hurt a person. It has nothing to do with who made that decision.

## The advice, as it has written

Start with what the bodies say, in their own words, before asking what stands behind it.

The Suzy Lamplugh Trust, the UK personal safety charity lessons 4 and 5 met, puts its street
advice under "Plan ahead". It opens: "Before you go out, consider how you are going to get home",
and on foot, "Try to use well-lit, busy streets and use the route you know best."[1] For running it
suggests "Consider just having one earphone in", and it tells you to let someone know where you are
going and for how long.[1] Both of its advice pages this course read carry the same disclaimer, and it's the
most honest sentence in this whole genre: the advice "is in no way intended to suggest that taking
such actions will eliminate incidents of aggression and violence. The guidance merely helps readers
better understand how to assess and mitigate risks to their personal safety."[1]

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) has a page on [solo and independent
travel](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/solo-and-independent-travel). "Leave an itinerary with someone
at home and agree how regularly you will be in contact". Consider "if walking alone, avoiding
poorly lit areas, particularly at night". And "never leaving your key where someone can note your
room number".[2] It says that travelling alone may bring an increased risk of being a victim of crime,
and its checklist for your room puts fire exits and carbon monoxide beside locks and
keys.[2]

The US State Department's page for [women
travelers](https://web.archive.org/web/20251226183516/https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/international-travel/before-you-go/travelers-with-special-considerations/women-travelers.html)
says: "Consider using app-based transportation companies, which offer a record of your ride";
"Think ahead and come up with a safety plan to deal with unsafe situations, in the event you end up
in one"; and "Consider bringing personal safety whistles or alarms. However, confirm legality before
traveling with items like pepper spray."[3] Its [traveler's
checklist](https://web.archive.org/web/20260103122443/https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/international-travel/before-you-go/travelers-checklist.html)
adds two practical steps: enrol in the Smart Traveler Enrollment Program (STEP), which "also helps
the U.S. embassy or consulate reach you or your emergency contact in an emergency", and copy your
documents: "Give a set of copies to a trusted friend or family member."[4]

Read those together and a pattern shows. Most of the advice is one of three kinds: plan before you
go, have someone who knows the plan, and stay where there are other people and light. Lesson 4
gave you the triangle those map onto. The question now is how strong the evidence under each one
is.

## What stands behind each piece

### Lights

:::predict Better street lighting is the one piece of this advice with a real research review behind it. A review pooled studies of areas that improved their lighting and compared them with similar areas that did not. If lighting works by letting people see who is about, what would you expect to happen to crime in the daytime, when the lights are off?
If seeing were the whole story, daytime crime should not change, because nothing about daytime visibility changed. The review found that when lighting was improved, "nighttime crimes did not decrease more than daytime crimes", which is why its authors looked for a second explanation. The next paragraphs give it.
:::

Welsh and Farrington's review for the Campbell Collaboration, published in 2008,
pooled 13 studies from the UK and the US over four decades. In the words of the Collaboration's
[plain-language summary](https://web.archive.org/web/20260920211306/https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/2015/09/improved-street-lighting-can-reduce-crime/),
crime "decreased by 21% in areas that experienced street lighting improvements compared to similar
areas that did not". The UK studies found 38% and the US studies 7%.[5] The summary adds that the
American studies were older and several counted only night-time crime.[5]

The summary names two ways lighting is thought to work: by making people easier to see, which
deters offenders, and by signalling that someone has invested in a place, which raises pride in it.
Because night-time crime didn't fall more than daytime crime, the summary says, "community pride
may play a crucial role".[5] That is a suggestion from the pattern, not a tested mechanism, and this
course read the summary, not the full review. So "attackers avoid light because they can be seen" is
more than the evidence says. The honest version is that lit areas had less crime, and nobody's
sure why.

Notice too what was measured: crime across an area, before and after. That is not the risk to you,
walking one street on one night. Choosing the lit road is sensible, untested, in this course's
labels, with area-level evidence nearby.

### Cameras

Piza and colleagues pooled forty years of evaluations of closed-circuit television in 2019.
Their summary: "The findings show that CCTV is associated with a significant and modest decrease in
crime. The largest and most consistent effects of CCTV were observed in car parks."[6] They also
found effects in residential areas, larger effects where someone was actively watching the
screens, and they advise that cameras be aimed at vehicle and property crime and not used on their
own.[6]

That is evidence about crime in places, with the largest effects in car parks. For your own safety a camera is
at best a record. It isn't someone who will step in, and in lesson 4's terms it is not a capable
guardian unless a person is watching and able to act. "Walk where there are cameras" is sensible,
untested for personal safety, and the evidence under it is about something else.

### The rest

Everything else on the lists above, from planning the way home to telling someone your plans,
follows from routine activity theory and the guardianship evidence lesson 4 gave you, and no study
this course found has measured whether following it prevents harm. That does not make it bad advice.
It makes it sensible, untested, and it's worth knowing which is which, because a piece of advice
you know to be untested is one you can weigh against its cost.

Here are ten pieces of advice from the pages above, sorted:

| Advice | Label | What stands behind it |
|---|---|---|
| Plan how you will get home before you go out | Sensible, untested | Routine activity theory; no outcome measured |
| Use well-lit, busy streets | Sensible, untested | Area-level lighting review; nothing on one walker's risk |
| Walk where there is CCTV | Sensible, untested | Modest area-level fall, largest in car parks |
| Tell someone your plans and when to expect you | Sensible, untested | Guardianship; no outcome measured |
| Put the phone away when walking somewhere unfamiliar | Sensible, untested | Phone calls cut noticing (lesson 4); crime not measured |
| Keep one earphone in when running | Practitioner opinion | No study read; music did not cut noticing in lesson 4's study |
| If followed, cross over, look, keep moving, get to people | Practitioner opinion | No study read |
| Set off a personal alarm and leave | Practitioner opinion | No study read |
| Book your first night's accommodation before you travel | Sensible, untested | The FCDO's reasoning; no outcome measured |
| Use an app-based ride that keeps a record | Sensible, untested | The State Department's reasoning; no outcome measured |

Not one row is tested. That's the most important thing in the table. On the street, the course's
question, measured on whom and measured as what, keeps getting the same answer: the nearest
measurements are of areas, or of noticing, not of whether a person who followed the advice was
harmed less.

## Why telling someone helps, when it does

If nothing here is tested, why does the course still pass it on? Because most of it rests on one
mechanism with solid theory behind it.

Lesson 4 gave you Cohen and Felson's three things that have to meet for most crime: "likely
offenders, suitable targets and the absence of capable guardians against crime."[7] A guardian is
usually thought of as someone standing next to you, and company is one form. The walking study in
lesson 4 found that people walking in pairs noticed an unusual event more often than anyone
else.[8] But most of the advice above adds a different kind of guardian, one who is not there at
all.

Think about what "tell someone where you're going" actually does. On its own, nothing. A friend
who knows you have gone out cannot act on that. What turns knowledge into guardianship is a plan that
can visibly break: you said you'd message by eleven, and at quarter past there is no message. Now
someone knows something's wrong, knows where you were meant to be, and knows when. That is the
course's reading of Cohen and Felson applied to a single evening, and the step from their theory
to your walk home is the course's, not theirs.

So the rule this lesson builds everything on is this: **a plan is only as useful as what happens
when it breaks.** A plan with no agreed check-in, and no agreement about what the other person does
if it is missed, is mostly a comfort.

:::checkpoint Two friends each "let someone know" before a night out. One texts her brother "out tonight, don't wait up". The other texts her flatmate "leaving the bar at 11, home by 11.30, I'll message when I'm in; if you haven't heard by midnight, ring me, and if I don't answer, ring the bar". Which one has added a guardian in the sense this lesson uses, and why?
The second. The first message tells her brother she's out, but there is no time, no place and nothing he is meant to do, so nothing would ever make him act. The second gives a time, a route and a place, a moment when silence means something, and a thing to do about it. Both are sensible, untested, but only the second can work the way the theory says a guardian works.
:::

## Owen's night out

Owen is twenty, a student, and he is going to a party across town on Saturday. He'll walk the
twenty minutes home afterwards, probably around one in the morning. He is not worried. The data
are not on his side there: lesson 3 showed that violence from strangers falls more often on men than
on women, and most often on people aged 16 to 24, in England and Wales in the year to March
2024.[11] That is a reason for a plan, not for staying in.

Here's his plan, built in five steps. Each step says what it is for and what label it carries.

**1. Decide how he is getting home before he goes out.** The Lamplugh Trust's first line.[1] What
it is for: the decision gets made while he's sober and fresh, not at one in the morning. If the walk
home looks bad on reflection, a night bus or a shared taxi can be planned now. Sensible, untested.

**2. A friend who knows.** His flatmate knows the address of the party, that he expects to leave
around one, and that he will message on getting in. If there is no message by two, she rings him; if
he does not answer, she rings the friend whose party it is. What it's for: a guardian who can act
when the plan breaks, as above. Sensible, untested.

**3. Stay where there are people and light.** The walk has two versions: along the high road, or a
shorter cut through a park. He takes the high road. What it is for: more people who might notice or
help, and the lit streets that the lighting review found had less crime at area level. Sensible,
untested, with area-level evidence nearby.[5]

**4. Phone in his pocket while he walks.** What it is for: his attention stays on the street. Lesson
4's walking study found a phone conversation cut what walkers noticed; it did not measure crime.[8]
Sensible, untested.

:::predict Step 5 is the one people leave out. His flatmate has a time and an action. What does Owen himself still need to agree, so that the plan can't break by accident and send her ringing round at two in the morning for nothing?
An agreed "home" message, and what happens if the plan changes. If he goes on somewhere else, or stays over, he sends a message saying so, and the new time. Without it, the check-in either raises a false alarm or, worse, teaches his flatmate to ignore a missed message. The agreed message is what keeps the guardian's trigger meaningful. Sensible, untested, like the rest.
:::

**5. An agreed "home" message, and a message if plans change.** What it is for: the check-in means
something only if a missed message is rare and real.

Look at what the plan did not ask for. It did not ask Owen to stay in, avoid the party, or give up
the walk. Lesson 1's test of functional worry asks whether a precaution leaves your life about the
size it was, and this one does: it cost a few messages and a few minutes. If something happened to
Owen on the high road anyway, the plan would not have failed him and he would not have failed the
plan. Responsibility would sit with whoever chose to hurt him.

## If you think you're being followed

The Lamplugh Trust's advice for this moment is a short sequence. If you think you're being
followed, it says, trust your instincts and act: as confidently as you can, "cross the road, turning
to see who is behind you. If you are still being followed, keep moving. Head to a busy area and
tell people what is happening". If you need to, call the police.[1]

Each step has a reason you can see. Crossing and turning tests the suspicion: someone who crosses
with you is different from someone who carries on. Keeping moving keeps options open. A busy area
brings other people in, and telling them turns bystanders into help. That reasoning is this
course's, and nobody has tested the sequence. It is practitioner opinion, from the UK's personal
safety charity. Lesson 4 said the same about "trust your instincts", and gave you the rule to go
with it: the instinct worth acting on is set off by what someone is doing, here following you
across a road and back, not by how they look.

On asking for help, the Trust says that if you can attract passers-by you are more likely to get
help if you shout a specific instruction, such as "Call the police!"[1] First Aid and CPR lesson 1
reached the same place from a different direction: in an emergency, point at one person and give
them a job. A named task gets done; a general cry for help leaves everyone waiting for someone
else.

On personal alarms, the Trust's own last word is the one this course keeps: "Don't wait to check
that your alarm has had the desired effect; just go."[1] An alarm is for setting off and leaving,
nothing more, and this course names no product.

:::checkpoint A man walking from a station at night notices the same person behind him after two turns. Using the UK guidance above, what does he do first, and what is the label on it?
He crosses the road and turns to see who is behind him, which tests whether he's actually being followed. If he still is, he keeps moving toward a busy place, tells people what is happening, and calls the police if he needs to. The label is practitioner opinion: it comes from the UK's personal safety charity, and no study this course read tested it.
:::

This course read the UK sequence only. What US police or US charities tell a person who thinks
they are being followed on their own street, it did not find in readable form, and it doesn't guess.
The next section says more about that gap.

## Travel

Travel is guardianship applied far from home, with one extra problem: you do not know the place.

The FCDO's reason for its most specific piece of advice is worth reading slowly. It recommends
booking accommodation before you go, "in particular your first night's accommodation. You are at
your most vulnerable when you first arrive in a foreign country as you are likely to be tired and
unsure of your surroundings".[2] That is lesson 4's triangle in one sentence. Tired means attention
is low. Unsure means you do not know which street is the lit one. Arriving late with nowhere booked
means walking around with luggage, looking at a phone, asking strangers for directions: a suitable
target, with no plan anyone else knows.

The rest of the travel advice follows. An itinerary left at home, with an agreed rhythm of contact,
is the check-in from Owen's plan stretched to a week.[2] Copies of your documents with someone you
trust, and STEP for US citizens, are ways for someone at home, or an embassy, to act if the plan
breaks.[4] "Never leaving your key where someone can note your room number" keeps where you sleep
from being something a stranger knows.[2] An app-based ride "which offer a record of your ride" puts
a record where there wasn't one, which the State Department presents as useful for identifying the
vehicle and driver afterwards.[3] Each is sensible, untested.

And the FCDO's lines on fire exits and carbon monoxide is a useful correction.[2] The biggest risks of
travel are often not crime, and a plan that checks the smoke alarm is a better plan than one that
only thinks about strangers.

One piece of travel advice carries a legal warning. The State Department says to "confirm legality
before traveling with items like pepper spray".[3] In Great Britain the answer is that it is a
prohibited weapon, and lesson 14, "The home, property, and carrying something for protection",
gives the law. This is general information, not legal advice. The law differs by state, and
between England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. Travel Skills, later on the Core, takes
travel further than this course does.

### What the US half of this lesson rests on

Be clear about the sources. The UK street advice here is the Lamplugh Trust's and the FCDO's. The
US advice is the State Department's, and the State Department writes for Americans travelling
abroad, not for someone walking home in their own city. The US National Crime Prevention Council
has street-safety material, but this course did not find it in a form it could read, and it didn't
read any US police force's advice on the street. So if you are in the US, the planning in this lesson
still applies, because guardianship is the same everywhere, but the specific wording is the UK's or
the traveller's, and this course has no US street guidance to set beside it.

## Meeting someone from online

Meeting someone you have only talked to online is a special case of the same idea, and the Lamplugh
Trust's [Safety Online](https://www.suzylamplugh.org/safety-online) page has the clearest version.
"Tell someone where you're going, who you're meeting (name, website arranged through etc) and check
in with them before, during and after the date." "Agree that if you don't check in as agreed or
respond then your friend would call the place where you've met or even go there to check on you."
"Meet in a public space and plan an exit strategy if needed." And, while you are still chatting:
"consider talking about where you've been rather than where you're going."[9]

Read that against Owen's plan and the parts line up: a named person, a place, times, and an action
if the check-in is missed. It's guardianship, built for a first date. The last line is the target
lever: where you are going is something a stranger can use; where you have been mostly is not. The page
also advises keeping your home address and other personal details to yourself on a first date.[9]
All of it is sensible, untested.

One line on the same page this course doesn't pass on. It advises changing passwords regularly,
and that is advice the UK's National Cyber Security Centre withdrew. Digital Literacy lesson 10
explains why, and it is where passwords belong.

### Graham's first date

Graham is forty-six, divorced two years, and he is meeting a woman from a dating app for coffee on
Saturday afternoon. He's read the Lamplugh steps and thinks they are for women. They are not: the
page is written for anyone, and nothing in its reasoning depends on who is meeting whom. A man
meeting a stranger is still meeting a stranger, and lesson 3 showed that in England and Wales men are
more often the victims of violence from strangers than women are.[11]

So Graham's plan uses the steps as written. His brother knows her first name, the app, the café
and the time. He'll message at the start, halfway and when he leaves, and if his brother hears
nothing by five, he rings, then rings the café. Graham drives himself there, so he can leave when
he likes.

The wrinkle comes after the coffee. Suppose it goes well, and they arrange a second meeting, then
a third. Most people's plans stop after the first date, because the stranger has become someone
they know. But lesson 3 showed that the harms that fall most on women, and a real share of those
that fall on men, come from people the victim knows: partners, ex-partners, acquaintances. A date
is the moment a stranger starts becoming one of those. That is the course's reading of lesson 3's
figures, not a finding about dating, and it does not mean that anyone Graham meets is a risk.

:::checkpoint What does that point change about Graham's plan for the second and third meetings, and what stays the same?
It changes how long the check-in lasts. The friend-knows-the-plan habit is worth keeping for the next few meetings, especially any that move somewhere private, such as her home or his, because that's where the public-place protection stops. It does not change how he treats her, and it is not suspicion: the check-in is invisible to her and costs almost nothing. If a partner later starts to control, track or frighten him, that is not a dating question any more, and the lesson "Domestic abuse and stalking" is the place to go, for men as well as women.
:::

## Where the street sits

Almost all of the advice in this lesson so far is about the street. Lesson 3 gave you the reason
that cannot be the whole picture.

:::predict Before you look back at lesson 3: of people in England and Wales who had been raped or assaulted by penetration since the age of 16, what share do you think said it happened in a street, car park, park or other open public space? And what share in their own home?
8.0% in a street, car park, park or open space, and 38.2% in the victim's own home, with the perpetrator's home next at 28.5%. Those figures pool the survey years to March 2020 and March 2025.[10] If you guessed the street was the commonest place, you've guessed what most safety advice implies.
:::

Violence from strangers has a different shape. In lesson 3's figures, the people most at risk are
men and the young: 1% of men against 0.4% of women, and 2.2% of 16 to 24 year olds,
in the year to March 2024.[11]

So "don't walk alone at night" is not wrong. It is the smaller part of the picture, and taken as the
core of women's safety it points attention away from where most serious harm to women happens. Measured
by violence from strangers, the people most at risk are men, and people aged 16 to 24, not women
walking alone.

That does not make the street safe, or the fear of it mistaken. What many people meet on the street
is real, and it is harassment. In an ONS survey of adults in Great Britain in February and March 2022,
27% of women and 16% of men said they had experienced at least one form of harassment in the previous
twelve months.[12] In the same survey, 82% of women said they felt very or fairly unsafe in a park
or other open space after dark, against 42% of men.[12] Lesson 1 showed what that feeling costs: in
the same survey, the share of people who felt unsafe who had stopped walking in parks after dark had
risen for both men and women.[12] Harassment is information about a place, as lesson 1 said, and
the cost of avoiding a place is real too.

## The argument about advice aimed at women

This is a contested question, part empirical and part value, and the course describes it rather
than settling it.

### The critique

The strongest statement of the case against advice like this lesson's comes from UK research on
violence against women. Vera-Gray and Kelly, writing in 2020, start from harassment in
public space and ask what it costs women beyond their safety: their freedom. The abstract describes
the "safety work" asked of women and girls in public, and says it functions "to limit women's space
for action and responsiblise them for preventing violence" (their spelling).[13] This course read
the abstract only.

Put in its strongest form, as this course's research summarised the position: the advice is misdirected,
because most of the risk to women is from men they know, not strangers on the street; it's costly,
because every "don't go there" and "don't go alone" is a piece of freedom given up, and shrinks the
lives of people who have done nothing; and it shifts responsibility, because a list of precautions
invites the question of which one a victim skipped. That is a mainstream position in UK research on
violence against women, not a fringe one, and the figures in the last section are consistent with
its first point. Lesson 1 met feminist criminologists' related argument, as Scott summarises it,
that street-crime framing hides violence behind closed doors.

### The defence

The case for teaching precautions starts from the same evidence and draws a different conclusion.
Precautions change the odds: lesson 5 showed the strongest evidence in the course, that locks and
lights in combination went with far lower burglary risk, and the guardianship above rests on the
routine activity theory lesson 4 taught.
On blame, the reply lesson 4 met in Clarke, written with Felson, is that routine
precautions let people take responsibility for precautions without taking any of the blame, and
do not excuse the offender at all.[14] And the Lamplugh Trust's own disclaimer, quoted at the top of
this lesson, refuses to promise that any precaution will prevent violence.

The defence has one more piece, and it comes from a randomised trial. A programme for
first-year university women that teaches them to "assess risk from acquaintances, overcome emotional
barriers in acknowledging danger, and engage in effective verbal and physical self-defense" lowered
the one-year risk of completed rape against a control group given brochures.[15] Lesson 12,
"Resistance training, and choosing a class", teaches the trial and its limits.

### How they fit

The two sides are less opposed than they look, and the evidence is what separates them. The
critique's target is advice that tells women to restrict where they go and when. The trial tested
something else: training that helps people recognise and resist the risks they actually face, and
it was aimed at acquaintances, not strangers. Vera-Gray and Kelly's own abstract ends by exploring
feminist self-defence as a way of making safety work visible and measurable.[13] So on the
empirical part, restriction and skills are different things, and the evidence speaks to them
differently.

What's left is a value question: who should carry the burden of prevention? One answer puts it on
the people who commit violence and on the institutions around them, and sees advice to individuals
as a burden that falls on potential victims. Another holds that people can reasonably take cheap
precautions for themselves while the larger work goes on, and that refusing them useful information
has costs too. The course does not choose. It holds one commitment that every position here shares:
responsibility for a crime lies with the person who commits it.

## Talking to children

This course is for adults, and child protection is not its subject. But many adults have children
in the house, and the commonest safety rule taught to them, "never talk to strangers", rests on the
same misweighting as "don't walk alone at night".

:::predict How many children in the whole of the US do you think were victims of a stereotypical kidnapping, the kind by a stranger or slight acquaintance that most parents picture, in a year?
An estimated 105, in 2011, "virtually the same as the 1997 estimate".[16]
:::

The US Justice Department's third national study of missing children defines a stereotypical
kidnapping narrowly: "abductions in which a slight acquaintance or stranger moves a child at least
20 feet or holds the child at least 1 hour, and in which the child is detained overnight,
transported at least 50 miles, held for ransom, abducted with the intent to keep permanently, or
killed".[16] It found the victims were most commonly aged 12 to 17.[16] And it
found how most of these kidnappings began: the kidnappers "lured almost 70 percent of victims
through deception or nonthreatening pretexts".[16]

The National Center for Missing & Exploited Children (NCMEC), the US charity that handles missing
child reports, puts it this way: "only one percent of missing child cases reported to NCMEC are the
classic nonfamily abduction, or stereotypical kidnapping, that many parents fear."[17] Its
[blog](https://www.missingkids.org/blog/2023/staying-away-from-stranger-danger) offers scripts to
use instead of "stranger danger":

- "It's important for you to get my permission before going anywhere with anyone."
- "Let me know right away if anyone asks you to keep a secret, makes you feel uncomfortable or tries
  to get you to go somewhere with them."
- "If you need help, look for a uniformed police officer, store clerk with a nametag or a parent
  with children."[17]

Look at what each script does. The first is aimed at the lure, which is how the kidnapping study
says most of them began, and it covers people the child knows as well as strangers. The second
teaches the child a behavioural cue, which is lesson 4's rule for adults: watch what someone does,
not what they look like. The third admits that a lost child will have to talk to a stranger, and
says which one. All three are sensible, untested: NCMEC recommends them, and no study this course
read measured what they prevent.

### Joanna rewrites a rule

Joanna's son is seven. She's told him "never talk to strangers" since he could walk, and she is
starting to see the problems: he will not answer the shop assistant, and he would happily go off with the
neighbour who gives him sweets. She rewrites the one rule into NCMEC's three.

"Never talk to strangers" becomes: "You can say hello to people. But you always ask me before you
go anywhere with anyone, even people you know." The first script, with the part about people he
knows said out loud, because that's where the one rule failed.

"Stay away from people you don't know" becomes: "If anyone tells you to keep a secret from me, or
makes you feel funny, or wants you to go somewhere with them, you tell me straight away. You won't
be in trouble." The second, with a promise added so that telling does not feel risky to him.

"If you get lost, stand still" becomes the third script as NCMEC wrote it, and she points out a
uniformed guard and a woman at a till with a name badge next time they are in a shop.

## What people get wrong

**"Stranger danger" is the core of child safety.** Stereotypical kidnappings were about a hundred
in the US in 2011, most began with a lure, and NCMEC recommends teaching permission and behaviour
instead.[16][17]

**"Don't walk alone at night" is the core of women's safety.** 8.0% of rape in England and Wales, in
the pooled survey years, happened in a street, car park, park or open space, and 38.2% in the
victim's home.[10] The advice is not wrong; it's the smaller part of the picture.

**"CCTV means someone will come."** Cameras went with a modest fall in area crime, mostly in car
parks.[6] A camera is a record, not a guardian, unless someone is watching and can act.

**"Streetlights work because attackers can be seen."** Night-time crime did not fall more than daytime
crime where lighting improved, and the review's summary suggests community pride.[5] The mechanism
is not settled.

**"Earphones make you a target."** In lesson 4's walking study, music players did not cut noticing;
phone calls did.[8] The Lamplugh Trust's one earphone for running is practitioner opinion.

**"Carry pepper spray when you travel."** The State Department itself says to check the law first,
and in Great Britain it's a prohibited weapon; lesson 14 has the law.[3]

**"Telling someone where you are is enough."** Only if they know when to expect you and what to do
when you do not turn up.

## Practice

:::exercise A plan, a check-in, and two rules rewritten
Take 40 minutes over these, on paper. Limits first, and they matter: do not walk any route at night, or while on the phone, to test it, and do not follow or watch anyone. Plan on paper; the only real-world action here is a conversation with someone you trust.

1. Choose a real trip or evening in the next month: a night out, a late shift, a trip, a first meeting with someone from online. Write a plan in the shape of Owen's, with four to six steps. For each step write what it's for, which of lesson 4's three levers it pulls (target, guardian, convergence), and its label from the four the course uses. If a step has no evidence you can point to, its label is sensible, untested or practitioner opinion, and that is fine.

2. Write the check-in as you would send it: who, what time, what the message will say, and what they will do, in order, if it doesn't come. Then agree it with one real person. Tell them what the plan is for, so a missed message means something to them.

3. Write, in one sentence, what your plan asks you to give up, and check it against lesson 1's test: does your life stay about the size it was? If a step fails that test, write a cheaper step that pulls the same lever.

4. Choose a child you know, or picture one. Write two rules you have heard given to children about strangers, and rewrite each into one of NCMEC's scripts, in words that child would use.

If you live with someone who frightens you, leave this plan off paper altogether, and do not agree a check-in that would tell them where you go. The lesson "Domestic abuse and stalking" starts with the helplines, and it is the better place to start.
:::

:::checkpoint A model plan, to compare with yours
Rachel is flying alone to a city she has not visited, landing at 10pm on a Friday.

1. Book the first night's room before she flies, with the address saved offline. For: she'll arrive tired and unsure of the place, which is when the FCDO says a traveller is most vulnerable. Levers: target and convergence. Sensible, untested.

2. Book the transfer from the airport, or use an app-based ride that keeps a record. For: no walking around with luggage at night, and a record exists. Lever: target. Sensible, untested.

3. Itinerary to her sister, with a check-in: a message on landing, one from the room, then one each evening at about nine. If nothing by eleven, her sister rings her, then the hotel. For: a guardian who acts when the plan breaks. Sensible, untested.

4. Copies of her passport and insurance with her sister. For: someone at home can act if documents go. Sensible, untested.

5. On arrival, check the fire exit and that the room door locks, and keep the key where nobody can read the room number. For: the biggest risks of travel are often not crime. Sensible, untested.

What it cost her: a booking she would have made anyway, a few messages, and five minutes in the room. If yours has a check-in with a time and an action, and a label on every step, it is doing the same job.
:::
