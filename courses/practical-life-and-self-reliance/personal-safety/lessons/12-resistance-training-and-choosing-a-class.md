A self-defence class promises something a page can't: that you'll practise, with other people,
what to do if someone tries to hurt you. Whether that promise is kept is a question the evidence
can partly answer. This lesson sets out the best-studied randomised trial of a programme that
teaches women to resist, what happened to its effect over two years, the trials that followed it
outside Canada, and the question underneath: whether this kind of teaching puts the job of
prevention on the people at risk. Then it turns to what a class can and can't do, whom the evidence
leaves out, and how to judge a class before you sign up.

Before any of the evidence, one thing this lesson will not do. It will not suggest that anyone who
was assaulted should have taken a class, or that a class they took should have worked. The people
who built the best-studied programme say the same in their own
[two-year paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5821219/): "The program makes clear that
perpetrators are entirely responsible for the crimes they commit."[2] Lesson 11, "Sexual assault:
the freeze, resistance and the law", showed that the body can stop a person resisting at all,
and that this is involuntary. A trial's result is an average over hundreds of people. It is never
a measure of what any one person could have done. That holds for someone who froze, and for
someone who fought, or had trained, and was hurt anyway.

## One trial, read closely

The programme is called Enhanced Assess, Acknowledge, Act, or EAAA. Senn and colleagues tested it
in a randomised trial at three Canadian universities, published in the *New England Journal of
Medicine* in 2015 ([the abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26061837/)), and followed the same
women for two years in a second paper.[1][2]

Each first-year student in it was randomly assigned either to the programme or to a
session giving access to brochures about sexual assault, "as was common university practice".[1]
The programme was "four 3-hour units ... with the goal of being able to assess risk from
acquaintances, overcome emotional barriers in acknowledging danger, and engage in effective verbal
and physical self-defense".[1] The word to notice is acquaintances. The abstract's own background
says young women at university are at substantial risk of being sexually assaulted, "primarily
by male acquaintances", and the programme was built for that, not for a stranger in a car park.[1] In all, 451 women were assigned to the
programme and 442 to the brochures, and 91% of the programme group came to at least three of the
four sessions.[1] The main outcome was completed rape over the following year, from the women's
answers to a standard survey.[1]

:::predict The trial's headline is that the programme cut the one-year risk of completed rape by 46%. Out of every hundred women in the brochure group, how many do you think were raped in that year? About two, about ten, or about thirty?

About ten. The abstract gives 9.8% for the brochure group, against 5.2% for the programme group.[1] A cut of 46% can sound like a cut from something large. It was a cut from about one in ten to about one in twenty: large for the women it describes, and a change of under five in every hundred.
:::

In the abstract's own words: "The 1-year risk of completed rape was
significantly lower in the resistance group than in the control group (5.2% vs. 9.8%; relative
risk reduction, 46.3% [95% confidence interval, 6.8 to 69.1]; P=0.02). The 1-year risk of
attempted rape was also significantly lower in the resistance group (3.4% vs. 9.3%, P<0.001)."[1]

### The same result, three ways

Logic and Argument lesson 9, "Bad reasoning in the news and everyday life", taught you to turn a
relative figure into an absolute one. The working below is this course's arithmetic from the
published figures.

**Relative.** 46.3% is the share of the brochure group's risk that the programme group didn't have.
Divide 4.6 by 9.8 yourself and you get about 47%; the published percentages are rounded.

**Absolute.** 9.8 minus 5.2 is 4.6 percentage points: out of every hundred women, about four or
five fewer were raped in the year.

**One in how many.** 100 divided by 4.6 is about 22. So about one completed rape was prevented in a
year for every 22 women who took the programme. A university deciding whether to run the
programme would want this number, and "46%" hides it.

**The interval.** The 95% confidence interval runs from 6.8% to 69.1%: the data fit a programme
that did a little good and one that did a great deal. The two-year paper gives the interval for the
absolute difference at twelve months as 0.6 to 8.5 percentage points,[2] so "one in 22" could be
anything from about one in 12 to about one in 170. The trial shows the programme lowered the risk.
It pins down the size much less tightly than the headline suggests.

:::checkpoint The abstract's second finding was attempted rape: 3.4% in the programme group against 9.3% in the brochure group. Work out the absolute difference, the "one in how many", and roughly the relative reduction, before opening this.

The absolute difference is 9.3 minus 3.4, which is 5.9 percentage points. 100 divided by 5.9 is about 17, so about one attempted rape was prevented in the year for every 17 women who took the programme. The relative reduction is 5.9 divided by 9.3, about 63%; the two-year paper gives 63.2% at twelve months, the gap again being rounding.[2]

If you got about 37% for the relative figure, you divided 3.4 by 9.3, which is the share of the risk the programme group still had, not the share it avoided. If you gave 63 as the absolute difference, you gave the relative figure in its place: 63% is the share of the brochure group's risk the programme group avoided, and the absolute difference is 5.9 points.
:::

### What it measured, and on whom

Lesson 1 gave this course's question for every piece of advice: measured on whom, and measured as
what? Here the answer is narrow. That's the trial's scope, not a flaw in it.

- **On whom.** First-year women at three universities in one country, who volunteered for a study
  about sexual assault. The two-year paper notes that volunteers came in with somewhat higher rates
  of past victimisation than students in general, and that the authors don't know whether the
  programme would work as well for "less privileged young women who do not attend university".[2]
- **As what.** Completed rape, reported by the women themselves in a survey, over one year.[1]
- **Against what.** Brochures and the chance to talk to someone about local services, not nothing
  and not another class.[1][2]

The trial was funded by the Canadian Institutes of Health Research and the University of
Windsor.[1] In this course's labels, the programme is tested, for first-year university women and
for the risk it was built around. For anyone else, the result is an inference.

## Two years on

The second paper, in *Psychology of Women Quarterly* in 2017, says that over two years the
programme significantly reduced completed and attempted rape, attempted coercion and
non-consensual sexual contact,
"yielding reductions between 30% and 64% at 2 years".[2] Its table shows how completed rape
changed, point by point.

:::predict Between the first year and the second, do you think the gap between the two groups in completed rape grew, held steady, or shrank?

In percentage points it narrowed a little and then held; in relative terms it shrank. The table gives the running totals as 5.2% against 9.8% at twelve months, 7.2% against 10.9% at eighteen months, and 8.1% against 11.8% at two years.[2]
:::

At twelve months the programme group was 4.6 points lower; at eighteen months and two years, 3.7.
So, on this course's arithmetic, the programme group did not pull further ahead in the second year.
The relative reduction fell to 34.0% at eighteen months and 31.3% at two years, and the table marks
both as no longer statistically significant (p = .10 and .13, against .02 at twelve months). At two
years the interval for the difference runs from 8.5 points fewer to 1.1 more.[2] Attempted rape
held, at about 63% or 64%, significant at each point.[2] And only the women who joined in the
first year of recruitment, 370 in all, were surveyed at two years, because, the authors write,
funding didn't allow following everyone.[2]

The programme went on working for at least two years, they write,
but its effect on completed rape diminished and was no longer statistically significant, and a
booster may be needed in the second year of university.[2] They offer one possible reason, which
the trial did not test: as students move through university, boyfriends may become the more likely
perpetrators, bringing in forms of partner violence a twelve-hour course may not cover well
enough.[2] A real effect that fades is a pattern this lesson comes back to.

### The critics' reply, and the authors' answer

A trial like this has a reply waiting, and the authors print it themselves: "Some feminist scholars (e.g., Basile, 2008, as well as one of the reviewers for this
article) have suggested that resistance education for women is misguided, in that perpetrators may
simply move on to another woman."[2] Prevented for the woman who resists, that is, but not
necessarily for the community. Lesson 6, "Out and away", met it briefly. This
course hasn't read the 2008 work they cite, so the critics' case here is as the trial's authors
report it.

Their answer: "We, along with other feminist scholars (e.g., Hollander, 2016), suspect that
perpetrators may learn important lessons that then have an impact on their subsequent
behavior".[2] And they grant the point that matters most:
"which explanation is correct is an empirical question".[2] The trial can't answer it. It measured
what happened to the women in it, not to other women the same men met afterwards.

:::checkpoint What kind of study could tell the two explanations apart? Describe it in two sentences, in terms of whom it would randomise and what it would measure.

Something like: a trial that randomised whole communities, such as entire campuses, to offer the programme or not, and then measured sexual assault among everyone in each community, not only among the women who took it. If the men simply moved on, the rate for the whole community would stay about the same; if some men were deterred, it would fall.

That design is this course's reasoning, not a study anyone has reported, and it shows why the question is hard: it needs many communities. If your design randomised individual women again, it would repeat the first trial and still couldn't see the women the programme never reached.
:::

## Later designs on campus

Two further studies of the same programme are weaker in design than the trial; a public body's
view follows them.

**Out in the world.** Once the programme was offered through a non-profit, the team studied it at
five Canadian universities between 2017 and 2021, comparing women who signed up but didn't attend.
On [the abstract](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2023.2290859), "Completed rape was significantly
reduced by 57.3% at 6-months."[3] Nobody was randomly assigned, and on this course's reading,
women who sign up and don't come may differ in ways that bear on their risk.

**Partner violence.** A 2025 substudy followed 153 women from the trial into new relationships. On
[its abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/08862605241270057), the programme "significantly reduced the
1-year risk of IPV by 54.4% (p = .037, 95% CI [2.9%, 79.8%])", IPV being intimate partner
violence.[4] The interval runs from almost nothing to a great deal, and the sample is small.

**A public body's view, dated.** In 2016 the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)
listed "Empowerment-based training for women to reduce risk for victimization" in [a package of
approaches](https://web.archive.org/web/20210608134353/https://www.cdc.gov/violenceprevention/pdf/SV-Prevention-Technical-Package.pdf) to preventing sexual violence, with this programme as its example. It said
intensive skills training of this kind has "greater research and theoretical support than
approaches focused primarily on physical self-defense training".[5] It added that these approaches had mostly been evaluated with college students.[5]
The CDC has since withdrawn the package, so this is its 2016 document, read from an archived copy,
not current CDC guidance.

## Beyond the campus

The other studies this course read come from Kenya, Malawi and South Sudan, of programmes teaching
girls and young women empowerment and self-defence skills, and they are more mixed.

**Nairobi, before and after.** A 2014 [cohort study](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2013-3414) near
Nairobi, the weakest design here, found sexual assault fell among adolescents who took the
programme, with no significant change among those given a life-skills class.[6]

**Nairobi, randomised by school.** The [cluster-randomised trial](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11121-016-0701-0) that followed, in 28 schools,
estimated "a 3.7 % decrease, p = 0.03 ... (initially 7.3 % at baseline)".[7] The abstract doesn't
say whether the 3.7 is points or a share of the 7.3; this course reads it as points. Boys in the
same schools took a parallel programme ("The boys' program promotes healthy gender norms"), so the
result is for the pair, not for self-defence alone.[7] The conflict-of-interest statement says the
instructors, "and thus the survey interviewers, were employees of Ujamaa-Africa", the organisation
that ran the programme and funded the data collection there, and that the Stanford evaluators had
no financial ties to it.[7] The authors stated it themselves, and it isn't an accusation, but the
people asking girls about assault were the people who had taught them.

**Nairobi, independent.** A later trial in the same setting followed 3,263 girls aged 10 to 14 for
two years. [Its abstract](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11121-023-01595-1) says it "improved on previous
ESD studies in this setting with longitudinal follow-up of individuals and independent data
collection", ESD being empowerment self-defence, and it found no effect: "Odds of reporting rape
were not significantly different in the intervention versus SOC group (OR: 1.21; 95% CI (0.40,
5.21), p = 0.63)", SOC being the standard-of-care comparison.[8] An odds ratio of 1 means no
difference, and this interval runs from well below 1 to well above it. It too paired the girls'
programme with one for boys, and the authors ask for the findings to be read cautiously because of
the study's limitations.[8]

That trial is itself disputed. The journal that had published its protocol, its plan, later issued
an [Expression of Concern](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8133355/): the surveys were
planned for groups of two to four girls with one interviewer, the ratio in the study was much
higher, which the protocol didn't reflect though it was published after the study ended, and this
"may have caused underreporting of sexual assault incidents". "The actual ratio is under dispute
among the authors", and some of them disagree with the Expression of Concern.[9]

**Malawi and South Sudan.** A [trial across 141 schools in Malawi](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-018-6220-0) found past-year sexual assault
fell among students in programme schools (a risk ratio of 0.68, 95% interval 0.56 to 0.82), but not in the others.[10]
A [study in South Sudan and the Kakuma refugee camp](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2023.10.033) found annual rape rates fell "from 10.7% to 5.5%"
in programme schools, and "there was no change in the control schools (10.0%-9.0%)".[11] The Malawi
paper calls itself an implementation trial, and authors of both papers list Ujamaa organisations,
the programme's provider, among their affiliations.[10][11]

Put together, on this course's reading: five studies, four finding a fall, and the one that
describes its data collection as independent finding none and carrying a dispute about its method.
The evidence is contested, and none of the results this course read is about men or boys.

## The meta-analysis argument

A meta-analysis pools many studies to find an average effect. In 2023 Kettrey and colleagues pooled
80 studies of sexual assault prevention programmes for American college students, published between
1991 and 2021. On [the abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2023.02.022), "Effects on
sexual assault victimization were significant but small (g = 0.15)", g being a standard measure of
an effect's size across studies, and "effects on sexual assault perpetration were
nonsignificant".[12] They found that "programs that use a risk reduction framework are associated
with less favorable outcomes than programs that do not", and recommended that prevention move
beyond a focus on individuals to an "ecological perspective", aimed at relationships, communities
and society as well.[12]

The abstract does not define a risk reduction framework, and its studies were of American students,
so it doesn't say whether EAAA, a Canadian trial, was among the 80, or in the group that did less
well. Researchers in empowerment self-defence, among them Hollander, Ullman and Senn, published a
short reply, and Kettrey's team answered it; this course read neither.[13]

Hollander's own work sits on the other side. In a [non-randomised study](https://doi.org/10.1177/1077801214526046) of a ten-week university
self-defence class, women who took it were "less likely to experience sexual assault" over a year
than similar women who didn't.[14] In a [2018 review](https://doi.org/10.1111/soc4.12597) she argues that empowerment-based
women's self-defence is "so far the *only* approach that has produced substantively significant
decreases in victimization rates".[16]

On this course's reading, a pooled average across 80 studies and one randomised trial of one
programme answer different questions, how campus programmes do on average and whether this one
worked, so both results can stand at once. That reading depends on which programmes the average
covers, which the abstract does not say.

## Where the effort should go

Underneath the evidence is a question evidence can't settle: should prevention effort go into
teaching people at risk to resist, or into changing the people who commit sexual violence and the
places where it happens? This is a value question, and the course describes it without choosing.

**The case that it puts the burden in the wrong place.** This course hasn't read a critic making
the case at length, so the case below is put together from what it did read. [Hollander](https://doi.org/10.1177/0959353516637393), who disagrees with the critics, lists
the objections that have kept self-defence out of prevention work as "concerns that it is
ineffective, encourages victim blaming, neglects acquaintance assault, and does not target the
underlying factors that facilitate sexual violence".[15] The critics the trial's authors report
argue that resistance may only move an offender on to someone else.[2] And Kettrey and colleagues,
from their pooled evidence, write: "Considering the limited effect of campus sexual assault
prevention programs on violence, we recommend programming efforts move beyond a focus on
individuals".[12] At its strongest, in this course's words: when the people at risk are taught to
fend off the people who harm them, the work of prevention lands on those at risk, and even a
programme that works for them may leave the offenders, and the settings that allow them,
untouched.

**The case that it is the best tool available now.** Hollander argues that those critiques are
"misguided", and that empowerment-based self-defence "helps to change the root conditions that
allow violence against women to flourish".[15] Senn and colleagues call their programme "one
critical piece of the solution", to be offered while wider change comes through bystander
education and "the few effective or promising interventions for boys". Their reason is that "few
interventions with men have been rigorously evaluated", and those that work usually change
attitudes for a few months "with no effect on the occurrence of rape or other forms of sexual
assault", with two recent exceptions that combine bystander and social-norms approaches.[2] They add that in most sexual assaults there is "no bystander present".[2] Kettrey's
pooled evidence bears on that too: effects on perpetration were nonsignificant.[12] The CDC's 2016
package calls empowerment training "a useful and effective complement" to preventing
perpetration, which it names as its own emphasis.[5] At its strongest, in this
course's words: on its proponents' reading, this approach has the strongest trial evidence of any
campus programme, and holding it back until the larger work succeeds leaves women without the one
tool shown to help them now.

**What both sides share.** Both sides, as this course read them, put the responsibility on the
offender, and two sources say so outright. The trial's authors write that interventions for women
"must not inadvertently suggest that women are responsible for sexual assault".[2] The CDC's
package says any programme aimed at reducing the risk of being victimised must avoid placing any
responsibility on its participants, stated or implied.[5] The critics' worry is that such
programmes may do it anyway.

The course holds that one shared commitment and takes no side on the rest. Lesson 6 found the two
sides closer on one point than they look, and [Vera-Gray and Kelly](https://repository.londonmet.ac.uk/5706/)
show why. Their critique of the "safety work" mandated for women and girls in public is that it
functions "to limit women's space for action and responsiblise them for preventing violence" (their
spelling), and their abstract ends by exploring feminist self-defence as a way of making that work
visible.[17] Their target is advice telling women to restrict themselves, which is not what the trial
tested. But training still asks women to do the work, which is the critics' point about cost and
responsibility.

:::checkpoint This question is a place in the course where all three kinds of claim the institute keeps apart appear together: established or tested, contested, and value. Sort these three statements, with a reason for each. (a) In a randomised trial of first-year university women in Canada, a twelve-hour programme lowered the one-year risk of completed rape. (b) Empowerment self-defence programmes reduce sexual assault wherever they're run. (c) A university's prevention budget should go to resistance classes before programmes for men.

(a) is tested, for that population: a randomised trial measured the outcome that matters, with a wide interval and an effect that faded in the second year. (b) is contested: the campus trials mostly point one way, but the independent Nairobi trial found no effect and is itself disputed, the meta-analysis found small average effects, and for most places and people this course found no evidence at all. (c) is a value question: evidence informs it, but who should carry the work of prevention is a judgement about what's fair.

If you filed (a) as established in general, notice the scope: the result is for the women studied, and lesson 1's labels keep that scope attached.
:::

## What a page, a class and practice can each do

**A page** can teach you to recognise trouble, the order of options from lesson 1 (avoid, leave,
talk, comply or resist, get away), what sudden fear does to the body, and how to choose a class. It
can't teach a strike, a release or an escape. Lesson 8, "Talking it down", quoted Project BETA's caution about
de-escalation: knowledge can come from a book, but skills come only with practice.

**A class** is where the practice happens. The US workplace safety regulator, the Occupational
Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), says in its [guidance for health and social care workers](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/osha3148.pdf)
that "Both de-escalation and self-defense training should include a hands-on component."[19]
Carrying that beyond those workplaces is this course's step. The University of Connecticut's
[police department](https://police.universitysafety.uconn.edu/education-and-programs/self-defense-classes/) runs a twelve-hour women's course that "progresses from
awareness, prevention, risk reduction, and avoidance to basic self-defense participation", and
says it "cannot offer self-defense classes of fewer hours than listed."[20] That's one department's
standard, practitioner opinion. EAAA ran for twelve hours too, and its authors write that in their
early pilots, shorter units with less practice time gave effects that didn't last.[2]

**Practice under pressure** is what lesson 10, "What fear does to you, and the robbery decision",
took from two police studies: after officers [trained](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3142543/) against an opponent who shot back, their
"shot accuracy ... no longer deteriorated under anxiety", while [mistakes in split-second decisions](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2014.12.006)
about whether to shoot "persisted" after three weeks of practice.[21][22] Those were police
officers with firearms. The 2011 abstract says the gain was "potentially related to changes in
visual attention on task-relevant information"; the 2015 one offers no mechanism, and this course
adds none.[21] What it takes from them, as its own inference, is that a class where you practise
against a partner who pushes back, with your consent and in steps you choose, may prepare you
better than one where you only watch: sensible, untested, in this course's labels.

### Why it fades

The EAAA effect on completed rape shrank in the second year, and its authors suggested a booster.[2]
You've seen the same shape before: lesson 8's Indianapolis officers, whose improved attitudes
mostly faded by the follow-up, and First Aid and CPR lesson 16's CPR skills, which fell within three
months. The EAAA authors offer one possible reason for their own fade, above; none of the sources
tests why, and none says how often a self-defence refresher is needed. The Malawi programme added
refreshers to its twelve hours.[10]

So "take a refresher" is sensible, untested, in this course's labels: it follows from the fading
seen in all three places, and no source this course read has measured the right interval for this
skill. Habits and Self-Discipline lesson 3, "What the cue is doing", gave the tool for making a
refresher happen: tie it to a cue you meet reliably and notice easily.

## The gap, said out loud

Everything controlled in this lesson is about women and girls, and about sexual assault. The
research behind this course found **no controlled evidence on self-defence classes for men, or for
general assault or robbery**.

That matters because of where men's risk lies. Lesson 3 showed that in England and Wales men were more likely than women to be victims of violence, with or
without injury, by a stranger: 1% of men against 0.4% of women in
[the survey year ending March 2024](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/articles/thenatureofviolentcrimeinenglandandwales/yearendingmarch2024).[23]
Stranger violence, where men's risk runs above women's, is exactly the kind of harm for which this
course found no trial of a class. UConn's police, whose women's course appears above, also run a
twelve-hour course for men, "to provide realistic self-defense options to men regardless of their
physical conditioning"; like every class for men, no trial this course found has tested it.[20]

Two wrong conclusions are easy to draw here. One is
that the EAAA result carries over: it doesn't, because it measured different people facing a
different harm, with a programme built around men they knew. The other is that classes for men or
for street violence don't work. Logic and Argument lesson 6, "How much should the evidence move
you", showed that absence of evidence is evidence of absence only when the evidence would have been
expected. This course's research found no such trials, and a search that turns up nothing is weak
evidence either way. The honest label for a class aimed at street violence is practitioner opinion:
experienced people teach it and no controlled study this course found stands behind it.

## Choosing a class

This course names no martial art, style, product or school, and ranks none. It gives two
checklists, each labelled.

### A practitioners' guide

The National Women's Martial Arts Federation, a non-profit, publishes a short guide, ["How to choose a
self-defense class"](https://nwmaf.org/nwmaf-blog/how-to-choose-a-self-defense-class/), by Amy
Eckert, 2025. This course read it in full.[18] It opens: "In a good empowerment self-defense program,
students learn awareness, assertiveness, and verbal and physical skills." It then lists what to look
for in an instructor or programme:

- One that knows the facts about violence and teaches to them: "a good class will address
  situations involving acquaintances and romantic partners as well as attacks by strangers".[18]
- One that "does not blame or judge survivors".[18]
- One that offers strategies "to help students make their own decisions ... instead of telling
  students what they should or should not do".[18]
- One where students "determine their own levels of participation", and where "no one should feel
  pressured into doing specific exercises".[18]

The guide sets no length and names no style. It credits part of its material to the National
Coalition Against Sexual Assault.[18] In this course's labels, the guide is practitioner opinion:
guidance from experienced teachers, not tested in a study. It was written with women, transgender
and non-binary people and others targeted for violence in mind: those are the groups whose risks it
asks an instructor to know.

### This course's checklist

The second list is this course's own inference from the sources in this lesson and the ones before
it, not a tested tool: sensible, untested, in this course's labels.

1. **Covers avoidance, talking and getting away, not only fighting.** Lesson 1's order of options;
   UConn's course starts from awareness and avoidance.[20]
2. **Covers people you know, not only strangers.** EAAA was built on acquaintance risk.[1][18]
3. **Includes practice with a partner, under some pressure, consented and in steps.** Lesson 10's
   police studies; OSHA's "hands-on component".[19][21]
4. **Counts getting away and calling for help as success.** Leaving is allowed at every rung.
5. **Covers the law where you live, as general information, with escape first.** Lesson 13, "The
   law on using force", sets it out.
6. **Never blames survivors.** The trial's authors, the CDC's 2016 package and the federation's
   guide.[2][5][18]
7. **Lets you opt out of any drill.**[18]
8. **Offers a refresher.** EAAA's second year.[2]
9. **Is candid about what a short course can't do**, including, for most classes, that no trial
   this course found has tested it.

**What not to count as evidence.** A black belt, a long lineage, a certificate from the school's own
association, years in business, or a promise that you'll never be a victim. The commercial "how to
choose" pages the research behind this course found lean on lineage, certificates and years
teaching, and they're written by schools selling classes. None of it tells you whether the teaching
prevents harm; the one controlled finding here was judged by what happened to the women afterwards.

**If you've been assaulted.** You don't have to take a class, and not taking one isn't a choice
anyone can hold against you. If you'd like to, you can take your time. The EAAA authors record that
a few survivors in the trial found their experience too recent and left, and that some took the
programme a semester or a year later.[2] And if you'd taken a class and were assaulted anyway, the
class didn't fail because of you, and nor did you. Even in the best trial, trained women were still
raped, and the programme itself teaches that "only the presence of a man willing to commit sexual
assault generates any 'risk' of sexual assault".[2]

## A class for the street

Dominic is 34 and works night shifts at a parcel depot on the edge of Exeter, and walks across a
big car park to his car at about three in the morning. He'd like "a class for the street" and has
found three near him. Which, if any, should he pick?

**What the evidence covers.** Not him: nothing controlled that this course found covers a man and
violence from a stranger. Lesson 4 gave the opportunity tools for a place like his car park, the
route he takes, the time, and staying near other people. A class is one option beside those, and
any class he picks is practitioner opinion.

**The three descriptions** (invented for this lesson):

- **Class A.** "Street Survival. One two-hour seminar. Learn the five moves that end any fight.
  Taught by a seventh-degree master with thirty years' experience."
- **Class B.** "Personal safety for shift workers. Four three-hour sessions: noticing trouble,
  talking it down, getting away and calling for help, and practice with padded partners in
  scenarios you build up to. A session on the law in England and Wales. You choose which drills you
  join. A refresher evening six months later."
- **Class C.** "Combat fitness. Get fit, get strong, feel confident. Pads, bags and conditioning,
  three evenings a week."

:::predict Before you read on: which of the three would you rule out first, and on which item of this course's checklist?

Class A, on the first item: nothing on avoiding, talking or leaving. It also makes a promise no source here supports, and offers grade and years as its only claim to quality. Class C may be good exercise, but on its own description it isn't a self-defence class.
:::

Class B meets most of this course's list, though it says nothing about people you know, which both
lists ask for. The federation's guide was written for a different audience, so for Dominic it's a
reference rather than a standard.

:::checkpoint Class B scores best, but what can it honestly promise Dominic, and what can't it? Answer in two or three sentences before opening this.

Something like: it can give him practice at noticing, talking and getting away, under some pressure, which, on this course's reading of lesson 10's police studies, may hold up better than reading alone, and it can tell him what the law says where he works, as general information. It can't promise he'll be safer in the car park, because the course found no controlled study of a class for men or for stranger violence, and whatever he gains will fade without the refresher.

If you wrote that Class B makes him safe, look at the gap above. If you wrote that no class is worth it, that goes too far the other way: finding no trials says little either way about whether classes work.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Self-defence means fighting off a stranger."** The best-studied programme was built around men
the women knew, and the federation's guide asks a good class to cover acquaintances and partners as
well as strangers.[1][18]

**"The trial settled whether resistance training puts the burden on women."** It didn't, and
couldn't: the trial measured rape among the women in it. Critics argue that training women puts the
work of prevention on them and leaves the "underlying factors" untouched; the authors answer that
their programme "makes clear that perpetrators are entirely responsible for the crimes they
commit". That part is a value question, and this course doesn't settle it.[2][15]

**"You can learn a technique from a page or a video."** Every source on training in this lesson
points to practice with other people, which is why this course teaches no technique.

Take 20 minutes for the first three parts, on paper; the fourth can wait for another day, or never. If any of it comes too close, stop; the lines are in the content note above.

**The numbers.** The Nairobi cluster trial estimated a 3.7-point fall in risk from 7.3% at the start, with a 95% interval for the fall of between 0.4 and 8.0 points, reading them as points, as the lesson did.[7] Work out the "one in how many" for the estimate and for each end of the interval.

**Which to trust.** In three sentences, compare that result with the EAAA trial's at two years on design, who collected the answers, and what later evidence says.

**Two descriptions.** Score each against the federation's guide and against this course's nine-item checklist, marking each item met, not met, or can't tell from the description.

- "Confidence and safety for women. Six evenings. Awareness, saying no clearly, verbal skills and practice with trained partners in a padded suit. Covers dates, partners and strangers. You decide what you take part in."
- "Reality defence. Train like it's real. Full contact from night one, no opting out. Our founder's system is the most effective in the world."

**Your decision.** Find one class near you, from its own web page, and score it the same way. Or write down that you've decided not to look for one. Either is a complete answer, and you don't need a reason.
:::

:::checkpoint Compare your answers.

**The numbers.** 100 divided by 3.7 is about 27, so about one in 27. The interval's ends give about one in 250 (100 divided by 0.4) and about one in 12 (100 divided by 8.0). That's this course's arithmetic on its own reading of the 3.7 as points.

**Which to trust.** A good answer notices that the two absolute figures are similar, 3.7 points each, but rest on different ground. Both are randomised, EAAA by woman and Nairobi by school. EAAA's difference at two years is no longer statistically significant. In Nairobi the interviewers worked for the programme's provider, the result is for the girls' and boys' programmes together, and the later independent trial in the same setting found no effect, though that trial is itself disputed. Neither says anything about men.

**Two descriptions.** The first meets some items clearly: acquaintances and partners as well as strangers, practice with a partner, and participation left to each woman. Most of the rest you can't tell from the description: getting away, the law, a refresher, how it treats survivors, and whether it's candid about its limits. A careful score has more "can't tell" than "met", and that's the right result for an advert. The second fails on opting out, which both lists ask for, claims a superiority nothing supports, and "full contact from night one" is the opposite of practice built up in steps.

**Your decision.** A scored class and a "not now" are both complete answers. For a man, or for a class aimed at street violence, the right label for whatever you find is practitioner opinion.
:::
