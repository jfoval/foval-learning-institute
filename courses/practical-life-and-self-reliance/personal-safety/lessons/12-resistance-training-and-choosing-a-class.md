A self-defence class promises something a page can't: that you'll practise, with other people,
what to do if someone tries to hurt you. Whether that promise is kept is a question the evidence
can partly answer, and this lesson takes it seriously. It sets out the one randomised trial that
found a programme cutting rape, what happened to that effect over two years, the trials that
followed it outside Canada and the argument about them, and the question underneath all of it:
whether this kind of teaching puts the job of prevention on the people at risk. Then it turns to
what a class can and can't do, whom the evidence leaves out, and how to judge a class before you
sign up for one.

:::callout Before you rely on this
This course is education, not legal advice, and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger now, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US. On a 999 call from a mobile, if you can't speak, cough or tap if you can and press 55 when you're prompted; in the US you can text 911 where that's available, but call if you can. If a partner, ex-partner or family member is frightening you, the lesson on domestic abuse and stalking starts with the helplines. Responsibility for a crime lies with the person who commits it: precautions change the odds, not the blame. The law differs between England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

Before any of the evidence, one thing this lesson will not do. It will not suggest that anyone who
was assaulted should have taken a class, or that a class they took should have worked. The people
who built the best-studied programme say the same in their own
[two-year paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5821219/): "The program makes clear that
perpetrators are entirely responsible for the crimes they commit."[2] Lesson 11, "Sexual assault:
the freeze, resistance and the law", showed that the body can stop a person resisting at all,
and that this is involuntary. A trial's result is an average over hundreds of people. It is never
a measure of what any one person could have done.

## One trial, read closely

The programme is called Enhanced Assess, Acknowledge, Act, or EAAA. Senn and colleagues tested it in a randomised trial at three Canadian universities, published in the *New England
Journal of Medicine* in 2015. This course read [the abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26061837/)
in full, and the two-year paper that followed it in full.[1][2]

The women were first-year students. Each was randomly assigned either to the programme or to a
session giving access to brochures about sexual assault, which the abstract describes as the usual
university practice at the time. The programme was "four 3-hour units ... with the goal of being
able to assess risk from acquaintances, overcome emotional barriers in acknowledging danger, and
engage in effective verbal and physical self-defense".[1] The word to notice is acquaintances. The
abstract's own background says the risk these students face comes mainly from men they know, and
the programme was built for that, not for a stranger in a car park.[1] In all, 451 women were
assigned to the programme and 442 to the brochures, and 91% of the programme group came to at
least three of the four sessions.[1] The main outcome was completed rape over the following year,
measured by the women's answers to a standard survey of sexual experiences.[1]

:::predict The trial's headline is that the programme cut the one-year risk of completed rape by 46%. Before you read the numbers: out of every hundred women in the brochure group, how many do you think were raped in that year? About two, about ten, or about thirty?

About ten. The abstract gives 9.8% for the brochure group, against 5.2% for the programme group.[1] A cut of 46% can sound like a cut from something large. It was a cut from about one in ten to about one in twenty: large for the women it describes, and still a change of under five in every hundred.
:::

Here is the result in the abstract's own words: "The 1-year risk of completed rape was
significantly lower in the resistance group than in the control group (5.2% vs. 9.8%; relative
risk reduction, 46.3% [95% confidence interval, 6.8 to 69.1]; P=0.02). The 1-year risk of
attempted rape was also significantly lower in the resistance group (3.4% vs. 9.3%, P<0.001)."[1]

### The same result, three ways

Logic and Argument lesson 9, "Bad reasoning in the news and everyday life", taught you to turn a
relative figure into an absolute one. The working below is this course's arithmetic from the
published figures, not a number the authors print.

**Relative.** 46.3% is the share of the brochure group's risk that the programme group didn't have.
Divide 4.6 by 9.8 yourself and you get about 47%; the published percentages are rounded, which is
enough to move a ratio of small numbers a little.

**Absolute.** 9.8 minus 5.2 is 4.6 percentage points: out of every hundred women, about four or
five fewer were raped in the year.

**One in how many.** 100 divided by 4.6 is about 22. So about one completed rape was prevented in a
year for every 22 women who took the programme. A university deciding whether to run the
programme would want this number, and "46%" hides it.

**The interval.** The 95% confidence interval runs from 6.8% to 69.1%: the data fit a programme
that did a little good and one that did a great deal. The two-year paper gives the interval for the
absolute difference at twelve months as 0.6 to 8.5 percentage points,[2] so "one in 22" could be
anything from about one in 12 to about one in 170. The trial shows the programme lowered the risk.
It pins down the size much less tightly than the headline suggests.

Now try one yourself.

:::checkpoint The abstract's second finding was attempted rape: 3.4% in the programme group against 9.3% in the brochure group. Work out the absolute difference, the "one in how many", and roughly the relative reduction, before opening this.

The absolute difference is 9.3 minus 3.4, which is 5.9 percentage points. 100 divided by 5.9 is about 17, so about one attempted rape was prevented in the year for every 17 women who took the programme. The relative reduction is 5.9 divided by 9.3, about 63%; the two-year paper gives 63.2% at twelve months, the gap again being rounding.[2]

If you got about 37% for the relative figure, you divided 3.4 by 9.3, which is the share of the risk the programme group still had, not the share it avoided. If you got 17% as the absolute difference, you divided 100 by 5.9 one step early: 5.9 points is the difference, and about 17 is the "one in how many".
:::

### What it measured, and on whom

Lesson 1 gave this course's question for every piece of advice: measured on whom, and measured as
what? Here the answer is narrow, and the narrowness is not a flaw in the trial but its
scope.

- **On whom.** First-year women at three universities in one country, who volunteered for a study
  about sexual assault. The two-year paper notes that volunteers came in with somewhat higher rates
  of past victimisation than students in general, and that the authors don't know whether the
  programme would work as well for young women who don't go to university.[2]
- **As what.** Completed rape, reported by the women themselves in a survey, over one year, in a
  group whose risk, the abstract says, comes mainly from men they know.[1][2]
- **Against what.** Brochures and the chance to talk to someone about local services, not nothing
  and not another class.[1][2]

The trial was funded by the Canadian Institutes of Health Research and the University of
Windsor.[1] In this course's labels, the programme is tested, for first-year university women and
for the risk it was built around. For anyone else, the result is an inference.

## Two years on

The second paper, in *Psychology of Women Quarterly* in 2017, followed the same women for two years.
Its abstract says that, over the follow-up as a whole, the programme significantly reduced
completed and attempted rape, attempted coercion and non-consensual sexual contact, "yielding
reductions between 30% and 64% at 2 years", and reduced beliefs that blame women for rape.[2] The
paper's table shows how completed rape, near the bottom of that range, changed point by point.

:::predict Between the first year and the second, do you think the gap between the two groups in completed rape grew, held steady, or shrank?

In percentage points it narrowed a little and then held; in relative terms it shrank. The table gives the running totals as 5.2% against 9.8% at twelve months, 7.2% against 10.9% at eighteen months, and 8.1% against 11.8% at two years.[2]
:::

At twelve months the programme group was 4.6 points lower; at eighteen months and two years, 3.7.
So, on this course's arithmetic, the programme group didn't pull further ahead in the second year:
its running total rose 2.9 points between twelve and twenty-four months, the brochure group's 2.0.
The relative reduction fell to 34.0% at eighteen months and 31.3% at two years, and the table marks
both as no longer statistically significant.[2] Attempted rape held, at about 63% or 64% at one
year, eighteen months and two years, significant at each.[2] And the two-year figures rest on
fewer women: only those who joined in the first year of recruitment, 370 in all, were surveyed at
two years, because, the authors write, funding didn't allow following everyone.[2]

The authors' reading is careful. The programme went on working for at least two years, they write,
but its effect on completed rape diminished and was no longer statistically significant, and a
booster may be needed in the second year of university.[2] They offer one possible reason: as
students move through university, boyfriends may become the more likely perpetrators, bringing in
forms of partner violence a twelve-hour course may not cover well enough.[2] That's their
suggestion, not something the trial tested. The other changes the programme made, in how women saw
their own risk, their confidence and their beliefs, stayed significant for two years while getting
smaller.[2] A real effect that fades is a pattern this lesson comes back to.

### The critics' reply, and the authors' answer

A trial showing that trained women were raped less often has a reply waiting, and the authors print
it themselves: "Some feminist scholars (e.g., Basile, 2008, as well as one of the reviewers for this
article) have suggested that resistance education for women is misguided, in that perpetrators may
simply move on to another woman. In other words, these critics suggest that sexual assault is
prevented for the individual woman who resists, but that this would not necessarily reduce rates of
sexual assault in the community overall."[2] Lesson 6, "Out and away", met this reply briefly. This
course hasn't read the 2008 work they cite, so the critics' case here is as the trial's authors
report it.

Their answer: "We, along with other feminist scholars (e.g., Hollander, 2016), suspect that
perpetrators may learn important lessons that then have an impact on their subsequent
behavior".[2] They tie it to the moment a man's intentions are spotted early and his attempt is
thwarted, whether by the woman or by someone else. And they grant the point that matters most:
"which explanation is correct is an empirical question".[2]

That concession is worth sitting with, because the trial can't answer it. It measured what
happened to the women in it, not to other women the same men met afterwards.

:::checkpoint What kind of study could tell the two explanations apart? Describe it in two sentences, in terms of whom it would randomise and what it would measure.

Something like: a trial that randomised whole communities, such as entire campuses, to offer the programme or not, and then measured sexual assault among everyone in each community, not only among the women who took it. If the men simply moved on, the rate for the whole community would stay about the same; if some men were deterred, it would fall.

That design is this course's reasoning from the authors' concession, not a study anyone has reported, and it shows why the question is hard: such a trial needs many communities and counts across whole populations. If your design randomised individual women again, notice that it would repeat what the first trial already showed and still couldn't see the women the programme never reached.
:::

## Later designs on campus

Two further studies of the same programme are weaker in design than the trial, and worth knowing
for exactly that reason.

**Out in the world.** Once the programme was offered to universities through a non-profit, the
team studied it at five Canadian universities between 2017 and 2021. On
[the abstract](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2023.2290859), the comparison group was women who
signed up but didn't attend, and "Completed rape was significantly reduced by 57.3% at
6-months."[3] Nobody was randomly assigned. On this course's reading, women who sign up and then
don't come may differ from those who come in ways that bear on their risk, which is the problem
randomisation exists to solve.

**Partner violence.** A 2025 substudy followed 153 women from the trial into new relationships. On
[its abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/08862605241270057), the programme "significantly reduced the
1-year risk of IPV by 54.4% (p = .037, 95% CI [2.9%, 79.8%])", IPV being intimate partner
violence.[4] The interval runs from almost nothing to a great deal, and the sample is small. An
online version is now in a randomised trial whose results this course hasn't seen.

**A public body's view, dated.** In 2016 the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)
listed "Empowerment-based training for women to reduce risk for victimization" in [a package of
approaches](https://web.archive.org/web/20210608134353/https://www.cdc.gov/violenceprevention/pdf/SV-Prevention-Technical-Package.pdf) to preventing sexual violence, with this programme as its example, and said:
"Empowerment-based approaches that focus on increasing participants' self-efficacy to identify and
reduce exposure to risky situations and people through intensive skills training have greater
research and theoretical support than approaches focused primarily on physical self-defense
training."[5] It added that these approaches had mostly been run and evaluated with college
students.[5] The CDC has since withdrawn the package, so this is its 2016 document, read from an
archived copy, not current CDC guidance.

## Beyond the campus

Every study above is of university women in Canada. The other trials this course read come from
Kenya, Malawi and South Sudan, of programmes teaching girls and young women empowerment and
self-defence skills, and they are more mixed. This course read each one's abstract, and the
Expression of Concern below in full.

**Nairobi, before and after.** A 2014 [cohort study](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2013-3414) of 1,978 adolescents near Nairobi found that
sexual assault fell from 17.9 to 11.1 per 100 person-years among those who took the programme,
with no significant change in a comparison group given a life-skills class.[6] A cohort can't
rule out other differences between the groups.

**Nairobi, randomised by school.** The [cluster-randomised trial](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11121-016-0701-0) that followed, in 28 schools,
estimated "a 3.7 % decrease, p = 0.03 ... (initially 7.3 % at baseline)".[7] The abstract doesn't
say whether the 3.7 is percentage points or a share of the 7.3; set beside a starting risk of 7.3%,
this course reads it as points. Its conflict-of-interest statement is candid: the instructors,
"and thus the survey interviewers, were employees of Ujamaa-Africa", the organisation that ran the
programme, which also funded the data collection there.[7] The authors stated it themselves, and
it isn't an accusation, but a reader needs the fact, because the people asking girls about
assault were the people who had taught them.

**Nairobi, independent.** A larger trial in the same setting followed 3,263 girls aged 10 to 14 for
two years. [Its abstract](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11121-023-01595-1) says it "improved on previous
ESD studies in this setting with longitudinal follow-up of individuals and independent data
collection", ESD being empowerment self-defence, and it found no effect: "Odds of reporting rape
were not significantly different in the intervention versus SOC group (OR: 1.21; 95% CI (0.40,
5.21), p = 0.63)", SOC being the standard-of-care comparison.[8] The authors add that the findings
should be read cautiously because of the study's limitations.[8]

That trial is itself disputed. The journal that published its protocol later issued an
[Expression of Concern](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8133355/): the surveys were planned
for small groups of girls with one interviewer, but the ratio in the study was much higher, and
post-publication review concluded that this "may have caused underreporting of sexual assault
incidents".[9] Five of the protocol's authors disagree with the Expression of Concern, three agree, and one
hadn't responded.[9]

**Malawi and South Sudan.** A [trial across 141 schools in Malawi](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-018-6220-0) found past-year sexual assault
fell among students in programme schools, "RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.56, 0.82", but not in the others.[10]
A [study in South Sudan and the Kakuma refugee camp](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2023.10.033) found annual rape rates fell "from 10.7% to 5.5%"
in programme schools, with little change in comparison schools.[11] The Malawi paper calls itself
an implementation trial, and the research behind this course records both as run with the
programme's provider, a point this course didn't check in the papers.

Put together, on this course's reading: five studies, four finding a fall, and the one with
independent data collection finding none and carrying a dispute about its method. The body of evidence is
contested, not settled, and none of its results is about men.

## The meta-analysis argument

A meta-analysis pools many studies to find an average effect. In 2023 Kettrey and colleagues pooled
80 studies of sexual assault prevention programmes for American college students, published between
1991 and 2021. On [the abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2023.02.022), "Effects on
sexual assault victimization were significant but small (g = 0.15)", g being a standard measure of
an effect's size across studies.[12] They found that "programs that use a risk reduction framework
are associated with less favorable outcomes than programs that do not", and recommended that
prevention move beyond a focus on individuals to an "ecological perspective", aimed at
relationships, communities and society as well.[12]

Researchers in empowerment self-defence, among them Hollander, Ullman and Senn, published a short
reply in the same journal in January 2024, and Kettrey's team replied to them in the next pages.[13]
This course read neither letter and won't guess at what either argues.

Hollander's own work sits on the other side. In a [non-randomised study](https://doi.org/10.1177/1077801214526046) of a ten-week university
feminist self-defence class, women who took it were, over a year, "less likely to experience sexual
assault" than similar women who didn't.[14] In a [2018 review](https://doi.org/10.1111/soc4.12597) she argues that empowerment-based
women's self-defence is "so far the *only* approach that has produced substantively significant
decreases in victimization rates".[16]

On this course's reading, a pooled average across 80 programmes and one well-run trial of one
programme answer different questions, how campus programmes do on average and whether this one
worked, so both results can stand at once.

## Where the effort should go

Underneath the evidence is a question evidence can't settle: should prevention effort go into
teaching people at risk to resist, or into changing the people who commit sexual violence and the
places where it happens? This is a value question, and the course describes it without choosing,
in each side's own words where this course has them.

**The case that it puts the burden in the wrong place.** This course hasn't read a critic of
resistance education making the case in their own words, so it's given here from what was read.
The critics the trial's authors report argue that resistance may only move an offender on to
someone else.[2] Hollander, who disagrees with them, lists their objections as including that such
training encourages victim blaming and neglects acquaintance assault.[15] Kettrey and colleagues,
from their pooled evidence, recommend moving beyond a focus on individuals.[12] And
[Vera-Gray and Kelly's](https://repository.londonmet.ac.uk/5706/) critique of the "safety work"
mandated for women and girls in public, which lesson 6 set out, says it functions "to limit women's
space for action and responsiblise them for preventing violence" (their spelling), though their own
abstract ends by exploring feminist self-defence as a response.[17] At its strongest, in this
course's words: when the people at risk are taught to fend off the people who harm them, the work
of prevention lands on those at risk, and even a programme that works for them may leave the
offenders, and the settings that allow them, untouched.

**The case that it is the best tool available now.** Hollander, in a
[2016 paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/0959353516637393), argues that those critiques are
"misguided".[15] Senn and colleagues call their programme one critical piece of the solution, to be
offered while wider change through bystander education and programmes for men takes hold, and point
out that in most sexual assaults no bystander is there to help.[2] The CDC's 2016 package calls
empowerment training a useful complement to work on preventing perpetration.[5] At its strongest,
in this course's words: on its proponents' reading, this approach has the strongest trial evidence
of any campus programme, and holding it back until the larger work succeeds leaves women without
the one tool shown to help them now.

**What both sides share.** Every source here puts the responsibility on the offender. The trial's
authors write that interventions for women "must not inadvertently suggest that women are
responsible for sexual assault".[2] The CDC's package says any programme aimed at reducing the risk
of being victimised must avoid placing any responsibility on its participants, stated or
implied.[5] The critics' worry is that such programmes may do it anyway.

The course holds that one shared commitment and takes no side on the rest. Lesson 6 found the two
sides closer on one point than they look: the critique was aimed first at advice telling women to
restrict where they go, which isn't what the trial tested. But training still asks women to do the
work, which is the critique's point about cost and responsibility.

:::checkpoint This question is the one place in the course where all three kinds of claim the institute keeps apart appear together: established or tested, contested, and value. Sort these three statements, with a reason for each. (a) In a randomised trial of first-year university women in Canada, a twelve-hour programme lowered the one-year risk of completed rape. (b) Empowerment self-defence programmes reduce sexual assault wherever they're run. (c) A university's prevention budget should go to resistance classes before programmes for men.

(a) is tested, for that population: a randomised trial measured the outcome that matters, with a wide interval and an effect that faded in the second year. (b) is contested: the campus trials mostly point one way, but the independent Nairobi trial found no effect and is itself disputed, the meta-analysis found small average effects, and most places and people have no evidence at all. (c) is a value question: evidence informs it, but who should carry the work of prevention is a judgement about what's fair.

If you filed (a) as established in general, notice the scope: the result is for the women studied, and lesson 1's labels keep that scope attached.
:::

## What a page, a class and practice can each do

**A page** can teach you to recognise trouble, the order of options this course has followed since
lesson 1 (avoid, leave, talk, comply or resist, get away), what sudden fear does to the body, and
how to choose a class. It can't teach a strike, a release or an escape, and this course never asks
you to practise one. Lesson 8, "Talking it down", quoted Project BETA's caution, about de-escalation,
that knowledge can come from a book but skills come only with practice.

**A class** is where the practice happens. The US workplace safety regulator, the Occupational
Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), says in its [guidance for health and social care workers](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/osha3148.pdf)
that "Both de-escalation and self-defense training should include a hands-on component."[19]
Carrying that beyond those workplaces is this course's step. The University of Connecticut's
[police department](https://police.universitysafety.uconn.edu/education-and-programs/self-defense-classes/) runs a twelve-hour women's course: "This comprehensive course progresses from
awareness, prevention, risk reduction, and avoidance to basic self-defense participation." It says
it "cannot offer self-defense classes of fewer hours than listed."[20] This is one department's
standard, practitioner opinion rather than a finding. EAAA ran for twelve hours too, and its
authors write that in their early pilots, shorter units with less practice time gave effects that
didn't last.[2]

**Practice under pressure** is what lesson 10, "What fear does to you, and the robbery decision",
took from two police studies: after officers [trained](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3142543/) against an opponent who shot back, their
"shot accuracy ... no longer deteriorated under anxiety", while [mistakes in split-second decisions](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2014.12.006)
about whether to shoot "persisted" after three weeks of practice.[21][22] Those were police
officers with firearms, and neither abstract gives a mechanism, so this course offers none. What it
takes from them, as its own inference, is that a class where you practise against a partner who
pushes back, with your consent and in steps you choose, may prepare you better than one where you
only watch.

### Why it fades

The EAAA effect on completed rape shrank in the second year, and its authors suggested a booster.[2]
You've seen the same shape before: lesson 8's Indianapolis officers, whose improved attitudes
mostly faded by the follow-up, and First Aid and CPR lesson 16's CPR skills, which fell within three
months. None of the sources explains why in the parts this course read, and none says how often a
self-defence refresher is needed. The Malawi programme added refreshers to its twelve hours.[10]

So "take a refresher" is sensible, untested, in this course's labels: it follows from the fading
seen in all three places, and nobody has measured the right interval for this skill. How to Learn
Anything lesson 7, "Getting good at a skill", gave the tools for practice that holds up, and Habits
and Self-Discipline lesson 3, "What the cue is doing", the tool for making a refresher actually
happen: tie it to a cue you meet reliably and notice easily.

## The gap, said out loud

Everything controlled in this lesson is about women and girls, and about sexual assault. The
research behind this course found **no controlled evidence on self-defence classes for men, or for
general assault or robbery**.

That matters because of where men's risk lies. Lesson 3, "Who is harmed, by whom, where and when",
showed that in England and Wales men were more likely than women to be victims of violence, with or
without injury, by a stranger: 1% of men against 0.4% of women in the survey year ending March
2024.[23] Stranger violence, where men's risk runs above women's, is exactly the kind of harm no
trial has tested a class against.

Two wrong conclusions are easy to draw from a gap like this, and both should be refused. One is
that the EAAA result carries over: it doesn't, because it measured different people facing a
different harm, with a programme built around men they knew. The other is that classes for men or for street
violence don't work. Logic and Argument lesson 6, "How much should the evidence move you", showed
that absence of evidence is evidence of absence only when the evidence would have been expected. No
one has run the trials, so nothing has been found either way. The honest label for a class aimed at
street violence is practitioner opinion: experienced people teach it and no controlled study
stands behind it.

## Choosing a class

This course names no martial art, style, product or school, and ranks none. It gives two
checklists, each labelled for what it is.

### A practitioners' guide

The National Women's Martial Arts Federation, a non-profit for women, girls, transgender and
non-binary people in the martial and self-defence arts, publishes a short guide, ["How to choose a
self-defense class"](https://nwmaf.org/nwmaf-blog/how-to-choose-a-self-defense-class/), by Amy
Eckert, 2025. This course read it in full.[18] It opens: "In a good empowerment self-defense program,
students learn awareness, assertiveness, and verbal and physical skills." It then lists what to look
for in an instructor or programme:

- One that knows the facts about violence and teaches to them: "a good class will address
  situations involving acquaintances and romantic partners as well as attacks by strangers".[18]
- One that "does not blame or judge survivors".[18]
- One that offers strategies "to help students make their own decisions ... instead of telling
  students what they should or should not do".[18]
- One where students "determine their own levels of participation".[18]

The guide credits part of its material to the National Coalition Against Sexual Assault.[18] In this
course's labels, the guide is practitioner opinion: guidance from experienced teachers, not tested in a
study. It was written for classes for women and others targeted for gender-based violence, and it
says so.

### This course's checklist

The second list is this course's own inference from the sources in this lesson and the ones before
it, not a tested tool.

1. **Covers avoidance, talking and getting away, not only fighting.** Lesson 1's order of options;
   UConn's course starts from awareness and avoidance.[20]
2. **Covers people you know, not only strangers.** EAAA was built on acquaintance risk.[1][18]
3. **Includes practice with a partner, under some pressure, consented and in steps.** Lesson 10's
   police studies; OSHA's "hands-on component".[19][21]
4. **Counts getting away and calling for help as success.** Leaving is allowed at every rung.
5. **Covers the law where you live, as general information, with escape first.** Lesson 13, "The
   law on using force", sets it out.
6. **Never blames survivors.** Every source in this lesson.[2][5][18]
7. **Lets you opt out of any drill.**[18]
8. **Offers a refresher.** EAAA's second year.[2]
9. **Is candid about what a short course can't do**, including, for most classes, that no trial has
   tested it.

**What not to count as evidence.** A black belt, a long lineage, a certificate from the school's own
association, years in business, or a promise that you'll "never be a victim". The commercial "how to
choose" pages the research behind this course found lean on exactly those, and they're written by
schools selling classes: marketing, not evidence. None of it tells you whether the teaching
prevents harm, and the one controlled finding in this lesson came from a programme judged by what
happened to the women afterwards.

**If you've been assaulted.** You don't have to take a class, and not taking one isn't a choice
anyone can hold against you. If you'd like to, you can take your time. The EAAA authors record that
a few survivors in the trial found their experience too recent and left, and that some took the
programme a semester or a year later.[2]

## A class for the street

Dominic is 34 and works night shifts at a parcel depot on the edge of Exeter, and walks across a
big car park to his car at about three in the morning. He'd like "a class for the street" and has
found three near him. What are his options now?

**What the evidence covers.** Not him. The trials studied women and sexual assault; nothing
controlled covers a man and violence from a stranger. Lesson 3 showed that stranger violence falls
more on men than on women, and lesson 4 gave the opportunity tools for a place like his car park. A
class is one option among several, and any class he picks is practitioner opinion.

**The three descriptions** (invented for this lesson):

- **Class A.** "Street Survival. One two-hour seminar. Learn the five moves that end any fight.
  Taught by a seventh-degree master with thirty years' experience."
- **Class B.** "Personal safety for shift workers. Four three-hour sessions: noticing trouble,
  talking it down, getting away and calling for help, and practice with padded partners in
  scenarios you build up to. A session on the law in England and Wales. You choose which drills you
  join. A refresher evening six months later."
- **Class C.** "Combat fitness. Get fit, get strong, feel confident. Pads, bags and conditioning,
  three evenings a week."

Class A fails most of this course's checklist: nothing on avoiding, talking or leaving, a promise
no source here supports, and grade and years as its only claim to quality. Class C may be good
exercise, but on its own description it isn't a self-defence class. Class B meets most of this
course's list and most of what the federation's guide asks for, though the guide was written for a
different audience, so for Dominic the guide is a reference rather than a standard.

:::checkpoint Class B scores best, but what can it honestly promise Dominic, and what can't it? Answer in two or three sentences before opening this.

Something like: it can give him practice at noticing, talking and getting away, under some pressure, which, on this course's reading of lesson 10's police studies, may hold up better than reading alone, and it can tell him what the law says where he works. It can't promise he'll be safer in the car park, because no controlled study has tested a class for men or for stranger violence, and whatever he gains will fade without the refresher.

If you wrote that Class B makes him safe, look at the gap above. If you wrote that no class is worth it, that goes too far the other way: the absence of trials is not evidence that classes fail.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Self-defence means fighting off a stranger."** The best-studied programme was built around men
the women knew, and the federation's guide asks a good class to cover acquaintances and partners as
well as strangers.[1][18]

**"One course and you're set for life."** The EAAA effect on completed rape faded in the second
year, and the authors suggested a booster.[2] Police attitudes after de-escalation training (lesson
8) and CPR skills (First Aid and CPR lesson 16) faded too.

**"A black belt or a long lineage means good self-defence teaching."** Neither is evidence that a
class prevents harm. The one controlled finding here was judged by outcomes, not grades.

**"Resistance training blames women."** This is the critics' concern, and a serious one: that
teaching women to resist puts the work of prevention on them. The trial's authors answer that their
programme "makes clear that perpetrators are entirely responsible for the crimes they commit".[2]
Whether resistance education is where the effort should go is a value question, and this course
doesn't settle it.

**"You can learn a technique from a page or a video."** Every source on training in this lesson
points to practice with other people, which is why this course teaches no technique.

**The numbers.** EAAA at two years: 11.8 minus 8.1 is 3.7 points, about one in 27 (100 divided by 3.7), on this course's arithmetic, with the paper marking this difference as no longer statistically significant.
