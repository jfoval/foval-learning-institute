# Outline: Personal Safety

*Stage 2, 2026-09-25. Built from `research/SOURCES.md` (the synthesis and Parts A to D, read in
full), after `npm run taught`, which shows eighteen earlier courses and 174 lessons, all written.
Sequenced for how the ideas build and for where the risk actually is. **A sensitive domain several
times over** (standards 3.4: crime, gender and family, law, and a live US argument about guns):
every lesson gets the Tier B Stage 4 and the neutrality audit, and every statute, number and
helpline is fact-checked against the primary, not against SOURCES.*

## How many lessons, and why

**Fifteen.** SOURCES suggested twelve and said lesson 8 might split. Planned against measured time,
twelve is four sittings short. First Aid's lessons were outlined at 65 to 85 minutes and measured
85 to 140, so this outline plans scope to fit about 90 measured minutes and splits wherever the
suggested sequence stacked two lessons' worth of objectives into one:

- **The suggested lesson 1** carried both halves of Part A's first idea (how crime is counted) and
  its second (what fear is and does), plus the course's scope. That is two sittings, and fear has to
  come first: it is what the reader arrives with. So **lesson 1 is fear, risk and the course**, and
  **lesson 2 is reading a crime number**, with lesson 3 on who is harmed.
- **The suggested lesson 7** put de-escalation and bystander intervention together. Each has its
  own evidence base (NICE, Richmond, ICAT and the health reviews; the 5Ds, Green Dot, Levine, Liebst,
  Lindegaard) and together they run past two hours. **Lessons 8 and 9.**
- **The suggested lesson 8** carried the stress response, the robbery question and sexual assault
  resistance, and lesson 9 carried the self-defence class. Sexual assault needs its own content note
  and its own opening on responsibility, and SOURCES requires the freeze before the resistance
  evidence. So **lesson 10** is the stress response and the robbery decision, **lesson 11** is sexual
  assault (the freeze, the resistance studies, the law), and **lesson 12** is resistance training and
  choosing a class, where the EAAA trial is taught once (reconciliation 3).
- **The suggested lessons 10 and 11** put all of the law of force in one lesson and weapons in
  another. The law of force across England and Wales, Scotland, the Model Penal Code and three states
  is a full sitting before the home is reached. So **lesson 13** is force in general (the shared core,
  escape, the aggressor and the end of the threat, stand your ground), and **lesson 14** is the home,
  property and carrying something for protection, with the gun evidence as one short contested block
  there, as Part D §10.3 recommends, not a lesson of its own.

1. **Fear, risk, and what this course is for.** What makes a crime frightening; fear as information,
   not error; functional and dysfunctional worry; the perception gap; comparing risks honestly; the
   paradox, four positions; the course's one value commitment, its labels and its scope.
2. **Reading a crime number.** Police counts and victim surveys in both countries; counts, rates and
   prevalence; the reporting and recording gaps; why one year is not a trend; the US argument since
   2020; how to check a headline yourself.
3. **Who is harmed, by whom, where and when.** Age, sex and circumstance; the stranger share taught as
   method; place, time, alcohol and the quarrel; injury; fraud as the commonest crime; repeat
   victimisation.
4. **Opportunity and attention.** Routine activity theory and situational prevention as tools for your
   own risk; awareness as attention and what degrades it; what the gait research measured; cues as
   behaviour, never appearance.
5. **Home, the door and the doorstep.** Locks and lights in combination; the alarm dispute; how
   burglars get in and whether anyone is home; don't go in; the weeks after; the doorstep con as the
   same pattern as the phone scam.
6. **Out and away: streets, travel and meeting people.** The official advice and the evidence behind
   each piece; if you are followed; travel; meeting someone from online; talking to children; the
   critique of safety advice aimed at women.
7. **Domestic abuse and stalking.** Numbers and the monitoring warning first; what the services
   describe; why separation can be the most dangerous time; safety planning as the services define it;
   technology; the silent call; lines for women and men; helping a friend.
8. **Talking it down.** The ladder (avoid, leave, talk, comply or resist, get away); de-escalation as
   the guidance names it, each technique with its evidence status; when to stop and leave.
9. **Stepping in for someone else.** The 5Ds; what cameras show bystanders do; the risk to interveners;
   the commercial robbery exception; the argument about calling the police.
10. **What fear does to you, and the robbery decision.** The defence cascade and the freeze; perception
    and memory under threat; practice under pressure; comply or resist, both sides at full strength;
    property against the person.
11. **Sexual assault: the freeze, resistance and the law.** Responsibility first; tonic immobility;
    consent and submission; what the resistance studies found; who and where.
12. **Resistance training, and choosing a class.** The EAAA trial and its follow-ups; the disputed
    trials and the meta-analysis argument; the value question; what a page, a class and stress-exposed
    practice can each teach; the gap for men and for robbery; two checklists.
13. **The law on using force.** The shared core (necessity, proportion, imminence, belief); escape in
    five jurisdictions; the aggressor, revenge and the end of the threat; stand your ground against a
    duty to retreat.
14. **The home, property, and carrying something for protection.** The householder rule and *Ray*;
    castle doctrine; property; offensive weapons, blades and pepper spray; the evidence on guns for
    protection, framed on the National Research Council.
15. **Afterwards.** 999, 101 and the silent call; 911, text and the local number; what reporting gets
    you; support whether or not you report; compensation; what happens after force; rights if
    questioned; the weeks after. The project.

**Why this order.** Data first, as SOURCES' synthesis and the only syllabus that fits (UT Dallas CRIM
6308, measurement before theory) both do, but **fear before data**, because a reader who is afraid
and is handed a table feels dismissed (Warr; Part A §11). Then the order follows the ladder the
course teaches (SOURCES essential 10): avoid (4 to 7), talk (8, 9), comply or resist (10 to 12), the
law of force (13, 14), and afterwards (15). Domestic abuse sits inside the avoidance block, straight
after the street, because **the street is the smaller part of the picture for women** (8.0% of rapes
in a public place, against 38.2% in the victim's home, B20), and putting it after three street
lessons as an afterthought would repeat the misweighting the evidence corrects (SOURCES finding 5).
It comes before de-escalation because lesson 8 must be able to say "this is not advice for an
abusive relationship; lesson 7 is" and point back.

**The two data lessons must not read as a statistics course** (SOURCES open concern 15). Each opens on
a decision a reader actually makes (whether to go out, whether a place got more dangerous, what to
worry about for a teenager), and every statistical idea is introduced only when that decision needs
it. Statistics for Citizens (Term 5) comes later on the Core, so this course teaches counts, rates,
prevalence and the two measuring systems itself, and no further.

**Why not fewer.** Every merge above produces a lesson past two hours. **Why not more.** Fraud is a few
paragraphs in lesson 5, because Personal Finance lesson 10 and Digital Literacy lesson 11 already
teach the con in depth (SOURCES open concern 2). Children get one household section (lesson 6).
Travel is guardianship applied and sits in lesson 6. Workplace aggression sits in lesson 8, where
NICE, OSHA and HSE are. The gun evidence is one block in lesson 14.

**Lesson length.** Planned between 65 and 90 measured minutes (measured minutes include exercises
and the Sources list). **Two lessons carry named seams**: lesson 13 splits at "Stand your ground"
into "The law on using force" and "Escape, and stand your ground" if its draft passes 100 minutes,
and lesson 14 splits at "Carrying something" into "The home and property" and "Carrying something
for protection" if its draft passes 100. Either split renumbers the course; cross-references here use
titles as well as numbers so a split is a find-and-replace.

## What is deliberately left out, and said so in lesson 1

- **Technique.** No strike, release, hold, escape or weapon use is taught, drawn or described step by
  step. A page cannot teach one (Part C §7), and the course never asks a reader to practise one.
  Lesson 12 is about choosing the class that can.
- **Anything about choosing, carrying, using or storing a firearm**, and any product: sprays, "legal
  alternatives", alarms by brand, apps, locks by brand (Part D §5.4, §10.3; SOURCES "What not to
  reproduce").
- **Legal advice.** The law is general information, labelled every time (decision 9). Nothing tells a
  reader what they may lawfully do in their own situation or what to say to police.
- **Medical care after an assault.** First Aid and CPR owns it; lesson 15 points there.
- **Mental health after a crime.** Mental Fitness lesson 7 (what reactions after a potentially
  traumatic event usually look like) and First Aid lesson 16 (support lines, the crisis response) own
  it; lesson 15 points to both and repeats neither.
- **Online security and the con in depth.** Digital Literacy lessons 10 and 11 and Personal Finance
  lesson 10 own passwords, phishing, the payment rail and the phone scam; Cybersecurity for Everyone
  (Term 6) goes further. This course keeps the doorstep, the distraction burglary, meeting people from
  online, and technology used to control or track a partner.
- **Child protection.** One household section on talking to children (lesson 6). Nothing else.
- **Ordinary conflict and relationships.** Conflict Resolution (Term 4) owns disagreement that is not
  a threat of violence; Relationships and Family (Term 4) owns relationships. This course owns safety
  planning when a relationship is abusive, and de-escalation when violence is threatened.
- **Not researched, and named as such:** terrorism and mass-violence response ("run, hide, tell" and
  its US equivalents), drink spiking, hate crime (Doerner's textbook has a chapter; nothing was read),
  online harassment beyond tracking and stalkerware, human trafficking, road safety beyond the risk
  comparison. Lesson 1 says the course did not research them. **For the orchestrator:** terrorism
  response and hate crime are candidates for a `docs/QUEUE.md` research-gap line.
- **Scotland and Northern Ireland crime data, and Northern Ireland law.** The crime data read are
  England and Wales (ONS) and the US (BJS, FBI); Scotland's law was read (Jury Manual), Northern
  Ireland's firearms and knife law was not. Decision 2 says how each is named.
- **Employers' duties.** OSHA and HSE are used for what they tell workers; a workplace violence
  programme is an employer's job and is not taught.

## Sixteen decisions, settled here so no lesson re-opens them

**1. Honest objectives, and no technique on anyone.** Every objective uses a verb a reader can be
tested on from a page: **explain, identify, compare, decide, state, describe, recognise, evaluate,
plan, sort**. No objective says "defend yourself", "escape a grab", "disarm" or "de-escalate a
fight", because none can be shown from a page, and the de-escalation evidence itself says attitude
gains from training fade (IMPD ICAT) and skills learned under realistic pressure are what hold (Part
C §5, §7). `course.yaml` outcomes are reworded to match (done at this stage).

**Exercises are written plans, spoken scripts, sorts, readings of real data pages and real published
guidance, never a physical rehearsal and never a test in the world.** Specifically, no exercise asks
a reader to:

- practise any strike, release or hold, on a person or on anything else;
- rehearse a confrontation with a stranger, follow or watch a stranger, or "test" whether someone
  looks like a target;
- call or text 999, 911, 101, a helpline or the police to practise (say the script aloud to yourself
  or a friend);
- test a home's security by forcing a lock, window or door (look, list, price);
- walk a route at night, or on the phone, to see what happens;
- look into another person's phone, accounts, location or history, including a partner's;
- **write a domestic abuse safety plan on a shared device or anywhere an abuser could find it**
  (lesson 7's exercises are about choosing the right line and supporting a friend, and say so);
- post, share or keep a recording of another person without their consent (Right To Be's rule).

A verbal role-play with a willing friend is allowed in lesson 8 only: seated, words only, no
touching, an agreed word that stops it, and either person may stop at any time. The drafter writes
these limits into each exercise, not into a footnote.

**2. Jurisdictions named exactly, side by side, none called better.** "The UK" is used only where the
source covers the whole UK, which in this course is almost never:

- **Crime data:** "England and Wales" (ONS, CSEW, Homicide Index); "the US" (BJS, FBI). Great Britain
  only where the source is GB (DfT road deaths; the ONS 2022 perceptions survey). Scotland and
  Northern Ireland count separately and the course did not read their figures; say so once, in
  lesson 2.
- **Law:** "England and Wales" (s.76's householder and retreat subsections, PCA 1953, CJA 1988
  s.139); "Scotland" (common law, the Jury Manual, the 1995 Act ss.47 and 49); "Great Britain"
  (Firearms Act 1968 s.5 extends to England, Wales and Scotland); "Northern Ireland" only as
  "different again", with the one fact read (s.76 extends there without the householder and retreat
  amendments) unless gate G15 closes. "US" law is always a named state, or the Model Penal Code
  labelled as a model code that is not law anywhere by itself.
- **Services:** each line with the nations or states its own page says it serves (gate G6).
- **Where the jurisdictions differ**, a short table or an "In England and Wales / In Scotland / In the
  US" set, never interleaved through the prose. Where they agree, one sentence says so and names
  each, because agreement is information too (no need to wait to be struck, lesson 13).
- **Neither country's law, and no state's, is called better or worse in the course's voice.** Where a
  difference is argued over (stand your ground), decision 8 applies.
- **Numbers are never merged across countries or measures.** A CSEW percentage of adults does not sit
  in a column with an NCVS share of victimisations (reconciliation 1); the US "someone was home" 28%
  and the England and Wales "over half" are reported side by side and not reconciled
  (reconciliation 2).

**3. Every figure carries its source, year, measure, geography and whose harm it counts.** The
convention is in the sentence: "In England and Wales, the crime survey for the year to March 2026
estimated...". Rules:

- **One figure, one source.** A sentence never mixes two sources' numbers without naming each
  (reconciliation 7: near-identical numbers are different measures).
- **Single-year figures are never taught as rates.** NCVS rape reporting (46% then 24%) and robbery
  (42% then 73%) are not taught as the reporting rate: BJS marks both changes significant, but each
  single-year figure is imprecise, and they are never taught as noise either; "roughly half of
  violence and under a third of property crime reaches the police" is the rate (Part A US-1).
- **Lifetime and annual figures are labelled as such** and never set side by side as if they measured
  the same thing (CDC NISVS lifetime against NCVS annual).
- **"Current" is always dated.** Never "violence is falling"; always "the survey's estimate for the
  year to March 2026 was 78% below its 1995 peak".
- **Series in transition are flagged at the point of use:** the NCVS instrument change in 2024; the
  CSEW's new sexual victimisation questions; the anti-social behaviour question change from April
  2025; police recorded crime without accreditation since 2014; UK fraud reporting now Report Fraud.
- **Re-check trigger.** ONS publishes *Crime in England and Wales* quarterly and the homicide
  article each February; BJS publishes *Criminal Victimization* each autumn; the FBI releases its
  annual figures each autumn.

**Proposed standing item for `docs/QUEUE.md`** (for the orchestrator; this session touches only the
course folder):

> Victimization* and the FBI its annual release, and each July when ONS publishes its annual

**5. The course's question: measured on whom, and measured as what?** Memory added the sample, Mental
Fitness what the comparison group got, Nutrition "instead of what?", Strength and Fitness "trained
whom, for how long, measured on what?", Cooking "what is this rule protecting?", First Aid "which
guideline, which year, and how strongly?". **Personal safety advice fails in one way more than any
other: it outruns what was measured.** The gait studies measured raters' judgements, not attacks
(Gunns 2002). The lighting review measured area crime, not one walker's risk (Welsh and Farrington).
WIDE compared households in a survey, not in a trial (Tseloni 2017). ICAT measured police officers'
use of force (Engel 2022). EAAA measured completed rape among first-year university women, most of it
by acquaintances (Senn 2015). The defensive gun use estimates measured different questions put to
different samples (NRC 2013). And a crime figure is always a police count or a survey estimate, of
some population, over some period (lesson 2). Introduced in lesson 1, taught properly in lesson 2 on
crime numbers, used in every lesson after, and joined to the institute's claim sort in lesson 12.

**The advice labels, taught in lesson 1 and used in every lesson.** Every piece of practical advice
the course passes on carries one of four labels, in the prose, not as a badge:

- **Tested**: a trial, or a strong comparison, measured the outcome that matters (WIDE against no
  security; EAAA; ICAT for police).
- **Sensible, untested**: it follows from established theory or evidence nearby, and nobody has
  measured the outcome (plan your route; tell someone; phone away when walking somewhere unfamiliar).
- **Contested**: the evidence points in more than one direction (alarms; comply or resist; the gun
  questions).
- **Practitioner opinion**: experienced people teach it and no study stands behind it (Quinn's rules;
  "social" and "asocial" violence).

A value question (who should carry the burden of prevention; whether the law should require
retreat) is not an advice label: it is described, each side in its own terms, and not resolved
(standards 3.1, kind 3).

**7. Readers who have been harmed, and readers in danger now.** Readers will include people who have
been robbed, assaulted, raped or abused, and people who are in danger as they read (SOURCES
opening). These rules are not optional and each reviewer checks them:

- **Never call a reader's fear irrational**, and never compare a victim's risk to falling downstairs
  as a way of dismissing it. A comparison with ordinary risks is made once, in lesson 1, with Warr's
  caution about why it is incomplete (Part A R-6, §11).
- **Never tell a reader what they, or anyone, should have done.** No scenario implies a victim chose
  wrongly by where they went, what they wore, drank or said, how they walked, whether they complied or
  resisted, or whether they froze. Scenarios are written forward (what are the options now?), never
  backward (what went wrong?). **Nobody who complied or resisted chose wrongly** (Part C §3, §11).
- **Content notes go before, not after**, any section on sexual violence, domestic abuse or homicide
  figures, with the support lines at that point (gate G8 decides which numbers) and the name of the
  next section so a reader can skip. The standard form:

  > :::callout Content note
  > The next section, "<heading>", discusses <rape and sexual assault / domestic abuse / killings>. If you'd like to talk to someone, <the lines for each country, from gate G8>. You can skip to "<next heading>".
  > :::

- **The domestic abuse lesson** opens with the helplines and the Hotline's warning that "Internet
  usage can be monitored and is impossible to erase completely" in its first screen, before any
  teaching. It never says "just leave". It says, in the services' own words, that the reader is the
  expert on their own situation and decides (Refuge, "We will never tell you what to do"; the
  Hotline, "you are the expert in your situation").
- **In the sexual assault material, the freeze comes before the resistance evidence**, and the first
  paragraph says the only cause of a sexual assault is the person who commits it (Part C §4 handling
  note).
- **Men are victims in this course too**, of stranger violence most of all, and of domestic abuse,
  stalking and sexual violence. Every lesson's worked examples include a man as the person at risk
  somewhere, and lesson 7 gives a line for men.
- **The alcohol and drugging figures for stranger rape (B20) are not used anywhere.** No lesson needs
  them, and SOURCES allows them only "alongside the statement that responsibility lies with the
  offender"; the course's judgement is that no teaching point here is worth the risk of reading as a
  judgement on victims.
- **Offender race and ethnicity tables are not used** (NCVS Tables 11 and 13; ONS ethnicity data). If a
  reviewer insists, ONS's caveat goes in verbatim (Part A §11). No worked example describes a person
  who poses a threat by race, ethnicity, religion, class, dress or accent; threatening people are
  described by what they do.
- **The Hotline firearms page's false-report tip is not reproduced, nor its tactics for when a gun is
  pointed at you, nor its "500 percent" figure** (gate G4). **Nor the Suzy Lamplugh Trust's unsourced
  "half of all burglaries" line, its password advice, or its alarm technique beyond "set it off and
  leave".**

**8. Contested questions: each side in its own words, and nothing resolved that the evidence has not
resolved.** The sixteen questions in SOURCES' table are handled as the table classifies them.
Language rules for all of them:

- **"Stand-your-ground"** is the term (the proponents' own); the lesson says once that opponents call
  them "shoot-first" laws, attributed (RAND notes the phrase). **"Duty to retreat"** is neutral.
- **"Defensive gun use"** is the term; "gun rights" and "gun control" are the neutral names for the

## Lesson sequence

Every lesson opens with its reason to care, then the safety callout (decision 4), and ends with
Connections, Go deeper, Sources and the quiz. Predicts and checkpoints: two or three of each per
lesson, at least one a decision on a new scenario. Every lesson's worked examples include a man and a
woman as people at risk across the course (decision 7), and no place name is reused across lessons
(the brief's used-places script).

### 1. Fear, risk, and what this course is for (~75 min)

- **Objectives:** explain what makes a crime frightening, using Warr's account of seriousness and
  likelihood, and why that makes fear information rather than a mistake; distinguish functional from
  dysfunctional worry and state what the evidence says each costs; compare a risk you fear with an
  ordinary risk and say what the comparison leaves out; state what this course covers and leaves
  out, what its four advice labels mean, and where its law and data apply.
- **Core ideas:** the reason to care is SOURCES finding 1, said plainly and without the word
  "irrational": most harm people fear is rarer, and most harm they risk is more ordinary, than the
  headlines suggest. **The perception gap**: Gallup October 2025 (49% said US crime rose in the past
  year, the lowest since at least 2018; 31% afraid to walk alone at night within a mile of home);
  Gallup's local-national gap (43 points on average over 25 years, an opinion piece, cited as such);
  ONS 2016 (60% thought national crime rose; 84% in 2008/09; "do not typically match well to
  reality"; local perceptions "match reality more closely") (F-5 to F-7). **What makes a crime
  frightening**: Warr and Stafford's multiplicative account, seriousness times likelihood, which is
  why burglary, not murder, topped US fear rankings; people overestimate rare offences and
  underestimate common ones but get the order right; the media report "precisely those crimes that
  are least likely to occur"; Warr's "perceived risk of robbery, plus rape, plus additional injury"
  as the structure of many women's fear (F-1). **Fear is not the enemy**: "Fear, then, is not
  intrinsically bad"; rationality "an ill-advised notion" for fear; nobody can tell people what to
  value (F-1). **Functional and dysfunctional worry**: Jackson and Gray 2010 (a quarter of the worried
  treated worry as problem-solving, with precautions that made them feel safer and cost them
  nothing in quality of life); Jackson and Stafford 2009 (worry harms health, which heightens worry);
  Gray, Jackson and Farrall 2008 (asking how often shrinks fear: 85% had not worried about robbery
  in the past year, 5% once a month or more) (F-2, F-3, B12). **Warr's dial**: less fear is not free
  ("by relieving fear, we stand the chance of increasing public injury"); more fear ends in a
  "fortress society"; spatial avoidance is the commonest reaction; industries sell to fear (F-1).
  **Ordinary risks, compared honestly**: US deaths per 100,000 from falls 14.2, road 12.1 and homicide
  6.8, with the mixed years (gate G23); GB roads 1,602 against 522 homicide victims in England and
  Wales, with the geography caveat (gate G24); then Covello, von Winterfeldt and Slovic via Warr on
  noncomparable risks, and the three reasons violence is not like a fall (chosen by another person,
  dread and moral injury, partly controllable) (R-1 to R-3, R-6). **Where fear and risk agree**:
  identity theft and scams are the only crimes most Americans worry about, and fraud is the
  commonest crime (R-5), a forward pointer to lesson 3. **The paradox** (contested question 1): the
  four positions (found, artefact, undercount, precaution), named, attributed and not resolved,
  with Scott's finding that women's fear is best explained by negative experiences with strangers
  (being followed, unwanted attention) (F-4, §7.1). **The course**: the one value commitment
  (responsibility lies with the offender; precautions change odds, not blame, Part A §7.4); the
  ladder previewed (avoid, leave, talk, comply or resist, get away, SOURCES essential 10); the four
  advice labels (decision 5); how jurisdictions are named (decision 2); law as general information
  (decision 9); what is left out, said once.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why fear follows seriousness times likelihood rather than frequency (Warr
  and Stafford, as Warr gives it); why standard survey questions inflate fear (a diffuse anxiety
  measured as if it were events, Gray and colleagues); why national perceptions drift from local
  ones (Gallup's offered account, labelled an editor's opinion: direct experience against "dramatic
  events and broader narratives").
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a man of seventy in a market town has stopped going to his
  Thursday evening club after a local news story about a mugging. The lesson walks through Warr's
  two factors for him, what the gap between local and national perception suggests, what his
  avoidance costs (Jackson and Stafford), and what a functional version might look like (a lift home,
  a charged phone), **without calling his fear wrong and without telling him what to do**; lesson 3
  will give his age group's numbers. (b) wrinkle: a woman in her twenties says, "I know the numbers
  say I'm more at risk from people I know, but I still feel unsafe walking home." The falls
  comparison fails her (Warr's caution), harassment is common (lesson 3), Warr's "robbery, plus rape"
  explains the structure of the fear, and the four paradox positions each say something different
  about her. The example ends on what the course will and will not do for her.
- **Misconceptions:** "crime is rising" as a default; "people who are afraid are being irrational";
  "less fear is always better"; "you're more likely to die falling down the stairs, so stop worrying";
  "personal safety advice is about making victims responsible" (answered by the value commitment and
  by lesson 6's full treatment of the critique).
- **Practice:** a fear inventory: list three harms you worry about for yourself or your household;
  score seriousness and likelihood separately; write what you do about each now and what it costs
  you in time, money and freedom; mark each worry functional or dysfunctional by Jackson and Gray's
  test. Then sort six pieces of safety advice (drafter-written, none from later lessons' examples) into
  the four labels, with the answer behind a checkpoint.
- **Images:** optional SVG: US deaths per 100,000 (falls, road, homicide), each bar labelled with its
  capture's data year, with Warr's caution printed under it as part of the figure. Drawn only if gate
  G23 closes or the mixed years are shown on the bars.
- **Sources:** Part A F-1 to F-8, R-1 to R-6, §7.1, §7.4, §11; Part B B12; synthesis findings 1 and 3.
- **Connects:** Mental Fitness lesson 4 (Worry): worry as ordinary, and when it stops helping;
  Digital Literacy lesson 1 (the five questions of a security plan, now asked of the body); First Aid
  lesson 1 (fear of acting, answered with numbers). Leads to lesson 2 (where the numbers come from).
- **Spaced review of:** Mental Fitness lesson 4; Logic and Argument lesson 6 (base rates), previewed
  for lesson 2.
- **Neutrality:** the paradox, four positions; Gallup's partisan symmetry reported for both parties;
  the feminist critique previewed in one sentence with a pointer to lesson 6.

### 2. Reading a crime number (~80 min)

- **Objectives:** explain the difference between a police count and a victim survey in the US and in
  England and Wales, and identify what each misses; identify whether a figure is a count, a rate or a
  prevalence, of which population, over which period, and restate it as natural frequencies; explain
  why recorded crime can rise when crime does not, and why a single year's figure is not a trend;
  evaluate a crime headline by naming its source, measure and year.
- **Core ideas:** opens on a decision, not a definition: a local headline says violent crime in your
  area jumped; should you change anything? **Two systems in each country**: the NCVS (nonfatal
  violence and household property crime, people 12 and over in households, reported or not, "Excludes
  homicide", about 240,000 persons in a typical year) and the FBI's UCR/NIBRS (offences known to
  police; 95.6% population coverage; the hierarchy rule; NIBRS up to 10 offences per incident); the
  CSEW (16 and over; excludes communal establishments; preferred for violence, theft, fraud) and
  police recorded crime (better for homicide, weapons, robbery), in ONS's own words (US-1 to US-3,
  UK-1). **Three kinds of number**: victimizations, incidents and prevalence (BJS p. 2 definitions);
  rates per 1,000 persons 12+ (NCVS), per 100,000 inhabitants (FBI), per million (ONS homicide),
  percentage of adults (CSEW). **The worked comparison**: 8.9, 5.3 and 3.7 per 1,000, all correct
  (US-3). **The reporting gap**: about half of US violence and under a third of property crime
  reported (US-1); 36% of CSEW headline crime excluding fraud (UK-1); 4.8% of mass-market fraud
  victims complained (R-5). **The recording gap**: HMIC 2014 (19% unrecorded; 33% violence, 26% sexual
  offences) and why recorded violence rose after 2014 (UK-5); accreditation removed in 2014 and the
  CSEW's suspension and reinstatement (UK-1, UK-6); the OSR's misunderstandings (both "police figures

### 3. Who is harmed, by whom, where and when (~85 min)

- **Objectives:** identify who is most at risk of violence by age, sex and circumstance in the US and
  in England and Wales, and say which harms the surveys undercount; explain why "most violence is by
  strangers" and "most violence is by people you know" are each true for some crimes and victims and
  false for others, naming the source and measure for each; describe where and when violence
  clusters and how often it causes serious injury; explain repeat victimisation and decide when a
  change is most worth making.
- **Core ideas:** **age**: NCVS 2024 rates per 1,000 by age (29.3, 34.8, 31.7, 27.1, 20.4, and 7.5 at 65
  and over); in England and Wales the 16 to 24 group highest (US-1, UK-2). Lesson 1's older man gets
  his number. **Sex, split by crime**: in England and Wales, men more often victims of stranger
  violence (1% against 0.4%); women more often of domestic abuse (6.6% against 3.0%), stalking (4.0%
  against 2.3%), sexual assault (3.4% against 0.8%) and harassment (11.2% against 6.6%) (UK-2, B21);
  NCVS 2024 male 24.7 and female 21.9 per 1,000 (US-1; BJS reports only that neither changed significantly from 2023, not that the two differ insignificantly: corrected at lesson 3's Stage 4). **Other
  circumstances**: separated people 74.7 per 1,000 (US-1); urban 34.0, suburban 23.3, rural 16.7
  (US-1); harassment higher for disabled people and for gay, lesbian and bisexual people, stalking
  peaking at 16 to 19 (UK-2). **Who harms whom, as method** (reconciliation 1, contested question 2):
  the table taught one row at a time with its measure (NCVS about 46% of nonfatal violent
  victimisations by strangers, the researchers' arithmetic; NIBRS 13.5% of assaults, 16.5% of

  ex-partner (40%) or family member (16%), adult men by a friend or acquaintance (20%) or stranger

- **Mechanism to explain:** why one question ("who harms whom?") has several correct answers: each

### 4. Opportunity and attention (~80 min)

- **Objectives:** explain routine activity theory and situational crime prevention, and apply them to
  find what you can change in a situation without judging anyone; explain what awareness is and what
  degrades it, using the evidence on phones and noticing; evaluate "walk confidently and you won't be
  picked" against what the gait studies measured and did not; distinguish a behavioural warning sign
  from a judgement about someone's appearance, and say why the course uses only the first.
- **Core ideas:** **the triangle**: "Most criminal acts require convergence in space and time of likely
  offenders, suitable targets and the absence of capable guardians against crime" (Cohen and Felson
  1979, gate G22), and their turn away from the offender to the circumstances; the three levers a
  reader holds (be a less suitable target, add guardianship, change the convergence) (B1).
  **Situational prevention**: Clarke's definition; the 25 techniques grid, with the personal-scale
  entries ("go out in group at night, leave signs of occupancy, carry phone"; conceal and remove
  targets; reduce provocations as the bridge to lesson 8) (B2, B3). **Displacement** (contested
  question 7, strong lean): "rather little displacement" (Clarke); Guerette and Bowers' figures not
  given (decision 6). **The critiques, from three directions**, in Clarke's summary (Right, Left,
  liberal) and the "blaming the victim" charge; what the frame claims and does not (lowering your
  own risk; not a theory of why people offend; not a judgement on anyone harmed). **Awareness as
  attention**: Hyman 2010 (25% of phone users noticed the clown, 51% of single walkers, 61% of music
  players, 71% of pairs; phone users walked more slowly and acknowledged fewer people); music did not
  reduce noticing in this study; pairs noticed most, which is guardianship. Endsley only after gate G2.
  **The gait research** (contested question 6): Grayson and Stein as the ERIC abstract and the line
  quoted by Gunns (gate G1); Gunns 2002 (raters sorted point-light walkers by ease of attack) with the
  authors' limits in their words ("making ratings of ease-of-attack rather than actually attempting
  such assaults"); Wheeler 2010's critique of the design; Book 2013 (higher Factor 1 psychopathy scores
  related to accuracy, judged against walkers' own past victimisation); Fulham 2020 (gait linked to
  victimisation history only when walkers did not know they were filmed; hypervigilance "may be an
  adaptive response"); the verdict as SOURCES states it (raters agree; partial link to past
  victimisation; not shown that changing your walk lowers risk; not shown that offenders choose this
  way in life). **Warning signs are behaviour**: someone who persists after "no", follows, or cuts off
  a way out (B13's framing); the Suzy Lamplugh Trust's "trust your
  instincts", and the State Department's "Being safe is more important than being polite" (its page
  never mentions instinct; SOURCES B13 corrected at lesson 04's Stage 4); the
  course's rule that a cue is something a person does, never how they look, labelled as the course's
  argument from Cohen and Felson's turn away from offender characteristics (decision 6).
  **The cost side again**: awareness that turns into a few habits and stops (lesson 1's functional
  worry).
- **Mechanism to explain:** convergence (Cohen and Felson's hypothesis that dispersing activities
  away from households raised opportunity); inattentional blindness as Hyman names and measures it
  (divided attention on a call); why a rating study cannot show an attack is prevented (Gunns'
  "necessary to demonstrate" sentence).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a hospital porter leaving a late shift walks ten minutes to a bus
  stop past a car park. The triangle and the grid applied, each change with its label (decision 5):
  phone in a pocket until the stop (sensible, untested, with Hyman as the nearby evidence), walking
  with a colleague (guardianship; sensible, untested), the hospital's own escort if it has one. (b)
  wrinkle: a friend says, "I walk with purpose, so I'm never picked." The lesson sorts the claim
  against what was measured (ratings, not attacks), then turns to the inverse the literature invites,
  "she was picked because of how she walked", and shows why the evidence cannot carry it and why
  responsibility lies with the attacker; most serious harm to women comes from known people, where
  gait is irrelevant (B20).
- **Misconceptions:** "walk confidently and you won't be picked"; "earphones make you a target"
  (Hyman: phone calls, not music); "awareness means scanning everyone for danger"; "precautions just
  move crime next door" (usually little displacement); "trust your gut" as a complete rule; "safety is
  about spotting the kind of person who looks dangerous".
- **Practice:** map one routine journey or evening onto the triangle and the 25-techniques grid and
  list three changes that cost little, each labelled. Then a noticing exercise that stays safe: sit
  five minutes somewhere public in daylight, phone away, and list what you noticed; do the same five
  minutes later reading your phone; compare. Finally sort six claims about awareness into the four
  labels.
- **Images:** SVG: Hyman 2010's noticing percentages (25, 51, 61, 71), labelled "one campus, 2009,
  walkers who passed the clown".
- **Sources:** Part B B1 to B3, B4 (named only), B5 (gate), B6 to B13, B20; contested questions 6
  and 7.
- **Connects:** Digital Literacy lesson 1 (what you have, who would want it, how much trouble you'll
  go to: the triangle is the same questions); lessons 1 to 3. Leads to lessons 5 and 6, which apply
  the grid to the home and the street, and lesson 8 (reduce provocations).
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 3's repeat victimisation (the grid's "remove targets" after a first
  offence); lesson 1's functional worry.
- **Neutrality:** the victim-blaming inverse named and refused; Clarke's three-direction critique;
  cues as behaviour.

### 5. Home, the door and the doorstep (~80 min)

- **Objectives:** explain what the England and Wales evidence shows about locks and lights in
  combination, and what "almost 50 times lower risk" does and does not mean; compare the two sides of
  the burglar alarm question and say what evidence would settle it; decide what to do if you think
  someone is in your home or has been, from the UK and US sources; recognise the doorstep con and the
  distraction burglary as the same pressure pattern as the phone scam, and apply the call-back rule.
- **Core ideas:** **the security hypothesis**: Tseloni 2017, "strong evidence that security caused the
  decline in burglary in England and Wales in the 1990s", with the fall "solely in forced rather than
  unforced entries" (B14). **WIDE**: window locks, indoor lights on a timer, door double or deadlocks,
  external lights on a sensor; SPF 49; "almost 50 times" lower risk of burglary with entry "than 'no
  security'"; said as "compared with no security at all", never "cuts your risk by 98%"; a comparison
  between households, not a trial; the survey's limits in its own words (presence, not quality or use)
  (B14). **Alarms** (contested question 5): Tseloni ("not identified as contributory"; "think twice
  before recommending an alarm"); Tilley 2015 (associated with increased risk; "treated cautiously";
  the untested explanations); the other side: UNC Charlotte's offender survey (83% would check for an
  alarm, 60% would seek another target) and Lee's Newark study (dense alarms, fewer burglaries), both
  funded by the alarm industry's foundation, said without implying funding decides the result; both
  could be true; what would settle it (B15, B16). **How burglars get in**: England and Wales, year to
  March 2017: a door about 70% of the time, a window about 30%; three-fifths in the evening or night;
  someone at home in over half of burglaries with entry; stranger or known almost equally likely; US
  2003 to 2007: a household member present in about 28%, violence in 7% of all household burglaries,
  40% of entries to unoccupied homes through an unlocked door or window, known offenders in 65% of
  violent burglaries (B17, gate G25). **Both slogans fail** (reconciliation 2): someone being home is
  common, violence is not; Warr's "burglaries normally occur when no one is home" given only with its
  2000 date and the figures beside it. **If you think someone is inside, don't go in**: the Suzy
  Lamplugh Trust ("call the police" and "don't go and investigate by yourself", quoted as two
  halves: a source's spaced dash is split around, never reproduced, because the build fails on it;
  "don't enter the property") and the CPS/NPCC leaflet ("Wherever
  possible you should call the police"), with a forward pointer to lesson 14 for the law if it comes
  to force. **The weeks after a burglary** (lesson 3's repeat risk): fix the way they got in first.
  **The doorstep**: bogus callers (Lamplugh: call the number "on your utility bill, rather than the
  one on the identity card"; lock other entrances, because an accomplice may come through the back);
  NCPC (spare keys "not under a doormat or planter"; "Illuminate or eliminate places an intruder might
  hide"). **One pattern**: the FTC's four signs and Take Five's "Only criminals will try to rush or
  panic you" (B24); urgency is the tell; the call-back rule defeats the man at the door as it defeats
  the phone call. One paragraph each names Personal Finance lesson 10 (the three gates, the safe
  account, spoofing) and Digital Literacy lesson 11 (the con's structure) and does not reteach them.
  Where to report: Report Fraud (UK) and ReportFraud.ftc.gov (US) (gate G5). Fitting a lock is Home
  Repair's.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why security works: it raises the effort of forced entry, and the fall was
  in forced entries (Tseloni's account); why an alarm might mark a risky home rather than make one
  (Tilley's hypotheses, labelled untested); why urgency is the constant in a con (it stops you
  checking, as the FTC and Take Five put it).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: two flatmates moving into a rented ground-floor flat work out what
  to ask the landlord for, using WIDE and its limits, pricing each item, and label each request
  (tested, against no security, in survey data). (b) wrinkle: a household burgled a month ago is
  offered an alarm system by a door-to-door salesman who says the last burglary proves they need it.
  Three threads: repeat risk is real and highest soon after (Pease; Tseloni); the alarm evidence is
  contested, with who funded what; the door-to-door sale carries the urgency pattern, so the call-back
  rule applies. The example never suggests the household caused the burglary. (c) short: a man says he
  is from the water company and needs to check the kitchen tap while a second man waits by the back
  gate: the Lamplugh steps, walked through as a spoken script.
- **Misconceptions:** "an alarm is the best protection"; "burglars strike when nobody's home" and
  "burglars attack people at home" (both fail); "an ID card proves who they are"; "if I hear someone
  downstairs I should go and check"; "it won't happen again".
- **Practice:** a WIDE audit of your own home: look, list and price, never force anything (decision 1);
  write and say aloud a doorstep script that ends with a call-back; write the numbers you would call
  back for your utility, bank and council, from a bill or card you already hold.
- **Images:** a two-column table, not a chart: "someone at home during a burglary", England and Wales
  2017 (over half of burglaries with entry) and US 2003 to 2007 (about 28%), with the line that the
  definitions and periods differ.
- **Sources:** Part B B14 to B17, B24; Part A RV-1, F-1 (Warr's line, dated); Part D §2.4 (the leaflet's
  first line); reconciliation 2.
- **Connects:** lesson 4 (target hardening and guardianship at home); lesson 3 (repeat
  victimisation); Personal Finance lesson 10; Digital Literacy lesson 11. Forward to lesson 14 (the
  householder rule) and to Home Repair and Maintenance.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 2's labels on the ONS and BJS burglary figures; lesson 4's grid.
- **Neutrality:** the alarm dispute, funding stated neutrally.

### 6. Out and away: streets, travel and meeting people (~85 min)

- **Objectives:** sort common street and travel advice by the evidence behind it; plan a journey
  home, a trip or a first meeting with someone from online using guardianship, and say what each step
  is for; decide what to do if you think you are being followed, from the UK sources read; explain
  what the evidence says about "stranger danger" and about "don't walk alone at night", and describe
  the critique of safety advice aimed at women in its own terms.
- **Core ideas:** **the advice, as written**: the Suzy Lamplugh Trust ("Before you go out, consider
  how you are going to get home"; "well-lit, busy streets"; one earphone; its disclaimer that the
  advice "is in no way intended to suggest that taking such actions will eliminate incidents");
  the FCDO's solo travel guidance (itinerary and check-ins; first night's accommodation because "You

  sprays before travelling, a pointer to lesson 14) and its checklist (STEP; copies with a trusted

  13 area studies (UK 38%, US 7%), night-time crime did not fall more than daytime, suggesting

  car parks, and a camera is not a guardian who will step in (Piza 2019, gate G22); the rest is
  sensible and untested (B19). **If you are followed**: the Lamplugh sequence (cross the road and look;
  keep moving; head to a busy area and tell people; shout a specific instruction such as "Call the
  police!"); a personal alarm is for setting off and leaving (decision 10). **Meeting someone from
  online**: Lamplugh's steps (tell someone who and where; check in before, during and after; a friend
  who acts if you don't; a public place and an exit plan; talk about where you've been, not where
  you're going), as guardianship applied (B25), with its password line corrected by pointing to
  Digital Literacy lesson 10. **The misweighting**: 8.0% of rapes in a street, car park, park or open
  space against 38.2% in the victim's home (B20); stranger violence falls mostly on young men (B21);
  the advice is not wrong, only the smaller part of the picture (SOURCES misconceptions). **What is
  real on the street**: harassment (27% of women and 16% of men in the 2022 ONS survey); more people
  stopped walking in parks after dark in the last month (men 18% to 24%, women 32% to 37%) (B12, B21).
  **The critique** (contested question 4): Vera-Gray and Kelly's "safety work" (abstract), stated as
  mainstream UK research and in its own terms; the defence (guardianship and target hardening lower
  risk; a trial of skills-based training, lesson 12); how they fit (the evidence separates advice
  that restricts where women go from training that helps people recognise and resist the risks they
  actually face; Vera-Gray and Kelly also end on feminist self-defence); the value question (who
  should carry the burden of prevention) described and not resolved (B29). **The household section on
  children**: 105 stereotypical kidnappings in 2011 by NISMART-3's definition, "virtually the same as
  the 1997 estimate", with most victims lured by deception; NCMEC's 1%; its scripts ("get my
  permission before going anywhere with anyone"; tell me "right away if anyone asks you to keep a
  secret"; look for "a uniformed police officer, store clerk with a nametag or a parent with
  children") (B23). **The US gap**: the US street advice read is written for travellers (NCPC's
  street material unread); the lesson says so.
- **Mechanism to explain:** guardianship: someone who knows your plan can act when it breaks (Cohen and
  Felson; Hyman's pairs); lighting's mechanism is not settled (the review's own suggestion); why the
  first night abroad is the riskiest, in the FCDO's words.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a student getting home from a night out: a plan of five steps, each
  labelled (plan the way home before going, a friend who knows, phone away while walking, stay where
  there are people and light, an agreed "home" message), with what each is for. (b) wrinkle: a man in
  his forties meeting someone from a dating app for the first time. The Lamplugh steps apply to him as
  much as to anyone; and the point lesson 3 made about known people matters here in a different way: a
  date is a stranger becoming someone known, which is why the plan keeps the friend in the loop after
  the first meeting as well. (c) short: a parent rewrites "never talk to strangers" into NCMEC's
  scripts for a seven-year-old.
- **Misconceptions:** "stranger danger" as the core of child safety; "don't walk alone at night" as the
  core of women's safety; "CCTV means someone will come"; "streetlights work because attackers can be
  seen"; "earphones make you a target" (lesson 4); "carry pepper spray when you travel" (lesson 14's
  law, and the State Department's own caution).
- **Practice:** write a plan for a real trip or evening in the next month, each step labelled with its
  evidence; agree a check-in with one real person (a safe, real action); rewrite two child-safety rules
  into NCMEC's scripts for a child you know. The answer key shows a model plan behind a checkpoint.
- **Images:** none needed; a small table sorting ten pieces of advice by label.
- **Sources:** Part B B11, B12, B18, B19, B20, B21, B23, B25, B29; contested questions 4 and 6.
- **Connects:** lessons 3 and 4; forward to lesson 7 (known people), lesson 12 (the trial the critique
  meets) and lesson 14 (sprays). Travel Skills (Term 24) will take travel further.

- **Neutrality:** contested question 4 in its own terms; neither side's advocates caricatured.

### 7. Domestic abuse and stalking (~90 min)

- **Objectives:** recognise the patterns the services describe as domestic abuse and stalking,

### 8. Talking it down (~85 min)

- **Objectives:** describe the ladder of options in a threatening encounter, and explain why leaving
  stays open at every step; describe the de-escalation techniques that NICE and the Project BETA
  consensus name, and state the evidence status of each; apply them to a described encounter at work
  or in public, and decide when to stop talking and leave; explain what the police trials show, what
  the health reviews show, and what neither shows about members of the public.
- **Core ideas:** **the ladder**: avoid, leave, talk, comply or resist, get away (Part C §8); leaving is
  allowed at every rung; de-escalation is for when leaving is not yet possible or someone depends on
  you staying; **this is not advice for an abusive relationship** (lesson 7). **Why talk matters**: half
  of homicides in England and Wales start as a quarrel, revenge or loss of temper (lesson 3). **The
  guidance**: NICE NG10, for health and social care staff, not the public (early signs; distraction and
  calming; personal space; one staff member takes the lead; self-regulation of posture and eye
  contact; respect and empathy; flashpoints: refusing a request, asking someone to stop, asking someone
  to do something they don't want to do) (C §2.1). **Project BETA** (Richmond 2012), said to be expert
  consensus by its own authors: the ten domains; two arm's lengths and "if a patient tells you to get
  out of the way, do so immediately"; hands visible and not clenched; stand at an angle; staring
  "can be interpreted as an aggressive act"; one voice; short sentences, repetition; three ways to agree
  and "agree to disagree"; limits "in a matter-of-fact way and not as a threat"; nothing humiliating
  (C §2.2). **At work**: OSHA's definition and risk factors (exchanging money with the public, working
  alone, where alcohol is served; no specific OSHA standard); HSE's definition (including verbal abuse
  and threats by phone and online) and workers' part (use the training, report incidents) (C §2.3).
  **The evidence, sorted**: ICAT's stepped-wedge trial in Louisville (use of force down 28.1%, citizen
  injuries 26.3%, officer injuries 36.0%); the Indianapolis replication (19.6% then 18.3% sustained;
  officer injuries no significant change; attitudes "often returning to baseline"); Engel 2020 (64
  evaluations, mostly nursing and psychiatry, "questionable quality"); the Cochrane review's one study
  and "uncertainty remains"; Heckemann (confidence up, no change in the incidence of aggression); Price
  and Baker; Hallett and Dickens (C §2.4, §2.5). **Practitioner opinion**: Quinn's five rules as
  MacYoung quotes them (paraphrased, coarse source), and the "social" and "asocial" model, untested
  (C §2.6). **The course's inference**, labelled (decision 12): a member of the public who reads this
  may do better; nobody has tested it. **When to stop talking**: the other person closes distance,
  others join, a weapon appears, or a demand for property or for you replaces the argument: leave, or
  go to lesson 10's decision (the ladder's next rung; the triggers are the course's reading of the
  sources, labelled).
- **Mechanism to explain:** the sources give reasons for each technique (multiple voices confuse; a
  threat escalates; humiliation provokes) and Hallett and Dickens found the proposed theories lack
  empirical support; the lesson gives the reasons as the sources give them and says the mechanism is
  not established (decision 11).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a pharmacy assistant has to refuse a sale (NICE's first flashpoint)
  to a customer who starts shouting. Each move named with its source and label: one voice (the
  colleague steps back), space and angle, hands visible, short sentences, agreeing in principle, a
  limit stated without threat, a face-saving exit ("I can call the pharmacist for you"), and the point
  at which she leaves the counter and calls for help. (b) wrinkle: two drivers argue over a scrape in a
  supermarket car park; the reader is one of them. Talking works for a minute, then the other man's
  friend walks round behind. The example shows why this is the moment to stop talking and leave, and
  why that is not losing (face-saving works both ways; ICAT's "tactical pause" and "reactionary gap" as
  the police training names them, with the caveat that they were measured in police officers).
- **Misconceptions:** "de-escalation is proven" (recommended everywhere; tested in police); "calming
  someone down means giving in or apologising for what you didn't do" (Richmond's three ways to agree);
  "hold eye contact to show you're not afraid"; "more people helping to talk them down is better";
  "you should always try to talk it down" (leaving is always allowed); "de-escalation will work on
  someone who has decided to rob you" (the asocial model, untested).
- **Practice:** rewrite six provocative sentences (drafter-written) into Project BETA's forms; list the
  flashpoints in your own work or home routine and write the limit you would state for each; a
  verbal-only role-play with a willing friend under decision 1's limits (seated, words only, a stop
  word), or said aloud alone.
- **Images:** SVG: the ICAT Louisville reductions (28.1, 26.3, 36.0) as labelled bars, titled "police
  officers, Louisville, stepped-wedge trial", so the chart itself says who was measured.
- **Sources:** Part C §2.1 to §2.6, §8, §11; Part A UK-3; reconciliation 10.
- **Connects:** lesson 4's "reduce provocations" column; lesson 3's quarrels; lesson 7 (not this);
  forward to lesson 9 (when it's someone else) and lesson 10 (when talking has stopped working).
  Conflict Resolution (Term 4) owns ordinary disagreement.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 5's pressure pattern (urgency is the tell; the de-escalator slows things
  down on purpose); lesson 1's functional worry.
- **Neutrality:** the police evidence reported as evidence about police training, with no position on
  policing; practitioner opinion labelled.

### 9. Stepping in for someone else (~65 min)

*Not drafted until gate G28 closes.*

- **Objectives:** describe the 5Ds and choose an option that fits a described situation and your own
  safety; state what camera studies show bystanders do, how often interveners are hurt, and why a
  commercial robbery is different; compare the positions on whether a bystander should call the
  police, each in its own terms, and decide what to do when someone is in immediate danger.
- **Core ideas:** **from First Aid lesson 1, not retaught**: the bystander effect is real and weakens in
  dangerous emergencies; someone intervened in 90.9% of filmed public conflicts (Philpot 2020); the
  lesson opens by naming that lesson and adding what it did not cover. **The 5Ds**: Distract, Delegate,
  Document, Delay, Direct, with what Right To Be says each is (gate G28); "four of them are indirect";
  their history (Green Dot's three Ds, Delay in 2015, Document in 2017); Document's rule ("ALWAYS ask
  the person who was harassed what they want to do with your recording ... NEVER post it online or use
  it without their permission"); Direct "with caution" (C §6). **The evidence**: no controlled
  evaluation of the 5Ds found (the commissioned 67% figure reported as self-reported, commissioned and
  uncontrolled); Green Dot's cluster trial in Kentucky high schools (perpetration rate ratios 0.83 and
  0.79 in years 3 and 4); Kettrey and Marx's meta-analysis on bystander efficacy and intervention.
  **What cameras show**: Levine 2011 (third parties more often conciliatory than escalating;
  conciliation works better from several people than one); Parks 2013 (more intervention when
  aggression was more severe, mostly non-aggressive); Liebst's preprint (interveners "rarely physically
  harmed", 3.6%, gate G20); Lindegaard 2026 (bystanders helped in 35% of 43 commercial robberies, more
  bystanders did not mean more help, "wary of generalizing bystander intervention rates across
  emergency types"). **The commercial robbery case**: the official advice to staff is to hand over
  (OSHA 3153, lesson 10's preview), so a bystander in a shop robbery helps most by staying safe, calling
  when it is safe, and remembering, not by stepping in. **Calling the police** (contested question 12):
  Right To Be's position with its reasons, attributed; GOV.UK's "Call 999 if ... someone is in
  immediate danger" and OVC's; the plain point that a violent assault in progress is an emergency; the
  call itself from First Aid lesson 2 (speaker, location). The course adopts neither stance.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why several people calm a conflict better than one (Levine's finding; the
  abstract gives no mechanism, so the lesson says so); why intervention rates differ by emergency type
  (Lindegaard's caution; no mechanism given).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: on a late bus, a man keeps making comments to a young woman who is
  looking at her phone and has moved seats once. The reader works through the Ds by what each costs and
  risks, chooses an indirect one, and checks in with her afterwards; Document only with her say. (b)
  wrinkle: the reader is third in the queue in a convenience store when a man shows a knife at the
  counter. Why this is different (Lindegaard; OSHA's advice to the staff), what helps (staying out of
  it, leaving if possible, calling when safe, noticing), and why nobody in the queue who froze did
  anything wrong (lesson 10).
- **Misconceptions:** "crowds mean nobody helps" (corrected in First Aid lesson 1, with the commercial
  robbery exception added); "filming is always helping"; "stepping in means confronting the person";
  "people who intervene usually get hurt"; "calling the police is always right" and its mirror.
- **Practice:** four scenarios (none from the body): choose a D, write the first sentence you would say,
  and say what would make you switch to a call; write your own rule, in one sentence, for when you
  would call 999 or 911 as a bystander.
- **Images:** none required.
- **Sources:** Part C §6, §2.3 (OSHA 3153); Part D §8.1, §8.3; First Aid lessons 1 and 2.
- **Connects:** First Aid lessons 1 and 2; lesson 8 (the same techniques, used for someone else).
  Leads to lesson 10.
- **Spaced review of:** First Aid lesson 1's "give people jobs"; lesson 8's one voice.
- **Neutrality:** the police question (decision 8).

### 10. What fear does to you, and the robbery decision (~85 min)

- **Objectives:** describe what acute threat does to action, perception and memory, including the
  freeze, and say which parts are established and which are proposals; explain why fine skill and
  judgement fail under threat, and what practice under pressure can and cannot fix; compare the case
  for handing property over with the case that resistance often helps, each in its own terms, and say
  what flaw the two share; distinguish a demand for property from an attack on the person, and state
  the one case where every official source read agrees.
- **Core ideas:** **the defence cascade** (Kozlowska 2015: arousal, fight or flight, freezing as "a
  flight-or-fight response put on hold", tonic and collapsed immobility as responses of last resort);
  freezing "not a passive state but rather a parasympathetic brake" (Roelofs), whose underpinnings in
  humans "remain largely unexplored"; the proposals labelled as proposals (Bracha's five Fs;
  tend-and-befriend; "fawn" from clinical writing, not experiments) (C §5). **Perception and memory**:
  Artwohl's survey of 157 officers after shootings (diminished sound 84%, tunnel vision 79%, memory
  loss for part of the event 52%), with its caveats in her own words ("the exact percentages don't
  matter"). **Skill and judgement**: Nieuwenhuys 2011 (training against an opponent who shot back kept
  accuracy from deteriorating, still present at 4 months) against 2015 (threat-induced decision errors
  "persisted" and "may be hard to prevent"): practice under pressure helps skills more than
  judgement, and neither comes from a page. **No heart-rate thresholds** (decision 10). **Robbery**
  (contested question 9), both sides at full strength: official advice (the Metropolitan Police:
  "Property can be replaced, you can't."; the DC police brochure, including "GIVE UP YOUR CAR!" for a
  carjacking with a weapon; OSHA 3153); Faulkner 2001 (injury "strongly associated with ... employee
  resistance", with "Numerous intercorrelations", and the abstract's silence on sequence); Smith 2003
  (one in four resisted; 43% injured; "probably as a consequence"; robberies with weapons less likely to
  injure); Block and Skogan (non-forceful resistance related to better outcomes; the survey excludes
  the dead and has no sequence); against Tark and Kleck 2004's abstract (self-protection "reduced the
  likelihood of property loss and injury"; injury followed resistance in 10% of incidents where both
  occurred); Guerette and Santana (completion, not injury); the POP guide's figure (a BJS figure not
  checked; self-report). **The shared flaw**: victims choose to resist where it looks survivable;
  surveys cannot count the dead; records rarely show the order (C §3.3). **The working consensus on the
  narrow case**: weapon shown, property wanted, every official source read says hand it over, and the
  resisters' data do not contradict it. **Property against the person**: when the demand is to move
  you, hurt you or assault you, the calculation changes, and the sexual assault evidence (lesson 11)
  points toward resistance. **The law requires neither** (lesson 13). **Nobody who complied or resisted
  chose wrongly.** Gun resistance: one sentence that Tark and Kleck's abstract lists it among the most
  protective actions and says some findings "were unstable due to the small numbers of sample cases",
  pointing to lesson 14.
- **Mechanism to explain:** the defence cascade as Kozlowska describes it, and Roelofs' account of the
  freeze as preparation rather than failure; the selection problem in the robbery data as the reason
  two honest bodies of evidence disagree.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: at a cash machine at night, a man is approached by two men, one
  holding a knife, who want his wallet and phone. The narrow case, walked through: what each side says
  and why both agree here; what his body is likely to do (the cascade) and why that is normal; what
  happens after (lesson 15). (b) wrinkle: in a multi-storey car park, a woman is told to get into a
  car. The demand is for her, not her property; what the sources say changes (non-forceful resistance:
  screaming, running away, in Block and Skogan's list; lesson 11's evidence), and why no page can tell
  her what to do in the moment. Written forward, without an outcome that judges her.
- **Misconceptions:** "resisting a robber always gets you hurt" and "resisting is always safer"; "in an
  emergency you'll rise to the occasion"; "freezing is cowardice"; "you'll remember exactly what
  happened"; "above a certain heart rate your fine motor skills fail at a set number".
- **Practice:** sort eight claims about the stress response into established, proposal and practitioner
  lore; write your own advance rule for the property case in one sentence, and say which source
  supports it (the course's inference that deciding in advance helps is labelled, from Nieuwenhuys'
  finding that judgement under threat is hard to train).
- **Images:** none required; a table of the robbery sources with what each measured (outcome, sample,
  whether sequence was known) is the lesson's centrepiece.
- **Content note:** a short one before the robbery cases (violence), gate G8's lines.
- **Sources:** Part C §3, §5, §8, §9, §11; Part A US-1 (weapons), UK-3 (5% theft); Part D §6.
- **Connects:** lesson 8 (the ladder's next rung); lesson 9 (the queue); forward to lessons 11 (the
  freeze in sexual assault), 12 (training under stress) and 13 (the law requires neither).
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 2 (what a survey of survivors cannot count); lesson 8's ladder.
- **Neutrality:** both sides in their proponents' terms; no "should have".

### 11. Sexual assault: the freeze, resistance and the law (~75 min)

- **Content note first**, before the lesson's opening teaching, with gate G8's lines and a "skip to"
  pointer; then the first paragraph: the only cause of a sexual assault is the person who commits it;
  freezing is involuntary and common; a survivor who did not fight did nothing wrong; the law in
  England and Wales requires no resistance (Part C §4 handling note).
- **Objectives:** state where responsibility for a sexual assault lies and what the England and Wales
  prosecutors' guidance says about resistance, freezing and consent, as general information; explain
  tonic immobility and why freezing is not consent; describe what the studies of resistance to rape
  found about completion and injury, and what their designs can and cannot show; identify who most
  often commits rape and where, and what that means for which precautions matter.
- **Core ideas:** **responsibility**, in Senn and colleagues' words ("perpetrators are entirely
  responsible for the crimes they commit"; "only the presence of a man willing to commit sexual assault
  generates any 'risk'") (C §4.2). **The freeze first**: Möller 2017 (298 women seen within a month of
  rape at one Stockholm clinic: 70% significant and 48% extreme tonic immobility; "a state of
  involuntary, temporary motor inhibition"; associated with later PTSD and severe depression), taught
  as one sample; lesson 10's cascade applied. **The law**: the CPS annex (gate G9): the myths it lists
  as false ("A real rape victim would never freeze when attacked"; "If the victim didn't scream, fight,
  or get injured then it wasn't rape"); "not just fight or flight, but flop, freeze or befriend";
  "Consent and submission are different"; *Malone* as quoted. Labelled general information; Scotland's
  and the US law on consent and resistance were not read, and the lesson says so (planned around).
  **Resistance** (after the freeze, never before): Ullman 1997 ("Consistent evidence suggests the
  effectiveness of forceful resistance strategies", with its call for data on sequence); Tark and Kleck
  2014 (most self-protective actions "reduce the risk of rape completion, and do not significantly
  affect the risk of additional injury"; additional serious injury after resistance "rare"); Brecklin
  and Ullman 2005 (women with prior training more often said their resistance stopped the offender or
  made him less aggressive; self-report, cross-sectional) (C §4.1). **What the designs can show**: the
  same selection and sequence problems as lesson 10, with Tark and Kleck's sequencing as the reason
  their result differs from older work. **Who and where**: 43.2% of victims since 16 by a partner or
  ex-partner, 16.3% by a stranger (15.0% of women, 37.5% of men); the victim's own home 38.2%, the
  perpetrator's 28.5%, a public place 8.0% (B20); so the precautions that matter most are about known
  people, which is lesson 12's trial. **Supporting someone who tells you**: believe them; the lines
  (gate G8); whether to report is theirs (lesson 15).
- **Mechanism to explain:** tonic immobility as Möller defines it and Kozlowska places it (a response of
  last resort to inescapable threat); why the order of events matters to what a resistance study can
  conclude.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple, the method: reading Tark and Kleck's 2014 abstract sentence by
  sentence (what "SP actions" are, completion against injury, why they coded whether harm came before
  or after), ending with what the abstract does not tell us. (b) wrinkle: a friend tells you that a man
  she knew assaulted her months ago, that she "just froze" and so maybe it wasn't really assault. What
  the CPS annex and Möller say, what consent and submission mean in England and Wales, and what you
  might say back; decisions stay with her. A short third case: a man who was assaulted by an
  acquaintance wonders whether any of this applies to him: the CPS text is general, Möller's sample was
  women, the ONS figures include men, and the lines serve him.
- **Misconceptions:** "a real victim fights back"; "if there's no injury it wasn't rape"; "freezing means
  she consented"; "resisting just makes it worse" (injury after resistance rare in Tark and Kleck's
  abstract); "most rapes are by strangers outdoors"; "men can't be raped".
- **Practice:** sort ten statements into the CPS's myths and the facts that answer them; rewrite three
  news-style sentences that put the responsibility on the victim (drafter-written) so the grammar puts
  it on the offender; write what you would say to a friend who tells you, in three sentences, with the
  lines for your country.
- **Images:** none. No chart in this lesson.
- **Sources:** Part C §4.1, §4.2 (Senn 2017's responsibility lines only), §4.4, §5; Part B B20.
- **Connects:** lesson 10 (the cascade); lesson 3 and lesson 7 (known people); forward to lesson 12
  (the trial) and lesson 15 (reporting and support).
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 2 (why rape estimates are volatile, the NRC); lesson 10's shared flaw.
- **Neutrality:** responsibility; no "should have"; men included; the gaps in Scottish and US law
  stated.

### 12. Resistance training, and choosing a class (~80 min)

- **Content note** before the trial material (it is about rape), gate G8's lines.
- **Objectives:** describe the EAAA trial's result in absolute and relative terms, what it taught, whom
  it studied and how its effect changed over two years; compare the evidence and arguments for and
  against resistance education, including the disputed trials and the meta-analysis dispute, each in
  its own terms; explain what a page, a short class and stress-exposed practice can each teach, and why
  skills fade; evaluate a self-defence class against the NWMAF checklist and the course's labelled
  checklist, and say whom the evidence does not cover.
- **Core ideas:** **EAAA** (taught once, here, reconciliation 3): first-year women at three Canadian
  universities, four 3-hour units (assess risk from acquaintances, acknowledge danger, act with verbal
  and physical self-defence), against brochures; 451 against 442; completed rape at one year 5.2%
  against 9.8% (relative risk reduction 46.3%, 95% CI 6.8 to 69.1); attempted rape 3.4% against 9.3%;
  both absolute risks always given and the width of the interval said (Senn 2015). **Two years**:
  reductions "between 30% and 64%", effects on completed rape diminishing, boosters suggested; the
  programme also reduced woman-blaming attitudes; the authors' reply to the displacement critique and
  their concession that it is "an empirical question" (Senn 2017). **Later and weaker designs**: the
  real-world quasi-experiment (57.3% at six months); Barata's intimate partner violence substudy
  (54.4%, small and wide); IDEA3 under trial (C §4.2). **The CDC's 2016 package**:
  empowerment-based training has "greater research and theoretical support than approaches focused
  primarily on physical self-defense training" (labelled the 2016 document). **Beyond the campus**:
  the Nairobi cohort and cluster trial with the provider's instructors as interviewers; the independent
  2024 trial's null (OR 1.21, CI 0.40 to 5.21) and the Expression of Concern on its protocol; Malawi
  and South Sudan as provider-run implementation trials (C §4.3). **The dispute**: Kettrey 2023 (80
  campus studies; significant but small, g = 0.15; risk-reduction framing associated with less
  favourable outcomes); the empowerment self-defence authors' reply and Kettrey's answer, by title only
  (planned around); Hollander 2014 (non-randomised) and her reviews' claim, attributed. **The value
  question** (contested question 10): where prevention effort should go, stated as Senn, Hollander and
  the CDC 2016 package state it, and as the critics do (the burden on women; displacement), joined to
  lesson 6's Vera-Gray and Kelly; described, not resolved. **The gap, said out loud**: no controlled
  evidence on classes for men, for general assault or for robbery, which is where most men's risk lies
  (C §7, open concern 9). **What training can do**: skills practised under realistic pressure hold up
  better (Nieuwenhuys 2011), judgement is hard to train (2015), gains fade (ICAT attitudes; EAAA's
  second year); OSHA's "should include a hands-on component"; the UConn programme's twelve-hour
  minimum as one department's standard. **Choosing**: the NWMAF checklist in its words (awareness,
  assertiveness, verbal and physical skills; acquaintances and partners as well as strangers; no blame
  of survivors; students decide their own participation); the course's checklist, labelled as the
  course's inference (C §7); commercial "how to choose" pages are marketing, not evidence.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why pressure-tested practice transfers to pressure (Nieuwenhuys'
  account) and why judgement does not follow as easily; why a relative risk reduction and an absolute
  difference tell different stories (Logic and Argument lesson 9).

### 13. The law on using force (~90 min; seam: split at "Stand your ground" if the draft passes 100)

- **Objectives:** state the ideas the law of self-defence shares in England and Wales, Scotland and the
  US (necessity, proportion, an imminent threat and the defender's belief), as general information;
  compare how England and Wales, Scotland, New York, Florida and Texas treat the chance to escape, and
  explain why escape comes first in practice everywhere; identify when force stops being
  self-defence (the aggressor, revenge, force after the threat has ended); compare the arguments for
  stand-your-ground laws and for a duty to retreat in their proponents' words, and state what RAND's
  review says about homicide.
- **Core ideas:** the label (decision 9), at the first legal sentence and again at each jurisdiction's
  table. **The shared core**: Palmer as quoted by the CPS ("may do, but only do, what is reasonably
  necessary"); MPC §3.04(1) "immediately necessary" (gate G11); s.76(3) (the circumstances as D
  believed them to be); s.76(7) and Lord Morris as quoted (not weighing "to a nicety"); CLA 1967 s.3
  (force to prevent crime or to arrest); the CPS's two questions and the objective element (D §2.1 to
  §2.3, §4.1, §6). **Belief**: England and Wales accept an honest belief even if unreasonable
  (s.76(4)), not a drunken one (s.76(5)); Scotland requires reasonable grounds and an "objective
  background" (Jury Manual; Leverick on *Lieser*); New York and Florida say "reasonably believes"; the
  MPC's subjective test and its caveat (D §2.1, §3.1, §4.1 to §4.3). **Imminence**: no rule that you
  must wait to be struck (CPS, citing *Deana*); Scotland's assault "started or be on the point of
  starting"; the crime.scot illustration ("if someone threatens to batter you tomorrow") as an
  illustration only (D §3.1). **Escape**: England and Wales, a factor, not a duty (s.76(6A)); Scotland,
  violence as "a last resort" and a safe escape "is the course he should have taken", except in defence
  of another; New York, retreat before deadly force outside your dwelling "with complete personal
  safety"; Florida, no duty for someone lawfully present and not engaged in criminal activity; Texas,
  the fact-finder "may not consider" a failure to retreat; the NCSL's "at least 31 states" and its
  cautions; teach the categories, not a count (D §4.5). **Escape first in practice everywhere**: even
  NRA Women's page: "if you are able to leave a situation without having to use force, I highly
  recommend you do so" (D §5.1, §6). **When it stops being self-defence**: the aggressor (*Balogun* as
  quoted; New York's withdrawal rule; Florida §776.041); revenge (Scotland's "revenge, retaliation or
  anger"; the CPS on *Rashford*); cruel excess (Scotland); force after the threat has ended. **Stand
  your ground** (contested question 13): for, in the proponents' words (Deering, NRA Women; NRA-ILA;
  RAND's neutral statement of the deterrence mechanism); for a duty to retreat (Giffords' two
  sentences; the race argument attributed, no figure, decision 8); the evidence (RAND's grading scale
  and "supportive evidence that stand-your-ground laws increase total and firearm homicides", attributed
  to RAND, with its case-law caveat and "inconclusive" for defensive gun use); what SYG does not change
  (a reasonable belief of imminent death or great bodily harm is still required). **Northern Ireland**:
  different again (decision 2).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why the law judges on the defender's belief and allows for the moment
  (s.76(7), Lord Morris: "a person defending himself cannot weigh to a nicety"); why the proponents of
  SYG expect deterrence and why RAND's reviewers find homicide effects (the mechanism as RAND states it,
  and no more).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: in a queue outside a takeaway, a man is shoved and the other man
  lifts a bottle. The same facts walked through England and Wales, Scotland, New York and Florida: which
  questions each asks (necessity, belief, imminence, escape), set out as a table, with no verdict and
  the label at the top. (b) wrinkle: the attacker drops the bottle and runs; the defender chases him
  and hits him. Force after the threat has ended; the aggressor and revenge rules; why the same person
  can be the defender at 11.02 and not at 11.03 in every jurisdiction read.
- **Misconceptions:** "you must wait to be hit first"; "the UK has a duty to retreat" (England and Wales
  no; Scotland in effect yes); "stand your ground means you can use deadly force whenever you feel
  threatened"; "a drunken mistake still counts" (not in England and Wales); "the US has one law of
  self-defence"; "the Model Penal Code is the law".
- **Practice:** a factor table: three scenarios (drafter-written), four jurisdictions, and for each the
  questions a court would ask, never a verdict (decision 9); find the misconception in five statements;
  write, for each side of the stand-your-ground argument, the one sentence its proponents would most
  want read.
- **Images:** a table, not a chart: the escape rule in five jurisdictions.
- **Sources:** Part D §2.1 to §2.3, §3.1, §4.1 to §4.5, §5.1, §6, §7; gates G10 to G12, G15.
- **Connects:** lesson 10 (the law requires neither compliance nor resistance); lesson 8 (leaving is
  always allowed, now in the law's terms); First Aid lesson 1 (the law as general information).
  Forward to lesson 14 (the home) and to Introduction to Law (Term 12).
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 10's stress response (why the law does not expect fine judgement);
  lesson 2 (RAND's grading as another kind of evidence label).
- **Neutrality:** read by F as a gun-rights reader and a gun-control reader (decision 15).

### 14. The home, property, and carrying something for protection (~90 min; seam: split at "Carrying something" if the draft passes 100)

- **Objectives:** explain the householder rule in England and Wales, including what *R v Ray* settled
  and where the rule stops, and compare it with castle doctrine in the US and with Scotland, as general
  information; state when, if ever, force may be used to protect property in each jurisdiction read;
  explain why carrying anything "for self-defence" is an offence in England and Wales, why pepper
  spray is a prohibited weapon in Great Britain, and how three US states regulate sprays; compare what
  the evidence says about defensive gun use and about a gun in the home, framed on the National
  Research Council's statement, each side in its own words.
- **Core ideas:** the label (decision 9) at each legal section. **The householder rule**: s.76(5A),
  "grossly disproportionate"; the four conditions of s.76(8A) (self-defence, not property; in a
  building that is a dwelling; not a trespasser; believed the other person a trespasser), vehicles and
  vessels, live-above-the-shop premises; *R v Ray* as the CPS summarises it (force short of grossly
  disproportionate "was not ... of itself the use of reasonable force"); not the garden. **The
  leaflet**: "Wherever possible you should call the police"; something to hand as a weapon is covered;
  "the more extreme the circumstances and the fear felt, the more force you can lawfully use"; the
  unconscious intruder kicked repeatedly; no need to wait to be attacked at home; chasing; traps; "the
  police have a duty to investigate"; "very few householders have ever been prosecuted" with no number
  supplied (D §2.4). **Scotland**: no householder rule; danger must be "personal danger, not material
  loss" (D §3.1). **US castle doctrine**: MPC §3.04(2)(b) no retreat from a dwelling; New York's
  dwelling exception; Florida's presumption for forcible entry into a dwelling, residence or occupied
  vehicle; Texas §9.32 (D §4). **Property**: England and Wales, "disproportionate force to protect
  property is still unlawful"; Scotland, no property defence; MPC §3.06(3)(d)'s narrow cases; Texas
  §9.42 as the exception readers may have heard of (D §6, §7). **Carrying something**: the Prevention of
  Crime Act 1953's third limb (an ordinary object carried to hit someone if attacked is an offensive
  weapon because of the intention); the CPS on *Evans v Hughes* as quoted ("a constant or enduring
  threat (even where genuine) does not amount to self-defence or a reasonable excuse"); blades (s.139,
  folding pocketknife under 3 inches; a lock-knife is not one; the work, religious and national costume
  defences); Scotland's ss.47 and 49 in the same shape; **pepper spray** a prohibited weapon under the
  Firearms Act 1968 s.5(1)(b) in England, Wales and Scotland, possession itself the offence (gate G13);
  Northern Ireland different again (gate G15); **US sprays**: California (who may not; 2.5 ounces; use
  except in self-defence an offence), New York (use tied to the article 35 force rules; who may sell),
  Michigan (18% OC; use tied to the force rules); the TSA's checked-bag rule; the pattern: lawful to
  carry, judged like any other force when used; no effectiveness figure, no product (decision 10).
  **Guns for protection**, one contested block (contested questions 14 and 15), framed on the NRC
  ("Defensive use of guns by crime victims is a common occurrence, although the exact number remains
  disputed"; NCVS 60,000 to 120,000; "up to 2.5 million"; why each is hard to interpret); Kleck and
  Gertz's estimate and their case against the NCVS; Hemenway's rare-event argument and his burglary
  plausibility check; Kleck and Gertz's reply, argument only (no accusation quoted); RAND's range
  (a little more than 100,000 to 4.7 million); Hemenway and Solnick's NCVS figures (0.9%; 4.2% and 4.1%
  injured); English (gate G18); what would settle it. **A gun in the home**: Anglemyer 2014 (suicide OR
  3.24; homicide victimisation OR 2.00; observational; misclassification possible); the NRC's lower
  injury among gun-using victims and its "could cancel or outweigh"; Kleck and Gertz's "24% claim to have
  fired"; the confounding critique only after gate G17. **The crisis callout** (decision 4), placed where
  suicide first appears. **A domestic abuse victim's gun** (contested question 16): Campbell's exact
  finding, the Hotline's position as the Hotline's, the proponents' statement from gate G16 or the
  general-level fallback; no verdict.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why the householder threshold differs (Parliament's choice, s.76(8A) to
  (8F) and s.76(9)'s statement that the rest only clarifies the existing law); why the intention limb
  catches ordinary objects (the statute's definition); why defensive gun use estimates differ twentyfold
  (question wording, rare events, false positives: each side's account).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: at 2am a woman in a terraced house in England hears someone coming
  through the kitchen window. The lesson sets out what the leaflet says first (call the police), then
  the questions the householder rule asks, and beside it how Scotland and New York would frame the same
  night, with no verdict. (b) wrinkle: after being followed home twice, a man in England decides to carry
  a heavy torch "just in case", and his cousin in Michigan carries pepper spray. The same intention,
  two legal results: why the torch can be an offensive weapon in England and Wales and why the spray in
  Michigan is lawful to carry but judged by the force rules when used; what lesson 6's lawful options
  were. Then the gun block's worked comparison: the NCVS and Kleck and Gertz estimates side by side, what
  each question asked and whom.
- **Misconceptions:** "householders can use any force that isn't grossly disproportionate" (*Ray*); "the
  householder rule covers the garden"; "you can shoot someone for stealing" (not under the MPC, in
  England or in Scotland; Texas §9.42 is narrow); "pepper spray is legal in the UK if it's for
  self-defence"; "carrying something ordinary for protection is fine"; "defensive gun use is rare" and
  "defensive gun use is common" each stated as settled; "using a household object makes it worse" (the
  leaflet says no).
- **Practice:** for six items (an umbrella, a lock-knife, a folding pocketknife under 3 inches for work,
  a personal alarm, pepper spray, a heavy torch carried "in case"), write which rule of England and
  Wales law is engaged and why, as general information; a factor table for two home scenarios across
  three jurisdictions; for the gun block, write each side's strongest sentence in its own words.
- **Images:** none; tables for the home and carrying rules.
- **Sources:** Part D §2.1, §2.3 to §2.6, §3.1, §4, §5.2 to §5.4, §6, §7, §8.4; Part B B27, B28;
  reconciliation 6; gates G10 to G18.
- **Connects:** lesson 13; lesson 5 (don't go in; call the police); lesson 7 (the abuser's gun); lesson
  6 (the State Department's caution about sprays). Forward to lesson 15 (the investigation after force)

### 15. Afterwards (~75 min)

  course does not judge. **Calling**: GOV.UK (999 for a crime in progress or someone in immediate

  postcode); the Silent Solution poster (listen; cough or tap; press 55 if prompted, gate G7); 911.gov

  reported to police; a violent crime; usually within 2 years; free to apply; £1,000 to £250,000; the

  Wales; in the US, state compensation programmes whose rules vary and were not read (D §8.3). **What

  physically safe" gate); OVC's directory. **After force**: "the police have a duty to investigate"; a
  forensic examination or an account may be needed (CPS/NPCC); Florida's immunity from arrest and
  prosecution (§776.032) and the NCSL's civil immunity count as the US contrast (D §2.4, §4.2, §4.5).
  **Rights if questioned in England and Wales**: free legal advice, telling someone where you are,
  medical help, the caution in full (GOV.UK); "get a lawyer's advice" and nothing more; the US after
  gate G19 (decision 9). **The weeks after**: repeat risk is highest soon after (lessons 3 and 5): the

  Mental Fitness lesson 7 (most people stay roughly steady; some struggle and recover), pointed to and

- **Mechanism to explain:** why compensation depends on reporting and support does not (each scheme's

- **Misconceptions:** "if you don't report, you can't get help" (Victim Support); "reporting doesn't

- **Practice:** complete your card (decision 1: private, kept where it is safe for you): the emergency

- **Sources:** Part D §2.4, §4.2, §4.5, §8.1 to §8.4; Part A US-1, UK-1 (reporting), RV-1; First Aid

## Neutrality notes

A sensitive domain several times over: crime, gender and family, race, law, and guns. **Every lesson
gets the neutrality audit.** The failures to guard against are **victim-blaming, false certainty and
silent picking**. The auditor checks, in every lesson: that no difference between jurisdictions is
resolved by preference (decision 2); that every piece of advice carries its honest label and nothing
untested is presented as tested (decision 5); that no scenario implies someone should have done
otherwise (decision 7); and that no mechanism was added from general knowledge (decision 11).

- **The fear paradox** (lesson 1). Contested empirical, four positions, none picked.
- **Crime trends in the US since 2020** (lesson 2). Politically charged; how to read, no conclusion;
  Gallup's partisan symmetry for both parties.
- **Who harms whom** (lesson 3). Established within each source; the answer depends on crime, sex and
  measure; taught as method.
- **Offender race** (lessons 2, 3). Not used (decision 7). No threatening person in any example is
  described by race, ethnicity, religion, class, dress or accent.
- **Gait and awareness; displacement; alarms** (lessons 4, 5). Contested empirical; alarm funding
  stated without insinuation.
- **Safety advice aimed at women** (lessons 1, 6, 12). Partly empirical, partly value: Vera-Gray and
  Kelly's critique stated as mainstream research in its own terms; the defence in its own; the evidence
  separating restriction from skills; the burden question described. The one value commitment
  (responsibility lies with the offender) is shared by every position and is the only one the course
  makes.
- **Domestic abuse** (lesson 7). The services' framing (the reader decides); men as victims; no claim
  about why people stay; the victim's gun deferred to lesson 14.
- **De-escalation and policing** (lessons 8, 9). ICAT reported as evidence about police training, not
  as a position on policing; Right To Be's stance on calling police attributed beside the official
  position; the course adopts neither.
- **Comply or resist** (lesson 10). Both sides at full strength; the shared flaw; the narrow consensus;
  nobody chose wrongly.
- **Sexual assault** (lessons 11, 12). Responsibility; the freeze first; men included; the resistance
  education value question described, not resolved; the provider-run trials described without
  implying bad faith; the letters not characterised.
- **Stand your ground** (lesson 13). Value question plus RAND's grading attributed to RAND with the
  deterrence rationale and RAND's caveat; the proponents' term with the opponents' named; the race

- **Guns for protection** (lesson 14). NRC-framed; Kleck and Gertz, Hemenway, RAND, Anglemyer each in
  their own terms; read by F as a gun-rights reader and as a gun-control reader; the crisis callout
