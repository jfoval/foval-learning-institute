  # --- Fear, and setting it beside an ordinary risk (L1) ---
  - q: >-
      After a mugging two streets away, a homeowners' association email in Fresno, California, sets
      the US death rates for road crashes and for homicide side by side. It then says the smaller
      number doesn't make fear of violence foolish, because another person chooses violence and it
      carries a dread a crash doesn't. On lesson 1's account, is that comparison fair or
      dismissive, and why?
    options:
      - Dismissive, since setting any crime beside an accident tells readers their fear is wrong
      - Fair, since it gives the sizes and then says why the size isn't the whole of the fear
      - Fair, since road deaths outnumber killings, which shows readers should fear crime less
      - Dismissive, since only the numbers belong in it, and dread and blame are just opinions
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Lesson 1 calls a careful comparison informative: here is the size of this risk beside others
      you accept every day, and here is why the size isn't the whole of it. The email does
      both. Warr passes on Covello, von Winterfeldt and Slovic's advice to "Avoid comparisons of
      risk that may appear to the audience to be noncomparable because of different qualitative
      characteristics", and naming those qualities, another person's choice and the dread, is what
      keeps this one honest. Refusing every such comparison goes further than the lesson, which
      itself sets CDC's 2024 US rates side by side (falls 14.2, road traffic 12.1, homicide 5.9
      deaths per 100,000). Reading the smaller homicide number as a reason to fear less is the
      careless version: Warr calls rationality "an ill-advised notion" for fear, because
      seriousness is each person's own valuation. And stripping out the dread would remove the
      very qualities his caution is about.

  # --- Reading a crime number (L2) ---
  - q: >-
      A charity's annual report quotes a US figure whose source line has been cut off. It is a rate
      of violent victimizations per 1,000 people aged 12 and over for one year, and it includes
      crimes nobody reported to the police. Which source is it from, and what does it count?
    options:
      - The FBI's police data, counting offences known to the police per 1,000 of all residents
      - The US victim survey, counting the share of people who were victims at least once
      - The US victim survey, counting times people were victims, so two robberies count twice
      - The FBI's police data, counting incidents, each of which may have several victims
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Only a victim survey reaches crime nobody reported, and people aged 12 and over is the
      population of the National Crime Victimization Survey, run by the Bureau of Justice
      Statistics. Its 2024 bulletin defines victimizations as "the total number of times that
      persons or households were victims", so a person robbed twice counts twice. The share of
      people who were victims at least once is prevalence, a different number the same bulletin
      gives separately: 1.45% of people 12 and over in 2024, against a rate of 23.3 violent
      victimizations per 1,000. The FBI counts offences known to police, per 100,000 inhabitants,
      so neither of the police options can include crime nobody reported, whether it counts
      offences or incidents.
  - q: >-
      In the year a US city's police department moves from the FBI's old Summary Reporting System
      to NIBRS, its recorded aggravated assaults rise sharply, while its recorded robberies and
      homicides barely move. A council member calls it an assault wave. What does lesson 2's
      account of the two systems suggest?
    options:
      - It may be less than it looks, since NIBRS logs assaults the old rule left out of the count
      - It's likely real, since the old system logged every offence in an incident, as NIBRS does
      - It's likely real, since a police record is a count of crimes, where a survey is an estimate
      - It may be larger still, since NIBRS counts only the one most serious offence per incident
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The old system's hierarchy rule, in the FBI's words, meant "only the one most serious offense
      within a criminal incident is counted", so an assault in the course of a robbery went into
      the count as the robbery alone. NIBRS records up to ten offences per incident. A switch can
      therefore add assaults to the count with no more attacks, while robberies and homicides,
      which ranked above assault, stay where they were. The two options about the rules have the
      systems the wrong way round. Treating a police record as the real number is the
      misunderstanding the UK's statistics regulator names: a police count is a precise count of
      what reached the police and was written down, under whatever counting rules applied. None of
      this shows assaults didn't rise; it means this rise can't be read as one until years counted
      the same way are compared.

  # --- Who is harmed, by whom, where and when (L3) ---
  - q: >-
      A speaker at a freshers' welcome talk for women students at a college in Coventry says, "The
      biggest danger to you is violence from a stranger in the street." Against the England and Wales crime survey's
      figures for the year ending March 2024, which correction fits what the survey measured?
    options:
      - It's right, since stranger violence was the commonest harm the survey measured for women
      - It's right, since women were more often victims of violence from strangers than men were
      - It can't be checked, since the survey doesn't split violence by who the offender was
      - For women, stranger violence was rarer than domestic abuse, stalking or harassment
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      ONS's article on violent crime for the year ending March 2024 found 0.4% of women aged 16 and
      over had been victims of violence from a stranger, against 6.6% for domestic abuse, 4.0% for
      stalking, 3.4% for sexual assault and 11.2% for harassment, each a percentage of adults in
      households in England and Wales. So stranger violence was the rarest of these for women, not
      the commonest. It fell more on men: "a higher proportion of men were a victim of violence
      with or without injury where the perpetrator was a stranger (1%) than women (0.4%)". The
      survey does split violence by the offender's relationship to the victim, which is how those
      figures exist. None of this makes a woman's unease in the street a mistake: harassment, the
      11.2%, is common. It means the street is not where most serious harm to women happens.
  - q: >-
      Two e-scooters, one of them a man's way to work, are taken overnight from the shared hallway
      of a block of flats in Portsmouth. Whoever took them seems to have come in by the street door,
      whose keypad code hasn't changed in years and is known to past tenants and delivery drivers.
      On lesson 3's evidence, which change is most worth making, and when?
    options:
      - Change the code this week, since repeats tend to come soon and use what a first crime showed
      - Change the door code in a few months, once whoever did it has probably moved on somewhere else
      - Tighten everything in the block this week, since a first crime says nothing about the next one
      - Nothing unless it happens again, since one theft alone probably says little about the next
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Pease's 1998 review for the Home Office concluded that "victimisation is the best single
      predictor of victimisation", that "when victimisation recurs it tends to do so quickly", and
      that "offenders take later advantage of opportunities which the first offence throws up". So
      the change most worth making closes the particular opening the thief used, the code, and
      soon. Waiting months runs against the finding that repeats come quickly, and waiting for a
      second theft is the "lightning doesn't strike twice" belief the research corrects. A general
      tightening misses Pease's point: the first crime says a good deal about the next one, namely
      which opening it showed. In this course's labels, acting quickly is sensible, untested: it
      found no trial of changing a code that week against changing it later. And none of it moves
      the blame from the person who took the scooters.

  # --- Opportunity and attention (L4) ---
  - q: >-
      Suppose a team films two dozen volunteers walking along a corridor, and each student in a
      large class rates how easy every walker would be to rob. The students largely agree, and the walkers they rate
      easiest share a short, uneven stride. A newspaper reports it as "Your walk tells muggers
      you're an easy target." On lesson 4's account, what did the study measure?
    options:
      - Who would be robbed, since raters who largely agree with each other are likely to be right
      - How muggers pick people in life, since students and muggers read a walk in the same way
      - Who had been robbed before, since an uneven stride is a known sign of harm in the past
      - How easy each walker looked to rob to people asked to judge it, not who gets robbed
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      This is the design of the rating studies lesson 4 read: people asked to judge ease of attack,
      and nothing checked against real lives. Gunns and colleagues (2002) said of their own point-light
      study that raters judged ease of attack without attempting any assault, that showing the
      raters accurate would need showing the walkers rated easiest actually were easiest to
      attack, and that whether anyone attends to such cues without being asked is unknown. Raters
      agreeing with one another shows a shared judgement, not an accurate one. How offenders
      choose in life is the row the lesson marks "not shown". And the imagined study compared the
      ratings with nobody's history: where Fulham and colleagues (2020) did, walking style went with
      past victimisation only among walkers unaware they were filmed. The headline is a claim
      about attacks, and no attack was counted.
  - q: >-
      A running club in Salford asks members to report anything worrying on its canal towpath
      route, and the organiser wants to keep only the reports that describe a warning sign in
      lesson 4's sense. Which report does that?
    options:
      - A man on the bridge with a look that made several runners feel he was up to something
      - Two men by the lock whose clothes suggested they weren't there to run or for a walk
      - A cyclist who kept pace beside a runner and stayed there after she asked him to go on
      - A man on a bench whose scruffy look and manner seemed wrong to the runners who passed
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The course's rule is that a warning sign is something a person does, never how they look,
      and its list includes someone who carries on after you've said no, or who keeps coming
      closer. The cyclist stayed beside the runner after she asked him to go, which is persisting
      after a no. A look that felt like trouble, clothes that didn't fit the place, and a scruffy
      look and manner are all descriptions of how people seemed to the runners, not of anything
      they did. The rule is this course's own argument, built on Cohen and Felson's turn away from
      "the characteristics of offenders" to "the circumstances", and on the point that a feeling
      set off by how someone looks can carry prejudice as easily as pattern. In the course's
      labels it is sensible, untested.
