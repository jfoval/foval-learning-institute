This course has taught one thing again and again: most of what lowers the odds is decided in
advance, on an ordinary day, and not in the moment. This project turns the course into a plan you
keep for your own household. It has six parts: a card of numbers, an audit of your home, two plans
for getting about, decisions made in advance, three short write-ups from scenarios the course
supplies, and a decision about a class.

It takes about six hours of your own time, spread over two or three weeks: half an hour for the
card, which you will already have if you did lesson 16's exercise, three quarters of an hour for the
audit, an hour for the two plans, three quarters of an hour for the decisions, about 45 minutes for
each write-up, because each means going back to two or three lessons, and three quarters of an hour
for the class decision.

:::callout Before you rely on this
This course is education, not legal advice, and not a self-defence class. If you're in danger now, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US. On a 999 call from a mobile, if you can't speak, cough or tap if you can and press 55 when you're prompted; in the US you can text 911 where that's available, but call if you can. If a partner, ex-partner or family member is frightening you, the lesson on domestic abuse and stalking starts with the helplines. Responsibility for a crime lies with the person who commits it: precautions change the odds, not the blame. The law differs between England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

## Three rules for every part

**It's private.** Nothing here is submitted anywhere, and nothing should carry your name or address
if it could be seen by anyone you have not chosen. Keep it on paper, or on a device only you can get
at. **If you live with someone who frightens you, skip any part that could be found**, keep nothing
on a phone or computer they can reach, and do not agree a check-in that would tell them where you
go. That is lesson 7's rule, and the lesson "Domestic abuse and stalking" says what the services
offer instead: its helplines can help you plan by phone, on this course's reading from a device the
person cannot get at.

**Nothing is tested in the world.** Do not call or text any emergency number, helpline or police line
to practise; say anything you want to rehearse aloud to yourself or to a friend. Do not force a lock,
door or window to see if it holds. Do not walk a route at night, or on the phone, to see what
happens. Do not follow or watch anyone, and do not look into anyone else's phone or accounts.

**No technique, and no legal conclusions about yourself.** Nowhere in this project do you write a
strike, a hold or an escape as an instruction, and nowhere do you decide whether something you might
do would be lawful. The legal write-up asks which questions a court would ask, labelled as general
information, which is how lesson 13, "The law on using force", taught it.

## Which country

Do the whole project for where you live. The course covered England and Wales, Scotland, and
Northern Ireland where it read something; in the US it covered 911 and the federal services, and,
for the law of force, New York, Florida and Texas, with the Model Penal Code, which is a model and
not law anywhere by itself. If you live somewhere the course did not cover, or in another US state, use the
numbers you can find on your own government's and services' pages, and in the legal write-up say
"not covered by what this course read" wherever that's true.

## What you keep

Six things, in one folder or one notebook:

1. The card.
2. The home audit, priced and labelled, and a line saying you said the doorstep script aloud.
3. Two plans, one for a regular trip and one for an occasional one, each step labelled, and one check-in agreed with a real person.
4. Three decisions made in advance, one sentence each, with a source.
5. Three write-ups from the scenarios below: one on talking someone down, one on stepping in, one on
   a legal question.
6. A class decision, with its reasons.

## Part 1: the card

The first part of the exercise in lesson 16, "Afterwards". For your country, or your nation of the
UK:

1. The emergency number, and how to make the call if you cannot speak: for the UK, the press-55 step
   on a mobile and what lesson 1 said about landlines; for the US, calling 911 and texting where
   that's available.
2. Your local non-emergency number: 101 in the UK; in the US, your own police department's number,
   looked up on its website.
3. The victims' support line for where you live, with its hours, and any condition it sets, such as
   VictimConnect's that you're physically safe.
4. From the lesson "Domestic abuse and stalking", the lines for your nation or country, including
   one for women and one for men.
5. Your bank's fraud number, from its app or website, never from a message or a call.

## Part 2: the home audit

From lesson 5, "Home, the door and the doorstep". Look round your home, without testing anything by
force, and write what you have of the four devices the WIDE study compared: window locks, an indoor
light on a timer, double or deadlocks on each outside door, and external lights on a sensor. Then
list the additions worth making, cheapest first, with a rough price for each, and give each the label lesson 5
gave it. Finish with the doorstep script: what you'd say, word for word, to a caller who wants to
come in, ending with the call-back on a number you already hold. Say it aloud once, to yourself or
a willing friend, and note that you did.

## Part 3: two plans

From lesson 6, "Out and away: streets, travel and meeting people". One regular trip, such as a
late shift home or a weekly class, and one occasional event: a trip, a night out, or a first meeting
with someone from online. Each plan has four to six steps. Each step carries its label, from the
four the course uses: tested; sensible, untested; contested; practitioner opinion. And one of the
two has a check-in, agreed with a real person: who, what time, what the message says, what they do
if it does not come, and what you'll send if the plan changes.

## Part 4: decisions made in advance

Three sentences, each with the source it rests on and one line on what that source did and did not
measure:

1. **When you leave.** From lesson 8, "Talking it down": the signals that a conversation has stopped
   being one you should stay in.
2. **The property case.** From lesson 10, "What fear does to you, and the robbery decision", in the
   form that lesson used: "If someone shows a weapon, wants my things, and I can't get away, I will
   ...".
3. **When you'd call as a bystander.** From lesson 9, "Stepping in for someone else": what would make
   you call 999 or 911, and your first step where nobody is in immediate danger. Both of the
   positions that lesson set out are defensible; say which one you follow, and why.

## Part 5: three write-ups

The legal write-up is general information, not legal advice. The law differs by state, and between
England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland.

Choose three of the eight scenarios below: one from A to C, one from D to F, and one from G or H.
Each write-up is 200 to 400 words. For A to F, set out the options in the course's order (avoid,
leave, talk, comply or resist, get away, with leaving open at every step), say what you choose, and
why, and give each step its label and its source lesson. For G or H, write the questions a court
would ask in the jurisdiction you chose, and never a verdict.

None of these scenarios asks what anyone should have done. Each is written forward: what are the
options now? Where a scenario makes leaving hard, that's on purpose, because it's where talking or
stepping in becomes the question at all.

After writing each one, open the checkpoint under it and compare.

### A. The kiosk

You work alone at night in the kiosk of a petrol station in Stevenage. A customer's card has been
declined three times. He starts shouting that your machine is rigged, and slams his hand flat on the
counter. The counter is between you, and the only door is behind him. You cannot leave the till.

:::checkpoint What a sound write-up of A contains

Why leaving is not the first option here, and that it stays open if the counter stops being between you. Lesson 8's sources, each with its label: one voice, yourself first, space, a short and respectful sentence, agreeing with what is true (the card has been declined three times), a realistic choice, and a limit said calmly as information, not as a threat. That NICE wrote its guidance for health and care staff, so using it at a kiosk is this course's step. Lesson 8's four signals to stop talking. The training your employer has given you, since HSE's page for workers says to use it, and OSHA's list of risk factors includes exchanging money with the public and working alone. Nothing about getting over the counter.
:::

### B. The waiting room

You're a receptionist at a vet's practice in Hilo, Hawaii. A man whose dog has been waiting to be
seen for two hours stands at your desk and tells you, louder each time, that he's going to go
through to the back himself. Your colleague is in surgery and the waiting room is full.

:::checkpoint What a sound write-up of B contains

That you're at work, with people depending on you staying, which is one of the two moments lesson 8 said talking is for. Project BETA's techniques, as practitioner opinion: space, hands visible, an angle rather than square on, one voice, concise sentences, agreeing with the truth (two hours is a long wait) or with the odds (anyone would be upset), a real choice, and a limit said matter-of-factly. What OSHA's workplace guidance is for, and what it is not. When to stop and get help, using lesson 8's signals, and the call if someone is in immediate danger.
:::

### C. The touchline

At your younger child's football match in Livingston, another parent is shouting at your teenage
son, who is refereeing, and has walked onto the pitch towards him. Your younger child is playing, and
you're not leaving without them.

:::checkpoint What a sound write-up of C contains

Why leaving is costly here (the younger child), and so why talking or getting help is the question. Delegate from lesson 9, getting the coach or the organiser, as an option beside talking. Lesson 8's techniques, labelled, and in particular not humiliating the parent and giving him a way to step back without losing face, which Quinn called a face-saving exit. If you and the coach both talk to him at once, Richmond and colleagues' one-voice rule says several voices can confuse and escalate: agree who speaks. Lesson 8's four signals. Getting your son away from him is itself a way of leaving, if he'll come.
:::

### D. The late train

On a late train to Maidstone, three men are taunting a young man sitting alone about his accent,
and one has taken his bag and is holding it out of reach. The young man is staring at the floor.
Nobody else in the carriage has moved.

:::checkpoint What a sound write-up of D contains

The five Ds from lesson 9, each with Right To Be's own description and its label as practitioner opinion: Distract, Delegate (the guard, or someone near you), Document only after assessing your own safety and only if someone is helping, Delay, and Direct with the page's four questions first. The two positions on calling the police, set out fairly, and which one you follow here. That taking the bag may change the picture: whether it is a crime is a legal question, but if anyone is in immediate danger, GOV.UK's rule makes it a 999 call. What Liebst and colleagues' 3.6% does and does not tell you.
:::

### E. The bakery counter

You're queuing in a bakery in Laramie, in the US, when a man holds up a wrench to the woman at the
counter and demands the cash from the till. He has not looked at you. The door is behind you.

:::checkpoint What a sound write-up of E contains

That this is lesson 9's other case: a commercial robbery, where Lindegaard and colleagues found bystanders helped in about a third of events against 90.9% in public assaults, in a setting the abstract describes as having fewer bystanders and more weapons (the course read the abstract only). That a weapon shown and only property wanted is lesson 10's narrow case, where the three official sources it read, London's Met, the Washington, DC police and OSHA's list of training topics for late-night shops, all point towards handing it over, while the wider comply-or-resist question stays contested. The property here is not yours, and nothing in lessons 9 or 10 asks a bystander to step between a weapon and a till. That the door behind you is lesson 1's second rung. 911 when you're safe to call, and Delay afterwards: checking on the woman at the counter. Nothing about tackling him.
:::

### F. The bowling alley car park

In the car park behind a bowling alley in Bridgend, late on a Saturday, two young men have a third
on the ground. One is going through his pockets; the other is kicking him. A few people are
watching from the far side of the car park.

:::checkpoint What a sound write-up of F contains

That someone is in immediate danger, so on GOV.UK's words it's a 999 call, whichever position on police calls you hold. First Aid and CPR lesson 1's point about giving people jobs, and lesson 9's Delegate: point at one person across the car park and ask them to call, or call yourself from where the men cannot hear. Why Direct is the D the page hedges most, with its four questions, and why they're unlikely all to be yes here. Delay: staying with the young man afterwards, and First Aid and CPR for his injuries. From lesson 16: anyone hurt stepping in might be able to claim under the scheme for England, Wales and Scotland for a "justified and exceptional" risk, if the crime is reported to the police, as the scheme requires.
:::

### G. The bus shelter

Choose your jurisdiction from those the course covered. At a bus shelter, a man you do not know says
he's going to "sort you out", raises his fist and steps in close. You push him away with both hands.
He falls backwards and hits his head on the shelter's frame.

:::checkpoint What a sound write-up of G contains

Lesson 13's four ideas, applied as questions and never as a verdict: necessity, imminence (a raised fist stepping in), belief (honest in England and Wales, reasonable in Scotland and the US statutes), and proportion. The escape question in the form your jurisdiction asks it: a factor in England and Wales, close to a condition in Scotland, in New York a duty before deadly force used against deadly force (and whether a push is deadly force at all, which turns on New York's own definition), none in Florida or Texas in the cases lesson 13 set out. The label, general information, not legal advice. What follows from lesson 16: an investigation of the injury, your rights if arrested, which lesson 16 gives for England and Wales only, and a lawyer's advice.
:::

### H. The friend

Choose your jurisdiction from those the course covered. In a pub, a stranger grabs your friend by
the collar and pulls back his fist. You pull the stranger away by his arm, hard, and he stumbles into
a table.

:::checkpoint What a sound write-up of H contains

That the law read in lesson 13 covers defending someone else: section 76's definitions in England and Wales, the third-person wording in New York's and Florida's statutes, and Scotland's model direction, which says the escape question does not arise at all when you're defending someone else, and asks instead whether violence was used only as a last resort. Then the same questions as G: necessity, imminence, belief, proportion. The label, general information, not legal advice. No verdict. Lesson 9's bystander, and the point in lesson 13 that the bystander is inside this law, not outside it.
:::

## Part 6: a class decision

From lesson 12, "Resistance training, and choosing a class". Find one class near you, from its own
description, and score it against both lists that lesson gave: the National Women's Martial Arts
Federation's guide and this course's own nine-item checklist, marking each item met, not met, or
cannot tell. Then decide: take it, look for another, or take none, and give your reason in three or
four sentences. Deciding not to take a class is a complete answer if it's reasoned. If you've been
assaulted, you do not have to do this part at all, and lesson 12 says why.

## If something doesn't fit your household

- **You rent.** In the audit, list what you would ask the landlord for, and price only what you can fit
  and take with you.
- **Nobody to check in with.** Name the service or person you would use (a friend in another town, a
  workplace), or say there is none, and plan the trip so it does not depend on one.
- **No class near you.** Score one online class description instead, and say what it cannot give
  you.
- **No banking app.** Take the fraud number from your bank's website, never from a message or a
  call.

## Rubric

Mark each part yourself, or with someone you trust, at one of three levels.

- **The card.** Full marks: every number right for your country or nation, the silent-call method
  right for your phone, and the local number looked up, not guessed. Partly there: one line missing,
  or hours or a condition left off. Not yet: a number missing from the first two items, or a number
  from another country.
- **The audit.** Full marks: all four devices checked, additions in order of cost, each with lesson
  5's label, and the script said aloud. Partly there: priced but with a label missing or wrong, or
  the script written but not yet said. Not yet: unpriced and unlabelled, or a script that lets the
  caller in before the call-back.
- **The plans.** Full marks: four to six steps each, every step labelled correctly, and a check-in
  with a time, an action and a person. Partly there: steps present but one or two unlabelled, or a
  check-in with no agreed action if the message does not come. Not yet: a step called tested that the
  course called untested, or a check-in nobody agreed to.
- **The decisions.** Full marks: one sentence each, consistent with its source, with what the
  source did and did not measure. Partly there: consistent with the source, but the line on what it
  measured missing. Not yet: a decision the source does not support, or no source.
- **The write-ups.** Full marks: the options in the course's order, labels and lessons named, and
  the legal one as questions in a named jurisdiction. Partly there: the right options with a label
  or a lesson missing, or the legal one with a question missing. Not yet: advice presented as tested
  when it is not, or a legal write-up that gives a verdict.
- **The class.** Full marks: both lists applied, item by item, and a reasoned decision. Partly
  there: one list applied, or reasons that do not use the lists. Not yet: a decision with no reasons,
  or a class chosen on belts or lineage alone.

A part you skipped under the privacy rule, or Part 6 skipped after an assault, counts as complete.
Write "skipped" and nothing else.

The project passes when no part is at "Not yet" and nothing on the list below appears.

The list below is general information, not legal advice. The law differs by state, and between
England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland.

**Any one of these fails the project outright, whatever else is right:**

- a wrong emergency number for your country;
- a plan that relies on carrying pepper spray in Great Britain, or on carrying anything "for
  self-defence" in England and Wales;
- going into a home you think has an intruder in it;
- a physical technique written as an instruction;
- telling someone in an abusive relationship to leave, or to confront the person abusing them, as an
  instruction;
- posting or sharing a recording of someone who was harassed or hurt without their permission,
  which lesson 9's second Document rule forbids;
- a false report to the police, in any form;
- a sentence that puts the responsibility for a crime on its victim;
- a legal conclusion stated as certain about a specific act.

## What this project is not

It is not a self-defence course, a legal opinion, or a promise. A plan lowers some odds and leaves
others where they were, and nothing on paper decides what happens if someone chooses to do harm.
Responsibility for that lies with them. What the plan does is move a few decisions from the worst
possible moment to an ordinary afternoon, which is where this course began.
