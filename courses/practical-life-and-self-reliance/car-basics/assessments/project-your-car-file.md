A car seldom lets you down somewhere convenient. The page on the red light, the jacking points, the
number for the recovery service and the date the test runs out are all easy to find on a quiet
afternoon at a table, and hard to find in a lay-by in the rain. Every lesson in this course ended
with a few lines headed "For your plan". This project gathers them into one private folder for one
car, in eight parts: **the handbook page**, **the checks log**, **the warning-light card**, **the
service page**, **the legal page**, **the breakdown plan**, **the seasonal checklist** and **the
garage page**.

It takes about eight and three quarter hours of your own time, spread over a month, because the
checks log needs a month of dated readings. An hour and a half goes on the handbook page, since it
gathers figures from ten lessons' handbook hunts; about two hours on the checks log over the
four weeks, a first full round taking about an hour and later rounds less (each extra round adds
about half an hour); twenty minutes on the
warning-light card; three quarters of an hour on the service page; an hour on the legal page, most
of it on official pages for your own country; an hour and forty minutes on the breakdown plan, which
has the most parts; half an hour on the seasonal checklist; forty minutes on the garage page; and
twenty minutes to mark it all against the rubric. If you did the lesson exercises as you went, much
of it is already written, and it'll take less.

:::callout Before the car lets you down
This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. If your car fails on a motorway or other fast road in Great Britain, leave at the next exit or services if you can; if you can't, go left into an emergency area or onto the hard shoulder, switch on your hazard lights, get everyone out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and don't go back to the car. If you're stuck in a live lane or can't get out safely, stay in with seat belts and hazard lights on and call 999 and ask for the police. In the US, pull as far onto the right shoulder as you can, switch on your hazard lights and stay in with your seat belt on, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind, in which case get out on the side away from traffic; call 911 in an emergency. Never put any part of your body under a car held up only by a jack, and never touch the orange high-voltage cables on a hybrid or electric car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

Parts 5 to 8 ask what the law says where you drive. This is general information, not legal advice.
The rules differ between England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and they
change.

## Five rules for every part

**It's private.** Nothing here is submitted anywhere. Keep the file on paper, or on a device only
you use. The registration number, the VIN and your address stay out of anything another person could
see, and you don't post a photograph of the car or its documents online. The registration or VIN
goes only into the official services the lessons named, reached by a route you chose yourself:
GOV.UK's MOT history and recall checks, and NHTSA's recall lookup (lessons 21 and 23).

**Nothing is done to the car that a lesson ruled out, and nothing is done for practice.** Every
reading in this file is made the way its lesson's exercise made it, with that exercise's limits. So,
for this project, nobody:

- lifts the car, loosens a wheel nut or takes out the spare;
- changes a wheel, or connects jump leads or a booster pack, to rehearse;
- puts any part of themselves under the car;
- opens a coolant cap or a brake-fluid reservoir;
- touches, unplugs, opens or reaches past anything orange, anything blue on a 48 V mild hybrid, or
  anything labelled high voltage;
- drives the car for any part of it;
- stands at a roadside, on a hard shoulder or in an emergency area for it.

Lessons 13 and 19 teach the jump start and the wheel change on paper for a reason, which lesson 1
gave: a page can't check a jack's footing. Every look under the bonnet starts with lesson 9's
preparation, and on a car that plugs in, at a time when it isn't charging and without unplugging
anything (lesson 14). If the engine has to run for the lights, it runs outdoors, never inside a
garage, even with the door open, which for a detached garage is this course's own precaution
(lesson 10).

**This page teaches no method.** Every instruction in it is a lesson's, and each part names the
lesson. Nothing here adds a step or lifts a condition a lesson set. If a line below seems to ask for
more than its lesson allowed, the lesson wins.

**Nothing is tested in the world.** Don't call 999, 911, a breakdown service, a roadside telephone
or any helpline to see if the number works, and don't book, contact or pay a garage for this
project. Reading a register or an official page is fine; that's how most of Parts 5 and 8 get
written.

**Every line carries its label and its source.** The question is lesson 1's: law, handbook or
advice: whose, and for which car? The labels are lesson 1's too: **Yours**; **Yours, on
conditions**, naming the condition; or **Call someone**. Where a lesson said a line was this
course's own reading, and not a source's, your file says so as well. And every per-car figure is the
maker's for this car, with its page (lesson 1), never a number from another car's handbook or a
website.

## If the car isn't yours

Many people drive a car on a lease, on finance, from an employer, from a hire company, or borrowed.
Lesson 1's line, which is this course's own and not a statement about any agreement, since the
course read none: the agreement and the company decide who arranges servicing, tyres, repairs and
recall work; tell them in writing, and do only the owner's checks without asking. Every look at the
car needs the keeper's agreement, as every exercise said.

You can do the whole project, and reach the top level on every part, this way. Wherever a part asks
what you would arrange, write "the keeper's", who the keeper is, and what you told them in writing and
when. "Told the lease company on 3 March, in writing, that the brake fluid was below MIN" is a
complete line. Readings you can't take because the keeper hasn't agreed are written as "not taken:
keeper not asked yet" or "keeper declined", with the date.

## If you don't have a car

Build the file for a named car whose handbook you can read online, and write at the top "not my car"
and which car it is. Lesson 2 noted that Mazda and Nissan UK publish their handbooks on their own
sites; this course didn't check other makers. Most of the file works from the handbook and official
pages. Where a line needs the car in front of you (the checks log's readings, the start-up check on
the card, where the kit is kept), write the layout, your chosen intervals, and "no car to check", with the date.
That counts as complete, with the reason written.

## Which country

Write at the top of the file where the car is kept and driven: England, Wales, Scotland, Northern
Ireland, or your US state. Lesson 1 asked for this line, because it decides whose law and whose
roadside advice applies. All three get the same eight parts, and they differ here:

| | Great Britain | Northern Ireland | A US state |
|---|---|---|---|
| Road rules to read | The Highway Code on GOV.UK, for England, Scotland and Wales (lesson 1) | Northern Ireland's own Highway Code, on nidirect (lessons 1 and 16) | Your state's official driver handbook (lesson 17) |
| Roadside steps on your plan | Rules 275 to 277 as lessons 16 and 17 teach them | Copied from Northern Ireland's own Code; this course teaches none of them (lessons 16 and 17) | Your state handbook's steps for a disabled vehicle, with the callout's US steps (lesson 17) |
| Law on the car's condition | Road Traffic Act 1988, ss.40A, 41A and 42 (lesson 20) | The 1995 Order, Articles 54, 56 and 58; penalties not read (lesson 20) | Your state's (lesson 22) |
| Periodic test | The MOT (lesson 21) | The DVA test (lesson 21) | Your state's, if it has one (lesson 22) |
| Recall check | GOV.UK (lesson 23) | GOV.UK (lesson 23) | NHTSA (lesson 23) |
| Emergency number | 999 | 999 (lessons 16 and 17) | 911 |

Two rules from the course hold throughout. **Never put another country's roadside steps on your
plan**, and that includes Great Britain's motorway steps on a Northern Ireland plan: lesson 17 found
that Northern Ireland's motorway rule differs from Rule 277. And where the course read nothing for
your place (Scotland's fines and consumer law, Northern Ireland's penalties, a US state outside the
ones lessons 22 and 25 read), write "not read by this course" and the official body to ask.

Eight pages, in one folder or one notebook. In front of them goes lesson 25's table, "The course's

1. The handbook page: the maker's figures for this car, each with its page.

4. The service page: the schedule and what's due next.
5. The legal page: the law, the test and the recall check, for your place.

7. The seasonal checklist.
8. The garage page: the garage, what to ask for, and the dispute route.

## Part 1: the handbook page

From lesson 1, "What a car is made of, and why the handbook comes first", with lines from lessons 2
to 6, 9, 12, 14 and 16. At the top, where the handbook is: the glovebox, or the maker's online
handbook for the exact model and year, with its address (lesson 1).

Then **lesson 1's ten figures**, each with its page number or, for an online handbook, its section
title: the cold tyre pressures front, rear and spare; the engine oil grade; the coolant type; the
service interval; the wheel-nut torque, with its units; the space-saver or sealant kit's speed
limit; where the jack and jacking points are; and the jump-start points. Where the handbook gives
none, write "none given". Don't fill a gap from another car: lesson 1's four handbooks gave three
different torque figures.

Then what the later lessons added, each from the handbook, with its page:

- **What kind of car it is.** Petrol or diesel, and the gearbox type (lesson 2); or, in the
  handbook's own words, which of lesson 4's four kinds of car with a motor it is.
- **The exhaust.** Whether it has a diesel particulate filter, an AdBlue (DEF) tank, or neither, and
  the handbook's oil specification in its own words (lesson 3).
- **The 12 V battery.** Where it is, or where the jump points are if it's hidden (lessons 4 and 5).
- **Brake fluid.** The grade the handbook names, and what the warning on or near the reservoir cap
  names, read with the cap shut (lesson 6).
- **Fuel.** What the handbook says, word for word: the grade, and whether it's "required" or
  "recommended" (lesson 12).
- **The engine bay.** The page with the picture of it, which lesson 9 had you hold while you looked.
- **Towing.** The title and page of the handbook's towing section, for the recovery call (lesson
  16).
- **If it's a hybrid or electric car:** where the handbook shows the high-voltage parts and labels,
  as seen in its pictures or from where you stood, never touched (lessons 4 and 14); the handbook's
  words for preparing the car before the bonnet is opened (lesson 14); and the charging page: the
  connector, the handbook's own charging rules, what it says about extension leads and adapters, and
  the questions to put to an electrician about charging at home (lesson 14). Getting a circuit or
  charge point checked or put in is **Call someone** in both countries (lesson 14).

## Part 2: the checks log and the tyre record

From lessons 7 to 10, with a line from lesson 6. This is the only part that needs the car in front
of you more than once, and every reading is made by its lesson's exercise, with that exercise's
preparation and limits.

**At the top of the log, your intervals.** For each check, write how often you'll do it and whose
advice that is. Lesson 1 set out three bodies' intervals for tyre pressures, and lesson 9 the
Highway Code's weekly advice on fluid levels, which is advice in Great Britain and not a legal rule.
Lesson 7 asked you to put your choice and its source at the top.

**A month of readings.** How many is this project's own requirement, not a lesson's: the log covers
four weeks; the pressure check, the under-bonnet check and the walk-round each appear at least twice,
at the start and at the end, and more often if your interval is shorter; the tread check and the
bounce test at least once. Each entry has the date, and for the under-bonnet check the mileage.

- **Pressures, cold, with a gauge** (lesson 7): the car stood at least three hours, off the road on
  level ground; each tyre's reading, the time, and its difference from the maker's figure. The spare
  only if it is carried inside the car. If a tyre is low, write how much and add the air the next
  time you're at an air line anyway, not as part of this project (lesson 7).
- **Tread at three points, and damage** (lesson 8): at least once in the month, each tyre at three
  points round it and across it, with the gauge or the coin you used; any cut, cord, lump, bulge or
  crack; anything stuck in the tread noted and left where it is.
- **The under-bonnet check** (lesson 9): oil on the dipstick or the dashboard display, by the
  handbook's method; coolant and brake fluid read through the side of each reservoir against MIN and
  MAX, caps shut; screenwash topped up. Oil is added only where lesson 9's conditions all hold.
  Coolant below MIN on a cold engine is a separate job after the round, by lesson 9's conditions,
  never part of it.
- **The walk-round** (lesson 10): lights front and rear, plates, glass and wipers, each marked
  working, dirty or faulty.
- **Once in the month, the bounce test** (lesson 6), off the road, with nobody in or near the car's
  wheels.

**What a reading means is the lesson's, not yours to improvise.** Brake fluid below MIN, or a level
that keeps falling from week to week, is **Call someone** (lessons 6 and 9), and for a level below MIN, Nissan's
handbook, for its own car, says not to drive until the brake system has been checked. A milky or
black, sludgy dipstick is **Call someone** (lesson 9). If anything looks wrong, stop, write it down,
and label it as its lesson did.

**The tyre record**, beside the log (lessons 7 and 8): the tyre size; the cold pressures with where
each came from; your trigger point, worked out and labelled as lesson 7's step 4 did it; each tyre's
date code, read as week and year; the wear pattern across each tread; and your own replacement line,
with whose advice it follows. Lesson 8 set out the positions, from the legal floor with Michelin's
case to the earlier replacement points, and who holds each; your line names one, with its source and that source's interest, as lesson 8 labelled them.

## Part 3: the warning-light card

From lesson 11, "Dashboard warning lights", with lines from lessons 2, 3, 5 and 6. One side of a
card, kept in the glovebox where you can read it in a lay-by (lesson 11). For each light, from the
handbook's warning-light pages: the page number, a sketch of the symbol, its colour, the handbook's
instruction in its own words, and a label.

- **The stop-now group** (lesson 11): oil pressure, coolant temperature, brake and charge; "none"
  where the car has no such light.
- **The charge light**, marked "stop now" or "check soon" from your handbook's words (lesson 5).
  Lesson 5's table showed two makers giving different instructions for the same light on petrol cars
  sold in the same country, which is why your card copies yours and nobody else's.
- **The red brake light and the amber ABS light**, in the handbook's words (lesson 6).
- **The check-engine light, steady and flashing**, or "not stated" (lesson 11); the airbag light;
  the tyre pressure light.
- **For a diesel:** the glow-plug light (lessons 2 and 11), and the DPF and AdBlue lights (lesson 3).
- **The start-up check** (lesson 11): which lights came on and went out when you switched on without
  starting, parked, and any that the handbook says should light and didn't, noted for a garage.

Across the top, one line: stopping "in a safe place" means the steps for your own country on your
breakdown plan (Part 6): the callout's and lessons 16 and 17's in Great Britain, lesson 16's, your
state handbook's and the callout's in the US, and Northern Ireland's own Code's in Northern Ireland.

## Part 4: the service page

From lesson 24, "Choosing a garage, servicing and warranties", and lesson 2, with lines from lessons
3 to 6. Reading the schedule is **Yours**; the servicing beyond the owner's checks is **Call
someone** (lesson 24). If the car is leased, financed or a company car, lesson 24 has you find the
schedule and note who arranges the rest; the other lines are "the keeper's".

- **The schedule** and where it is printed: the handbook, a service booklet or the maker's site.
  Whether it gives a normal and a severe schedule; the handbook's list of severe conditions, word
  for word; and one honest sentence on which list your driving matches, and why (lesson 24).
- **An oil-life monitor**, if the car has one, with the limits the handbook puts round it (lesson
  24).
- **The next three items due**, each with its miles or date, whichever the handbook says comes first
  (lesson 24).
- **The cambelt**: the handbook's interval in miles and years; the date and mileage of the last
  change, or "no record"; and when the next is due, whichever comes first. Or "the handbook gives
  none", with the question to ask at the next service. The due date goes in your calendar, because
  the car won't remind you (lesson 2). Changing it is **Call someone**.
- **Brake fluid**: the handbook's change interval, and the last change if a record shows it (lesson
  6).
- **The 12 V battery**: its age and last test, if known, and a line on your driving pattern from
  lesson 5's week of trips you made anyway.
- **For a diesel with a filter or AdBlue**, lesson 3's line for your routine. The longer run lesson
  3 describes belongs to your ordinary driving; it's never a drive made for this project.
- **For a hybrid or electric car**, the traction battery's warranty terms from the warranty booklet,
  if there is one (lesson 4).
- **Where the records are kept**: the service book, the invoices, and the paperwork for any filter
  sent away for cleaning (lesson 3).

## Part 5: the legal page

This is general information, not legal advice.

From lessons 8 and 20 to 23. Write it for your own place only, from the set below that fits. No line
on this page says what you may lawfully do in your own case; it records which rules apply where you
drive and whom to ask.

**In Great Britain**

- **The law on the car's condition** (lesson 20): the three offences in one line each, with whether
  each carries points; section 48's two conditions, in its own words; and a line pointing to your
  checks log, since, on this course's reading (lesson 20), a dated record like that is the kind of
  thing that bears on "no reasonable cause to suspect". Lesson 20's pound figures are England and
  Wales's; in Scotland, write "not read by this course" beside them.
- **The tread rule** in one sentence, with its source and the date you read it (lesson 8).
- **The test page** (lesson 21): the date the MOT runs out, the earliest date that keeps it by
  GOV.UK's month-minus-a-day rule, any fail, minor problem or advisory from the last two tests from
  the [MOT history service](https://www.gov.uk/check-mot-history), and how to appeal, with the date
  you read it.

**In Northern Ireland**

- **The law on the car's condition** (lesson 20): the Road Traffic (Northern Ireland) Order 1995's
  three articles, and "not read by this course" for their penalties and for whether Northern Ireland
  has a rule like section 48.
- **The tread rule** in Northern Ireland's own regulations, in one sentence (lesson 20).
- **The test page** (lesson 21): the DVA test's due date; what nidirect says about a car that fails,
  which differs from Great Britain's rule; and the appeal route and its time limit, with the date
  you read it. The MOT history service covers Northern Ireland's tests since 2017.

**In a US state**

- **The state page** (lesson 22): your state; whether it inspects private cars for safety, and how
  often; whether your area tests emissions; the tread rule and how it is measured, or "none on the
  page"; the fee, if the page gives one; and the page's name and the date you read it, from a web
  address ending in .gov. If your state has no inspection, write that with the date, and beside it
  the checks from lessons 7 to 10, which are then the only regular ones the car gets (lesson 22).

**Recalls, in every country** (lesson 23). Checking is **Yours**, and it's free. Write the date of
your last check, the service you used ([GOV.UK's recall
check](https://www.gov.uk/check-vehicle-recall) in the UK, [NHTSA's
lookup](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls) in the US), the result word for word, and the dates of the
next two checks, six months apart. If a recall says stop drive or Do Not Drive, the car isn't driven
until it's repaired, and if it isn't yours, the keeper hears from you in writing that day (lesson
23). Add whether your details on the V5C log book or your state registration are current, and, if
you bought tyres in the US, their identification numbers from the invoice and whether they were
registered.

## Part 6: the breakdown plan

This is general information, not legal advice.

From lessons 13 to 19. Everything on this page is for your own country and written for the roads you
use. It's a plan on paper: nothing on it is rehearsed at a roadside, and no job on it is tried for
practice.

**1. The numbers.** The emergency number; your breakdown service's number and your membership or
policy number, if you have cover; and, if you don't, whether a free public service covers roads you
use and how to find a recovery service (lesson 16). In England, the numbers lesson 17 gave for
England's motorways and major A-roads (it read no road operator's page for Scotland or Wales); in
Northern Ireland, what its own Code's page gives; in the US, any assistance number your state's
handbook gives (lesson 17).

**2. The steps, for your country only.** For ordinary roads: in Great Britain, lesson 16's Rules 275
and 276, where to stop and where everyone stands, with which of Rule 276's lines are advice; in
Northern Ireland, where to stop and where everyone stands as its own Code's "Breakdowns and
incidents" page gives them, which lesson 16's exercise has you open, not Great Britain's; in a US state, lesson 16's US advice, with
the callout's exceptions. For motorways and fast
roads, lesson 17's: in Great Britain, the callout's steps as Rule 277 gives them, with each line
marked MUST or should; in Northern Ireland, the motorway steps copied from [its own Highway
Code](https://www.nidirect.gov.uk/articles/breakdowns-and-incidents-274-287), not Great Britain's;
in a US state, your state handbook's steps for a disabled vehicle, and the callout's exceptions (a
shoulder too narrow, or a guardrail to wait behind). Add the one sentence for the operator if you
were stuck in a lane of traffic, with how to tell them where you are (lesson 17).

**3. Your roads.** The ordinary road you use most, where on it to try to stop, and where everyone
would stand (lesson 16), by your own country's steps. The fast road you use most, its exits and services in order, and whether it
has a hard shoulder all the way, or "not known" and a plan for both, which is also lesson 17's
answer in Scotland and Wales.

**4. What the car carries, and where.** A warning triangle, light-coloured or high-visibility
clothing and a torch, and what's missing (lesson 16); the spare, space saver, sealant kit or
run-flats, the jack, the wheel brace, any locking wheel nut key, and a chock or what to use
instead (lessons 18 and 19). The winter kit goes in Part 7.

**5. What to tell a recovery service** (lesson 16): the make and model, whether it is electric or
hybrid, and the handbook's towing section.

**6. The jump page** (lesson 13): the handbook's page, the connection order in its own words, where
the last clamp goes, with your sketch, what it says about taking the leads off, and every condition
it lists. A jump start is **Yours, on conditions**, and lesson 13 gathered them in one place; write
them out. Add your own line on where you'd never do it. For a hybrid or electric car, what its
handbook says about being a donor and about the main battery after a jump.

**7. The wheel page** (lessons 18 and 19): what the car carries and its limits in the handbook's
words; the sealant's expiry date, read from the label without opening anything; the handbook's
wheel-change section and pages; its jack warning, word for word; where the jacking points are; the
torque; the three places on your roads where you would change a wheel and the one where you wouldn't,
each with lesson 19's reason; one line on the law on a space saver where you drive, as lesson 18's
exercise set it (in Northern Ireland, "not read by this course"); and the number to call for a
flat you won't change yourself.

**8. The fire, crash and flood card** (lesson 14): the emergency number for your country; the words
to say, "it's an electric car" or "it's a hybrid"; your handbook's crash steps, if it gives them;
the distance your sources give for keeping a damaged car from buildings, with whose each figure is;
and the rule that nobody fights the fire or goes back to the car. For a petrol or diesel car, write
the card anyway, with "not electric or hybrid" where a line is only for those cars: lesson 14 also
gave the Highway Code's fire advice for any car in Great Britain.

Two cautions travel with the whole page. **Nothing on it happens on a motorway, its hard shoulder or
an emergency area.** No repair, wheel change or jump start, and no warning triangle. In Great
Britain, the Highway Code's Rule 277 advises "DO NOT attempt repairs on your vehicle", and a jump
start there is a repair on this course's reading (lessons 13 and 18); and Rule 276 advises never
using a triangle on a motorway (lesson 16). Elsewhere, Northern Ireland included, the same stops are
this course's reading (lesson 25's table gives it for a repair, wheel change or jump start). On this course's reading the same goes for any other fast road, a lay-by on one included, and a wheel
change or jump start on a US freeway shoulder is a call (lessons 13 and 18). And **a place on your
list is not permission on the day**: lesson 18's conditions are checked at the scene, and darkness,
on this course's reading, tips a close decision towards a call.

## Part 7: the seasonal checklist

From lesson 15, "Winter and summer". Two lists, "Before winter" and "Before summer", for the country
you drive in, and each item labelled with whose it is (law, handbook or advice, and whose), for
which car, and the job label, as lesson 15's exercise set it out. An item that doesn't apply stays
on the list with the reason ("no engine oil: electric"), so a reader can see you considered it.

Add, from the handbook: what it says about running the engine in snow or an enclosed space; the
washer fluid it gives for cold weather; and anything on snow chains or winter tyres for this car.
Then the winter kit: each item, which list it came from, and where it goes in the car. Don't buy
anything for the project (lesson 15).

For the law, this is general information, not legal advice. In Great Britain, Rule 229's two MUST
lines in your own words, and one sentence on Rule 123 and defrosting (lesson 15). In Northern
Ireland, write that this course didn't read Northern Ireland's Code on winter (lesson 15), and the
page on nidirect to read. In a US state, which agency's page to check for winter rules such as
chain controls, and the question to ask it.

Three items lesson 15 labelled, which belong on the winter list in its words: the exhaust pipe
cleared of snow before the engine starts, which is **Yours**; the battery tested before winter if
you follow the advice of AAA, whose clubs sell roadside assistance and battery service (lesson 15),
which is **Call someone**; and exhaust fumes inside the car, which is **Call someone** on this
course's reading. On the summer list: your two hot-car habits from NHTSA's list, in your own
words, and where the reminder item goes in the car.

## Part 8: the garage page

This is general information, not legal advice.

From lesson 24 and lesson 25, "Quotes, estimates, the bill and disputes".

  any time limit, and what lesson 25 says about the car while a bill is disputed where you live.
  Where the course read nothing (Scotland's consumer law, Northern Ireland's consumer advice
  service), write "not read by this course" and whom to ask. In a US state other than California
  and New York, the route is lesson 25's "elsewhere" one, the FTC's.
- **The paperwork**: where every estimate, work order and invoice is kept, from the first day,
  because each route asks for them (lesson 25).

If the car isn't yours, the garage page records who arranges servicing and repairs under the
agreement, and what to do if a fault turned up between services: tell the keeper in writing.

## If something doesn't fit your car

- **It's electric.** Lines for engine oil, the cambelt, the glow plugs and the DPF become "none:
  electric", with the reason. Lesson 4 found coolant, brake fluid, washers, wipers, lights, tyres
  and a 12 V battery still on Nissan's LEAF; your handbook says which of them yours has.
- **It plugs in.** Every look under the bonnet is at a time when it isn't charging, and nobody
  unplugs anything for it (lesson 14).
- **The spare is under the car.** It's left out of the pressure check; nobody goes under a car in
  this course (lesson 7).
- **It carries no spare.** The wheel page lists the sealant kit or run-flats and their limits
  instead (lesson 18).
- **The handbook is lost.** Use the maker's online handbook for the exact model and year (lesson 1),
  and write "none found" where you can't find a figure. A gap is written down, never filled from
  another car's handbook.
- **The dipstick, the screenwash filler or the oil filler sits by the fan**, and your handbook says
  to disconnect the battery before working there: that job is **Call someone** in this course
  (lesson 9).

## Rubric

Mark each part yourself, or with someone you trust, at one of three levels. Wherever a level asks
for a reading, a check or a lookup, a line written "no car to check", "not taken: keeper not asked
yet" or "keeper declined", with its date, meets that level.

- **The handbook page.** Thorough: every figure lesson 1 listed and every line the later lessons
  added that applies to this car, each with its page or section and the maker named, and "none
  given" where the handbook is silent. Sound: lesson 1's ten figures with pages, with some later
  lines missing. Not yet: figures without pages, or any figure taken from another car's handbook or
  a website.
- **The checks log and tyre record.** Thorough: a month of dated readings, each check as often
  as Part 2 asks, your intervals at the top with whose advice each is, and the tyre record complete with the
  date codes and your replacement line and its source. Sound: the month covered, with the tyre
  record or the intervals incomplete. Not yet: fewer than two rounds without a reason written, readings without dates, a
  reading taken in a way its lesson ruled out, or a Call someone finding written as something you'll
  fix.
- **The warning-light card.** Thorough: every light the exercise listed, each with the page, symbol,
  colour, the handbook's own words and a label; the charge light marked from your handbook; the
  start-up check done; the card in the glovebox. Sound: the stop-now group complete, with some of
  the rest missing. Not yet: a stop-now light missing, or an instruction taken from a source other
  than this car's handbook without saying so.
- **The service page.** Thorough: the schedule, normal or severe with the handbook's list and your
  reason, the next three items, the cambelt line (or "none given" and the question), brake fluid,
  the 12 V battery, and where the records are. Sound: the schedule and next items, with the cambelt
  or the severe-use reasoning missing. Not yet: no schedule, or a garage job written as yours to do.
- **The legal page.** Thorough: every line for your own place, each with its source and the date you
  read it; law, handbook and advice told apart; "not read by this course" where that's true; the
  recall check done with its two reminder dates. Sound: the right country's rules and the recall
  check, with dates or sources missing in places. Not yet: another country's rule on the page,
  advice called law, or no recall check.
- **The breakdown plan.** Thorough: all eight items, for your own country only, the numbers right,
  each step with whose it is, and the two cautions kept. Sound: the numbers and your country's steps
  right, with some of the jump, wheel or fire pages incomplete. Not yet: a wrong number, a step from
  another country's page, or any item on the fail list below.
- **The seasonal checklist.** Thorough: both lists, every item with whose it is and a label, the
  handbook's snow and enclosed-space words, the kit and where it lives, the law line for your place,
  and the two hot-car habits. Sound: both lists, with labels or sources missing in places. Not yet:
  a list missing, or a Call someone item written as yours.
- **The garage page.** Thorough: a garage checked on the body's own site by your own route, with the
  date; what you'll ask for; your place's estimate rule; the dispute route with its time limit; and
  where the paperwork lives. Sound: the garage and the dispute route, with the asking list or the
  estimate rule missing. Not yet: a garage checked through its own badge or link, or a dispute route
  for a place other than yours.

A part, or an item in one, that you skipped for safety, because the car isn't yours, or because you
have no car, counts as complete if the reason is written down.

The project passes when no part is at "Not yet" and nothing on the list below appears.

**Any one of these fails the project outright, whatever else is right**, because each is a step a
lesson ruled out, and most are steps a lesson said can hurt someone. Each counts whether it's
written into the file as a step or done for the project:

- any part of anyone under a car held up by its jack, or anything put on or under the jack (lesson
  19);
- a repair, wheel change, jump start or warning triangle on a motorway, its hard shoulder or an
  emergency area, or a wheel change on a US freeway shoulder (lessons 13, 16, 18 and 19);
- another country's roadside steps on your plan, including Great Britain's motorway steps on a
  Northern Ireland plan (lessons 16 and 17);
- a hot coolant cap opened (lessons 9 and 11);
- anything orange, blue on a mild hybrid, or labelled high voltage touched, unplugged, opened or
  worked on (lessons 4 and 14);
- a domestic multi-socket extension lead written into your charging setup as what you'll use (one
  you find there goes on the page as something to stop, with lesson 14's sources);
- a hybrid or electric car as the donor for a jump start, unless its own handbook says it can be one
  (lesson 13);
- a frozen, damaged or leaking battery jumped (lesson 13);
- a DPF or other emission-control part deleted, that is, removed or switched off rather than
  repaired or replaced (lesson 3);
- an airbag disabled while a recall waits (lesson 23);
- an engine run in snow without the exhaust cleared first (lesson 15);
- a child or an animal left unattended in a car, for any length of time (lesson 15).

## What this project is not

It isn't an inspection of your car, a mechanic's qualification or a legal opinion, and it doesn't
make any job on it safe to do. It's the file you'd want to have made before the morning the light
comes on: what the maker says about this car, what you've been checking and when, what the law asks
where you drive, and who you'll call, written down while nothing was wrong.
