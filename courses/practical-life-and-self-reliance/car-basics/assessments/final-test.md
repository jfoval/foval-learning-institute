  # --- The cambelt: miles or years, whichever comes first (L2) ---
  - q: >-
      A courier in Arbroath, Scotland, drives about 40,000 miles a year. In this question's example,
      her handbook says to replace the cambelt every 10 years or 100,000 miles, whichever comes
      first. The service record shows a change 2 years ago at 40,000 miles, and the odometer now
      reads 120,000. The car runs perfectly. When is the next change due?
    options:
      - At 140,000 miles, about six months away at her yearly mileage
      - Now, since the odometer has passed the handbook's 100,000 miles
      - In eight years, when ten years since the last change are up
      - Only once the car shows one of the RAC's five signs of failure
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Count from the last change, not from new: 80,000 miles and 2 years so far. From that change the
      limits are 140,000 miles on the odometer (40,000 plus 100,000) or 10 years, and at about 40,000
      miles a year the miles come first, in about six months (this course's arithmetic, on the
      example's figures). The RAC, a motoring organisation that sells breakdown cover and cambelt
      changes, says the handbook's interval "will be after a specific number of years or miles,
      whichever comes first." Reading 120,000 against 100,000 counts from new and ignores the change
      on the record. Eight years is the years limit, which the miles will beat. And waiting for a sign
      misses the RAC's own caution: after its five signs, and its advice to have a car showing any of
      them checked "as soon as possible by a qualified mechanic", it adds "Many cambelts show no
      obvious symptoms before failure." Working out the date is yours; the change is Call someone.

  - q: >-

      DPF light comes on. The fuel gauge shows about a fifth of a tank, and the handbook says to
      drive at a steady speed for a while. A dual carriageway with a 60 mph limit starts a mile
      away, with a filling station on the way. On the AA's and the RAC's advice, what comes first?
    options:
      - The run now, as ten minutes above 40 mph clears the light whatever the fuel level
      - A forced regeneration at a dealer, since short trips mean a run can't clear it
      - Fill up first, as under about a quarter of a tank can stop it; only then the run
      - Nothing yet, as the car starts its own regeneration once the tank is nearly dry
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The AA says a fuel level "generally less than a quarter of a tank" will "prevent active
      regeneration taking place", and the RAC gives the reason: the car "may avoid DPF regeneration

      about ten minutes above 40 mph, on a road where that is legal and safe, and this handbook allows

      mode, when "Driving at speed alone won't be enough"; a light after short trips is the case the

      light that won't clear is a garage's. Both the AA and the RAC sell breakdown cover and repairs.

  # --- The US TPMS trigger, and the table's floor (L7) ---
  - q: >-
      As general information: a small US car on standard-load passenger tyres has, for this
      question, a placard figure of 26 psi for its rear tyres, cold. A rear tyre is slowly losing
      air. Under FMVSS 138, at or below what pressure must the low-pressure light be on within 20
      minutes?
    options:
      - 19.5 psi, since the trigger is 25 per cent below the placard, whatever the tyre
      - 23 psi, since that is the floor Table 1 of the standard sets for any car's tyre
      - 20 psi, since Table 1's floor for these tyres is higher than 25 per cent under
      - 13 psi, since the standard only requires the light once half the air has gone
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      FMVSS 138 requires the light once a tyre is at or below "the pressure 25 percent below the
      vehicle manufacturer's recommended cold inflation pressure, or the pressure specified in the
      3rd column of Table 1 of this standard for the corresponding type of tire, whichever is higher".
      A quarter off 26 psi is 19.5 psi; Table 1's figure for a standard-load passenger tyre is 20 psi;
      the higher is 20 (this course's arithmetic). The 25 per cent point only wins above about 26.7
      psi on these tyres. 23 psi is Table 1's figure for extra-load tyres, and half the pressure is in
      no part of the standard. It's the latest point the light may come on, not the car's own
      threshold, and the required manual text says the system "is not a substitute for proper tire
      maintenance": the gauge check, cold, is yours.

  # --- Cord showing, and section 48 (L20) ---
  - q: >-
      As general information: near Nantwich, England, police stop a car whose rear tyre has cord
      showing through a cut in its sidewall. The driver says she hadn't looked at the tyres for months
      and didn't know. On the Road Traffic Act 1988, Schedule 2 and section 48 of the Road Traffic
      Offenders Act 1988, which fits?
    options:
      - Section 42, with no points, since the cut is in the sidewall, not in the tread itself
      - Section 41A, with 3 points; s.48 lifts them only if she proves no cause to suspect
      - Section 41A, but s.48 is a defence that clears her if she says she didn't know
      - Section 40A only, since cord showing is a danger of injury in the Act's own words
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Regulation 27(1)(e) bars a tyre with "any of the ply or cord exposed", a requirement "as to
      brakes, steering-gear or tyres", so section 41A, which Schedule 2 gives obligatory endorsement
      and 3 points. Section 48 is an exemption, headed "Exemption from disqualification and
      endorsement", not a defence: the court must not endorse or disqualify "if he proves that he did
      not know, and had no reasonable cause to suspect, that the facts of the case were such that the
      offence would be committed", and the conviction and fine stay. The burden is hers, and on this
      course's reading a defect a look would have shown gave cause to suspect. Section 42 is for the
      other requirements. Section 40A could also meet the facts if they showed a danger of injury,
      but that is a question of fact and a prosecutor's choice, so "only" goes too far.

  # --- Driving away after a dangerous fail, Great Britain (L21) ---
  - q: >-
      As general information: a car in Peterhead, Scotland, fails its MOT with brake pads worn below
      1.5 mm, a Dangerous defect, and nothing else. Its current certificate has four months left. The
      owner wants to drive it to a garage across town for the repair. What does GOV.UK's guidance say?
    options:
      - It can be driven away, since the old certificate still has four months left to run
      - It can go only to a garage, as GOV.UK allows that one journey after any MOT fail
      - It can be driven until the retest window closes, 10 working days after the test
      - It must be repaired before it's driven, since a dangerous problem was on the list
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      GOV.UK: you can take the vehicle away if your current MOT is still valid and no "dangerous"
      problems were listed. "Otherwise, you’ll need to get it repaired before you can drive." Here a
      dangerous problem was listed, so four months on the old certificate don't help, and GOV.UK adds:
      "You can be fined up to £2,500, be banned from driving and get 3 penalty points for driving a
      vehicle that has failed its MOT because of a ‘dangerous’ problem." Driving "to or from somewhere
      to be repaired" is on GOV.UK's list for a car whose MOT has run out, which this one's hasn't.
      The 10 working days are for a free partial retest, not permission to drive. The repair is
      Call someone.

  # --- What would settle the inspection question (L22) ---
  - q: >-
      A researcher preparing a briefing for a state legislature asks what evidence would help settle
      whether periodic safety inspection reduces crashes. On GAO's 2015 account of why the question
      is hard, which would do most to settle it?
    options:
      - Crash data from thorough vehicle exams, by state, with inspection and without
      - Another count of cars that fail and are repaired, like Pennsylvania's 529,000 in 2014
      - A survey of the officials who run the programmes, on whether their programmes work
      - A list of what modern cars' onboard diagnostics watch, since that settles it
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      GAO found the research "remains inconclusive", and said why: component failure is "a factor in
      about 2 to 7 percent of crashes", there's "no comprehensive database for all police reported
      crashes in the United States", the national sample "is not set up to be representative at the
      state level", and, as some literature and safety advocate organisations told GAO, police
      officers "often do not have the time and resources to conduct a thorough vehicle check". On
      this course's reading of those gaps, data that fills them, representative state by state and
      with other causes of crashes controlled for, is what would test the question. Pennsylvania's count shows defects found and fixed, not crashes prevented. The
      officials' view is one side's case, as GAO reports it. The Texas sponsors' claim that onboard
      systems "can detect any potential issues" would need its own test, a count of inspection
      defects the car had already flagged, and wouldn't measure crashes either. None of this says
      which side is right.

  # --- A "Stop Drive" recall letter, UK (L23) ---
  - q: >-
      As general information: a keeper in Pwllheli, Wales, gets a maker's letter headed "Safety Recall
      (Stop Drive)". The dealer's first free slot is in three weeks, and she plans to drive to work
      gently until then. What do DVSA's code and GOV.UK say?
    options:
      - Driving on gently is fine meanwhile, since the delay in the repair is the dealer's
      - The code isn't law, its own text says, so its Stop Drive wording sets her no limit
      - A current MOT shows the car is fit, so the notice only matters on the repair date
      - The code's Stop Drive row says the car must not be driven, and GOV.UK says so too
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      DVSA's code describes a "Safety Recall (Stop Drive)" as: "The definition of a safety defect is
      met, and there is an immediate threat to safety so the vehicle must not be driven." GOV.UK: "If
      your vehicle has a serious safety defect, the manufacturer will write to you to tell you what to
      do. You must not drive the vehicle." A wait for a slot changes neither. The code's line that it
      "is not an interpretation or reframing of the law" describes what the code is; nothing read
      turns it into leave to drive a stop-drive car, and GOV.UK adds that you're legally responsible
      for a vehicle that is "safe to drive whenever you drive it". An MOT records one day. On this
      course's reading she asks the maker and the dealer how the repair will be arranged; the repair
      is Call someone.

  # --- An extended warranty, an independent garage, UK (L24) ---
  - q: >-
      As general information: in Bridgnorth, England, an owner bought an extended warranty four years
      after buying her car. Its terms require servicing at a main dealer. She used an independent
      garage, and a claim is refused on that ground. What does the CMA's guidance say that bears on
      this?
    options:
      - The refusal is unlawful, since UK law gives owners a statutory right to use any garage
      - Sold years after purchase, it's less likely to shut out independents despite its terms
      - The refusal is barred by 16 CFR 700.10, which forbids tying cover to named servicing
      - It says nothing, since an extended warranty isn't a warranty under UK law, as the FTC says
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The CMA's guidance says a warranty, "whether standard or extended", that makes cover depend on
      dealer-only servicing is "likely to be caught by the Chapter I prohibition"; and, at 5.39, that
      where an extended warranty is sold "years after the purchase of the vehicle", independent
      garages seem less likely to be shut out "even if" it carries servicing restrictions. Which? notes
      that some extended warranties require a main dealer. So, on this course's reading, the terms she
      bought matter, and the guidance promises nothing either way. It speaks of "the consumer's right"
      without creating one, and this course read no UK statute giving one. 16 CFR 700.10 is US law.
      The FTC's "not a warranty as defined by federal law" is US law too; The Motor Ombudsman's code
      calls such cover a warranty. Reading the booklet and asking for the refusal in writing is yours.

  # --- A small estimate in Michigan (L25) ---
  - q: >-
      As general information: a shop in Traverse City, Michigan, gives a written estimate of $90 for a
      small repair. The customer signs no waiver and doesn't ask to be consulted, and the shop doesn't
      ask again. Under MCL 257.1332 as read, what's the most it may charge?
    options:
      - $99, as the lesser of 10% or $50 is only $9 on an estimate of $90
      - $100, since the allowance is the greater of $10 or 10%, up to $50
      - $140, as Michigan lets a shop add $50 to any written estimate
      - $90, since no Michigan shop may go over an estimate without consent
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Michigan: if no waiver is signed "and the estimated price is exceeded by not more than 10% or
      $50.00, whichever is lesser, the facility is not required to obtain the written or oral consent
      of the customer for the excess charge unless specifically requested by the customer." Ten per
      cent of $90 is $9, less than $50, so $99 (this course's arithmetic). The greater of $10 or 10%,
      capped at $50, is Florida's rule, and it would give $100: the two states part only below $100.
      $50 over is Michigan's cap only from $500 up, and allowing nothing is California's and New
      York's rule. Whose rule is it? Law, Michigan's, as general information. Checking a bill against
      it, and keeping every estimate and invoice, is yours. If the shop won't settle, the FTC's routes
      are the state's Attorney General or a local consumer protection agency, or small claims court.

:::callout Before the car lets you down
This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. If your car fails on a motorway or other fast road in Great Britain, leave at the next exit or services if you can; if you can't, go left into an emergency area or onto the hard shoulder, switch on your hazard lights, get everyone out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and don't go back to the car. If you're stuck in a live lane or can't get out safely, stay in with seat belts and hazard lights on and call 999 and ask for the police. In the US, pull as far onto the right shoulder as you can, switch on your hazard lights and stay in with your seat belt on, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind, in which case get out on the side away from traffic; call 911 in an emergency. Never put any part of your body under a car held up only by a jack, and never touch the orange high-voltage cables on a hybrid or electric car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::
