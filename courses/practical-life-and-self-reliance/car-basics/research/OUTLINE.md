# Outline: Car Basics

*Stage 2, 2026-09-26. Built from `research/SOURCES.md` (the synthesis read in full; Parts A to E read
for the lessons below), after `npm run taught`, which shows twenty earlier courses and 213 lessons,
all written. Modelled on Home Repair and Maintenance, the last practical UK/US course, and carrying
over from the start every convention that course's reviews had to find lesson by lesson (its
`research/pipeline-briefs.md`, "Course conventions settled so far", and `reviews/course-wide.md`).
**Not a standards 3.4 domain as a whole** (`sensitive_domain: false` stays), but it states law in
Great Britain, Northern Ireland and nine US states, and it gives instructions that can hurt someone
if they are wrong: a car falling off a jack, a hot coolant cap, a person standing between a stopped
car and traffic, an orange cable. So every legal statement, phone number, threshold and safety step
is checked against the primary, not against SOURCES, and three lessons carry public policy questions
that get a neutrality pass (smart motorways, whether inspections work, right to repair).*

## How many lessons, and why

**Twenty-five, and a project.** SOURCES suggested twenty-four and a project (open concern 1). This
outline makes three changes and keeps the rest:

- **Steering and suspension join brakes (lesson 6), and the transmission joins the engine (lesson
  2).** The free official sources run out at steering, suspension and the alternator (gate G-H1).
  What an owner can be taught from what was read is the symptoms (Highway Code Annex 6: the pull,
  the bounce; AAA's wear patterns) and fueleconomy.gov's account of CVTs and dual-clutch boxes. A
  lesson of its own would be thin and would be drafted into a research gap, which is the defect
  `courses/CLAUDE.md` rule 1 warns about. If G-H1 closes with a textbook read in full, the mechanism
  goes into lesson 6; it does not become a lesson.
- **Fuel economy gets a short lesson of its own (lesson 12).** SOURCES left the choice open. The
  withdrawn-figures story (finding 11: fueleconomy.gov's air filter "as much as 10 percent" in 2007,
  gone by 2010; the tyre figure cut about fivefold in 2016; the 4% tune-up and 40% oxygen-sensor
  figures dropped between 2021 and 2023) is dated from the archive, specific, and teaches something
  that transfers: how good advice is revised, and how to tell a best case from an average. Threaded
  through four lessons it would be four asides.
- **The garage is two lessons (24 and 25), as SOURCES suggested.** Choosing a garage, servicing and
  warranties is one sitting; quotes, estimates, the bill and disputes, in the Consumer Rights Act
  and five US states, is another, and it is the law-heavy one.

Everything else follows the synthesis's six groups:

**How a car works**
1. **What a car is made of, and why the handbook comes first.** The system map; the handbook as the
   authority for every per-car figure; UK and US words; the course's labels, its question, its scope.
2. **The engine: petrol, diesel, and the drive to the wheels.** Four strokes; spark and compression
   ignition; where the energy goes and why an engine needs oil and coolant; the cambelt; gearboxes.
3. **Diesel exhaust: the DPF and AdBlue.** What each does, what each needs from how the car is driven,
   and the law on removing them.
4. **Hybrids and electric cars: how they work.** Motor, controller, traction battery, regenerative
   braking, the 12 V battery that every one still has, charging, and what an EV owner still maintains.
5. **The 12 V battery: what it does, and why short trips flatten it.** How each kind of car recharges
   it; heat, cold and short trips; the signs of failure; the charge warning light; lead-acid hazards.
6. **Brakes, steering and suspension.** Hydraulics and brake fluid; ABS; the red brake light and the
   falling level; the owner-visible signs of worn steering and suspension.

**Checks and warnings**
7. **Tyres I: pressure, the placard and TPMS.** The carmaker's figure, not the sidewall's; cold
   checks; what the TPMS light is required to do; reading a sidewall.
8. **Tyres II: tread, damage, age, and when to replace.** The legal floor in each place; coin tests
   and what they show; damage that is illegal at any depth; the argued margin above the floor; age.
9. **Under the bonnet: oil, coolant, brake fluid and screenwash.** Each check by the handbook; never a
   hot cap; what a falling brake-fluid level means; hybrids and EVs that start or spin a fan silently.
10. **Lights, wipers, glass and the walk-round.** What the law requires in Great Britain; the
    windscreen zones; the weekly or monthly routine.
11. **Dashboard warning lights.** The colour code and where it is law; the stop-now group; the steady
    and the flashing check-engine light.
12. **Fuel economy: what maintenance buys, and the figures that were withdrawn.**

**Batteries, high voltage and the seasons**
13. **A flat battery and jump-starting.**
14. **High voltage: hybrid and EV safety, charging at home, fire and flood.**
15. **Winter and summer.** Preparing the car; clearing it (a legal duty in Great Britain); a
    snow-blocked exhaust; a hot car and a child.

**When it goes wrong on the road**
16. **When the car stops: where, who stands where, and the call.** Any road; the Highway Code's place of
    relative safety; triangles; what to tell the operator; making a scene safe and handing casualty
    care to First Aid.
17. **Motorways and fast roads: go left, the live lane, and the smart motorway question.**
18. **A flat tyre: the decision, and spare, space saver, sealant or run-flat.**
19. **Changing a wheel safely.**

**The law**
20. **The law on a car's condition: Great Britain and Northern Ireland.**
21. **The MOT and Northern Ireland's vehicle test.**
22. **The US: state inspections, emissions tests and tread law.**
23. **Recalls: checking, what's free, and "stop drive".**

**The garage**
24. **Choosing a garage, servicing and warranties.**
25. **Quotes, estimates, the bill and disputes.**

**Why this order.** The car before the checks, because every check needs its reason (SOURCES open
concern 2: oil because an engine sheds most of its fuel's energy as heat and has moving parts to
protect; coolant because that heat has to go somewhere; brake fluid because it absorbs water). The
checks before the roadside, because most roadside trouble is a check not made (National Highways:
over 40 per cent of the breakdowns its officers deal with are fuel, tyres, power loss and engine
trouble). The roadside before the law, because the law lessons then have jobs the reader has met.
**The law comes late on purpose**, as Home Repair's did: whether a bald shoulder on a tyre is lawful
means nothing until lesson 8 has shown what "the central three-quarters" is. Every earlier lesson
still names the jurisdiction of any rule it states and points to lesson 20 or 22 by title. The
garage comes last because choosing and dealing with one draws on everything before it.

**Why not fewer.** Home Repair outlined sixteen lessons and split seven of them at drafting; every
draft ran long against its estimate. The two merges tried here (tyres I and II; the two roadside
lessons 16 and 17) each came out past two hours on paper.

**Why not more.** Jump-starting stays one lesson (the battery's chemistry is lesson 5's). Hot-car and
snow CO sit in the seasons lesson. Recalls are one lesson for both countries, because the shape is
the same (a maker's campaign, a lookup, a free remedy with limits). The emissions test sits with each
country's periodic test, not in a lesson of its own.

**Lesson length, planned for the 100-minute ceiling from the start.** Every lesson below is planned at
60 to 85 measured minutes (measured minutes include exercises and the Sources list; `npm run
minutes`). Home Repair's lessons came in 10 to 40 minutes over their estimates, so the estimates here
are deliberately low and the scope is sized to them. **There are no named seams.** Each lesson lists
"Cut first if long": the sections to drop, in order, if a draft passes 100 minutes. A drafter who has
cut everything on the list and is still past 100 stops and tells the orchestrator, who decides; the
default is to cut further, not to split (the convention Home Repair settled on 2026-09-26). A split,
if one is ever accepted, renumbers the course, so cross-references use the title as well as the
number.

## What is deliberately left out, and said so in lesson 1

- **Driving.** No course on the map teaches driving skill, the driving test or road craft (SOURCES,
  checked against TAXONOMY on 2026-09-26). This course uses Highway Code rules only where they bear on
  the car's condition (Annex 6; rules 89, 97, 116, 120, 121, 123, 229) or on a breakdown or incident
  (rules 258, 269 to 287). Blowout handling (NHTSA; Annex 6) is the one borderline item and is kept,
  because it is what to do when the car fails. Weather driving technique is out; preparing the car for
  the weather is in.
- **Work under the car, and axle stands.** Garage work. Not taught as a procedure anywhere (safety
  guidance "must never say").
- **Changing oil, pads, discs, filters, bulbs behind panels, fuses, belts and wiper arms.** SOURCES
  open concern 5. Bulbs and wiper blades are "a handbook-by-handbook judgement": the lesson points to
  the handbook and does not generalise. Fuses: no source read (gate G-H1).
- **Anything on the high-voltage system** (decision 8).
- **Puncture repair by the owner** (plugging a tyre). The course teaches what a proper repair is so
  the reader can ask for one (lesson 18).
- **Reading fault codes with a scanner.** Auto Upkeep lists it as an activity (Part A §4, contents
  only); no source read teaches it.
- **Forced DPF regeneration, remapping, "deletes" of any kind** except to say they are illegal and
  fail the test (lesson 3).
- **Buying a car, car finance and car insurance.** Personal Finance Fundamentals lessons 3 and 8 own
  loans and insurance; whether to buy a petrol, diesel, hybrid or EV is Energy's argument, and this
  course takes no side (decision 15).
- **Casualty care.** First Aid and CPR owns it (decision 15).
- **Threat from other people at the roadside.** Personal Safety lesson 6 (decision 15).
- **The household emergency kit.** Emergency Preparedness (Term 24).
- **Motorcycles, vans over 3.5 tonnes, towing a trailer, caravans.** Not researched.
- **Northern Ireland's own road traffic statute and construction and use regulations, its Highway
  Code, and Scotland's fines and consumer law** (SOURCES open concern 4; gates G-L4, G-N3): named as
  "different, and not read by this course", with the one thing that was read (the DVA test).

## Twenty-two decisions, settled here so no lesson re-opens them

**1. Honest objectives, and exercises that cannot hurt anyone.** Objectives use verbs a reader can be
tested on from a page: **explain, identify, check, read, decide, compare, state, apply, plan,
sort**. None says "repair", "replace", "service" or "fix". `course.yaml` outcomes are reworded to
match (done at this stage). Exercises are handbook hunts, look-and-record checks, measurements,
written plans, lookups and sorts, and the owner's checks the sources give: **tyre pressures cold
with a gauge** (NHTSA; Highway Code Annex 6); **tread with a gauge, the wear bars or a coin at
several points** (TyreSafe; NHTSA; AAA); **reading the placard and the sidewall date code**; **the
oil dipstick by the handbook's method**, engine off and on level ground (Ford p. 308; the AA;
National Highways); **coolant and brake-fluid levels read through the reservoir without opening
it** (FMVSS 135 S5.4.4 requires a reservoir that can be read closed); **topping up screenwash**;
**the lights walk-round** with a helper or a reflection (National Highways); **the start-up bulb
check** (Highway Code Annex 6); **the Annex 6 bounce test** on a parked car; **locating** the jack,
jacking points, chock, spare or sealant kit, jump points and high-voltage warning labels from the
handbook, by looking; **the MOT history and recall lookups**. **No exercise asks a reader to:**

- lift the car on any jack, loosen a wheel nut, or change a wheel for practice (lesson 19 teaches the
  order on paper; a page cannot check a jack's footing);
- connect jump leads or a booster pack for practice;
- open a coolant cap or reservoir, or open a brake-fluid reservoir;
- touch, unplug, open or go near the covers of anything orange or labelled high voltage;
- put any part of the body under the car;
- stand at the roadside, on a hard shoulder or in an emergency area for the exercise;
- drive for the exercise (no "take it for a run to clear the DPF" as homework);
- do anything to a car the reader does not keep, without the keeper's agreement (decision 16).

The drafter writes these limits into each exercise, not into a footnote. An exercise that has the
reader look under the bonnet writes in the preparation (engine or motor off, key or fob away from the
car, parking brake, P or in gear, cold; for a hybrid or EV the handbook's own "off" state; lesson 9's
reasons) and what to do if something is wrong (a falling brake-fluid level or a milky dipstick is
Call someone, lesson 9).

**2. Jurisdictions named exactly.** SOURCES: "'The UK' is right only where Parts C and E read a rule
that covers Northern Ireland too. The MOT does not." Rules:

- **Great Britain** (England, Wales, Scotland) for the Road Traffic Act 1988 ss.40A to 47, the Road
  Vehicles (Construction and Use) Regulations 1986, the Road Vehicles Lighting Regulations 1989, the
  MOT and DVSA's inspection manual, and **the Highway Code** ("This Highway Code applies to England,
  Scotland and Wales"). **Northern Ireland** for the DVA test (nidirect), and for nothing else unless
  a gate closes: its own road traffic law, construction and use rules and Highway Code were not read
  (G-N3), and a lesson says so in one line rather than implying GB law reaches it.
- **England** for National Highways (its motorways and major A roads), smart motorways, emergency
  areas as a network, and 0300 123 5000. **England and Wales** for the Sentencing Council's guideline
  and the Sentencing Act 2020 s.122 standard scale (Scotland's scale was not read, G-L4), and for
  Citizens Advice's England page. **England, Wales and Northern Ireland** for the Torts (Interference
  with Goods) Act 1977 (it does not extend to Scotland). **The UK** for the Consumer Rights Act 2015.
- **US federal** for NHTSA, the FMVSS, 49 U.S.C. 30120, Magnuson-Moss and 16 CFR 700.10, the Clean Air
  Act, the FTC; **FMCSA's rules are for commercial motor vehicles only** and are always labelled so.
  Everything else by named state: **Pennsylvania, New York, Virginia, Texas, California** (inspection,
  tread, Smog Check, Move Over, lien, repair), **Florida, Michigan, Ohio** (repair law),
  **Massachusetts** (right to repair). **A lesson never says "the US minimum tread is 2/32 inch" or
  "US law requires"** anything a state requires. It sends the reader to their own state's DMV or
  state police page.
- **Emergency numbers**: 999 in the UK and 911 in the US. **112 is not printed** unless re-read on an
  official page at drafting (SOURCES: "112 also works, not verified in the parts"; gate G-N1).

**3. The UK and the US side by side, and neither called wrong.** Where official or handbook advice
differs, each country's advice is taught as its authorities give it, and the course's voice calls
neither wrong, better or behind. The cases: **stay in or get out** at a breakdown (Rule 277's out and
behind the barrier, against AAA's and the California handbook's stay in and belted unless there is
no room or a guardrail); the tread floor and how it is measured (1.6 mm across the central
three-quarters, against 2/32 inch in two adjacent grooves); the coin tests (20p against penny and
quarter); how often to check pressures (weekly in the Highway Code, monthly for NHTSA, AAA and
TyreSafe); warning-light colours (UN R121 fixes more of them than FMVSS 101); triangles (45 m and
never on a motorway, against no federal rule for private cars); recalls; garage estimate law; the
periodic test. Rules:

- **Differences go in a table or an "In the UK / In the US" set, never interleaved.** Where they
  agree, one sentence says so, because agreement is information too (a live lane: stay in, belts on,
  hazards on, call 999 or 911).
- **A UK reader is never handed the US breakdown steps, and the reverse,** in anything that could be
  acted on at the roadside. Lesson 17 says plainly: follow the advice for the road you are on.
- **Where no source gives the reason for a difference, the lesson says so** and supplies none
  (decision 14).
- **Words and units both, at first use in each lesson:** bonnet (hood), boot (trunk), tyre (tire),
  petrol (gasoline), hard shoulder (shoulder), space saver (compact spare), cambelt (timing belt),
  handbrake (parking brake), engine management light (check engine light), AdBlue (DEF), MOT (state
  inspection); mm and 32nds of an inch; psi with bar or kPa; N·m and lbf·ft; km/h and mph; °C and °F.
  **Spelling:** UK spelling in the course's prose ("tyre", "mould"); US spelling only inside US
  quotations and titles ("TireWise"). SOURCES: "Pick one per sentence and gloss the other once per
  lesson."
- **Numbers are never merged across countries.** No UK figure exists for hot-car deaths or for jack
  injuries; the US figure is given as US, and the lesson says the course found no UK one.

**4. A safety callout on every lesson, word for word, directly after the opening paragraph(s), and the
build enforces it.** The pattern is Home Repair's (`HOME_REPAIR_SAFETY`) in `scripts/build.mjs`. The
text, whose body is **one line in the file**:

```
:::callout Before the car lets you down
This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. If your car fails on a motorway or other fast road in Great Britain, leave at the next exit or services if you can; if you can't, go left into an emergency area or onto the hard shoulder, switch on your hazard lights, get everyone out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and don't go back to the car. If you're stuck in a live lane or can't get out safely, stay in with seat belts and hazard lights on and call 999 and ask for the police. In the US, pull as far onto the right shoulder as you can, switch on your hazard lights and stay in with your seat belt on, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind, in which case get out on the side away from traffic; call 911 in an emergency. Never put any part of your body under a car held up only by a jack, and never touch the orange high-voltage cables on a hybrid or electric car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::
```

Sources for each clause: "education, not ... legal advice" (standards 2.5); "not mechanical
training" (the course's scope, decision 7; the IMI and ASE programmes in Part A §4 are what training
means). The Great Britain steps are Highway Code Rule 277 and National Highways, Part E §1 and §2:
"leave the carriageway at the next exit or pull into a service area if possible"; "move into the
left lane"; "pull into an emergency area or onto a hard shoulder if you can"; "switch your hazard
warning lights on"; "exit your vehicle by the side furthest from traffic, if it is safe and possible
to do so, and ensure passengers do the same"; "get behind a safety barrier where there is one"; "DO
NOT return to your vehicle even if it's raining, cold or dark"; and the live-lane box, "stay in your
vehicle", "keep your seat belts and hazard warning lights on", "call 999 immediately and ask for the
police". The US steps are AAA Club Alliance ("pull over as far to the right as you're able and turn
on your hazard lights"; "exit the vehicle from the passenger side and wait beyond the protective
guard rail, if one exists") and the California Driver Handbook ("Stay inside your vehicle with your
seat belt on until help arrives"; the exceptions "Not enough space on the shoulder." and "A
guardrail."; "Exit on the right side so you are away from traffic"), with 911.gov for 911 ("An
emergency is any situation that requires immediate assistance from the police, fire department or
ambulance"). The jack clause is every handbook read (Toyota p. 372 "Do not put any part of your body
under the vehicle while it is supported by the jack"; Honda "never get under the vehicle when it is
supported only by the jack"; Nissan p. 8-2 "Never get under the vehicle while it is supported only
by a jack"; Mazda) and HSE ("do not rely on jacks alone"). The cable clause is NFPA ("Do not touch the
orange high voltage cables"), Nissan EV-8 and Ford p. 184. The law sentence is decision 2.

**Why "Great Britain" and not "the UK" for the breakdown steps:** the Highway Code "applies to England,
Scotland and Wales", and Northern Ireland's own Highway Code was not read. If gate G-N3 closes before
lesson 1 ships and Northern Ireland's rules give the same steps, the orchestrator changes "in Great
Britain" to "in the UK" in the constant and in lesson 1 in one edit; otherwise the callout stays as
written and lesson 17 says Northern Ireland's rules were not read.

**Why these five things and no others:** the callout is not the lesson. The coolant cap, the jump
start, the fire and the child in a hot car are each taught fully where they belong (lessons 9, 13,
14, 15), and a callout that tried to carry them all would stop being read. What it carries is what a
reader must have before any lesson: the two roadside positions, the two ways a car kills its owner
while being worked on, and the fact that the law moves at every border.

**For the orchestrator:** the check goes beside `HOME_REPAIR_SAFETY` as `CAR_BASICS_SAFETY`, keyed on
`cdir.name === "car-basics"`, with the callout's sentences added to `REPETITION_EXEMPT`. It should be
in `build.mjs` before lesson 1 is drafted, so that the first draft is checked by the build rather
than by eye. A short paragraph after the callout may explain one clause where the lesson needs it
(lesson 17 explains why the two countries' advice differs as far as any source says, which is not
at all).

**5. The course's question: law, handbook or advice: whose, and for which car?** Home Repair asked "law
or advice, whose, and where?". A car adds a third kind of rule and a second kind of "where". **Car
advice fails in one way more than any other: one car's figure, or one organisation's commercial
advice, repeated as if it bound every car everywhere.** "Torque the nuts to 108 N·m" (Honda's and
Nissan's; Toyota's is 103, Mazda's a range of 108 to 147). "50 mph on a space saver" (every handbook
read, and still the handbook's figure, not a law of nature; the GB legal limit in reg 27(3) is 50
mph too). "Replace at 3 mm" (TyreSafe, a charity backed by the tyre industry). "Change the oil every
3,000 miles" (CalRecycle: "out of date"). "Wait 10 minutes before opening the coolant cap" (Ford's,
for a Ford). "A red light means call recovery" (the RAC's table, which the same page softens). Every
rule a lesson gives carries, in the sentence: **who says it** (a statute or regulation, a
regulator's guidance, the Highway Code's MUST or its should, a maker's handbook for its own car, a
motoring organisation, a trade body, a charity); **whether it is law, the handbook or advice**; and
**which car and which place it covers**. Introduced in lesson 1 and used in every lesson.

**The job labels, taught in lesson 1 and used in every lesson,** in the prose, not as badges:

- **Yours**: the sources put it in any owner's hands (check pressures and tread, read the dipstick,
  top up screenwash, walk round the lights, check for recalls, read the MOT history).
- **Yours, on conditions**: allowed if a stated condition is met, and the lesson names it: topping up
  oil (the handbook's grade, level ground, engine off); topping up coolant (only when cold, the
  handbook's coolant, never a hot cap); a jump start (the handbook allows it; the battery is not
  frozen, damaged or leaking; same voltage; off the road and never on a motorway, hard shoulder or
  emergency area); changing a wheel (lesson 19's list, and never on a motorway); a sealant kit
  (within the kit's limits); placing a triangle (ordinary roads only, and only if it is safe).
- **Call someone**: the law reserves it, the handbook says the owner must not, or every source read
  advises against it (anything on the high-voltage system; brakes beyond reading the level; a
  brake-fluid level that keeps falling; a flashing check-engine light's cause; any repair or wheel
  change on a motorway; a puncture repair; forced DPF regeneration; airbags; anything needing the car
  lifted beyond the handbook's wheel change; a home charge point's installation).

Where the law allows the owner more than the advice does, **both are given, law first, in adjacent
sentences, and the label follows the advice** (decision 10).

**6. The handbook is the authority for every per-car figure, and the course never prints one number as
universal.** Every source that gives an interval, a pressure, a fluid grade, a jacking point, a
torque, a coolant wait or a space-saver speed sends the reader to the handbook (SOURCES essential 1:
BAR, CalRecycle, fueleconomy.gov, AAA, the RAC, Annex 6 and every handbook read). Rules:

- A figure from a handbook is always that maker's, for that car, in that market, in the sentence:
  "Toyota, in the handbook for the 2023 Corolla sold in the US, gives 76 ft·lbf (103 N·m)". It is
  never "the torque" or "the right pressure".
- Where handbooks differ, the lesson shows the spread as the point (torque; coolant wait, Ford's 10
  minutes against "until cold"; Mazda's rule against a space saver on the driving wheels, which
  Toyota does not have).
- The handbooks read are the 2023 Corolla (US, page numbers), 2024 Civic (US, online), Mazda3
  (European English, online), 2023 LEAF (UK, online), 2024 LEAF (US, page numbers), 2024 Maverick
  petrol and hybrid (US, page numbers, archive capture), a 1998 Corolla (one line), and one page of
  the Tesla Model 3 UK manual (October 2024 capture). **No other maker is named.** SOURCES access
  problem 3.
- **A reader with no handbook** is sent to the maker's own online handbook, which Mazda and Nissan UK
  publish (read); that other makers do is not claimed.

**7. What an owner does by hand, and what the course does not teach.** SOURCES open concern 5 settles
the scope and this outline adopts it: the checks; topping up screenwash, oil and coolant (cold);
reading warning lights; jump-starting by the handbook; changing a wheel where it is safe and the
handbook gives the method. **Not taught as a procedure anywhere**, in the body, an exercise, a
checkpoint answer or a quiz explanation: getting under the car; axle stands; changing oil, pads,
filters or fuses; bulbs behind panels; plugging a tyre; forced DPF regeneration; reading fault
codes; anything on the high-voltage system. **Lesson 1 says why, as the course's own scope decision:**
a page cannot check a jack's footing, a torque, or a technician's training, and every source read
sends these jobs to a garage or a trained technician. It is not a claim that doing them is unlawful.

**8. High voltage is not for owners, and every lesson says it the same way.** SOURCES essential 9 and
Part B §5:

- High-voltage cables are **orange** (Nissan; Ford: "a solid orange convolute, or orange striped tape,
  or both"; NHTSA; NFPA), with two caveats from DVSA's MOT manual that every lesson keeps: "some
  imported vehicles may have high voltage wiring insulation of a different colour", and "Many mild
  hybrids only use 48V systems which may use blue coloured insulation". High voltage is defined by
  ASE as "greater than 30 VAC or 60 VDC". Voltages are "several hundred volts in most hybrids and
  EVs" (AFDC's "400 to 1,000 volts" is too broad for mild hybrids, gate G-H5) and Nissan's "up to
  approximately DC 400 volt" is Nissan's.
- "The vehicle high voltage system has no user serviceable parts" (Nissan). Ford: "This battery pack
  should only be serviced by an authorized electric vehicle technician."
- **Silent starts and moving parts:** a hybrid's engine "could restart at any time" unless switched
  off (Ford); Nissan's cooling fan "may come on at any time without warning"; the MOT manual: the
  engine "may start without warning when electrical equipment is operated or if the battery voltage
  drops". So every lesson that opens a bonnet gives the preparation in decision 1.
- **After damage:** assume it is energised (Ford; Tesla). Fire: get out, get away, call 999 or 911,
  say it is an electric or hybrid car (Ford p. 284; Derbyshire FRS; Annex 6). A damaged EV is kept
  away from buildings: NHTSA "within 50 feet", Ford "49 ft (15 m)"; each given as its own.
- **Who may work on it:** high-voltage work is graded by training level and is not owner work (IMI
  TechSafe's own list of three awards; ASE's "Light Duty Hybrid/Electric Vehicle Specialist" test).
  **TechSafe is not mandatory**: the IMI is "calling on government to mandate its use". Nothing is
  said about what IMI levels permit beyond that (gate G-H6).
- **No comparative claim about EV and petrol fire rates** (gate G-R6).

**9. The law is general information, labelled at the point of use, by Home Repair's rule.** The
sentence for this course: **"This is general information, not legal advice. The rules differ between
England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and they change."** (England and
Wales together, because the Sentencing Council, the standard scale and Citizens Advice's page are
theirs; Scotland because its fines and consumer law were not read; Northern Ireland because its road
traffic law was not read.) Rules, carried over from Home Repair's settled convention (its briefs,
2026-09-25):

- **The full sentence goes once, in the lesson's opening** (after the callout), in any lesson that
  states law. After that, **"This is general information, not legal advice."** opens each H2 section
  that states law and each exercise that asks about law. It does **not** go at every mention, in an
  H3 under a labelled H2, in a hidden checkpoint or predict answer, or in a quiz explanation; a quiz
  item on the law says "as general information" in its stem instead. A lesson that states law gives
  it its own H2 rather than an H3 inside a practical section. The within-lesson 25-word duplicate
  check will warn on the repeated sentence if a lesson carries it twice; carry it once.
- **No lesson tells a reader what they may lawfully do in their own case.** Legal worked examples and
  exercises ask which rule applies in a named place and what to ask the named body.
- **The Highway Code's two voices.** Rules with MUST or MUST NOT are law and cite the regulation;
  should rules are advice that "may be used in evidence in any court proceedings" (Introduction).
  **The 45-metre triangle distance and hazard lights when stopped are should, not MUST** (SOURCES
  safety "must never say"). Every lesson that quotes a rule says which kind it is.
- **Statutes, regulations and thresholds are re-read on the live page at drafting**, and the drafter
  reads the whole section once, not only the lines SOURCES recorded. Dated figures (the MOT maximum
  fee £54.85, the DVA's £38.00, Virginia's $20, Texas's $7.50, Florida's $150 and $50, the Sentencing
  Act levels, the FMVSS and 30120 limits, the Move Over position) are re-read too.
- **Secondary sources' paraphrases of law are checked against the primary** before use: the AA's
  "running out of washer fluid ... will be considered an offence" is not repeated (reg 34(6) is
  quoted instead); TyreSafe's "three penalty points PER tyre" and "To stay compliant with the law" on
  BS AU 159 are not repeated; National Highways' "All tyres are legally required to have a minimum
  tread depth of 1.6mm" is not repeated.
- **Insurance** keeps the sources' hedge every time: an unroadworthy car "may invalidate your
  insurance" (Annex 3); the ABI via nidirect, a lapsed MOT "would not necessarily invalidate" a

**10. Where the law and the advice differ, both are stated together.** The shape recurs: tread (1.6 mm
or 2/32 inch in law, against 3 mm from TyreSafe and the RAC, 2 mm from the AA, 4/32 inch from AAA);

the law first and labelled, then the advice with its source; the job label follows the advice; a
lesson never states only one half.

## Lesson sequence

Every lesson opens with its reason to care, then the safety callout (decision 4), and ends with "For
your plan", Connections, Go deeper, Sources and the quiz. Two or three `:::predict` and at least two
`:::checkpoint` per lesson, one worked example with a gap, at least one exercise that opens "Take NN
minutes", with decision 1's limits written in. Every lesson that is not wholly about one country
includes a UK case and a US case somewhere. **"For your plan"** names what the lesson adds to the car
file (the project), so the project assembles itself.

### 1. What a car is made of, and why the handbook comes first (~70 min)

- **Objectives:** explain the car as a set of systems (engine or motor, 12 V electrics, brakes,
  steering and suspension, tyres and wheels, transmission, and in a hybrid or EV the high-voltage
  system) and say which owner's check belongs to which; identify in a handbook the per-car figures no
  course can give (pressures, fluid grades, intervals, jacking and jump points, wheel-nut torque,
  space-saver speed) and say why; sort owner jobs with the course's three labels, saying for each
  whether its rule is law, handbook or advice, whose, and for which car; translate the UK and US names
  for the parts and jobs the course uses.

  noise), and the handbook in the glovebox says what each notice means for this car. **The system

  Pells, the IMI Level 1 units, ASE's 2024 standards and Auto Upkeep; contents only, named as further

### 2. The engine: petrol, diesel, and the drive to the wheels (~75 min)

- **Objectives:** explain the four-stroke cycle and the difference between spark ignition and
  compression ignition; explain, from where a fuel's energy goes, why an engine needs a cooling system
  and what the oil and coolant checks protect; explain what a cambelt does and why it is replaced by
  the handbook's miles or years, whichever comes first, rather than by symptoms; compare manual,
  conventional automatic, CVT and dual-clutch gearboxes.
- **Core ideas:** DOE's four strokes ("intake, compression, combustion and power stroke, and
  exhaust"); spark against compression ignition (DOE; AFDC's two pages, and the caution that DOE's
  sentence describes port injection only); combustion to crankshaft to wheels (DOE); the ECM (AFDC);
  **where the energy goes**: "Only about 12%–30% of the energy from the fuel ... is used to move it down
  the road" (fueleconomy.gov; the dash inside the range is the source's), diesels "generally one-third
  more efficient"; the rest leaves as heat, which is why "Most engines are water-cooled" (Annex 6).
  **Oil** by gate G-H12. **Glow plugs** (DENSO, a glow-plug maker: wait for the light to go out; its
  DPF line and its 10-to-15-seconds rule not used). **The cambelt** (the RAC, which sells cambelt
  changes: what it does; "60,000 to 100,000 miles" or "five or six years" as the RAC's range; the
  handbook's interval governs, "whichever comes first"; "Many cambelts show no obvious symptoms before
  failure"; "At your annual MOT, your mechanic will not check your cambelt"). **Gearboxes**
  (fueleconomy.gov: CVTs' pulleys and belt or chain; DCTs' two clutches; 6 to 8 speeds give "2%–4%";
  automatics often match or beat manuals on economy). The EV's single reduction gear is lesson 4's.
- **Mechanism to explain:** four strokes and the two ignitions; why most of the fuel becomes heat and so
  why a cooling system exists; what a cambelt keeps in step (crankshaft and camshafts, the RAC).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a cold morning in Thurso, a diesel's glow-plug light: wait for it
  to go out, then start (DENSO; the handbook). (b) wrinkle: a used car bought in Middlesbrough at seven
  years and 58,000 miles with no record of a cambelt change, and a handbook that (in the example, and
  labelled as the example's) gives six years or 60,000 miles. Due, because the years came first. Gap:
  the reader does the next car (a low-mileage ten-year-old) before the answer.
- **Misconceptions:** every petrol engine mixes fuel and air before the cylinder (AFDC: port or direct
  injection); the MOT checks the cambelt (the RAC: no); a cambelt warns before it fails (the RAC);
  automatics always use more fuel (fueleconomy.gov's myths page).
- **Practice:** label a four-stroke schematic; from your handbook, find whether your engine has a
  cambelt interval and what it is, or write "the handbook gives none"; a checkpoint on 12 to 30 per
  cent of a 50-litre tank (the course's arithmetic).

- **Sources:** Part A §1, §3 (transmission), §4 (contents only), (a)1, (d); gate G-H12.
- **Connects:** lesson 1's handbook authority; leads to lesson 3 (the diesel's exhaust), lesson 9 (the
  oil and coolant checks), lesson 12 (octane and warm-up).
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's "whose figure, for which car" (the cambelt interval).
- **Neutrality:** none contested.
- **Places:** Thurso, Middlesbrough.

### 3. Diesel exhaust: the DPF and AdBlue (~65 min)

- **Objectives:** explain what a diesel particulate filter does and why short, slow journeys block it;
  decide what to do when the DPF light comes on, from the handbook and two motoring organisations'
  advice; explain what AdBlue (DEF) is, where it goes, how much warning a car gives, and what to do if
  it goes in the fuel tank; state, as general information, that removing a DPF is an offence in Great
  Britain and removing or defeating emission parts is prohibited under US federal law, and that both
  fail the test.

  a trade name (the AA); a separate tank; the early warning "around 1500 miles"; you "won't be able to
  restart" if it runs out; "a litre of AdBlue every 600 miles" as typical; in the fuel tank: "don't touch
  the ignition" and have the tank drained; diesel in the AdBlue tank: "Don't turn on the ignition or
  start the engine" and call for help (all the AA's) [corrected 2026-09-26, Stage 4 lesson 03: the
  "Don't turn on" line sits under the AA's diesel-in-the-AdBlue-tank heading; SOURCES "Gate closures,
  lesson 03"]. **US**: DEF is mainly a truck
  and heavy-pickup matter and diesel cars are rare (Part A (d)); EPA's derate facts with the page's

- **Objectives:** explain what the 12 V battery does in a petrol or diesel car, a hybrid and an EV, and

### 25. Quotes, estimates, the bill and disputes (~80 min; Tier B)

- **Objectives:** state, as general information, what a UK garage owes under the Consumer Rights Act 2015
  (ss.49 to 52 and 54 to 57) and how Citizens Advice separates a quote from an estimate; compare the
  estimate, authorisation and old-parts rules in California, New York, Florida, Michigan and Ohio, and
  compute each state's tolerance for a described bill; explain why a garage can keep but not sell a car
  while a bill is disputed (England, Wales and Northern Ireland) and what California's lien and Florida's
  rule on unauthorised repairs say; take a dispute through the routes each country offers, starting with
  the free ones.
- **Core ideas:** **UK**: CRA ss.49 to 52, 54 to 57, quoted from the live text (care and skill; what was
  "said or written"; "a reasonable price ... and no more"; reasonable time; repeat performance then price
  reduction, "the full amount" where appropriate, refund within 14 days; s.57 against excluding s.49),
  with Home Repair lesson 23 for services in general; Citizens Advice (quote against estimate, both
  quoted; an estimate may go up "within reason"; "whatever needs to be done" instructions; "paying under
  protest"; disclaimer signs as unfair terms; the escalation routes; the helpline, G-N1; section 75 in one
  line to Personal Finance lesson 10); The Motor Ombudsman's code (2.8 and 2.9; 2.10 on deposits; 3.2
  authorisation; 3.4 guarantee; 3.7 old parts; 4.2 and 4.3 invoices; eight weeks; free; binding if the
  consumer accepts), labelled (decision 11); the 1977 Act (s.12; Sch 1 paras 6(3) and 7); the lien by
  G-G4. Scotland differs and was not read (G-L4); Northern Ireland's advice service not named (decision
  13). **US**: California B&P §§ 9884.6 to 9884.10 (registration; written estimate; "No work shall be
  done and no charges shall accrue before authorization"; no tolerance; the oral-consent record;
  sublet work; the invoice; old parts on request at the work order); BAR (the sign; "Do not sign a blank
  work order"; teardown estimates; "A repair shop is not required by law to guarantee its work";
  mediation; what it cannot do); Civil Code § 3068 (the lien, "legally entitled", 30 days); New York
  § 398-d (estimate on request, a reasonable fee; inspect replaced parts; the insurer sign); Florida
  §§ 559.905 and 559.909 (over $150; the signed choice; "$10 or 10 percent, whichever is greater, but not
  to exceed $50"; storage charges; no keeping the car over refused unauthorised repairs; old parts);
  Michigan § 257.1332 (written estimate; "not more than 10% or $50.00, whichever is lesser"; pay only the
  actual if less; the waiver); Ohio 109:4-3-13 (over $50; written, oral or none; the ten per cent notice;
  itemised lists; replaced parts); the FTC (labour pricing; diagnostic fees; the signed estimate;
  disputes to the state Attorney General or small claims); New York's DMV complaint route and its 90-day
  or 3,000-mile limit.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why an estimate and a quote bind differently (Citizens Advice: a quote is a
  contract at a price; an estimate is a best guess), and why a state's tolerance matters only where the
  state sets one.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a garage in Malton rings to say an estimate of £320 is now £410:
  Citizens Advice's "within reason", TMO's authorisation, what to ask for in writing. (b) wrinkle, law: a
  California shop in Merced adds $180 without asking; then the same bill in Fort Myers, Florida. Gap: the
  reader computes Florida's tolerance on a $600 estimate (the greater of $10 or $60, capped at $50) before
  the answer.
- **Misconceptions:** an estimate is a fixed price, or a shop can always go 10 per cent over (#10); the
  garage can sell my car if I don't pay a disputed bill; a "no responsibility" sign means no
  responsibility (s.57; Citizens Advice); a shop must guarantee its work (BAR).
- **Practice:** compute the tolerance in four states for three described bills; write a "paying under
  protest" note; the car file's garage page (what you'll ask for before any work, in your country or
  state), behind a checkpoint.
- **Images:** none needed; a Markdown table of the five states' rules.
- **Sources:** Part C §7, (a)9, (b), (e), (f)5, 10; Part D §5, (a)5, (b), (e); Home Repair lesson 23 by
  pointer; gates G-G4, G-L4, G-L8, G-L14, G-N1.
- **Connects:** lesson 24; Home Repair lesson 23; Personal Finance lesson 10. The end of the course: a
  short section that gathers the course's stop points (Home Repair lesson 23's shape), each with whether
  it comes from the law, a handbook or advice.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's labels, applied to every job the course has named; lesson 24's

- **Neutrality:** none contested; The Motor Ombudsman labelled; Citizens Advice's recommendation of it
  attributed.
- **Tier B, Reviewer F additionally:** re-reads CRA ss.49 to 52 and 54 to 57, the 1977 Act s.12 and Sch

## Neutrality notes

The course is not a 3.4 domain, but it has three public policy questions, one argued safety margin
with commercial voices on every side, and a subject where almost all consumer-facing advice comes from
bodies that sell tyres, cover or repairs. The failures to guard against are **false certainty about the
law, one car's figure given as everyone's, silent picking between countries, silent picking between
commercial voices, and an unlabelled interest**. Every reviewer checks: that no rule is stated without
its jurisdiction and its kind (law, handbook or advice, decision 5); that no per-car figure is printed as
universal (decision 6); that neither country's advice is called wrong (decision 3); that the law and the
advice sit together where they differ (decision 10); that every industry-funded source carries its label
(decision 11); and that no mechanism or method was added from general knowledge (decision 14).

- **Smart motorways** (lesson 17). Contested empirical with a value element. National Highways'
  findings and the AA's criticism each in its own words; the ORR's framing; the policy dates as facts with
  the right word for each; the government's stated reasons for cancelling reported as its reasons; no
  verdict. Reviewer F's pass; perspectives: the operator and the DfT, the AA, a daily user.
- **Whether periodic safety inspection reduces crashes** (lesson 22). Contested empirical. GAO's
  "inconclusive" and its three studies; the state officials' view as GAO reports it; Texas's change as a
  fact; the proponents' and opponents' reasoning in their own words only if G-N5 closes; no verdict.
  Reviewer F's pass; perspectives: a state inspection official, a proponent of ending inspection, an owner.
- **Right to repair** (lesson 24). Legal and value. The statute and the votes as facts; the court case with
  its outcome stated as unknown to this course unless read; each side in its own words (G-N2) or no
  argument block at all. Pass inside Tier A; perspectives: an independent repairer, an automaker, an owner.
- **When to replace tyres above the legal floor** (lesson 8). Contested empirical with a value element;
  TyreSafe, the RAC, the AA, AAA and Michelin each in their own words, each with its interest stated once;
  the legal floor as law. Pass inside Tier A; perspectives: a road-safety charity, a tyre maker arguing for
  full use, a driver paying for tyres.
- **Practice disagreements, not policy** (lessons 5, 13, 14): the charge light, jump-lead removal,
  extension leads and charging habits. Each source attributed; the handbook governs; a fire-safety claim
  the sources do not support (80 per cent) is not taught.
- **Windows or air conditioning** (lesson 12): one line; each body attributed; no rule.
- **Buying an EV, a hybrid or a diesel**: out of scope; no side (decision 15). The AA's and RAC's DPF
  remark attributed.
- **Makers, motoring organisations, trade bodies and charities** (every lesson): cited for what each says,
  labelled for what each is (decision 11), neither endorsed nor suspected in the course's voice. National
  Highways reports on roads it runs; the AA campaigned against all-lane running; TyreSafe is backed by the
  industry it advises on; The Motor Ombudsman is funded by the garages it adjudicates; ASE and the IMI
  describe the value of their own credentials. Each label is stated once where it matters, without
  insinuation.
- **Religion.** Nothing in SOURCES bears on any tradition's teaching; the course makes no claim either
  way.

can assume the GB offences on a car's condition and s.48's defence as a worked example of strict
liability with a knowledge defence to the penalty, the Highway Code's MUST and should, and the US
state-by-state inspection and repair laws as another case of one rule changing at every border.
**Economics courses later on the Core** can assume the inspection evidence (GAO) and the credential
contrast (voluntary ASE against Michigan's certificate) as cases beside Home Repair's licensing argument.

deliberately stops short of. **Personal Finance** is earlier and is only pointed to. A future driving
course, if one is added, takes driving technique, weather driving and the driving test; this course keeps
the car's condition and the breakdown.
