S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Car Basics, on lesson ten. Lights, wipers, glass and the walk-round.

S2: The usual line first.

S1: This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes. Where today's lesson states law, it's general information, not legal advice.

S2: Lights seem easy, though. I'd notice if one was out.

S1: Some of them. But the brake lights, the rear indicators, the number-plate lamp: you can't see those from the driver's seat, and they're the ones other people depend on most. National Highways, the government-owned company that runs England's motorways and major A-roads, puts it plainly: if your indicators, brake lights or other lights aren't working properly, you're putting yourself and others at risk. And they can be a reason for your vehicle to fail its MOT.

S2: So how do I check a light I can't see?

S1: National Highways again. Ask a friend to help, or park near a reflective surface, a window or a garage door. Then, in sequence, the indicators, hazard lights, headlights and fog lights. Press the brake pedal for the brake lights, and finally select reverse for the reversing light.

S2: Reverse, with somebody standing near the car?

S1: That's where the lesson adds precautions, and they're the course's own, not National Highways'. Select reverse only with the parking brake on and your foot held firmly on the brake, with any helper standing to the side of the car, never behind it. If you can't do that, leave the reversing lamp for another day. And if the engine has to run for the lights to work, do it outdoors, never inside a garage, even with the door open.

S2: Even with the door open?

S1: That's the course's own line. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the US public-health agency, says never run your car or truck inside a garage that is attached to a house, even with the garage door open. Its words are about a garage joined to a house. This course keeps a running engine out of every garage.

S2: And take a cloth, I'm guessing.

S1: Yes, and that's law, not tidiness. The Highway Code, which applies in Great Britain, says lights, indicators, reflectors and number plates must be kept clean and clear. That's a capital-letters MUST, a legal requirement.

S2: And wiper blades? I've heard change them every year.

S1: That's from the Met Office's winter checklist, which reproduces the RAC's, and the RAC sells breakdown cover and repairs. It says it's recommended to change wiper blades every twelve months. That's advice, not law. Nissan's handbook for the twenty twenty four LEAF, sold in the US, ties it to how they work instead: check for cracks or wear if they don't wipe properly, and replace them if they're worn.

S2: Now the law. Say my number-plate lamp's out, and it's broad daylight. Isn't there a rule that a dead bulb's fine in the day?

S1: That's the half-remembered version. Here's the case in the lesson. Sunday afternoon in Welshpool, a neighbour helps with the walk-round and finds the car's only number-plate lamp is out. The owner means to drive to work on Monday morning, in daylight, and home again after dark.

S2: So which rule?

S1: Regulation twenty three of the Road Vehicles Lighting Regulations nineteen eighty nine, for England, Wales and Scotland. No one may use a vehicle on a road unless every lamp it applies to is in good working order and, for a lamp, clean. It covers lamps the car is required to have, and the rear registration plate lamp is on that list. It also covers some lamps whenever they're fitted, like every stop lamp and direction indicator.

S2: And the daylight exception?

S1: Read the words slowly, because each one narrows it. It's only between sunrise and sunset. And it's only for a lamp that became defective during the journey in progress, or where arrangements have been made to remedy the defect with all reasonable expedition.

S2: So Monday morning. The lamp died on Sunday, not on this journey.

S1: Right. So on this course's reading of the words, the exception fits the morning only if the repair has already been arranged. The regulation doesn't say what counts as reasonable expedition, and the course read nothing that does. The drive home after dark, the exception doesn't reach at all. What the owner does is theirs to decide; what the course can answer is what the regulation says.

S2: And at the MOT?

S1: That's a separate thing. The duty applies every time the car's used on a road; the test is one day a year. DVSA's manual for testers says a car with two number-plate lamps and one out is a Minor defect, so it can still pass. A single plate lamp that's out is Major, a fail. So the Welshpool car would fail.

S2: And windscreen chips at the MOT? I thought any chip fails.

S1: No. The manual has a zone A, two hundred and ninety millimetres wide, centred on the steering wheel, inside the area the wipers sweep. Testers check damage over ten millimetres across there, and over forty millimetres in the rest of the swept area. But the figures are only half the rule. Failure is only justified if the damage significantly affects the driver's view of the road. And a repair finished flush with the glass, invisible or barely detectable, doesn't count as damage at all.

S2: Give me a chip.

S1: Twelve millimetres, just right of the wheel's centre line, inside the swept area. That's inside zone A, because it's within a hundred and forty five millimetres of the wheel's centre, half of two hundred and ninety, which is the course's arithmetic. And it's over ten millimetres. So the tester looks at it, and it fails if it significantly affects the view.

S2: And in the US?

S1: Still general information. There's no single US rule; whether a car's inspected at all depends on the state. Take the same car to an inspection in Poughkeepsie, New York. The DMV's page, as archived in July twenty twenty six, has the windshield checked for presence and condition, and one rule with a number: no crack eleven inches long or longer if any part of it is within the area the wiper clears.

S2: So my twelve millimetre chip passes.

S1: Not quite. The rule doesn't reject it, because it isn't that crack. But the page also has the inspector check the windshield's condition, with no figure for a chip, so what the inspector makes of it, the course can't say from the page.
