S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Car Basics, on lesson two. The engine: petrol, diesel, and the drive to the wheels.

S2: The usual line first.

S1: This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. The rules differ between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and they change. And nothing in this lesson asks you to open the bonnet. Its exercise is done at a table, with the handbook and the service record.

S2: Why does a driver need to know what's inside an engine?

S1: Because three of the jobs in this course only make sense once you do. The coolant check, the cambelt, and a diesel's glow-plug light. The US Department of Energy puts the whole engine in a few sentences. Burning gas pushes a piston, the piston turns a crankshaft, and gears take the turning to the wheels. Most car engines do it in four strokes: intake, compression, combustion and power, and exhaust.

S2: Petrol and diesel both?

S1: Same four names, lit differently. A petrol engine is spark ignition. Fuel and air come in, the piston compresses them, and a spark from a spark plug lights the mixture. One wrinkle. Another Energy Department site, the Alternative Fuels Data Center, says the fuel is injected either into the intake manifold or straight into the combustion chamber. On the course's reading, the manifold comes before the chamber, so that fuel meets the air on the way in. Either way, the spark does the lighting.

S2: And a diesel?

S1: Compression ignition. Only air comes in, and the piston compresses it, which makes it hot. Then fuel is sprayed into that hot air, and it ignites. The compression is the lighter. That's why a diesel has no spark plugs.

S2: Then what are glow plugs? I'd assumed they were the diesel's spark plugs.

S1: That's the usual mistake. DENSO, a company that makes glow plugs, so it's writing about its own product, says that if the air is too cold, some of the fuel may not be burnt. Glow plugs provide extra heat to the air. They don't make a spark.

S2: Give me the cold morning.

S1: January in Thurso, on the north coast of Scotland. A diesel has stood outside all night. The driver switches on the ignition and the glow-plug light comes on. DENSO says to wait until it goes out before starting the engine, which gives the plugs time to heat. And if the light stays on once the engine is running, DENSO says it shouldn't be ignored. Lesson eleven takes that one.

S2: Whose rule is that?

S1: A glow-plug maker's general advice. Your handbook is the authority for yours. Waiting for the light is yours. Replacing glow plugs isn't. On this course's reading, that's a garage job at the handbook's interval, so it's call someone.

S2: Now the fuel. If I put ten litres in, how much of it actually moves the car?

S1: Most people guess high. fueleconomy.gov, the US government's fuel-economy site, says only about twelve to thirty percent of the energy in the fuel moves a conventional petrol car down the road, depending on the drive cycle. On ten litres, that's somewhere between about one and three litres' worth, by the course's own arithmetic. And most of the rest, the site says, is lost in the engine, primarily as heat. Smaller amounts go on engine friction, pumping air, and combustion inefficiency.

S2: So most of what a petrol engine does is make heat.

S1: On those figures, yes. And that's why there's a cooling system. The Highway Code, for Great Britain, says most engines are water-cooled. Toyota, in the handbook for the twenty twenty three Corolla sold in the US, says coolant is neither plain water nor straight antifreeze. And it warns, for that car, that the system may be under pressure and may spray hot coolant if the cap is removed. The Highway Code's advice, a should, not a legal must, is to wait until the engine has cooled naturally.

S2: And oil?

S1: The harder case. The RAC, a motoring organisation that sells breakdown cover and repairs, names the job: if oil isn't lubricating the engine effectively, it could lead to expensive or even irreparable damage. Toyota says an engine uses some oil up, and warns against overfilling. But how oil does its job, the mechanism, isn't in anything this course read, and the lesson won't supply it from general knowledge.

S2: Okay. The cambelt.

S1: The RAC, which also sells cambelt changes, says the camshaft opens and closes the engine's valves. The cambelt is a rubber belt with teeth that grip the cogs on the crankshaft and camshafts, and keep them in sync. Americans call it a timing belt. Same part. If it fails, the RAC says, it could result in catastrophic damage to the engine.

S2: Surely it warns you first.

S1: Not reliably. The RAC lists five signs, and says to have any of them checked as soon as possible by a qualified mechanic. Then it adds that many cambelts show no obvious symptoms before failure. So the rule is the handbook's interval: a number of years or miles, whichever comes first. And in Great Britain, the RAC says, your MOT won't check the cambelt. The course read nothing saying a US state inspection does either.

S2: Walk me through one.

S1: Someone in Middlesbrough buys a used petrol car, seven years old, fifty eight thousand miles, and no record of a belt change. In this example, with made-up figures, the handbook says six years or sixty thousand miles. With no record, the course's reading is that you treat the belt as the original. Years: seven against six. Past it. Miles: fifty eight thousand against sixty thousand. Not yet.

S2: So the odometer says two thousand miles to go.

S1: And the years ran out a year ago. Whichever comes first. The belt is due now. Working that out is yours. Changing it is call someone.

S2: Last, the gearbox.

S1: You'll meet four kinds. A manual, where you change gear, and a conventional automatic, where the car does, both with a fixed number of gears. A CVT, a continuously variable transmission, which uses a pair of variable-diameter pulleys joined by a belt or chain instead of gears. And a dual-clutch, which works much like a manual, but with two clutches and automatic shifting.

S2: Does a manual always use less fuel?

S1: fueleconomy.gov says two things. Its transmission page says automatics are less efficient than manuals, due to parasitic losses. Its myths page says the automatic version of a model often gets the same or better fuel economy now. The course's reading is that they don't contradict each other. One is about the kind of gearbox, the other about the two versions of one model you'd actually compare. So often, not always.
