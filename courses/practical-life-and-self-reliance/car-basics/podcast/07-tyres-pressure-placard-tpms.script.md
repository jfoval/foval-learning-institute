S2: Alright. Lesson one gave us two numbers. The one on the door pillar and the one on the tyre.

S1: And this lesson starts from why they differ. The sidewall figure is about the tyre. The US standard for new car tyres, FMVSS one thirty nine, defines it as the maximum cold inflation pressure to which a tyre may be inflated. Same on any car. The placard figure is about the car. FMVSS one ten ties the carmaker's pressure to the weight the car puts on each tyre: at the carmaker's recommended pressure, the normal load on a tyre can be no more than ninety four percent of its load rating. On this course's reading, the carmaker picks a pressure at which each tyre can carry what that car puts on it, with a margin.

S2: So the sidewall's a limit, not a target. And I just put a gauge on it whenever?

S1: Cold. NHTSA says cold means the car hasn't been driven for at least three hours. Annex 6 of the Highway Code, for Great Britain, says warm or hot tyres may give a misleading reading. And AAA, whose clubs sell roadside assistance, puts a size on it: a warm tyre can read up to five pounds per square inch higher. Five P S I is about a third of a bar, by the course's conversion.

S2: Give me a case.

S1: A driver fills up in Morpeth after a forty-mile drive and checks the tyres with her own gauge. Her handbook's cold figure is thirty two P S I, a number made up for the example. The fronts read thirty five. She thinks, let three out.

S1: It's the wrong move. Thirty two is a cold figure and these tyres are warm. Let them down to thirty two now and they could be a few P S I under once they cool. NHTSA says to measure cold, or compensate for the extra pressure in warm tyres. Reading NHTSA and AAA side by side, and this is this course's reading, not a sentence either one writes: a warm reading isn't a reason to let air out down to the cold figure. Leave it and check again cold.

S2: Now the dashboard light. Surely that's the real safeguard.

S1: In the US, NHTSA says all passenger cars, light trucks and vans of model year two thousand and eight or newer must have a tyre pressure monitoring system. The rule is FMVSS one thirty eight. As general information, here's what it requires. The light must come on within twenty minutes once a tyre is at or below twenty five percent under the carmaker's cold figure, or a floor set in a table, whichever is higher. On a standard-load passenger tyre that floor is twenty P S I. Extra-load and light-truck tyres have higher floors, which can be the figure that counts.

S2: So on an ordinary car, the light means I'm a quarter low?

S1: No, and this is the point people miss. On standard-load passenger tyres, the latest point the light is required to come on is twenty five percent under, or the twenty P S I floor, whichever is higher. Whether a car's system warns earlier is its maker's design, and no source this course read says how makers set it. Take a made-up placard of thirty six, on standard-load tyres. On this course's arithmetic, the light isn't required until the tyre is down to twenty seven. That's nine P S I you can lose without the standard requiring any warning.

S2: And the standard says so itself?

S1: It makes carmakers print it in the owner's manual. The system is not a substitute for proper tyre maintenance, and it's the driver's responsibility to keep the right pressure, even before the light comes on.

S2: What if it comes on and then goes out?

S1: NHTSA covers that. On cold mornings the symbol may come on briefly and then go off. Its likely explanation is a pressure that dips below the warning threshold overnight and rises as the tyres warm. So the light going out doesn't mean the tyres are fine. Check cold with a gauge. A different pattern, flashing for sixty to ninety seconds and then staying on, may mean the system itself has a fault. NHTSA says contact your dealer. The standard's manual text adds that the system may not be able to detect low pressure as intended. Having it repaired is call someone. Checking by hand until then is yours.

S2: And in Britain?

S1: This course read no UK law that requires a car to have one, and no UK rule that sets a trigger, so on a UK car the US trigger is only a comparison. What it did read is the MOT manual. It checks the system on ordinary cars, M one vehicles, first used from the first of January twenty twelve, and a system that's malfunctioning or obviously not working is a major defect, which fails the test.

S2: Last, the law on pressure itself.

S1: Still general information. In Great Britain, the Highway Code states it as a must rule in Annex 6: tyres must be correctly inflated to the vehicle manufacturer's specification for the load being carried. That's the Code's statement of the law. The regulation it cites, regulation twenty seven of the Construction and Use Regulations, says it differently: inflated so as to be fit for the use the car is being put to. The regulation's own words don't name the maker's figure. The Code's paraphrase does.
