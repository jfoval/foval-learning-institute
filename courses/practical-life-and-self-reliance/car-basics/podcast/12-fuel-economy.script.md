S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Car Basics, on lesson twelve. Fuel economy: what maintenance buys, and the figures that were withdrawn.

S2: The usual line first.

S1: This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes. Where today's lesson states law, it's general information, not legal advice. And most of today's figures are American, in US gallons and dollars, because the source that measured and revised them is American.

S2: So, fuel-saving tips. I've definitely heard that a new air filter gives you ten per cent.

S1: And correct tyre pressures give you three per cent, and fixing a faulty oxygen sensor gives you forty. They sound official, and they were. Each one was printed by fueleconomy.gov, the US government's fuel economy site. And each one was later withdrawn or cut. The course read the site's maintenance page now, and six copies the Internet Archive saved between December two thousand and seven and July twenty twenty three, so you can date when.

S2: Start with the air filter.

S1: In December two thousand and seven the page said replacing a clogged air filter could improve mileage by as much as ten per cent. By December twenty ten it had a heading, new information, and a different answer: on cars with fuel-injected, computer-controlled petrol engines, a new filter does not improve fuel economy. Today's page puts diesel engines in that line too.

S2: Why would a filter stop mattering?

S1: The site's myths page says modern fuel-injected engines have on-board computers that adjust the fuel-air ratio automatically, and it goes no further. The exception is an older car with a carburettor: the twenty ten page gave two to six per cent there normally, and up to fourteen if the filter was so clogged it significantly affected drivability.

S2: And tyres?

S1: In two thousand and seven, around three point three per cent. In twenty ten and twenty thirteen, up to three point three. Today it says you can improve your gas mileage by zero point six per cent on average, up to three per cent in some cases, and a footnote dates the change: revised June the twenty seventh, twenty sixteen. That's a cut of about five and a half times, which is the course's arithmetic, not the page's. The old figure didn't vanish. It became the top of the new range.

S2: Why such a range?

S1: The footnote says the estimates assume an average under-inflation of ten per cent across all the tyres, and twenty five per cent for the worst case. So zero point six is what fixing the average amount buys, and three is what fixing a car a quarter under buys. And on this course's reading of that footnote, a car whose tyres are already at the placard figure has nothing to fix, so checking them saves no fuel at all. It's still the check lesson seven told you to do, for other reasons.

S2: And the oxygen sensor, the forty per cent?

S1: The page said fixing a serious maintenance problem like a faulty oxygen sensor could improve mileage by as much as forty per cent, next to an average of four per cent for a tune-up. The capture of the eighth of July twenty twenty one still has both. The capture of the sixth of July twenty twenty three doesn't. None of the pages read says why they went, so the course doesn't guess. And forty was never an average. The page said as much as.

S2: This as much as, on average business. Is that the real lesson?

S1: It's half of it. NHTSA, the US road safety regulator, says properly inflating your tyres can save you as much as eleven cents a gallon. fueleconomy.gov, from the same government, puts the average benefit at three cents a gallon. They don't contradict each other. One's a best case, one's an average. When you meet a figure, ask which kind it is, and then what it assumes.

S2: Put it in money for one car.

S1: This is the course's arithmetic. A driver in Mesa does twelve thousand miles a year at thirty miles per gallon. That's four hundred gallons. Say her tyres are about ten per cent under, like the page's average car, and she starts keeping them at the placard figure. Zero point six per cent of four hundred gallons is two point four gallons. At the page's assumed price of four dollars thirty two a gallon, that's about ten dollars a year. If her tyres had been a quarter under, up to three per cent, about twelve gallons, roughly fifty dollars.

S2: Switching off at a stop, then. Doesn't restarting burn more fuel than idling?

S1: fueleconomy.gov says it only takes about ten seconds' worth of fuel to restart, and idling can use a quarter to a half gallon an hour. It says turn the engine off when the car's sitting still, except in traffic or a line where you'd restart often. The Energy Saving Trust, an independent UK organisation funded largely by government contracts, says turn it off if you'll be stationary for more than a minute.

S2: And in Great Britain it's law as well?

S1: General information, not legal advice. Rule one hundred and twenty three of the Highway Code says you must not leave a parked vehicle unattended with the engine running, or leave an engine running unnecessarily while the vehicle is stationary on a public road. The rule cites regulations ninety eight and one hundred and seven of the Road Vehicles Construction and Use Regulations nineteen eighty six, which apply in England, Wales and Scotland. Then comes a should, which is advice: generally, if you'll be stationary for more than a couple of minutes, switch off. And running it is permissible when you're stationary in traffic or diagnosing faults. Northern Ireland has its own rules, which the course didn't read, and it didn't research US anti-idling rules either.

S2: Last one. Windows or air conditioning?

S1: A real disagreement. The Energy Saving Trust says it's more fuel efficient to open a window than use air conditioning, even at higher speeds. fueleconomy.gov says windows down at lower speeds, air conditioning at highway speeds, because open windows add drag that grows with speed. It cites two published studies the course didn't read; the Energy Saving Trust gives no reason or test. Neither page gives a figure for how much one beats the other at speed, and the course doesn't settle it.
