S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Car Basics, on lesson eight. Tyres, part two: tread, damage, age, and when to replace.

S2: The usual line first.

S1: This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes. Where today's lesson states law, it's general information, not legal advice.

S2: Start with Great Britain. The limit's one point six millimetres, isn't it?

S1: Yes, but read the words. Regulation twenty seven of the Construction and Use Regulations says the grooves must be at least one point six millimetres deep throughout a continuous band across the central three-quarters of the tread, and round the entire circumference. The MOT manual says either side of that band can be bald, so on depth, a bald edge can meet the rule.

S2: And in the US?

S1: Still general information. Federal tread rules cover commercial vehicles; for private cars, tread law is set by the states. The three the course read, Pennsylvania, New York and Virginia, all use two thirty-seconds of an inch at inspection. Same number, measured differently. Take an inspection in Lynchburg, Virginia. At a front tyre's thinnest spot, two neighbouring grooves read one thirty-second each. Virginia's manual has the inspector measure at two more points round the tyre, and there they read three thirty-seconds or more. Virginia rejects only if two neighbouring grooves are under two thirty-seconds at every point. So, on this course's reading, Virginia doesn't reject it on depth, and Pennsylvania, where one such point is enough, would.

S2: One thirty-second gets through?

S1: Through Virginia's depth check, and that's all passing says. One thirty-second of an inch is below every floor and every piece of advice in this lesson. NHTSA, the US federal road safety regulator, advises that tyres are not safe and should be replaced at two thirty-seconds. This one's past that.

S2: So where's the argument?

S1: Above the floor. Everyone the lesson read agrees the floor is where each place's law puts it. And every party arguing about replacing above it agrees wet braking gets worse as a tyre wears, even the tyre maker arguing for full use. They part on where, above it, a tyre should come off. That turns on disputed facts, and on a value question: how much safety margin is worth how much money and waste. The course takes no side.

S2: Then give me each side, and what it sells.

S1: In the UK, TyreSafe, a tyre safety charity backed by tyre makers, carmakers and retailers, says tyre and safety experts recommend replacing at three millimetres. Its reason: wet grip, it says, in general decreases as the tread approaches the legal minimum. The RAC, which sells breakdown cover and tyres, says tyre and safety experts believe the legal minimum is insufficient to guarantee safety. Both cite a difference in wet braking distance of up to forty four percent between one point six millimetres and three. The course hasn't read the report behind it, so the figure is theirs. The AA, which sells breakdown cover and repairs, says you should consider replacing at three millimetres, and aim to replace before the tread wears below two.

S2: And in the US?

S1: AAA, whose clubs sell roadside assistance and run a repair network, tested new tyres against tyres worn to four thirty-seconds, on wet pavement at highway speed. The worn ones took on average eighty seven feet longer to stop in a passenger car, which AAA reports as a forty three percent increase. Its position is to replace at four thirty-seconds.

S2: And the other way?

S1: Michelin, which makes and sells tyres. It concedes ground first: wet braking performance, which it calls key to safety, decreases as tyres are used. Its case: contrary to popular belief, tread depth does not guarantee safe tyres, and the real difference lies in how well tyres are designed. It calls its approach good for the environment and consumers' budgets, because tyres still safe when worn mean changing them less often, which saves resources and reduces CO2 emissions.

S2: Put it as its holder would.

S1: This is the course's summary, not Michelin's words. Every tyre taken off at three millimetres throws away tread that was still lawful. What makes a worn tyre safe or not is how it was designed. So judge the tyre, not the depth alone.

S2: And the regulator?

S1: NHTSA sells nothing, and puts its advice at the floor: tyres are not safe and should be replaced at two thirty-seconds. That's advice, not law, at the same depth as the three states' rules. In the UK, the course found no official body giving a replacement point above Great Britain's legal floor.

S2: And the person paying for the tyres?

S1: TyreSafe, the industry-backed charity, and the AA, which sells repairs, both urge replacing before the floor, and give rough figures. TyreSafe says tyres at three millimetres may last between ten and twenty thousand miles, though actual lifespan can vary widely, and at two millimetres about a thousand. The AA says around a thousand at two. Those are estimates, not measurements. No source read puts a price on those miles, and Michelin, which makes and sells tyres, gives no figure for its case on budgets either.

S2: Can the evidence settle it?

S1: AAA's own test, from the clubs that run a repair network, was new tyres against four thirty-seconds, not four against two. AAA's tyre-care page reports a comparison of the disputed depths, from research by Tire Rack, a US online tyre retailer that runs its own tyre tests, which the course didn't read. As AAA reports it, a pickup truck at seventy miles an hour on wet pavement stopped a hundred and twenty two feet shorter, twenty four percent, on tyres passing the quarter test, at four thirty-seconds, than on tyres passing only the penny test, at two. TyreSafe's and the RAC's forty four percent comes from a report the course didn't read. Michelin, which makes and sells tyres, gives no test comparing depths. It does claim one of its own tyres, after over eighteen thousand miles of use, has a braking distance below the official level for a new tyre, with no test cited.

S2: So the disputed-depth comparisons reach us through parties who cite them, and Michelin's claim cites no test.

S1: None of it answers, from a report the course read, the question in dispute: how much stopping distance, on which tyres, replacing at three millimetres or four thirty-seconds buys over the floor. What would settle it is independent wet-braking tests across tread depths and across tyre designs, published in full. The course read none. Until then, each position is a reading of partial evidence plus a judgement about cost, and the law in each place is the only fixed line.
