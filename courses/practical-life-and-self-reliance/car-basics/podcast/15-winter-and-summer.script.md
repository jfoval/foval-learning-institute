S2: Is scraping the car law?

S1: In Great Britain, partly. The Highway Code's Rule two hundred and twenty nine has two lines marked MUST, which by the Code's convention means a legal requirement. You MUST be able to see, so clear all snow and ice from all your windows. And you MUST make sure lights are clean and number plates clearly visible and legible. All your windows, so a clear patch in front of your face doesn't meet it. The rule's other lines carry no MUST, so they're advice.

S2: Can I start the engine to defrost and go back in for a coffee?

S1: That's Rule one hundred and twenty three. Its MUST line: you MUST NOT leave a parked vehicle unattended with the engine running, or leave a vehicle engine running unnecessarily while that vehicle is stationary on a public road. On this course's reading, leaving it running on the street while you go back inside is what the first part forbids.

S2: And if I stand beside it?

S1: On the same reading, that answers only the first part. The second part is about running it unnecessarily, and this course read nothing that says whether defrosting counts. But the rule's should line, which is advice, is clear: generally, if the vehicle is stationary and is likely to remain so for more than a couple of minutes, you should apply the parking brake and switch off the engine.

S2: So switch off and scrape.

S1: And never a running engine inside a garage, even with the door open. That's the course's own line. The CDC, the US public health agency, says never run your car or truck inside a garage that is attached to a house, even with the garage door open. Its words are about a garage joined to a house. This course keeps a running engine out of every garage.

S2: And outdoors, in snow?

S1: Toyota's handbook for the twenty twenty three Corolla sold in the US says exhaust gases include harmful carbon monoxide, which is colorless and odorless. A CDC report on a blizzard in January nineteen ninety six says it can seep into the cabin through leaks or cracks in the floorboard if the exhaust pipe is blocked. One of its cases: a family digging their car out put a cold four-year-old in it with the engine and heater running, while deep snow surrounded the rear. After about thirty minutes she was found unconscious. She was treated and discharged. They were trying to warm a cold child. Clearing the pipe before the engine starts is Yours.

S2: And stuck in snow overnight?

S1: In Great Britain, on a motorway or other fast road, the steps don't change with the weather: everyone out on the side away from traffic, behind the barrier if there is one. If you can't get out safely, stay in with seat belts and hazard lights on and call nine nine nine and ask for the police. The Highway Code's Rule two hundred and seventy seven, which is advice, says do not return to your vehicle even if it's raining, cold or dark. Lesson seventeen teaches that, and this course read no UK official advice for a car stuck in snow anywhere else. The rest are US steps. NHTSA says stay with your car and don't overexert yourself, let it be seen, and make sure the exhaust pipe is clear of snow and run the car only sporadically, never for long periods with the windows up.

S2: Does everyone agree on short spells?

S1: No. Toyota's Corolla handbook says don't leave the engine running where there's snow build-up, or where it is snowing. The two agree on the danger, a blocked exhaust and long running. On this course's reading, the handbook governs its own car: if yours says don't, don't. If it says nothing, NHTSA's short spells, with the pipe checked each time, are the advice this course read, for the US.

S2: Now summer's danger, the hot car.

S1: NHTSA, the US federal road safety regulator, gives American figures. In twenty twenty five, thirty one children died of heatstroke in vehicles. More than one thousand have died of heatstroke because they were left or became trapped in a hot car over the past twenty five years. This course found no UK figure, and it doesn't borrow the American one. NHTSA gives one reason children are at such risk: a child's body temperature rises three to five times faster than an adult's.

S2: Surely five minutes in the shade with the windows down is fine.

S1: NHTSA: never leave a child in a vehicle unattended for any length of time. Rolling windows down or parking in the shade does little to change the interior temperature. There's no safe number of minutes.

S2: How does it happen?

S1: NHTSA's figures point at two ways. Over half of these deaths come from a parent or caregiver forgetting a child in the car. The answers are habits: check your entire vehicle, back seat included, before locking and walking away, and put a personal item like a bag in the back seat as a reminder. The second way: over two hundred and thirty children have died since nineteen ninety eight after getting into a car by themselves and becoming trapped. The answer is to always lock your car when you aren't using it. That's for everyone, because the child who climbs in might be a neighbour's.

S2: And in Great Britain?

S1: The Highway Code's Annex six has one line, a should, so advice: never leave children or pets in an unventilated car. It doesn't say an open window makes a car safe. This course read no law, in Great Britain, Northern Ireland or any US state, on leaving a child or an animal in a car, or on breaking into a car to reach one, and it makes no statement about either.

S2: And if I find a child alone in a car?

S1: NHTSA, for the US: act immediately and call nine one one, and a child in distress due to heat should be removed from the vehicle as quickly as possible and rapidly cooled. It doesn't say who removes the child or how. In the UK the emergency number is nine nine nine, and this course read no UK page that sets out a bystander's steps. The cooling and the signs of heatstroke are First Aid and CPR lesson fifteen, worth reading before a summer.
