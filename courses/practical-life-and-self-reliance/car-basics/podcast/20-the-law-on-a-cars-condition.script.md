S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Car Basics, on lesson twenty. The law on a car's condition, in Great Britain and Northern Ireland.

S2: The usual line first.

S1: This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes. And since this whole lesson is law, here's its label. This is general information, not legal advice. The rules differ between England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and they change.

S2: So where does it start?

S1: With two defects at the same roadside check after dark. A tyre worn below the legal depth, and a number plate lamp that doesn't light. Most drivers would call both illegal. The law in Great Britain doesn't stop there. One of them falls under a section of the Road Traffic Act nineteen eighty eight that carries three penalty points. The other, on this course's reading, falls under a later section that carries none. And an MOT pass last month changes neither, because the duty runs every time the car is used.

S2: Where does the law actually sit? I'd have said the Highway Code.

S1: It sits in three layers. The Act creates the offences. The regulations, mainly the Construction and Use Regulations nineteen eighty six, set the standards, like the tread rule. And the Highway Code restates the law and adds advice. Its Rule eighty nine covers this whole lesson in a line: "You MUST ensure your vehicle and trailer comply with the full requirements of the Road Vehicles (Construction and Use) Regulations and Road Vehicles Lighting Regulations". The Code doesn't create that duty. It points to the regulations that do.

S2: And the Act has three offences.

S1: Sections forty A, forty one A and forty two, each applying in England, Wales and Scotland. Forty A is a dangerous condition. It makes a person guilty who "uses, or causes or permits another to use, a motor vehicle or trailer on a road when", and the first thing that follows is "the condition of the motor vehicle or trailer, or of its accessories or equipment", where that condition "is such that the use of the motor vehicle or trailer involves a danger of injury to any person." It names no part.

S2: Forty one A?

S1: Narrower. It's breaking "a construction and use requirement as to brakes, steering-gear or tyres", or using a car that doesn't comply. No danger has to be shown. A tyre that breaks regulation twenty seven is enough. And section forty two reaches the other construction and use requirements, apart from two more that have sections of their own. Lamps, glass, wipers, washers and avoidable smoke are of that kind, and that a lamp falls there is this course's reading of the words.

S2: So what does each one carry?

S1: The penalties are in Schedule two of the Road Traffic Offenders Act nineteen eighty eight, and the Sentencing Act twenty twenty turns them into pounds for England and Wales. Scotland has its own scale, which the course didn't read. For a car, section forty A is a maximum of two thousand five hundred pounds, must endorse, and three points. The ban there is a must if it's within three years of an earlier forty A conviction, otherwise a may. Section forty one A is also two thousand five hundred pounds, must endorse, three points, and a ban is a may. Section forty two is one thousand pounds, no endorsement, no points, and no ban.

S2: Give me the case.

S1: A wet night near Spalding, and police stop a car. One front tyre measures under one point six millimetres across the middle of the tread, all the way round, and one of the two stop lamps is out. The tyre breaks regulation twenty seven, a requirement as to tyres, so it points to section forty one A, three points. The Sentencing Council's guideline for magistrates in England and Wales adds a line about tyres: "Fine is for each single tyre (but ordinarily only one set of points apply where several offences committed on the same occasion)." One tyre here, so one fine.

S2: And the lamp?

S1: It breaks regulation twenty three of the Lighting Regulations, which isn't about brakes, steering gear or tyres. So on this course's reading it's section forty two. One thousand pounds maximum in England and Wales, no points.

S2: Now the one everybody half knows. If I didn't know about the defect, I'm off, aren't I?

S1: No. That's section forty eight of the Offenders Act, and its own heading calls it an "Exemption from disqualification and endorsement". It isn't a defence to the offence. It tells the court not to disqualify and not to endorse. The conviction and the fine stay. For section forty one A the condition is "if he proves that he did not know, and had no reasonable cause to suspect, that the facts of the case were such that the offence would be committed." Section forty A has its own subsection, about the danger of injury.

S2: "If he proves." So it's on the driver.

S1: The burden in those words is on the person convicted, not the prosecution. And "no reasonable cause to suspect" is wider than "didn't know". On this course's reading, a defect a look would have shown gave cause to suspect, whether or not anybody looked. So, on that reading, the checks in lessons seven to ten carry legal weight in Great Britain. A driver who never looks is in a weaker position under section forty eight, not a stronger one.

S2: Does any of this reach me if I only lend the car?

S1: All three sections reach a person who "uses" the vehicle and one who "causes or permits" it to be used. The course read no case law on what "permits" requires. As this course's reading, not a statute's: if you lend a car, the tyres and lights are worth a look before the keys change hands.

S2: And Northern Ireland?

S1: None of those sections extends there, and nor do the regulations. Northern Ireland has its own Road Traffic Northern Ireland Order, of nineteen ninety five. An Order isn't an Act, and where an Act has sections, an Order has Articles. Article fifty four opens with the same words as section forty A. Article fifty six covers "a construction and use requirement as to brakes, steering-gear or tyres", and Article fifty eight the other requirements. But the course didn't read the penalties Northern Ireland attaches to those Articles, or whether it has a rule like section forty eight. So there's no penalty figure for Northern Ireland in this lesson, and a reader there checks nidirect.
