S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Car Basics, on lesson thirteen. A flat battery and jump-starting.

S2: The usual line first.

S1: This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes. If your car fails on a motorway or other fast road in Great Britain, leave at the next exit or services if you can; if you can't, go left into an emergency area or onto the hard shoulder, switch on your hazard lights, get everyone out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and don't go back to the car. Never touch the orange high-voltage cables on a hybrid or electric car. That motorway part matters today, because a jump start is never done on a motorway. Nothing in this lesson asks you to connect leads for practice. The exercise is done on paper, from your handbook.

S2: So how do I know it's the battery?

S1: Lesson five gave the signs. The AA, a motoring organisation that sells breakdown cover and repairs, says if the dash lights are flickering or don't come on, it's likely the battery is discharged. And a jump start answers only a flat twelve volt battery. It won't fix an empty tank, and it can't help a battery that's damaged.

S2: You said never on a motorway. Where does that come from?

S1: Rule two hundred and seventy seven of the Highway Code, for Great Britain, says do not attempt repairs on your vehicle on a motorway. That's advice, a should, not a must rule. And no source the course read mentions jump starts by name there. So this is the course's reading: a jump start is a repair in the sense that matters, two cars parked close, bonnets up, people standing between them, next to traffic at motorway speed. So nobody jumps a car on a motorway, a hard shoulder or an emergency area. In the US the course found no official source on jump-starting at the roadside, and its reading is the same for a freeway shoulder.

S2: Now the dangerous part. What actually explodes?

S1: Hydrogen. Nissan says explosive hydrogen gas is always present near the twelve volt battery. And the Health and Safety Executive, Great Britain's workplace safety regulator, in a leaflet written for workplaces in twenty eleven, says many explosions happen when batteries are being connected or disconnected. The sparks produced when that's done incorrectly may cause the battery to explode.

S2: So where does the last clamp go?

S1: On metal away from the battery. HSE, for the case where no maker's instructions are available, says unpainted metal on the dead car's chassis or engine, at least fifteen centimetres from the battery, and away from fuel and brake lines. That's about six inches, which is the course's conversion. Nissan says body ground, not to the twelve volt battery. Ford and the AA say the same kind of place.

S2: Give me the order, then.

S1: I'll give it as the handbooks give it, because the lesson teaches it on paper and not as one order for every car. Ford's handbook for the twenty twenty four Maverick, sold in the US and Canada, has four steps: positive to the flat battery's positive, the other end to the donor's positive, negative to the donor's negative, and the last clamp on metal away from the battery. Toyota's for the US twenty twenty three Corolla has the same four in the same order. Nissan's drawing for the US twenty twenty four LEAF numbers the same sequence, ending on body ground.

S2: So everyone agrees.

S1: On two things. Every source read runs positive to positive first, and puts the last clamp on metal away from the flat battery. But not on which end of the red lead goes on first. Ford, Toyota and Nissan start at the flat battery. HSE describes the lead without saying which end. And the AA's page gives both, one way in its steps and the other when you run its list for taking the leads off backwards. So there's no single correct order to carry from car to car. HSE's jump-starting section opens with the rule: whenever possible, follow the manufacturer's instructions. The AA says the same of its own steps.

S2: And taking them off?

S1: Every source read takes them off in the reverse of the order they went on. Where they disagree is whether the engines are running. Ford's and Toyota's steps never switch either engine off before the leads come off, which the course reads as both running. Nissan keeps the LEAF in its ready-to-drive state. HSE says stop the engine of the good vehicle and leave the other one running, and it says why: the battery is still flat, so you will need to drive several miles before it has enough energy to restart the engine. The AA, which sells breakdown cover, switches both engines off first, and it gives its own reason: removing the leads with the engines running can cause serious damage to the cars' electronics.

S2: Two reasons pointing opposite ways. Which one's right?

S1: The course doesn't say. HSE and the AA each give a reason, neither answers the other, and the lesson won't invent a reconciliation. What's established is narrower. The handbook for the car you're rescuing governs whether its engine runs, and the donor's handbook governs the donor. If your handbook is silent, the course's reading is that it's a question for the maker's dealer or a garage that works on that make, not a gap to fill with whichever rule you heard last.

S2: Last thing. Electric cars. Can they be jumped?

S1: The small battery can, the big one can't. NHTSA, the US road-safety regulator, says the high-voltage battery in electric cars and hybrids cannot be jumped, but in most cases the twelve volt battery can. And Nissan says jump starting does not charge the LEAF's Li-ion battery, the big one. It still has to be charged before the car can be driven.

S2: And can my electric car rescue my neighbour?

S1: An electric car shouldn't be the donor. Nissan says the LEAF cannot be used as a booster vehicle, because it cannot supply enough power to start a gasoline engine, though a gasoline car can jump the LEAF's twelve volt battery. The AA, of the donor car in general, says avoid a hybrid or electric car, as this could cause damage. Nissan's statement is about the LEAF, and the AA's is general advice. The course read no maker that says the reverse.

S2: What if I've got one of those portable booster packs?

S1: It changes the donor, not the conditions for a jump start. Ford's handbook sends you to the pack maker's own instructions. The AA's page gives its own booster steps and defers to the car's handbook where they differ. And the course recommends no pack.
