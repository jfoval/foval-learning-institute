S1: This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes. If your car fails on a motorway or other fast road in Great Britain, leave at the next exit or services if you can; if you can't, go left into an emergency area or onto the hard shoulder, switch on your hazard lights, get everyone out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and don't go back to the car. In the US, pull as far onto the right shoulder as you can, switch on your hazard lights and stay in with your seat belt on, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind, in which case get out on the side away from traffic; call nine one one in an emergency. And the clause this lesson belongs to: never put any part of your body under a car held up only by a jack.

S2: So where can I change a wheel?

S1: Changing a wheel is Yours, on conditions, and the first is the place. Not on a motorway or other fast road, and not on a hard shoulder or in an emergency area. For motorways in Great Britain, the Highway Code's Rule two hundred and seventy seven, which is advice, says do not attempt repairs on your vehicle. Green Flag, which sells breakdown cover and repairs, says not to change a tyre on a motorway even on the hard shoulder or in an emergency area. On other fast roads in Great Britain the callout's steps apply, so on this course's reading there's no wheel change there either, lay-by or not. On a US freeway shoulder, this course's reading is the same.

S2: And everywhere else?

S1: Firm, level ground: Toyota's handbook says a hard, flat surface, and Honda's and Mazda's say much the same. Off the road, with nobody between the car and the traffic. Mazda has everyone out and away from the vehicle and traffic, and in Great Britain Rule two hundred and seventy six, again advice, says don't stand between your vehicle and oncoming traffic. So on this course's reading, a flat on the traffic side with traffic passing close is Call someone, however good the ground.

S2: Then the engine off.

S1: Off in the way that car's handbook describes. A hybrid or electric car can be silent and still on, and so can a petrol car whose stop-start has stopped the engine. Ready isn't off, and silence proves nothing. Toyota says don't start the engine or drive while the car is on the jack, and Mazda gives the reason: starting the engine or shaking the car could make it fall off the jack. Then hazard lights on, and everyone out. Toyota: do not raise the vehicle while someone is inside. Mazda: an occupant could cause the vehicle to fall.

S2: Now the chock. Surely that's optional if it's in P with the handbrake on?

S1: No, and Mazda says why. Without wheel blocks the vehicle may move and fall off the jack, even in P, or in first or reverse. The chock goes against the wheel diagonally opposite the flat. Honda and Mazda put a block both in front of it and behind it. Toyota's diagram shows one, behind it for a front flat, in front for a rear flat. No chock in the boot? Honda allows a rock, and Mazda rocks or wood blocks of sufficient size. On this course's reading a small stone isn't one, and if there's nothing solid to use, the job ends and you call for help.

S2: Then the nuts.

S1: All three have you loosen each about one turn while the wheel is still on the ground, and Mazda adds, don't remove any until the tyre is off the ground. None says why. And if a nut won't turn with the car's own wheel brace, on this course's reading the job ends there. No handbook read offers a way to force one.

S2: Now the jack.

S1: The maker's jack, at the maker's jacking point. Honda says other jacks may not support the weight or fit the jacking point, and Mazda that at other positions the vehicle could slip off the jack and seriously injure or even kill someone.
