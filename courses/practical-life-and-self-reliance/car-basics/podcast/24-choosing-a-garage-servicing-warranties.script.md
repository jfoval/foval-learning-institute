S2: I grew up on an oil change every three thousand miles.

S1: CalRecycle, California's waste and recycling agency, says that figure no longer applies to most cars. What replaces it is your handbook, which often gives more than one schedule. Nissan's handbook for the twenty twenty four LEAF, sold in the US, lists the conditions for the severe one, among them repeated short trips under five miles and short trips in freezing weather. The test is one sentence: if your vehicle is mainly operated under the severe conditions, follow the severe use maintenance intervals. On Nissan's wording, a car used mainly for a short town commute somewhere with cold winters is severe use. That's one handbook's list, and yours may define it differently.

S2: Now the argument. Dealer or independent?

S1: No source this course read compares them on quality or safety. It's a question of value and preference, with interested voices, so each side in its own words and no verdict. The makers first. Mazda, in its European handbook: Authorised Mazda technicians and genuine Mazda parts are dedicated to your vehicle. Ford, under the heading Why Maintain Your Vehicle at Your Dealership: Service technicians participate in extensive factory-sponsored certification training to help them become experts on the operation of your vehicle. The makers' interest: their dealers do this work and sell these parts, and the maker answers for the car under its warranty.

S2: And the independents.

S1: The Auto Care Association, the US trade body for independent parts makers, sellers and repair shops: We fight to secure choice and competition in the auto repair industry to put vehicle maintenance decisions back in the vehicle owners' hands. In the UK, YourCar-YourChoice, a campaign by the independent aftermarket, administered by its trade body: Choosing an independent garage or autocentre could save you time and money. Their interest: both bodies' members sell the parts and the service.

S2: So where do they meet?

S1: Every voice agrees the schedule and the right parts matter; they differ on whether the maker's network is the best way to get them. What would settle it is the same jobs at both kinds of garage, checked independently.

S2: But if I go independent, don't I lose my warranty?

S1: What people miss: the law binds the warrantor, not the owner. In the US it's federal law, and the FTC, the federal consumer regulator, puts it plainly: it's illegal for a dealer to deny your warranty coverage because you had routine maintenance or repairs done by someone else. If the warranty provides work or parts free, the maker can choose where. And if an aftermarket part is faulty or badly fitted and damages a covered part, cover for that damage can be refused, but the manufacturer or dealer must prove the part caused it.

S2: And the UK?

S1: It's competition law between makers and their dealer networks. The Competition and Markets Authority's guidance says a warranty made conditional on non-warranty servicing being done only by the maker's authorised network is likely to be caught by the ban on anti-competitive agreements. Likely. A maker can still legitimately refuse a claim causally linked to a repairer's incorrect work, or to a third-party part that failed. For an extended warranty sold years after the car, it says independents seem less likely to be shut out. The guidance speaks of the consumer's right to use an independent without creating one, and this course read no UK consumer statute on the point.

S2: So what do I do?

S1: On this course's reading of the guidance, servicing by an independent to the maker's schedule, with parts of matching quality and a record of the work, shouldn't by itself cost you the warranty. An extended warranty bought later may require the dealer, so read its terms. And a bad repair or a failed third-party part can still get a claim refused. Keep every invoice.

S2: Right to repair.

S1: Massachusetts requires carmakers to sell owners and independent repairers the repair information and tools they give dealers. A twenty twenty ballot added that cars from model year twenty twenty two that send data wirelessly to the maker must give owners, and independents they authorise, access to the car's mechanical data. Seventy five percent voted yes. The carmakers' trade association sued. A federal court entered judgment in February twenty twenty five and the association appealed, but this course read the docket, not the decision, so the outcome is unknown to it.

S2: The case for the law?

S1: The ballot committee for it, Massachusetts Right to Repair, in the state's voter guide, which says the state does not endorse these arguments: as new cars become more computerized auto manufacturers are using a loophole to restrict access to data needed to diagnose problems, make repairs, and perform maintenance. And: It's your car, you paid for it, you should get it fixed where you want. The Auto Care Association adds: Without that data, independent shops can't fully service today's connected vehicles. Auto Care's interest: independent shops and parts sellers gain the work.

S2: And against?

S1: The committee against, the Coalition for Safe and Secure Data, in the same guide: Question one is a data grab by third parties who want to gather your personal vehicle information and access it remotely, including location data in real time. The Alliance for Automotive Innovation, the carmakers' trade association, in its complaint: Massachusetts's new Data Law will reduce the security of these systems. And: consumers already enjoy the freedom to have their vehicles repaired at the shops of their choice. The Alliance's interest: the makers' dealers compete for the work, and the makers answer for the systems' security.

S2: So who's right?

S1: The course takes no side, and didn't read who funded either ballot committee. Part of it is contested empirical: does an open platform make cars less secure, and do independents in fact lack data they need? Neither argument quoted cites a measurement, and the course read none. Independent security testing, and evidence of which repairs independents can and can't do, would settle that part. Part is legal, and that's for the court. And part is value: who should control the data a car sends. Voters answered that for Massachusetts, which decided the statute, not whether it survives the court or is wise.
