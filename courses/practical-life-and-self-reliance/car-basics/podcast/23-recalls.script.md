S2: So what is a recall, really? The letter looks like marketing.

S1: It's a maker admitting, in writing, that something it built is unsafe, or fails a safety standard, and offering to fix it. GOV.UK says a serious safety defect is likely to cause injury or death, and happens suddenly and without warning. Things found in routine servicing don't count. In the US, NHTSA lists ordinary wear, like brake pads and batteries, among defects it doesn't consider safety related. So worn pads are a job for a garage, not a recall.

S2: How do I check my own car?

S1: Checking is yours, in both countries, and it's free. In the UK, GOV.UK's recall check takes the car's registration number. Parts and accessories, like child car seats and tyres, have their own search, by maker and model. In the US, NHTSA's lookup takes a VIN or a licence plate. The VIN is seventeen characters, on the lower left of the windshield, and on the registration card. A car with nothing open shows zero unrepaired recalls associated with this VIN.

S2: And zero means I'm clear.

S1: Not quite. NHTSA's search won't show six things. A recall already repaired. Some recently announced recalls where not all VINs have been identified yet. Recalls more than fifteen years old, except where a maker offers more coverage. Recalls by small makers. Non-safety campaigns. And recalls on international vehicles.

S2: Fifteen years. Is that the same fifteen years as some law?

S1: The same number as the free-remedy limit in the law. But the lookup counts the recall's age, and the law counts the car's. And NHTSA doesn't say whether one comes from the other. Its advice is to register your vehicle, tyres, car seats and equipment, and check recalls twice a year. GOV.UK gives no list like that for the UK checker, so this course can't say what, if anything, it misses. On this course's reading, the same logic applies: a lookup shows what the maker has reported and loaded, and a check twice a year costs nothing.

S2: What does the fix cost me?

S1: Usually nothing, within limits. In the UK, GOV.UK says you will not usually have to pay for any repairs or parts under a safety recall. Read the usually as written. It's GOV.UK's word, and DVSA's code itself doesn't state that recall work is free. In the US it's a statute, section three oh one twenty of title forty nine of the US Code. The maker shall remedy the defect without charge. But not if the car was bought by its first purchaser more than fifteen calendar years before notice. For a tyre, the limit is five calendar years from first purchase, and a tyre must be presented within a hundred and eighty days of the notice, or of a replacement being available, if that's later.

S2: Does every recall mean I stop driving?

S1: No. DVSA's code sorts a maker's response into four actions, and two matter here. A Safety Recall, Stop Drive, is where the definition of a safety defect is met, and there's an immediate threat to safety, so the vehicle must not be driven. A plain Safety Recall is where the threat isn't immediate, or can be mitigated with reasonable consumer action. Now GOV.UK, on the owner's side. If your vehicle has a serious safety defect, the manufacturer will write to you to tell you what to do. You must not drive the vehicle.

S2: That sounds like every recall, then.

S1: GOV.UK's page doesn't say which recalls that last sentence covers. On this course's reading, it goes with the letter. The code's table is what separates a stop-drive recall from the rest, and the letter says which one yours is. If the letter doesn't make clear you may keep driving until the repair, ask the maker before you drive the car.

S2: And in the US?

S1: The words are Do Not Drive. NHTSA, on the Takata airbags: Do Not Drive warnings have been issued for certain vehicles that are at a far higher risk for an air bag explosion that could injure or kill vehicle occupants. These vehicles can and should be repaired immediately.

S2: Could I just switch the airbag off while I wait for the part?

S1: NHTSA's answer is flat. It's much more likely that your air bag will perform properly and protect you in a crash than cause harm, and an air bag that is purposely disabled has a one hundred percent chance of failing to provide any protection in a crash. The airbag is a call to the dealer.

S2: What about an old recall on a used car?

S1: Someone buys a used car in Louth, in England. The check shows a safety recall from twenty nineteen, never done. The letters went to the previous keeper's old address. DVSA's code says a recall can be closed for reporting purposes once a high proportion, normally ninety percent, of vehicles believed to be in service are fixed and the remaining risk is low. But the recall is still in place, and makers are still expected to act on it if a vehicle is presented. So she books it with the maker's dealer, and GOV.UK says she will not usually have to pay.

S2: Why did the letters go astray?

S1: Records go stale. UK makers may ask DVLA to find registered keepers, so her details on the log book decide where the next letter goes. In the US, makers notify registered owners by first class mail. Tyres are the weak link, because the tyre maker doesn't know who bought them unless someone tells it. So when you buy tyres in the US, ask whether they've been registered.
