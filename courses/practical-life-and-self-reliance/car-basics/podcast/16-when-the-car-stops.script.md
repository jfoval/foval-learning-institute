S1: This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes. If your car fails on a motorway or other fast road in Great Britain, leave at the next exit or services if you can; if you can't, go left into an emergency area or onto the hard shoulder, switch on your hazard lights, get everyone out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and don't go back to the car. If you're stuck in a live lane or can't get out safely, stay in with seat belts and hazard lights on and call nine nine nine and ask for the police. In the US, pull as far onto the right shoulder as you can, switch on your hazard lights and stay in with your seat belt on, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind, in which case get out on the side away from traffic; call nine one one in an emergency. Those fast roads are lesson seventeen's. Today is every other road, and what to do at an incident on any road.

S2: So on an ordinary road, where should I stop?

S1: The Highway Code, which covers England, Scotland and Wales, says in Rule two hundred and seventy five to try to stop in a place of relative safety, where you're less likely to be at risk from moving traffic. The safest is somewhere designed for parking. Lay-bys and hard shoulders count too, though the Code says hard shoulders give less protection. And relative is the word to hold on to. Less likely, not free of it.

S2: And once I'm stopped, I stand next to the car.

S1: No. The same rule says keep well away from your vehicle and moving traffic where possible, because traffic could collide with your vehicle, forcing it into you. The car isn't a shield. It's the thing that gets pushed.

S2: Rule two hundred and seventy six is the breakdown rule. How much of it is law?

S1: None of it. The Code's own convention is that legal requirements say must or must not, and the rest is advice, which can still be used in evidence in court proceedings under the Traffic Acts. Rule two hundred and seventy six never says must. So all of it is advice, from hazard lights if you're causing an obstruction to not standing between your car and oncoming traffic.

S2: And the warning triangle?

S1: The Code advises one on the road at least forty five metres, a hundred and forty seven feet, behind the car, on the same side, never on motorways, and placed with great care. This course reads great care as a real condition. Carrying it back means walking towards the traffic you're warning, so if there's no verge or pavement to walk on, this course's reading is that it stays in the boot, the trunk in the US. No source read says to put it out at any cost. Northern Ireland has its own Highway Code, close to this one but not the same. This lesson doesn't teach it, and a reader there follows that Code.

S2: Now the US. Same advice?

S1: No, and you follow the advice for the road you're on. AAA Club Alliance, whose clubs sell roadside assistance, says pull over as far to the right as you're able and turn on your hazard lights. Then, as the callout says, stay in, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind, and in those two cases get out on the side away from traffic. Their reason for staying in is that airbags, side curtain bags and your seat belt protect you if the car is struck. If you do get out, they say leave by the passenger side, wait beyond the guardrail if there is one, and stay at least forty feet from the car. That's about twelve metres, on this course's conversion.

S2: Why does the US say stay in and Great Britain say get away?

S1: No source read gives a reason, and the course doesn't supply one.
