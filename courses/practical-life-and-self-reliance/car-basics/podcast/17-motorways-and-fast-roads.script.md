S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Car Basics, on lesson seventeen. Motorways and fast roads: go left, the live lane, and the smart motorway question.

S2: The usual line first.

S1: This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes. Follow the advice for the road you're on. Northern Ireland has its own Highway Code, with a different motorway breakdown rule. This lesson doesn't teach it, and a reader there follows that Code.

S2: So my car's losing power on a motorway.

S1: Rule two hundred and seventy seven of the Highway Code, for Great Britain, has three headings: Go left, Get safe and Get help. Leave at the next exit or services if possible. If you can't, move into the left lane, pull into an emergency area or onto a hard shoulder if you can. Where the hard shoulder is now a lane, emergency areas are the places to stop, and the Code marks them with blue signs with an orange SOS telephone symbol. Stop as far left as possible, wheels turned left, just beyond an emergency telephone if you can. Hazard lights on, and sidelights if it's dark or visibility is poor. Almost all of it is should, which is advice.

S2: Then Get safe.

S1: Get out by the side furthest from traffic, if it's safe and possible, and passengers too. Get behind a safety barrier where there is one. Don't stand where your car could be forced into you, and don't go back to it, even if it's raining, cold or dark. No repairs, and no warning triangle on the carriageway.

S2: And if I can't? Say I've stopped in a live lane.

S1: If you can't get the car to the left lane or a place of relative safety, and can't get out safely to get well away from it and the traffic, stay in, keep seat belts and hazard lights on, and call nine nine nine immediately and ask for the police, or press your SOS button if the car has one. Rule two hundred and seventy nine says the same for a driver with a disability that stops them following the steps, and National Highways, the government-owned company that runs England's motorways and major A-roads, widens it to anyone in the car who can't, for any reason. On this course's reading, stay belted in because stepping out puts a person in the lane, and call at once because the call is how the lane gets closed.

S2: Now Get help, from an emergency area.

S1: Use the free emergency telephone, which goes straight to National Highways' control room. From a mobile, call National Highways on oh three hundred, one two three, five thousand. That number covers England's roads only. Face the traffic while you speak. Then your breakdown service. Leaving an emergency area is a must under Rule two hundred and seventy eight: use the telephone and follow the operator's advice, because a lane may need to be closed.

S2: And the US?

S1: California's official driver handbook, for its freeways, says pull onto the right shoulder and stay inside with your seat belt on until help arrives, unless there's not enough space on the shoulder, a guardrail, or an area to stay safely away from the lanes. Then get out on the right side, away from traffic, and stay away. And in the US, nine one one in an emergency.

S2: Now, were smart motorways safe?

S1: Informed people disagree, and the course doesn't settle it. What's settled: in January twenty twenty two the Department for Transport paused new all-lane running schemes, and in April twenty twenty three it cancelled all new smart motorways, citing the current lack of public confidence felt by drivers and cost pressures. That release claims no finding that they were less safe. Existing ones stay.

S2: The case that they're as safe or safer?

S1: National Highways, which runs the roads it reports on, in its fourth-year report: overall, all three types of smart motorway continue to be better than conventional motorways on the measures which consider deaths or serious injuries, though no one type, smart or conventional, is ranked best on every measure. The hard shoulder does not provide a completely safe place to stop: from twenty eighteen to twenty twenty two, one in every eighteen motorway deaths involved one. Against the same road before its upgrade, in most cases smart motorways are safer than the roads they replaced, though at some, rates rose. And they were built to increase capacity without widening the road.

S2: And the case against?

S1: The AA, which sells breakdown cover and repairs, through its president Edmund King: stopped vehicle incidents are more frequent on all-lane running motorways than on motorways with hard shoulders, so the hard shoulder needs to be reinstated, or at the very least emergency areas radically increased. The RAC, which also sells breakdown cover and repairs: the hard shoulder is by no means a safe location, but in the event of a breakdown, it is far safer than being stranded in a live lane of traffic, waiting for the red X closed lane sign to be turned on and then for other drivers to do the right thing and move into another lane.

S2: Did any families speak?

S1: One the course read in her own words: Meera Naran, described by the Department as an independent road safety campaigner, whose eight-year-old son died in a motorway crash in twenty eighteen. As the Department's release gives them: Conventional and smart motorways both have their risks and benefits. I welcome this pause in the rollout of smart motorways, which will give us all a positive opportunity to assess the future of our motorway network. Her words welcome the pause; they don't ask for the hard shoulder back. She's one campaigner, in words a government release chose to print.

S2: And the numbers?

S1: The operator's figures, twenty eighteen to twenty twenty two, per hundred million vehicle miles. Injury collisions with a stopped vehicle: nought point two nine on all-lane running, nought point one nine on conventional, and the report is confident conventional's is lower. Killed or seriously injured, all collisions: one point three nine conventional, one point three eight all-lane running. But the report says those rates cannot be tested yet. Its test needs whole-number counts, and these are estimates, adjusted because police forces have recorded injury severity in different ways.

S2: So what would settle it?

S1: Partly time: several years of stopped-vehicle and killed-or-seriously-injured rates after the detection fixes and extra emergency areas, tested once police reporting allows, and, on this course's reading, checked by someone other than the operator. No data settles the value half: how to weigh doing as well or better on deaths and serious injuries overall, on the operator's figures, against more injury collisions with stopped cars, what the extra capacity is worth, and how much drivers' confidence should count. The course takes no side.
