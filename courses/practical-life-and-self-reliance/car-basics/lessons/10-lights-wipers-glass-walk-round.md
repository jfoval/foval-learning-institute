There are lights on your car you can't see from the driver's seat, and they're the ones other people
depend on most. The brake lights and the rear indicators tell the driver behind what you're about to
do; the number-plate lamp and the rear lights tell them you're there at all. National Highways, the
government-owned company that runs England's motorways and major A-roads, puts it plainly: "If your
indicators, hazard lights, headlights, fog lights, reverse lights or brake lights aren't working
properly, you're putting yourself and others at risk." And, it adds, "They can be a reason for your
vehicle to fail its MoT."[1] The windscreen (windshield in the US) and the wipers are the same bargain the other way round:
they're how you see. This lesson is the walk-round that checks all of it, and the law in Great
Britain that sits behind each item.

:::callout Before the car lets you down
This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. If your car fails on a motorway or other fast road in Great Britain, leave at the next exit or services if you can; if you can't, go left into an emergency area or onto the hard shoulder, switch on your hazard lights, get everyone out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and don't go back to the car. If you're stuck in a live lane or can't get out safely, stay in with seat belts and hazard lights on and call 999 and ask for the police. In the US, pull as far onto the right shoulder as you can, switch on your hazard lights and stay in with your seat belt on, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind, in which case get out on the side away from traffic; call 911 in an emergency. Never put any part of your body under a car held up only by a jack, and never touch the orange high-voltage cables on a hybrid or electric car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

## The walk-round

The practical part comes first, because the law makes more sense once you have stood behind your own
car and looked.

### The lights

National Highways gives the method, and it solves the problem in the first paragraph: "Ask a friend to
help you, or park your vehicle near a reflective surface eg a window or garage door." Then, "In
sequence, turn on your indicators, hazard lights, headlights and fog lights to check they're
working", and "Press the brake pedal to check your brake lights, and finally select reverse to
ensure your reverse light is working."[1]

Nissan's handbook for the 2024 LEAF, sold in the US, covers the same ground in its own list and adds two things: "Clean
the headlights on a regular basis. Make sure that the headlights, stop lights, taillights, turn
signal lights, and other lights are all operating properly and installed securely. Also check
headlight aim."[2] Aim is the one item on that list a walk-round can't settle. The MOT checks it in
Great Britain, and lesson 21, "The MOT and Northern Ireland's vehicle test", covers the test.

Two lights are easy to forget. The number-plate lamp, which only comes on with the other lights and
sits where you never look. And the reversing lamp, which needs reverse selected. Both precautions
that follow are this course's own, not National Highways'. Select reverse only with the parking
brake on and your foot held firmly on the brake, with any helper standing to the side of the car,
never behind it. If that isn't possible, leave the reversing lamp for another day. And if the engine
has to run for the lights to work, do this outdoors, never inside a garage, even with the door open.
The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the US public-health agency, says: "Never run
your car or truck inside a garage that is attached to a house, even with the garage door
open."[14] Its words are about a garage joined to a house; this course keeps a running engine out
of every garage. Home Repair and Maintenance lesson 11, "Carbon monoxide and the alarms that warn
you", explains why.

The Highway Code, which applies in Great Britain, adds that "lights, indicators, reflectors, and
number plates MUST be kept clean and clear".[3] That is a MUST, a legal requirement, and it is why the
walk-round includes a cloth. In winter, lesson 15, "Winter and summer", takes up clearing snow and ice
from them.

### Glass

Look through the windscreen from the driver's seat, then walk round and look at it from outside,
along the glass. You're looking for chips (small, roughly round pits) and cracks (lines), and for
anything that blocks the view. Nissan's handbook says "Check the windshield at least every six months
for cracks or other damage", and adds, "Have a damaged windshield repaired by a qualified repair
facility."[2] Repairing glass is **Call someone**; finding the damage early, while it may still be a
small repair, is **Yours**.

Nissan also gives a test for clean glass that needs nothing but a hose: "Your windshield is clean if
beads do not form when rinsing with clear water."[2]

### Wipers and blades

The RAC's winter checklist (the RAC sells breakdown cover and repairs), as the Met Office page
reproduces it, says to "check your wipers are doing the
job by spraying some washer fluid and checking they clean the windscreen", then run your fingers
along the blades "to check for splits". It adds: "It's recommended to change wiper blades every 12
months."[4] That is advice, not law, and it comes from that checklist, on a weather service's
page.

Nissan's handbook ties the blades to how they work rather than to the calendar: "Check for cracks or
wear if they do not wipe properly", and "Replace the wiper blades if they are worn." It also says
what a worn blade does: "Worn windshield wiper blades can damage the windshield and impair driver
vision."[2] Ford's handbook for the 2024 Maverick, sold in the US and Canada, answers the question
"Why are there streaks and smears on the windshield?" the same way: "The wiper blades could be
dirty, worn or damaged." Ford also gives a reason to spray before you wipe: "Do not operate the wipers on a dry
windshield. This could scratch the glass or damage the wiper blades."[5]

Changing a blade is **Yours, on conditions**: where your handbook shows how for your car, and only
by its method. Nissan's and Ford's handbooks each describe it for their own car, with steps that
differ. This course doesn't generalise from them, and teaches nothing on the wiper arms themselves.
Washer fluid was lesson 9's, "Under the bonnet: oil, coolant, brake fluid and screenwash".

## The law on lamps, in Great Britain

This is general information, not legal advice.

On a Sunday afternoon in Welshpool, a neighbour helps with the walk-round and reports that the car's
only number-plate lamp is out. Everything else works. The owner means to drive to work on Monday
morning, in daylight, and home again after dark. Two questions: which rule reaches that lamp, and do
the words of the daylight exception fit the plan? The rules follow; try to answer before the
checkpoint below.

The rule is regulation 23 of the Road Vehicles Lighting Regulations 1989, which applies in England,
Wales and Scotland. [Regulation 23(1)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1989/1796/regulation/23)
says: "No person shall use, or cause or permit to be used, on a road a vehicle unless every lamp,
reflector, rear marking and device to which this paragraph applies is in good working order and, in
the case of a lamp, clean."[6] It applies to two groups. The first is lamps a vehicle is required by
the Regulations to have, and the list includes headlamps, front and rear position lamps (your
handbook will show which switch position lights them), the rear
registration plate lamp, the rear fog lamp, reflectors, daytime running lamps and the reversing lamp.
The second is lamps it has, whether required or not, and that list includes "every" stop lamp (the
brake lights), direction indicator, hazard warning signal device and front fog lamp "with which it is fitted".[6]
Which lamps your car is required to have is set elsewhere in the Regulations, which this course
didn't read.

Then comes the exception people half-remember as "you're allowed a dead bulb in daylight".
Regulation 23(3)(c) says the duty doesn't apply to "a defective lamp, reflector, dim-dip device or
headlamp levelling device on a vehicle in use on a road between sunrise and sunset, if any such lamp,
reflector or device became defective during the journey which is in progress or if arrangements
have been made to remedy the defect with all reasonable expedition".[6]

Read the words slowly, because each one narrows it. It is only between sunrise and sunset. And it is
only for a lamp that failed during the journey you are on, or one whose repair has already been
arranged "with all reasonable expedition". The regulation doesn't list which lamps the exception
covers; it says "a defective lamp". Nothing in it covers the trip home after dark.

:::checkpoint Back to Welshpool. Which rule reaches the number-plate lamp, and do the words of the daylight exception fit each half of the owner's plan? Answer as "which rule, and what its words say", not "is this allowed".

Regulation 23(1) reaches the rear registration plate lamp, which is on the list of lamps it applies to
where the Regulations require one. For Monday morning, the exception's words need either a lamp that
failed during the journey in progress, which this one didn't (it was dead on Sunday), or repair
arrangements already made "with all reasonable expedition"; on this course's reading of the words,
the exception fits the morning only if the second is true, and the regulation doesn't say what
counts as reasonable expedition; this course read nothing that does. For the drive home after dark, the
exception's words don't reach at all, because they apply only between sunrise and sunset. What the
owner does is theirs to decide; the question this course can answer is what the regulation says.
:::

The Highway Code adds two MUST lines from its Annex 6, citing the Lighting Regulations among other
laws: the lights, indicators, reflectors and number plates must be kept clean and clear, and "lights
MUST be properly adjusted to prevent dazzling other road users."[3]

### What a dead lamp is at the MOT

The MOT, Great Britain's periodic test, is a separate thing from the duty above: the duty applies
every time the car is used on a road, and the test is one day a year. DVSA's inspection manual for
testers sorts lamp defects into its categories, and a Major one fails the test.[7]

:::predict Two cars at the MOT. One has two number-plate lamps, and one of them is out. The other has a single number-plate lamp, and it's out. Will the manual treat the two the same?

No. The first is Minor, and the car can still pass; the second is Major, a fail. The manual's two rows
turn on whether any lamp is still lighting the plate. It gives no reason beyond that, and the table
below has its words.
:::

| Lamp | Minor (the car can still pass) | Major (a fail) |
|---|---|---|
| Headlamp | "up to ½ light sources not functioning in the case of LED" | "missing, inoperative or more than ½ not functioning in the case of LED" |
| Stop lamp | "with a multiple light source up to 1/2 not functioning" | "missing, inoperative or in the case of a multiple light source more than 1/2 not functioning"; all of them out is Dangerous |
| Direction indicator | "with a multiple light source up to 1/2 not functioning" | "missing, inoperative or in the case of a multiple light source more than 1/2 not functioning" |
| Rear number-plate lamp | a lamp or light source out "when rear registration plate has 2 or more lamps or light sources" | a lamp or light source out "when rear registration plate has only one lamp or all lamps not working" |

Source: DVSA, MOT inspection manual, section 4, read 26 September 2026.[7]

So the Welshpool car, with its single plate lamp out, would fail. Lesson 21 covers
the whole test and what Minor and Major mean for the certificate.

Northern Ireland has its own road traffic rules, which this course didn't read, and its own vehicle
test, which lesson 21 covers.

## The law on glass and wipers, in Great Britain

This is general information, not legal advice.

Two parts of the Road Vehicles (Construction and Use) Regulations 1986, which apply in England, Wales
and Scotland, cover what you see through. [Regulation 30](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1986/1078/regulation/30)
has two halves that are easy to run together. Its first paragraph is about how a car is built: it
"shall be so designed and constructed" that the driver has a full view ahead. Its third is about
upkeep: "All glass or other transparent material fitted to a motor vehicle shall be maintained in
such condition that it does not obscure the vision of the driver while the vehicle is being driven on
a road."[8] The Highway Code's Annex 6 puts the same duty as a MUST: "windscreens and windows MUST be
kept clean and free from obstructions to vision".[3]

For wipers, regulation 34 requires "one or more efficient automatic windscreen wipers" on a car with
a windscreen, unless the driver can see ahead without looking through it, and lesson 9 quoted its
paragraph (6): fitted wipers and washers are to be kept in efficient working order, and properly
adjusted, at all times while the car is used on a road.[9] Notice what it doesn't say. It sets no
age for a blade. On this course's reading, a blade that clears the glass meets its words at two
years old; one that smears doesn't at six months.

Window tints get two sentences in Annex 6: "You MUST NOT use a vehicle with excessively dark tinting
applied to the windscreen, or to the glass in any front window to either side of the driver." And:
"There are no VLT limits for rear windscreens or rear passenger windows."[3] (VLT is visual light
transmittance, how much light the glass lets through.) For the tint rule Annex 6 cites a regulation
this course didn't read, so the lesson goes no further than those two sentences.

## The windscreen at the MOT: zone A

This is general information, not legal advice.

At the MOT, the windscreen is judged by where the damage is as well as how big it is. DVSA's manual
tells testers to check for "damage in windscreen zone A more than 10mm in diameter" and "damage in the
remainder of the windscreen's swept area more than 40mm in diameter". Zone A is "in the swept area of
the windscreen", "290mm wide" and "centred on the steering wheel".[10]

The drawing is this course's schematic of the manual's words, on a right-hand-drive car, and it isn't
to scale: the manual gives zone A's width and says it sits in the swept area, and gives no height in
words; its own drawing, linked below, shows the band running the full height of the swept area.

The figures are only half the rule. The manual goes on: "Failure for damage is only justified if the
damage significantly affects the driver's view of the road." And a good repair isn't damage at all:
"An 'invisible' or barely detectable repair, finished flush with the surrounding glass, does not
count as damage."[10] So the 10 and 40 mm figures tell the tester what to look at; whether it fails
is a judgement about the view. If it doesn't, damage "not adversely affecting driver's view" is
still recorded as a Minor defect in the manual's table, which doesn't fail the car.[10]

The wipers are in the same section. A "Wiper blade defective" is Minor; "Wiper blade missing or
obviously not clearing the windscreen" is Major.[10] Washers "not working or not providing
sufficient fluid to clear the windscreen" are Major too.[10]

### One chip, two inspections

A car has a chip 12 mm across in the glass, just to the right of the steering wheel's centre line,
inside the area the wiper sweeps. At an MOT in Great Britain, that is inside zone A (it is within 145
mm of the wheel's centre, half of 290, which is this course's arithmetic) and over the 10 mm figure,
so the tester looks at it, and it fails if it significantly affects the view. A repair finished
flush with the glass would take it out of the question.

Now put the same car through a safety inspection in Poughkeepsie, New York. The state DMV's
inspection page, as archived in July 2026, lists the windshield, to be checked "for presence and
condition", and gives one rule with a number in it: "No crack of 11 inches long or longer is allowed
if any part of the crack is within the area cleared by the windshield wiper."[11]

:::predict Apply New York's rule to the 12 mm chip. What does the rule decide, and what doesn't it?

The rule is about cracks 11 inches (about 28 cm, this course's conversion) or longer with any part in
the wiped area. A 12 mm chip is not that, so the rule doesn't reject it. But that is not the same as
saying it passes: the page also tells the inspector to check the windshield's condition, and gives no
figure for a chip, so what the inspector makes of it is not something this course can say from the
page. If the chip grows into a crack that reaches 11 inches with any part in the wiped area, the
rule does apply.
:::

## In the US

This is general information, not legal advice.

There is no single US rule for lights and glass. Whether a car is inspected at all depends on the
state, and lesson 22, "The US: state inspections, emissions tests and tread law", covers which states
this course read. Among them, the lists look like this:

- **New York** (the DMV's inspection page, archived July 2026): lights including headlamps, tail
  lamps, stop lamps, directional signals, backup lights and "License plate lights"; the glass; the

  states the duty behind the test: "Motor vehicles are required by law to be in safe operating
  condition whenever they are driven on a public street or roadway in New York State."[11]
- **Pennsylvania** (PennDOT): the inspection covers "lighting and electrical systems, glazing (glass),
  mirrors, windshield washer, defroster, wipers", among other things.[12]
- **Virginia** (the State Police): the glass for "Cracked, scratched or broken glass", the wipers for
  "Condition of wiper blades", and the rear plate for "Illumination of rear plate".[13]

## What people get wrong

**"In daylight, a dead lamp doesn't matter."** The daylight exception in regulation 23(3)(c) is
narrower than that: a lamp that failed on the journey you are on, or whose repair is already
arranged, and only between sunrise and sunset. The section on lamps above has the words.

**"Washer fluid is optional."** In Great Britain, fitted washers are to be kept in efficient working
order at all times while the car is used on a road (regulation 34(6), lesson 9).

**"A peephole in the ice will do."** The Highway Code's rules on clearing glass before a winter drive
are lesson 15's, "Winter and summer". The regulation above already says fitted glass must not obscure
the driver's vision.

**"Any chip fails the MOT."** The manual's figures are 10 mm in zone A and 40 mm elsewhere in the
swept area, and even then it fails only if the view is significantly affected; a flush repair
doesn't count as damage.[10]

**"You can see whether your brake lights work from the driver's seat."** You can't. A helper, or a
window or garage door behind the car, as National Highways says.[1]

## Practice

:::exercise The walk-round
Take 15 minutes over this, in daylight. Use your own car, or one whose keeper agrees, parked off the
road on level ground with the parking brake on. Ask a helper, or, if the car is already parked with
its back toward a window or garage door, use the reflection; don't move the car for this. If the
engine has to run for the lights, do it outdoors, never inside a garage, even with the door open.
Don't drive the car for the exercise, and don't open or take apart any lamp.

1. **Front.** Headlamps (dipped and main beam; low and high beam in the US), front position lamps
   (your handbook shows the switch position), front indicators, hazard lights
   and any front fog lamps. Note each as working or not, and wipe each lens clean.
2. **Rear.** Rear lamps, indicators, brake lights (your helper or the reflection), rear fog lamp and
   the number-plate lamp. Check the reversing lamp only with your foot firmly on the brake and your
   helper standing to the side, never behind; if you can't do that, skip it and say so.
3. **Plates.** Both number plates and the reflectors: clean, clear and readable.
4. **Glass.** From the driver's seat and from outside, look for chips and cracks. Measure any chip with
   a ruler, in mm, and note whether it's within 145 mm either side of the steering wheel's centre and
   inside the area the wipers sweep.
5. **Wipers.** Spray, wipe and watch for streaks; then, with the wipers off, run your fingers along each
   blade for splits.
6. **Log it.** Date, and each item as working, dirty or faulty, in your car file's checks log.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your log. Which findings are yours to deal with, which need someone else, and which would matter at a Great Britain MOT?

A dirty lens or plate is **Yours**: clean it. A lamp that doesn't work needs putting right:
**Yours, on conditions** only where your handbook shows how to change that bulb, and otherwise **Call
someone**. Damage to the glass is **Call someone**, and the sooner the better while it may still be a
repair. A streaking blade is **Yours, on conditions** where your handbook shows the change. At a GB
MOT, a headlamp, stop lamp or indicator that doesn't work is a Major defect by the table above
(Dangerous if every stop lamp is out), and so
is a dead number-plate lamp unless another still lights the plate; a chip over 10 mm in zone A, or
over 40 mm in the rest of the swept area, is one the tester will look at.
:::
