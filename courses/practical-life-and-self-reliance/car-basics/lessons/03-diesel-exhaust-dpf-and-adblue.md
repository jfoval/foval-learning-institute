The AA, a motoring organisation that sells breakdown cover and repairs, says its patrols "are often called to
cars with a blocked DPF".[3] Picture the kind of car that means. A diesel does short trips for months, school runs and shopping, and then one morning an
amber light shows a box with dots in it. A few weeks later the car won't pull properly. Another
diesel shows a message about something called AdBlue and a number of miles, and its owner ignores
it until the car won't restart outside a shop. Neither car has anything wrong with its engine. Each
has an exhaust system that needs something from its owner, and this lesson is about what that is.

## Why a diesel's exhaust needs two extra parts

Lesson 2, "The engine: petrol, diesel, and the drive to the wheels", showed that a diesel has no
spark plug: it lights its fuel by the heat of compression.

Burning fuel that way leaves two things in the exhaust that matter here. The first is soot, which
the regulators call particulate matter. The second is a group of gases called nitrogen oxides,
written NOx. fueleconomy.gov, run by the US energy department and the Environmental Protection
Agency (EPA), says "Today's diesels must meet the same emissions standards as gasoline vehicles."
(Gasoline is petrol.) It adds that diesel emissions of particulates and NOx "are still relatively
high", and that cleaner fuels and "advances in emission control technologies will reduce these
pollutants also."[6] The Alternative Fuels Data Center (AFDC), part of the US Department of Energy,
says "many diesel vehicles have additional aftertreatment components" in the exhaust to deal with
them, and this lesson is about two:[5]

- **The diesel particulate filter (DPF)** catches the soot.
- **Selective catalytic reduction (SCR)** deals with the NOx, and it is the system that uses AdBlue,
  which the US calls diesel exhaust fluid (DEF).

## The DPF: a filter that has to empty itself

DVSA, the agency that runs the MOT (Great Britain's periodic test, the nearest thing to a US
state inspection), published a short guidance note with the
Department for Transport in 2013. [It starts plainly](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/diesel-particulate-filters-guidance-note/diesel-particulate-filters):
"A diesel particulate filter (DPF) is a device fitted to a diesel vehicle which filters
particulate matter (PM) from exhaust gases." It does this "by trapping solid particles while letting
gaseous components escape."[1]

A filter that traps things fills up. So the note goes on: the filter is emptied "by a process
called regeneration, which involves burning the soot to gas at a very high temperature, leaving
behind only a very small residue."[1] Everything else about owning a DPF follows from those words
"a very high temperature". Soot only burns off when the exhaust is hot enough, for long enough.

There are two ways that happens. The RAC and the AA both describe them. Both are motoring
organisations that sell breakdown cover and repairs, and neither is an official body.

**Passive regeneration** happens by itself when the exhaust gets hot on a long, fast run. The AA
says it happens "on motorways or fast A-roads" (A-roads are main roads in Great Britain).[3] The RAC advises that drivers "regularly give
their diesel vehicle a good 30 to 50 minute run at sustained speed on a motorway or A-road to help
clear the filter."[2]

**Active regeneration** is the car's own backup, for owners who do not drive like that. The engine's
software senses the filter filling and injects extra fuel to raise the exhaust temperature. The RAC
says this starts when the filter reaches a set limit, "normally about 45%".[2] The AA says it
happens "every 300 miles or so depending on how you use your car and will take 5 to 10 minutes to
complete."[3] That is the catch. Five to ten minutes can be longer than the whole trip to the shops.

:::predict A diesel starts an active regeneration two minutes into a four-minute trip to the shops, and the driver parks and switches off. What do you expect has happened to the soot?

It hasn't all gone. The RAC, which sells breakdown cover and repairs, says that when a journey is too short "the regeneration process may not
complete fully", and the warning light then shows the filter is still partly blocked.[2] Repeat that
trip every day and the filter fills faster than it empties.
:::

You can sometimes tell when active regeneration is running. The AA and the RAC give nearly the same
list: the cooling fans running, a faster idle, the automatic Stop/Start (which switches the engine off at a standstill) not working, a little more fuel used,
the engine sounding different, and "A hot, acrid smell from the exhaust."[2][3] Neither page says
what to do when you notice them. Your handbook may.

### What stops a DPF emptying

The RAC's [DPF page](https://www.rac.co.uk/drive/advice/emissions/diesel-particulate-filters/)
puts the main cause in one line: "Short journeys at low speeds are the prime cause of blocked diesel
particulate filters."[2] The AA's list of what prevents normal regeneration starts with "Frequent
short journeys where the engine doesn't get hot" and goes on to others an owner controls:[3]

- **Low fuel.** The AA: a level "generally less than a quarter of a tank" will "prevent active
  regeneration taking place". The RAC gives the reason as the car's own choice: it "may avoid DPF
  regeneration in order to save fuel."[2][3]
- **The wrong oil.** The RAC: "some oils contain additives that can actually block filters." The AA
  says "check your handbook".[2][3] This is the reason the handbook's oil grade matters on a DPF
  diesel, and lesson 9 comes back to it.
- **A missed service.** The AA lists "Going over the recommended service interval".[3]
- **A fault elsewhere**, in the fuel system or the exhaust gas recirculation valve (a part of the engine's emissions system), which the AA
  says can cause excess soot.[3]

One more thing no amount of driving fixes. Burning the soot leaves "a tiny ash residue", in the AA's
words, and "The ash can't be removed" except by taking the filter off the car and sending it away
for specialist cleaning. Even so, the AA says a DPF "in a car used correctly should be good for well
over 100,000 miles", and the RAC says they should "generally" last "at least 100,000 miles", though
one on a poorly serviced car "may fail sooner".[2][3] Those are the motoring
organisations' general figures, not a replacement interval for your car.

## When the DPF light comes on

The light is usually amber and, in the description of the RAC (which, like the AA, sells
breakdown cover and repairs), looks like "a piped box that has dots in
the middle", though it varies by maker.[2] Your handbook shows yours, and it comes first. The AA's
page says it too: "Follow any advice in your vehicle handbook."[3]

What the AA and the RAC then advise is the same, in almost the same words. The RAC ties the light
to regenerations cut short by journeys that are too short. Both say it should be possible to finish
a regeneration and clear the light by "driving for 10 minutes or so at speeds greater than 40mph",
in the RAC's words (about 64 km/h, this course's conversion), and the AA gives the same ten
minutes above 40 mph.[2][3] Two things about that advice. It is
the motoring organisations' advice, not the law or a maker's instruction, so your handbook's
version wins where it gives one. And it means a road where 40 mph is legal and safe at that time.
Neither organisation suggests breaking a limit to clear a filter, and this course does not either.

Ignore the light, and the AA says what follows. Keep driving "in a relatively slow, stop/start pattern" and
the soot builds until the car goes into "restricted performance mode" to prevent damage. At that
point "Driving at speed alone won't be enough", and "You'll have to get a dealer to do a manual or
'forced' filter regeneration."[3] The RAC calls the same state "limp-home mode", and adds that "On
some models the engine may not restart after a number of miles", sending you to the handbook for
yours.[2]

The RAC also says when to stop trying: "If your warning light continues to stay on, turns red, or
additional DPF lights come on, do not leave it too long before getting it checked out." It says a
light that "fails to extinguish" goes to a garage.[2] The AA adds that a fault in the filter or its
pressure sensor can bring on the engine management light (check engine light) as well.[3]

So the jobs sort like this, using the labels from lesson 1:

- **Yours:** knowing what the light looks like, reading the handbook's page on it, and keeping the
  tank above a quarter.
- **Yours, on conditions:** the run to clear the light. The conditions are that your handbook allows
  it or says nothing against it, the tank has enough fuel, and the road lets you drive at over 40
  mph legally and safely.
- **Call someone:** a light that will not clear, turns red or is joined by others; restricted
  performance mode; and forced regeneration, which is garage work. This course does not teach it.

### Two cars, one light

A diesel hatchback in Ripon does the school run: three miles there, three back, twice a day. It
hasn't been further than the supermarket in weeks. On Tuesday the DPF light comes on.

The owner opens the handbook first. Hers, say, has a page on the light and tells her to drive at a
steady speed for a while. The fuel gauge shows just over half. So she does what the handbook and
both motoring organisations describe: on the way back from the school, instead of going home, she
takes a stretch of A-road where 50 mph is the limit and the traffic is moving, and
keeps going for a good ten minutes at over 40 mph. The light goes out. The mechanism explains why: a
regeneration that the short runs kept interrupting finally had the heat and the time to finish.
What she writes in her car file is the useful part. This car, driven this way, will do it again, so
a longer run now and then, "regularly" in the RAC's word, is part of owning it.

Now change one thing. The same car, the same light, but the fuel gauge is showing an eighth of a
tank.

:::predict Before reading on: what should she do first, and why?

Fill up. The AA says a fuel level "generally less than a quarter of a tank" will prevent active
regeneration, and the RAC gives the reason (both sell breakdown cover and repairs): the car may skip regeneration to save fuel.[2][3] A run
on an eighth of a tank could be ten minutes of driving that does nothing for the filter. With the
tank well above a quarter, the run has a chance of working.
:::

## AdBlue: the fluid that deals with NOx

The AA's [AdBlue page](https://www.theaa.com/breakdown-cover/advice/adblue) (the AA sells breakdown cover and repairs) begins with the
misunderstanding people bring to it: "AdBlue is a diesel exhaust fluid, not a fuel additive. It's
stored in a separate tank".[4] The filler is "(usually) blue", the AA says, and sits next to the
fuel filler, in the boot (trunk) or under the bonnet (hood). The fluid is not blue. It is "a
colourless, non-toxic mixture of urea and de-ionised water. It's not actually blue at all." The
name is a trade name, registered by the German car manufacturers' association, the AA says; the
generic term, and the one the US uses, is DEF.[4]

What it does is the SCR half of the exhaust. AFDC calls DEF "an aqueous urea solution", injected
into the exhaust, where the NOx is "reduced to nitrogen and water by chemical conversion within the
selective catalytic reducer (SCR)".[5] So unlike the DPF, which needs heat, this system needs a
supply. Run out of the fluid and there is nothing to treat the NOx with, and, as the next section
shows, the car reduces its own power.

### How much warning you get

In the AA's account, a car gives a lot of notice. "You'll usually see a text warning on the dashboard
when there's around 1500 miles' worth of AdBlue left", usually with a countdown of miles.[4] If you
ignore it: "If you run out of AdBlue while you're driving, then the engine's power and performance
will be reduced to limit its emissions." And: "Once you've stopped, you won't be able to restart the
engine if the AdBlue tank's empty."[4]

How often that comes round depends on the car. The AA says "Typical consumption is around a litre
of AdBlue every 600 miles", but "it could be as high as a litre every 350 miles", and with tank
sizes varying, "you could need to refill somewhere between 3,000 and 12,000 miles".[4] Those are the
AA's general figures. Your handbook gives your tank's size, and the countdown gives your car's
reading.

Topping up is **Yours, on conditions**: from a sealed container, into the AdBlue tank only, as your
handbook describes. The AA says "You can refill the
Adblue tank yourself", and its conditions are the ones to keep: "Follow any instructions given in
your handbook or on the pack", and the fluid is "non-toxic but can cause irritation to your skin and
eyes and may damage the paintwork", so wash your hands and rinse off spills. If it has run out
completely, the AA says 5 litres "should be enough to make sure your car will restart", and the
handbook says how much your car needs.[4] The AA also says spilled AdBlue crystallises on carpet and
upholstery as its water evaporates, and "These crystals will cause damage over time."[4]

Which cars have it? The AA says a diesel from Jaguar, Land Rover or any French or German maker,
registered new after September 2015, will use it, and that some older cars and other makers' cars do
too. To check your own: the handbook, a blue filler cap, "Blue" or "SCR" in the model name, or asking
a dealer.[4]

### The wrong tank

Two mistakes happen at the pump, and the AA gives each its own advice. Getting them the right way
round matters.

**AdBlue in the fuel tank.** The AA says it "could cause expensive damage to your fuel tank, pump and
injection system. If you start the engine it's likely to be an expensive write-off, so don't touch
the ignition." The tank has to be drained and the contents disposed of safely before it is
refilled.[4] **Call someone.** The AA's "call us" is a breakdown provider offering its own service;
if you're not a member, the point is that you need someone equipped to drain a fuel tank, and the
engine stays off until they have.

**Diesel in the AdBlue tank.** The AA's words: "Don't turn on the ignition or start the engine and
call us for help." If the engine runs, it says, the whole SCR and AdBlue injection system may need
replacing.[4] **Call someone** again.

Both pieces of advice come to the same thing, which is this course's summary of them rather than a
line either page prints: whichever tank it went into, do not switch on the ignition.

Now sort six dashboards:

1. An amber DPF light after a fortnight of short trips, fuel at half.
2. The same light with the fuel near empty.
3. The DPF light still on after the run, or turned red, or the car in restricted performance mode.
4. "AdBlue: refill in 1,200 miles".
5. The AdBlue countdown at zero and the car already parked.
6. The DPF light and the engine management light together.

## In the US: DEF, and what EPA says about derates

This course read no figure for how many US cars have diesel engines. What it can say is that the US
sources it read on DEF are written mostly for trucks, heavy pickups and farm machinery. EPA's
[DEF page](https://www.epa.gov/regulations-emissions-vehicles-and-engines/diesel-exhaust-fluid), last
updated 9 July 2026, is addressed to "farmers, truckers, and other diesel vehicle operators".[7] The
chemistry is the same as AdBlue's. What differs is what the sources say happens when it runs out, and
that has been changing.

EPA says SCR systems "can force a vehicle to drastically reduce speed or become inoperable if DEF runs
out or if system sensors fail."[7] That is what EPA calls a derate: the system reduces the vehicle's
speed or power. Its August 2025 fact sheet describes how that works on vehicles whose software hasn't
been changed: "within four hours, the vehicle speed is reduced to five miles per hour" (about 8
km/h).[8] The same fact sheet sets out guidance urging makers to update their software. For the
class it calls "HD Pickups and Light Duty Cars", its table gives the final step as a 45 mph (about 72
km/h) limit, with the timing written "4,200 miles / 80 hours", where before it was 5 mph within four
hours.[8] It is guidance, not a rule you can rely on for your truck. EPA's page says "By law, EPA
cannot mandate field fixes for in-use vehicles and nonroad equipment", which is why the guidance is
voluntary.[7]

What happens next is not settled. EPA's page says it finalised a rule in 2022 for heavy-duty vehicles
from model year 2027, and on 14 July 2026 it published a proposal to replace the derate requirement
for newly made diesel highway vehicles, light-duty ones included, with "audible and/or visible
notifications".[7][12] A proposal is not a rule. This course read EPA's summary page of the proposal,
not the proposal itself.

| | In the UK (the AA, which sells breakdown cover and repairs; for cars) | In the US (EPA, mostly for trucks) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is called | AdBlue, a trade name | DEF, diesel exhaust fluid |
| The warning | A text warning at around 1,500 miles left, usually with a countdown | A warning light, which EPA requires the system to give |
| When it runs out | Power reduced; won't restart once stopped | Speed cut to 5 mph within four hours on vehicles whose software hasn't been changed; for light-duty cars and heavy pickups, a 45 mph step under EPA's 2025 guidance, if the maker has updated the software; a July 2026 proposal would replace derates on new vehicles with warnings |

A pickup owner near Laredo sees a DEF countdown on the dashboard. What does this lesson let him do
with it? Not much by itself, and that is the honest answer. What happens at zero on his truck depends
on its software and whether its maker has issued an update under EPA's guidance, and only his
handbook, and the maker's dealer or a garage that works on that make, can tell him that. This course read no diesel pickup's handbook. What it can
offer is its own reading of the sources together, which is the part that is his: DEF goes in its own
tank, a warning is the time to act, and a truck topped up before it runs out never meets a derate
for want of fluid.

## Removing a DPF or an emission part: the law and the test

This is general information, not legal advice.

Blocked filters are expensive, and DVSA's note says openly that the cost "has led to some diesel
vehicle owners removing their DPFs." The AA, which sells breakdown cover and repairs, puts the pitch in its own words: it is sometimes
suggested you can have the filter cut out and the engine software reprogrammed rather than pay for
a repair.[1][3] That's the "delete" you may see advertised. Here's what each country's law says
about it.

### In Great Britain

The rule is regulation 61A of the Road Vehicles (Construction and Use) Regulations 1986, which
extends to England, Wales and Scotland and applies to vehicles first used on or after 1 January
2001. [Regulation 61A(3)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1986/1078/regulation/61A) says "no
person shall use, or cause or permit to be used, on a road a motor vehicle if the motor vehicle does
not comply with such limit values as may apply to it", unless conditions are met, and the first
condition is that "the failure to meet the limit values does not result from an alteration to the
propulsion unit or exhaust system of the motor vehicle".[9] So regulation 61A makes using the car the
offence, and causing or permitting it to be used. It doesn't mention the cutting itself, and this
course read no other law on that. That is this course's reading of the regulation's words.

DVSA's note applies that to a missing filter. It is an offence under regulation 61A(3) "to use a
vehicle which has been modified in such a way that it no longer complies with the air pollutant
emissions standards it was designed to meet. Removal of a DPF will almost invariably contravene these
requirements, making the vehicle illegal for road use." The note gives the potential penalties as "fines
of up to £1,000 for a car or £2,500 for a light goods vehicle."[1] Notice the note's hedge, "almost
invariably", and its date: it was last revised in December 2013, and lesson 20 covers how fines and
penalties for a car's condition work now.

Insurance is the other thing people ask about. The AA and the RAC, both of which sell breakdown
cover and repairs, hedge: the AA says removing a DPF "could also invalidate any insurance cover", and the RAC
says it "can also invalidate" a policy.[2][3] Neither is the insurer. What a policy says is Personal Finance Fundamentals lesson 8's subject, "Handing off
what you cannot absorb".

Then there is the MOT, which lesson 21 covers in full. DVSA's note says: "Since February 2014 the
inspection of the exhaust system carried out during the MOT test includes a check for the presence of
a DPF. A missing DPF, where one was fitted when the vehicle was built, will result in an MOT
failure."[1] Great Britain's current [MOT inspection manual](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/mot-inspection-manual-for-private-passenger-and-light-commercial-vehicles/8-nuisance)
lists "Evidence that the diesel particulate filter has been tampered with" and "Exhaust on a vehicle
fitted with a diesel particulate filter emits visible smoke of any colour" as Major defects, which
fail the test.[10] It also tells testers that a filter "clearly been cut open and rewelded" should be
rejected unless the presenter can show a valid reason, "such as for filter cleaning".[10] So, on
this course's reading, if a filter is ever sent away for the specialist cleaning the AA describes,
the paperwork belongs in the car file.

Northern Ireland has its own road traffic law, which this course didn't read; the regulation above
is Great Britain's.

### In the US

The rule is federal, in the Clean Air Act at [42 U.S.C. 7522](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title42-section7522&num=0&edition=prelim).
Among the acts it prohibits, paragraph (a)(3)(A) covers any person "knowingly to remove or render
inoperative any such device or element of design after such sale and delivery to the ultimate
purchaser", meaning an emission-control part fitted to meet the federal rules. Paragraph (a)(3)(B)
covers making, selling, offering to sell or installing a part "where a principal effect of the part
or component is to bypass, defeat, or render inoperative any device or element of design" installed
for that purpose, where the person knows or should know it is for that use.[11] On this course's
reading, a DPF or DEF "delete" kit sold or fitted to switch a filter or the DEF system off is the
kind of part (B) describes.

The statute also says what is not prohibited. Work "for the purpose of repair or replacement of the
device or element" is not a prohibited act under the paragraph where it "thereafter results in the
proper functioning of the device or element", and nothing in the paragraph requires the maker's own parts for maintenance or
repair.[11] This course read the statute as it stood on 25 September 2026. It didn't check how EPA
is enforcing it now, so it says nothing about that. How state inspections and emissions tests treat
a car with missing parts is lesson 22's subject.

## What people get wrong

**"AdBlue is a fuel additive" (or "it's blue").** It goes in its own tank, it is colourless, and in
the fuel tank it can wreck the fuel system (the AA, which sells breakdown cover and repairs).[4]
The US name, DEF (diesel exhaust fluid), says what it is.

**"A DPF delete is a repair."** A repair leaves the filter working: cleaned, or replaced. A delete
takes it away, and the section on the law above sets out what each country's rules say about that
and about the test.

**"Any engine oil will do."** The RAC says some oils "can actually block filters", and the AA says
to check the handbook; both sell breakdown cover and repairs.[2][3] The grade in your handbook is the one to use.

**"A good long run fixes any DPF light."** It often clears the light when short trips were the cause.
It can't remove ash, it won't work on a near-empty tank, and a light that returns or turns red is,
on the advice of both the AA and the RAC (both sell breakdown cover and repairs), one for a garage.[2][3]

The last one is not a misconception so much as a question people ask: should I have bought a diesel?
The AA, which sells breakdown cover and repairs, gives buying advice: if you're buying a new car and will use it "mainly for town-based,
stop/start driving you'd be wise to avoid diesels fitted with a particulate filter".[3] The RAC, which
sells the same, gives none in its own voice. It says short, slow journeys are "the prime cause" of blocked filters, and it
says car makers often recommend petrol or electric for short-hop drivers, which this course didn't
check with any maker.[2] This course takes no side on which car to buy; whether to choose a petrol, diesel, hybrid or electric car
is argued in Energy: How the World Is Powered, later in the Core. What the sources do support is
narrower: if you have a DPF diesel and drive it mostly on short trips, the longer run is part of
owning it.

## Practice

:::exercise Your car's exhaust, from the handbook
Take 15 minutes over this. Use your own car, or one whose keeper agrees. You'll be reading the
handbook and looking, nothing more: the engine off and the key or fob away from the car, the parking
brake on, and the car in P or in gear. Don't open the AdBlue filler, the fuel filler or anything
under the bonnet for this exercise, and don't drive the car for it. If you don't have the handbook,
the maker may publish one online.

1. **Fuel and filter.** Is the car a diesel? If it is, does the handbook mention a diesel particulate
   filter, or the maker's own name for one? Write the page or section.
2. **The DPF light.** Copy or sketch the symbol, write its colour, and write what the handbook says to
   do when it comes on. If it gives no advice, write "none".
3. **AdBlue.** Does the handbook mention AdBlue, DEF or SCR? If so, write the tank's size, where the
   filler is, and what the car shows when the fluid runs low. Then find the filler on the car by
   looking. It's usually beside the fuel filler, in the boot or under the bonnet (the AA); don't open
   the bonnet to look, since lesson 9 comes first.
4. **Oil.** Write the oil specification the handbook gives, in its own words.
5. **Your driving, and one line for the car file.** Write, honestly, how many of your journeys last
   a week were under ten minutes, and when the car last had a run of ten minutes or more at over 40
   mph. Then, using this lesson, write one line on what this car needs from you, or "petrol, hybrid
   or electric: nothing here applies".
:::

:::checkpoint Check your answer to step 5. When does your driving point to a problem, and what should the line say for a DPF diesel driven mostly on short trips?

If most journeys were under ten minutes and there has been no longer run for weeks, the car is in the
pattern the RAC calls the prime cause of blocked filters.[2] For that car, the line should say
something like: keep the tank above a quarter, take a run of ten minutes or more at over 40 mph on a
suitable road regularly, use the handbook's oil, and treat a DPF light that won't clear as
**Call someone**. That is this course's reading of the advice of the AA and the RAC, which both
sell breakdown cover and repairs, put together for one car, not a schedule either of them gives; if your handbook gives one, use that.
:::

## For your plan

This lesson adds three things to your car file. First, whether the car has a DPF, an AdBlue tank or
neither, with the handbook's pages for each warning light. Second, the handbook's oil specification.
Third, a line in your routine: for a DPF diesel on short trips, the tank above a quarter and a longer
run on a suitable road now and then; for an AdBlue car, a top-up when the countdown starts, not when
it ends.
