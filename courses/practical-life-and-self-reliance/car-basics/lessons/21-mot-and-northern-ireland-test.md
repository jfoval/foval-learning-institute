An MOT certificate is probably the most misread piece of paper a car owner keeps. Most people read it
as "this car is fine for a year". It says something much narrower: on one day, a tester looked at a
fixed list of things without taking anything apart, and found none of them bad enough to fail. And
the rules around it change at the Irish Sea. In Great Britain the first test comes at three years,
at a DVSA-approved test centre, and a car that fails can sometimes be driven away. In Northern
Ireland it generally comes at four, at a government test centre run by the DVA, and a car that fails
shouldn't be on the road at all. Here's both: when, where, what it costs, what the result means, and
what the certificate doesn't promise.

:::callout Before the car lets you down
This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. If your car fails on a motorway or other fast road in Great Britain, leave at the next exit or services if you can; if you can't, go left into an emergency area or onto the hard shoulder, switch on your hazard lights, get everyone out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and don't go back to the car. If you're stuck in a live lane or can't get out safely, stay in with seat belts and hazard lights on and call 999 and ask for the police. In the US, pull as far onto the right shoulder as you can, switch on your hazard lights and stay in with your seat belt on, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind, in which case get out on the side away from traffic; call 911 in an emergency. Never put any part of your body under a car held up only by a jack, and never touch the orange high-voltage cables on a hybrid or electric car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

## What the test is, and what it isn't

This is general information, not legal advice.

In Great Britain the test's legal purpose is written into the Road Traffic Act 1988. Section 45 says
it is for finding out whether two requirements are met: the prescribed requirements on a vehicle's
construction and condition, and "the requirement that the condition of motor vehicles should not be
such that their use on a road would involve a danger of injury to any person."[1] It's a check on
those requirements, on one day.

Three official lines mark its limits. GOV.UK: "The test does not cover the condition of the engine,
clutch or gearbox."[2] DVSA (the Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency), in its older plain-English guide: "It isn’t the same as having your vehicle
serviced and doesn’t check its general mechanical condition."[3] And DVSA's inspection manual, which
is written for testers: "The MOT test must be carried out without dismantling, so it is not always
possible to inspect some testable items."[4] Lesson 2 quoted the RAC, which sells breakdown cover
and repairs, on one consequence: the annual MOT doesn't check the cambelt.

Northern Ireland says the same about its own certificate. nidirect: "The MOT certificate confirms that
your vehicle at the time of its test met the minimum acceptable environmental and road safety
standards needed by law. It doesn’t mean that the vehicle is roadworthy for the life of the
certificate and isn’t a substitute for regular maintenance."[5]

So the test and the law run on different clocks. The Department for Transport put it in one line in
2018, for Great Britain: "By law, all vehicles must be roadworthy, regardless of whether they have passed an MOT".[6]
Lesson 20, "The law on a car's condition: Great Britain and Northern Ireland", set out that law. This
lesson is about the test.

## When: three years in Great Britain, four in Northern Ireland

This is general information, not legal advice.

### Great Britain

[GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/getting-an-mot): "You must get an MOT for your vehicle by either" the third anniversary of its registration,
or "the anniversary of its last MOT, if it’s over 3 years old".[2] The law behind it is section 47 of
the Road Traffic Act 1988, which makes it an offence to use a car on a road with no test certificate
issued within "a period of twelve months or such shorter period as may be prescribed", once it was
first registered "not less than three years" before.[1] Taxis, ambulances and vehicles with more than eight passenger seats are tested from one
year old.[2]

"An MOT lasts for a year," GOV.UK says, and you don't have to wait for it to run out: "You can get an
MOT up to a month (minus a day) before it runs out and keep the same renewal date." Its example: if
your MOT runs out on 15 May, the earliest test that keeps the date is 16 April. Go earlier than that
and the new expiry becomes "one year (minus a day) from the date the vehicle last passed its MOT".[2]
One exception on the same page: the early window "does not apply if your last MOT was in Northern
Ireland and your vehicle’s next test is in Great Britain."[2]

### A car from Goole

A car was first registered in Goole in June 2023. By GOV.UK's rule its first MOT was due by the third
anniversary, in June 2026. It passed, and its certificate shows it runs out on 19 June 2027.

:::predict What's the earliest date in 2027 the owner can have it tested and keep 19 June as the renewal date?
20 May 2027. GOV.UK's example runs from a 15 May expiry to 16 April: back a month, then forward a day.
The same pattern from 19 June gives 20 May. That's this course's arithmetic on GOV.UK's example, and
the page's own example is the thing to check it against.
:::

Now the gap. The owner books the test for 12 May 2027, eight days before the window opens, and the
car passes. Work out the new expiry date before reading the answer.

:::checkpoint The car passes on 12 May 2027. When does the new certificate run out?
On 11 May 2028. A test before the window moves the renewal date to "one year (minus a day) from the
date the vehicle last passed its MOT". GOV.UK's own example does the same: a pass on 14 April changes
the expiry to 13 April the following year. Tested inside the window, the car would have kept 19 June
and run to 19 June 2028. Tested eight days early, it runs to 11 May 2028: 39 days of certificate
given up, on this course's arithmetic, and the renewal date has moved for good.
:::

### Why three years, and not four

You may read that the first MOT is moving to four years. It isn't, as read on 26 September 2026. The history, from the Department for Transport: "The test was introduced in 1960,
requiring vehicles to undergo a first check after 10 years. It was changed in 1967 to 3 years."[6]
In 2018 the government decided to keep three years after a consultation.[6] It consulted again in
2023, and in January 2024 published its outcome: "Given the significant concerns about road safety
that were raised, and that any potential savings made by motorists would be limited by the
additional cost of potential defects not identified at the MOT, the government does not intend to
proceed in changing the date of a vehicle’s first MOT."[7] Those are the government's reasons,
in its words. The Act would let a minister change the three years to "such other
period (not being more than ten years)" by an order approved by both Houses of Parliament.[1] The Act's
text, as read, still says three years.

### Northern Ireland

[nidirect](https://www.nidirect.gov.uk/articles/how-mot-scheme-works) gives the timing as a general
rule "during normal operating times", and its words keep the hedge: "as a general rule the
points below suggest when your vehicle will be due a MOT test", with "cars and motorcycles which are
four years old and over" first on the list.[8] Then yearly. It adds: "It is an offence to use a
vehicle of MOT test age that doesn’t have a current test certificate on a public road."[8] No source
this course read gives a reason why Northern Ireland's first test comes a year later than Great
Britain's, so this lesson gives none.

:::predict A car passes its MOT in Great Britain in March and moves to Northern Ireland in August. Is its certificate good there, and what happens to the months it has left when it's next tested in Northern Ireland?
The certificate is good: "An official MOT vehicle test certificate issued within the United Kingdom
is valid in Northern Ireland." But the months don't carry over: "If you get your next MOT in Northern
Ireland, you cannot retain any credit from a test done in another part of the United Kingdom." The
new certificate runs 12 months from the day of the Northern Ireland test.[8]
:::

One dated arrangement, because it will change. nidirect, on its booking page as read: "Private cars
that were first registered between 1 June 2019 and 31 May 2020 or between 1 June 2021 and 31 May 2022
and have a valid Northern Ireland MOT will be given a Temporary Exemption Certificate (TEC)." "This
means they will not need a vehicle test (MOT) for 12 months from the date their current certificate
expires."[9] The page says a TEC lets the car be driven "if it is kept in a roadworthy condition, is
taxed and insured". Check nidirect before relying on it.

## The result: dangerous, major, minor and advisory

This is general information, not legal advice.

Great Britain's test sorts defects into three categories, defined in DVSA's manual:[4]

- **Minor**: "defects that have no significant effect on the safety of the vehicle or impact on the
  environment and other minor non-compliances"
- **Major**: "defects that may prejudice the safety of the vehicle, have an impact on the
  environment, put other road users at risk or other more significant non-compliances"
- **Dangerous**: "defects that are a direct and immediate risk to road safety or having an impact on
  the environment"

Then the rule that decides the result: "If a vehicle has only minor defects, it will pass its MOT
inspection and a test certificate will be issued. If a vehicle has any major or dangerous defects,
it must be failed and a refusal notice issued."[4] An advisory isn't a defect category at all. The
manual says that where an item "is not sufficiently deteriorated to justify rejection, there may be
an option to select ‘advisory’ to inform the presenter of this fact", and "Unlike advisory items, the
use of minor defects, where appropriate, is mandatory."[4] GOV.UK calls minor and advisory items
"problems to monitor or fix in the future".[2]

Earlier lessons met the test's items one system at a time, each with its category in the manual's
words. Gathered here, with where each was taught:

| System | Examples from the manual, with category | Lesson |
|---|---|---|

| Brakes | Pad worn down to its wear indicator: Major; below 1.5 mm: Dangerous; fluid below the minimum mark: Minor | 6, "Brakes, steering and suspension" |
| Tyre pressure | Tyre obviously under-inflated: Minor; TPMS not working: Major | 7, "Tyres I: pressure, the placard and TPMS" |
| Tyres | Tread below the legal requirement: Dangerous; a lump, bulge or tear: Dangerous | 8, "Tyres II: tread, damage, age, and when to replace" |

| Warning lamps | Engine, airbag or ESC lamp showing a fault: Major | 11, "Dashboard warning lights" |

Northern Ireland's test uses its own paperwork. nidirect says a failed car gets "a notification of
refusal containing details of the faults which will have to be repaired for the re-test", and that
"Any defects that are not serious enough to result in a test failure will be shown on a vehicle test
certificate. It's important that these defects are repaired as soon as possible."[5][12] Whether the
DVA uses Great Britain's four words is not something the pages this course read say, so this lesson
doesn't assume it.

## After a fail: where the two places part

This is general information, not legal advice.

**In Great Britain**, GOV.UK: "You can take your vehicle away if" your current MOT is still valid and
no "dangerous" problems were listed. "Otherwise, you’ll need to get it repaired before you can
drive." And, whichever applies: "If you can take your vehicle away, it must still meet the minimum
standards of roadworthiness at all times." Then the penalty: "You can be fined up to £2,500, be
banned from driving and get 3 penalty points for driving a vehicle that has failed its MOT because of
a ‘dangerous’ problem."[2] On this course's reading, that matches lesson 20's section 40A row
for a car.

**In Northern Ireland**, [nidirect](https://www.nidirect.gov.uk/articles/after-mot-vehicle-test): "If your vehicle fails an MOT test it is treated as not being
roadworthy and it should not be on the road, regardless of whether you have time remaining on an
existing MOT certificate. Anyone using a vehicle on the road or in a public place without a valid
test certificate or with defects may be committing an offence."[12] nidirect gives no reason for the
difference, and neither does this lesson.

### One result, two places

A car is tested with three weeks left on its current certificate. The tester finds brake pads worn
down to the wear indicator, a Major, and a bulge in a tyre's sidewall, a Dangerous. Take it first in
Great Britain.

The certificate is still valid, but a dangerous problem is listed, so GOV.UK's two conditions aren't
both met. The car has to be repaired before it's driven, and driving it on that fail is what the
£2,500, ban and 3 points line is about. Now suppose the bulge weren't there, only the pads. Both
conditions would be met: it could be taken away, and it would still have to meet the roadworthiness
standards every time it's used, which is lesson 20's law.

Now the gap. The same car, with the same three weeks left, fails its test at the DVA centre in Omagh.
Before reading on, say what nidirect's words mean for driving it home, and whether it matters that the
pads alone would have let it leave in Great Britain.

:::checkpoint What do nidirect's words mean for the car that failed at Omagh?
nidirect says a car that fails "is treated as not being roadworthy and it should not be on the road,
regardless of whether you have time remaining". So the three weeks don't help, and neither would it
help if only the pads had been found: Northern Ireland's page draws no line between a dangerous fault
and any other. Its certificate page, writing about cars without a current certificate, lists the journeys that are
exceptions, including "bringing it away from a test centre after it has failed the test, to a place of
repair", and adds that "you may still be prosecuted for driving an unroadworthy vehicle if it doesn’t
meet various regulations affecting its construction and use."[5] Home isn't one of the journeys on
that list. And Northern Ireland's tyre regulation, which lesson 20 found in almost the same words as
Great Britain's, says a car "shall not be used on a road, if" a tyre has "any lump, bulge or tear
caused by separation or partial failure of its structure", whatever the journey.[18] So on this
course's reading, that car doesn't go anywhere on that tyre. For the pads-only case, whether a given
journey fits is nidirect's and the DVA's to answer, not this course's.
:::

### Retests and appeals

Retests and appeals have their own clocks, and they differ.

| | Great Britain (GOV.UK) | Northern Ireland (nidirect) |

Numbers and time limits as read on 26 September 2026. The reason neither place wants repairs before
an appeal is given by nidirect: "any change to your vehicle may affect the outcome of the
appeal."[12]

## An expired certificate

This is general information, not legal advice.

**Great Britain.** "You cannot drive or park your vehicle on the road if the MOT has run out. You can
be prosecuted if caught." The only exceptions GOV.UK lists are driving it "to or from somewhere to be
repaired" and "to a pre-arranged MOT test". The fine: "You can be fined up to £1,000 for driving a
vehicle without a valid MOT."[2] That offence carries no penalty points at present (lesson 20).
And it catches tax: "you cannot renew your vehicle tax if your MOT has expired."[2]

The government has asked whether that should change. Its consultation "Proposed changes to penalties
for motoring offences" ran from 7 January to 11 May 2026 and asked, as Question 29: "In your view,
should the government introduce penalty points for the offence of driving or using a vehicle with no
current MOT?" Question 30 asked the same about vehicle seizure.[13] It gave its reason for asking:
"Police and road safety groups have told the government they are concerned that some offences do not
carry extra penalties, such as penalty points or vehicle seizure." And it stated: "This consultation
does not present a preferred option".[13] As read on 26 September 2026, no outcome had been
published. It is a question, not a law.

**Northern Ireland.** nidirect: "It's generally an offence to use a vehicle of test age that doesn’t
have a current test certificate on a public road", except taking it to a test booked earlier,
bringing it away after a fail to a place of repair, and taking it to or from pre-arranged repairs.
Then: "Even in the above circumstances you may still be prosecuted for driving an unroadworthy
vehicle if it doesn’t meet various regulations affecting its construction and use. The insurance may
also not be valid."[5] Keep the "may".

## Classic cars: out of the test, not out of the law

This is general information, not legal advice.

**Great Britain.** GOV.UK's criteria: a vehicle doesn't need an MOT if it was built or first
registered more than 40 years ago and "no substantial changes have been made to the vehicle in the
last 30 years". The same page: "Your vehicle must be roadworthy whether it is exempt or not, you may
wish to voluntarily undertake an MOT test anyway."[14] GOV.UK's guide puts the consequence where it can't be missed:
"You do not have to apply to stop getting an MOT for your vehicle each year. However, you must still
keep it in a roadworthy condition." "You can be fined up to £2,500 and get 3 penalty points for using
a vehicle in a dangerous condition."[15]

**Northern Ireland.** nidirect's page on the MOT scheme gives a 40-year rule for vehicles of
historical interest, with no major change in the last 30 years, and: "Even if the vehicle is exempt
from annual testing, it must be maintained in a roadworthy condition."[8] nidirect's Highway Code
pages still carry an older line exempting vehicles made before 1960, so the two pages differ.[19]
This lesson goes by the scheme page, which is the DVA's page on testing, and a reader relying on the
exemption should confirm it with the DVA.

An exempt car has left the test. It hasn't left sections 40A, 41A and 42, or their Northern Ireland
equivalents.

## The MOT history service

This is general information, not legal advice.

Checking a car's history is **Yours**. GOV.UK's page for the service says it shows "if
it passed or failed", "the mileage recorded when it was tested" and "when the next MOT is due", and,
for tests in England, Scotland or Wales, lets you "see what parts failed at each test, and if any
parts had minor problems".[16] Results go back to 2005 for cars tested in Great Britain; for a test
in Northern Ireland, GOV.UK's page says since 2017 and nidirect says since December 2017.[16][9]

Two limits. First, the recall line is hedged: "Your MOT history may also tell you if your vehicle has
been recalled for a safety reason, depending on the manufacturer."[16] Lesson 23, "Recalls: checking,
what's free, and 'stop drive'", covers the proper recall check. Second, this course couldn't open the service
itself, only GOV.UK's page about it, so it can't say exactly how the service shows advisories. Look
for yourself.

On this course's reading, a history is most useful for patterns: the same minor problem recorded two
years running, a mileage that goes down, a fail for tyres every spring. It tells you what testers saw
on a few days, which is exactly what a certificate is.

## What people get wrong

**"It passed, so it's roadworthy until next year."** The certificate records one day (nidirect: it
"doesn’t mean that the vehicle is roadworthy for the life of the certificate"), and lesson 20's law
applies every time the car is used.

**"The MOT is a service."** It "does not cover the condition of the engine, clutch or gearbox", and
it's done without dismantling. Your handbook's service schedule is a different thing.

**"The first MOT moves to four years in 2026."** The government decided against it in 2018 and again
in January 2024. Northern Ireland's first test comes at four years on nidirect's page, which is a
different place's rule, not a change to Great Britain's.

**"It failed, but there's time on the old certificate, so I can drive it anywhere."** In Great
Britain it depends on whether a dangerous problem was listed, and in Northern Ireland nidirect says a
failed car shouldn't be on the road; both are in "After a fail" above.

**"Classic cars don't have to be roadworthy."** Exempt from the test, not from the law, in both
places; see "Classic cars" above.

**"An advisory means nothing."** It means a tester saw an item that had deteriorated, though not enough
to fail. GOV.UK puts
it among "problems to monitor or fix in the future".

only the number plate. Nothing here touches the car, and don't drive it for the exercise.

1. Open GOV.UK's [MOT history service](https://www.gov.uk/check-mot-history) and enter the number
   plate. In Northern Ireland, the same service covers tests since 2017.
2. Write down the date the current MOT runs out, and work out the earliest date that keeps it, by
   GOV.UK's month-minus-a-day rule.
3. List every fail, minor problem and advisory across the history, with the year, and mark any that
   appear more than once.
4. Note whether the history mentions a recall, and write "checked separately in lesson 23" beside
   it whichever way it comes out.
:::

:::checkpoint Look at your list. Which items are yours to act on, and which need someone else?
Keeping the date and booking the test are **Yours**. Anything the history records as a fail or a
minor problem on brakes, steering or suspension is **Call someone**, and so is a repeated advisory
on them; lesson 24, "Choosing a garage, servicing and warranties", covers choosing who. A tyre item
is a check you can make yourself with lesson 8's gauge, and the repair or replacement is a garage's.
A mileage that goes down between tests is a question for the seller, not a fault in the car, and on
this course's reading a reason to slow down before buying.
:::

certificate runs out, the earliest date that keeps it in Great Britain, any fail, minor problem or
advisory from the last two tests, and how to appeal (DVSA's complaint form in Great Britain, the DVA on
0300 200 7862 in Northern Ireland) with the date you read it.
