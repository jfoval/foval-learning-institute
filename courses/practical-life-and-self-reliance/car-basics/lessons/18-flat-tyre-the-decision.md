A flat tyre is the one breakdown most drivers expect to sort out themselves, and the part that goes
wrong is usually not the wheel. It is the decision made in the first minute: where to stop, whether
to get the jack out at all, and whether what is in the boot can take the car where it needs to go. The
Highway Code, which covers England, Scotland and Wales, puts the whole of it in two sentences in its [Annex 6](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/the-highway-code/annex-6-vehicle-maintenance-safety-and-security): "If you
have a flat tyre, stop as soon as it is safe to do so. Only change the tyre if you can do so without
putting yourself or others at risk - otherwise call a breakdown service."[1] What "safe" and "at
risk" mean at the roadside, and what each kind of spare will and won't do for you, is the rest of the
page.

:::callout Before the car lets you down
This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. If your car fails on a motorway or other fast road in Great Britain, leave at the next exit or services if you can; if you can't, go left into an emergency area or onto the hard shoulder, switch on your hazard lights, get everyone out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and don't go back to the car. If you're stuck in a live lane or can't get out safely, stay in with seat belts and hazard lights on and call 999 and ask for the police. In the US, pull as far onto the right shoulder as you can, switch on your hazard lights and stay in with your seat belt on, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind, in which case get out on the side away from traffic; call 911 in an emergency. Never put any part of your body under a car held up only by a jack, and never touch the orange high-voltage cables on a hybrid or electric car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

The callout's jack clause is lesson 19's subject, "Changing a wheel safely", which follows this one.
This lesson decides whether you get that far. Two sections below state law: the space saver's, for
Great Britain and Virginia, and the run-flat's, for Great Britain. This is general information, not legal advice. The rules differ between England and
Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and they change.

UK words come with the US word the first time: tyre (tire), space saver (compact spare), boot
(trunk), bonnet (hood), hard shoulder (shoulder). Speeds are in mph with km/h, as the handbooks give
them.

## If a tyre bursts while you're driving

A slow puncture gives you time. A burst does not, and the one thing that matters then is what your
hands and feet do in the next few seconds.

:::predict A front tyre bursts at speed with a loud bang, and the car lurches to one side. What should your feet do, and what's the mistake?

Nothing sudden with either foot. NHTSA, the US federal road safety regulator, names the two sudden moves as the mistake: "Any overreaction", including "slamming on the brakes or abruptly removing your foot from the accelerator", "can result in a loss of control over the vehicle."
:::

[NHTSA's page on tyres](https://www.nhtsa.gov/vehicle-safety/tires) gives one goal: "The goal in any blowout is to keep the vehicle
balanced and controllable."[3] Beyond that goal NHTSA gives no physics, and this course adds
none. The two countries' advice is short, and it agrees on the point that
matters:

| In Great Britain (Highway Code, Annex 6) | In the US (NHTSA) |
|---|---|
| "If a tyre bursts while you are driving, try to keep control of your vehicle. Grip the steering wheel firmly and allow the vehicle to roll to a stop at the side of the road."[1] | "Hold the steering wheel with both hands." "Maintain your vehicle speed if possible and if it's safe to do so." "Gradually release the accelerator." "Correct the steering as necessary to stabilize your vehicle and regain control." "Once your vehicle has stabilized, continue to slow down and pull off the road where and when you judge it's safe to do so."[3] |

They agree on the part that matters: a firm grip on the wheel (NHTSA says with both hands), nothing
sudden with the brake or the accelerator, and slowing down only as the car allows. This course reads the
two together that way. NHTSA adds that
the steps are the same whichever tyre goes; you just feel a front burst "more in the vehicle's
steering" and a rear one "more in the seat or body of the vehicle".[3] Honda's handbook for the 2024
Civic sold in the US covers a tyre that goes flat while you drive: "grasp the steering wheel firmly,
and brake gradually to reduce speed. Then, stop in a safe place."[5] Honda's is for a tyre going flat, not a sudden burst, and it has
you brake gently; NHTSA, for a blowout, holds speed first and slows once the car is stable. Neither
has you do anything sudden. What to
do with a car that pulls or wanders is driving, which this course does not teach; what to do once
it's stopped is the rest of this lesson.

## Where to stop: as soon as it is safe, and no sooner

Three sources pull slightly different ways here, but they aren't really in conflict.

Toyota, in the handbook for the 2023 Corolla sold in the US, says: "Do not continue driving with
a flat tire. Driving even a short distance with a flat tire can damage the tire and the wheel beyond
repair, which could result in an accident."[4] The RAC, a UK motoring organisation that sells
breakdown cover and repairs, weighs it the other way: "It's better to drive further and risk damaging
the wheel rim than stop somewhere dangerous", naming a busy road with no hard shoulder as the
example, "but only drive to the nearest safe point."[8] Mazda, in its European English handbook for
the Mazda3: "If you have a flat tyre, drive slowly to a level spot that is well off the road and out
of the way of traffic to change the tyre. Stopping in traffic or on the shoulder of a busy road is
dangerous."[6]

On this course's reading, they're describing one decision from two sides. Toyota is telling you what
driving on a flat costs (the tyre and the wheel, and possibly an accident); the RAC, which sells
breakdown cover, and Mazda are
telling you what stopping in the wrong place costs. Annex 6's "stop as soon as it is safe to do so"
joins them: the shortest drive, slowly, to the nearest place off the road and out of the traffic.
Lesson 16, "When the car stops: where, who stands where, and the call", named those places: the
Highway Code's "place of relative safety", with somewhere designed for parking the safest.[2]

## The decision: change it yourself, or call

In Great Britain, Annex 6's test is whether you can change the tyre "without putting yourself or
others at risk".[1] This is advice, with no MUST in it, and it leaves "risk" to you. The handbooks
and the motoring organisations fill it in.

### Night on the M62

Before the table, try one. It is night on the M62 over the Pennines, and a rear tyre goes flat. The
driver moves left and stops in an emergency area. The boot holds a space saver and a jack, the
flat is on the nearside (the left, in Great Britain), away from the traffic, and she thinks she could
have it done in fifteen minutes.

:::predict Before opening this: does she change it? If not, what does she do, step by step, and does the space saver make any difference?

She does not change it. On a motorway, Rule 277 says "DO NOT attempt repairs on your vehicle", National Highways says the same, and Green Flag, which sells breakdown cover and repairs, says not to change a tyre there "even if you're on the hard shoulder or in an emergency area". The side the flat is on, the space saver and her skill don't come into it. So the callout's steps: hazard lights on, sidelights because it is dark, everyone out by the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and nobody goes back to the car. From there she calls. If she has stopped near one of the free emergency telephones, she uses it. National Highways also gives 0300 123 5000 for a mobile, from a hard shoulder, an emergency area or a lay-by. If she hasn't stopped near a free emergency telephone, Rule 277 and National Highways both say to call 999 and ask for the police. Then her breakdown service, if she has one. When the car is ready to rejoin the motorway, leaving an emergency area is law, not advice: Rule 278 says she MUST "use the emergency telephone provided and follow the operator's advice for exiting the emergency area". Lesson 17, "Motorways and fast roads: go left, the live lane, and the smart motorway question", has the rest.
:::

### The conditions

Changing a wheel is **Yours, on conditions**, and these are the conditions:

| Condition | Whose words |
|---|---|
| Not on a motorway or other fast road, and not on any hard shoulder or emergency area | Highway Code Rule 277 (advice): "DO NOT attempt repairs on your vehicle"; National Highways, the same words, on a page that opens "Whatever part of National Highways' network you're driving on" (its motorways and major A roads); Green Flag, which sells breakdown cover and repairs: "even if you're on the hard shoulder or in an emergency area"[2][9][10] |
| Off the road and out of the way of traffic | Mazda, above; Nissan: "Never repair tires if oncoming traffic is close to your vehicle. Wait for professional road assistance."[6][7] |
| Firm, level, not slippery | Toyota, "a hard, flat surface"; Honda, "a firm, level, non-slippery surface"; Nissan: "Never repair tires when the vehicle is on a slope, ice or slippery areas."[4][5][7] |
| Your car's own kit, and a handbook that gives the method | Toyota, Honda and Mazda give the method for their own cars; Honda: "Use the jack provided in your vehicle." Lesson 19 has the steps[4][5][6] |
| Nobody between the car and oncoming traffic | Highway Code Rule 276 (advice): "do not stand (or let anybody else stand) between your vehicle and oncoming traffic"[2] |

If any condition fails, it's **Call someone**. Two readings are this course's own. The last row
depends on the scene: a flat on the traffic side with the traffic close is a call. And a lay-by on a
fast road counts as the fast road, so the callout's steps apply there and the wheel is a call. In the US, the rows that come
from the handbooks apply as they stand; the motorway and Rule 276 rows are Great Britain's, and the
US freeway paragraph below takes their place.

Nissan's two lines come from the handbook for the 2024 LEAF sold in the US, and they are written about
its sealant kit, because that car carries no spare. On this course's reading they apply at least as
strongly to a wheel change, which keeps you at the wheel for longer.

No source read says not to change a wheel in the dark, and the RAC, which sells breakdown cover and repairs,
lists a torch "for working at night" in its kit.[8] Rule 276's advice is not to stand, at night,
where you block other drivers' view of your lights,[2] and on this course's reading, kneeling at
a rear wheel can do just that. Darkness raises every risk in the table (you're harder to see, and so
is the ground), and this course's reading is that it tips a close decision towards a call.

For a US freeway, no official source read tells a driver whether to change a wheel on the shoulder
herself. The California Driver Handbook's freeway steps are to stay in the car, belted, unless one of
three things holds: not enough space on the shoulder, a guardrail, or an area to stay safely away from
the freeway lanes;[16] they don't include a wheel change. This course's reading is the same as for a
motorway: on a freeway shoulder, it is a call, and the callout's US steps are the ones to follow.

Most of the time, calling means a breakdown service you pay for or already have cover with. In the UK
the AA, the RAC and Green Flag sell cover and repairs, and in the US AAA clubs sell roadside
assistance and run a repair network; this course recommends none of them. Free help is narrower. In parts of California, the
Highway Patrol's Freeway Service Patrol, at commute times, will "Change a flat tire."[16] In England,
National Highways can send "other help as required, such as a Traffic Officer", once the emergency
services alert it,[10] but no source read says traffic officers change wheels.

## What your car carries

Before any of this, you need to know what's in your boot, because cars differ more than people
expect. Of the four handbooks read for this lesson, the 2023 Corolla sold in the US carries a compact
spare; Honda's handbook for the 2024 Civic sold in the US covers a compact spare and a sealant kit,
each marked "Not available on all models"; the Mazda3's European handbook covers both a temporary spare and a kit on "Some Models"; and
Nissan's handbook for the 2024 LEAF sold in the US says plainly: "This vehicle does not have a spare
tire."[4][5][6][7] Some cars have run-flat tyres instead of any of these, and Mazda's handbook warns
that a car "may or may not be equipped with a spare tyre, jack, wheel brace, and tool bag".[6]

### A full-size spare

This course read no handbook for a car with a full-size spare. Lesson 8, "Tyres II: tread, damage,
age, and when to replace", gave NHTSA's warning that every spare ages because spares seldom get
replaced, and that a full-size spare is for emergencies such as a flat, not a replacement for worn
tyres.[3] Lesson 7, "Tyres I: pressure, the placard and TPMS", had you check its pressure with the
others and showed how to read the date code on its sidewall.

### A space saver

A space saver is a narrow spare. Mazda calls its own "lighter and smaller than a conventional tyre",
and "designed only for emergency use and should be used only for VERY short periods."[6] Toyota's is marked
"TEMPORARY USE ONLY" on its sidewall.[4] Each handbook read that has one sets limits for its own car:

| Handbook, car, market | Speed | Other limits the handbook states |
|---|---|---|
| Toyota, 2023 Corolla, US | "Do not drive at speeds in excess of 50 mph (80 km/h)" | One at a time; no tyre chains on it; with it fitted, ABS, stability control and other systems "may not operate correctly"; its pressure is not watched by the tyre pressure warning system[4] |
| Honda, 2024 Civic Sedan, US | "keep the vehicle speed under 50 mph (80 km/h)" | Pressure 60 psi (420 kPa); no chains; on manual-transmission models, not on the front; "Replace with a full-size tire as soon as possible"[5] |
| Mazda, Mazda3, European English | "Do not exceed 80 km/h (50 mph)." | One at a time; no chains; "Do not install the temporary spare tyre on the front wheels (driving wheels)"[6] |

The speeds agree, and they're still three handbooks, not yours. Lesson 1, "What a car is made of, and
why the handbook comes first", made the same point with a fourth, Nissan's for the 2023 LEAF sold in
the UK, which covers a temporary spare with the same 80 km/h (50 mph). The 2024 LEAF sold in the US
has no spare at all: same model, two markets, two answers. The other rows do not agree, and that's
the lesson in them. Mazda forbids its temporary spare on the front of a Mazda3, because "Handling will
be affected. You could lose control of the vehicle, especially on ice or snow bound roads". Honda
forbids it on the front of a manual Civic, for a different reason: it "may damage the differential",
part of the drive to the wheels.
Toyota has no such rule, and for a front flat on snow or ice it says to put the compact spare on a
rear wheel and move a full-size rear tyre to the front, so that the chains go on the front.[4][5][6]

**Why the car's systems change.** Toyota gives the reason for its own car: with the compact spare
fitted, "The vehicle speed may not be correctly detected", and so a list of systems, starting with
"ABS & Brake assist", VSC and TRAC (Toyota's stability and traction control), "may not operate correctly".[4] That's Toyota's account for the
Corolla, and it goes no further; the other handbooks read do not give one.

**The pressure warning.** Lesson 7 taught what the tyre pressure warning light does. That light may
not watch the spare. Toyota: the compact spare has no warning valve and transmitter, so "low
inflation pressure of the spare tire will not be indicated by the tire pressure warning system".
Honda says its light comes on and a message appears when the spare is fitted, and "this is normal".[4][5]
Mazda has an instruction about its own system once the spare is on, and your handbook will say whether
yours has one.[6] So on a space saver
the gauge check is yours, and the light isn't telling you about that tyre.

:::checkpoint A friend's car has a space saver on the front left after a puncture. The handbook says the speed limit on it is 50 mph and has a line about which wheels it may go on. The tyre pressure warning light is on. She plans to drive 90 miles home tonight on a motorway at 50 mph. What would you point out?

Three things. Her handbook's line about wheels comes first: Mazda's and a manual Civic's rule the front out, and if hers does too, the spare is in the wrong place. That's her maker's rule, and moving it is a second wheel change, somewhere safe and off the road. The light may be telling her nothing about the spare, depending on her car, so its pressure needs a gauge. And 90 miles is not the "VERY short periods" Mazda has in mind, or Honda's "as soon as possible": the job of a space saver is to get the car to a tyre fitter, not home across the country. If she's in Great Britain, the law adds that above 50 mph the spare is no longer excused.
:::

## The space saver and the law

This is general information, not legal advice.

In Great Britain, [regulation 27](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1986/1078/regulation/27) of the Road Vehicles (Construction and Use) Regulations 1986 lists
the tyre defects that stop a car being used on a road, starting with a tyre that is "unsuitable having
regard to the use to which the motor vehicle or trailer is being put or to the types of tyres fitted
to its other wheels". Paragraph (3) deals with a temporary spare against that rule:
"Paragraph (1)(a) does not prohibit the use on a road of a passenger vehicle (not being a bus) by
reason only of the fact that a wheel of the vehicle is fitted with a temporary use spare tyre, unless
the vehicle is driven at a speed exceeding 50 mph."[11] The regulations define a temporary use spare
as one designed for use only "in the event of the failure of one of the tyres normally fitted to a
wheel of the vehicle" and "at a speed lower than that for which such normally fitted tyres are
designed".[11]

So the law allows a space saver only up to 50 mph. That isn't a speed limit for the car; it is
the point above which the exemption no longer applies, and the unsuitable-tyre rule can. The
handbook sits beside it, and here the three handbooks read agree with it. Another car's handbook
may be stricter, and if it is, the handbook is the one to follow. Fitting and driving on a space saver
is **Yours, on conditions**: the handbook's speed and its other limits, and a tyre fitter as soon as
you can get there. The regulations extend to Great Britain; Northern Ireland's rules weren't read by
this course.

In the US, this course read no federal or state rule on how fast a car may go on a compact spare, so
the limit there is the handbook's. One state rule does touch it. [Virginia's safety inspection](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/admincode/title19/agency30/chapter70/section130/), run by
the State Police, rejects a car if it "has installed on any axle a space saver emergency spare tire
that is intended for temporary use".[12] So in Virginia a car on its space saver fails inspection.
On this course's reading, that's one more reason to get the tyre fixed and the spare off. Lesson 22, "The US: state
inspections, emissions tests and tread law", covers inspection itself.

## A sealant kit

Many cars carry a kit instead of a spare: a bottle of sealant and a small compressor that plugs into
the car. It's for one small hole in the tread, and the handbooks spell out when it will not work. Using
one is **Yours, on conditions**, and these are the conditions, in each maker's words:

| Don't use the kit if | What the handbooks say |
|---|---|
| The sealant is out of date | Honda: "The tire sealant has expired." Mazda: its "period of effective use" has expired. Nissan: past its "expiration date". |
| More than one puncture or flat tyre | Honda: "More than one tire is punctured." Mazda: "two or more punctures". Nissan: "when two or more tires are flat". |
| The hole is too big | Honda: "larger than 5/32 inch (4 mm)". Mazda: "exceeds about 4 mm (0.16 in)". Nissan: "approximately 0.25 in (6 mm) or longer". |
| Outside the tread | Honda: the side wall "is damaged or the puncture is outside the contact area". Mazda: "an area of the tyre other than the tread". Nissan: "when the tire sidewall is damaged". |
| The tyre was driven very flat | Honda: "extremely under inflated". Mazda: "nearly no air remaining in the tyre". Nissan: "extremely low tire pressure". |
| The tyre is off the rim, or the rim is damaged | Honda: "The tire bead is no longer seated." "The rim is damaged." Mazda and Nissan: the same two conditions in their own words. |

Sources: each maker's handbook for that car.[5][6][7]

Look at the size row. Two makers say about 4 mm and the third says about 6 mm, so "the kit handles
small holes" is true and "the kit handles holes up to 6 mm" is Nissan's figure for the LEAF, not a
rule. The puncture row differs too: Honda and Nissan count tyres, Mazda counts holes in the tyre. All
three also say to leave the nail or screw in the tyre. Honda gives the reason: "If you
remove it from the tire, you may not be able to repair the puncture using the kit."[5]

Four more cautions come from the handbooks:

- **Where you run it.** Honda's compressor runs with the engine on, and its warning is about carbon
  monoxide: "Only run the engine to power the air compressor with the vehicle outdoors." It adds that
  "an enclosed or even partly enclosed area" is enough to be dangerous.[5] Honda also says to put the
  kit "on flat ground near the flat tire, and away from traffic."
- **Where you stand.** Nissan: "Do not stand directly beside the damaged tire while it is being
  inflated because of the risk of the rupture."[7]
- **How far and how fast.** Each kit has you drive about 10 minutes to spread the sealant, below 50
  mph (80 km/h), then check the pressure. If it has fallen below about 19 psi (130 kPa), the repair has
  failed: Honda, "Do not add air or continue driving. The leak is too severe. Call for help and have
  your vehicle towed."[5][6][7]
- **What it is not.** Before its kit instructions, Nissan says of a flat LEAF: "If possible, have the vehicle towed to a
  facility that can repair or replace the flat tire." And: "The Genuine NISSAN Emergency Tire Sealant cannot permanently seal a
  punctured tire. Continuing operation of the vehicle without a permanent tire repair can lead to a
  crash." Mazda recommends "that the tyre be replaced with a new one as soon as possible".[6][7] Toyota
  and Nissan both warn that liquid sealant may stop the tyre pressure sensor in the wheel working
  properly, and both say to have it replaced.[4][7]

### A nail in an Ocala car park

A driver comes out of a supermarket in Ocala to find her 2024 Civic's rear
tyre soft, with a nail head showing in the middle of the tread. The car park is open-air and the car
is in a bay well away from the traffic lane. The boot has Honda's kit.

**Step 1: can the kit be used?** Go down Honda's list. The sealant's date is on the bottle: in date.
One tyre. The nail is in the tread, in the part that touches the road, not the sidewall. The hole
around a nail is small; she can't measure it, but a nail is exactly what Honda's "small puncture, from
a nail for instance" means. She did not drive on it flat, and the tyre is still on the rim. Every
condition is met.

**Step 2: where she runs it.** Outdoors, in the open, as Honda requires for the engine, with the kit
on flat ground by the tyre. A multi-storey car park would change this answer: it's "partly enclosed",
and she would call instead of running the engine there.

**Step 3: leave the nail.** Honda says not to remove it.

**Step 4: drive, check, decide.** About 10 minutes, under 50 mph, then check the pressure. Honda
gives three answers. At 32 psi (220 kPa) or more, its figure for a rear tyre on this car, she drives
on for up to another 10 minutes towards a tyre fitter, under 50, and checks again if she isn't there
yet. Between 19 and 32 psi, she pumps it back up to 32 and does the same. Below 19 psi (130 kPa), the
kit has failed and she calls for a tow. At the fitter, the tyre is repaired or replaced.

## Run-flat tyres

This is general information, not legal advice.

A run-flat tyre is built to be driven on for a limited distance after it loses air; TyreSafe, a UK
tyre safety charity backed by tyre makers, carmakers and retailers, says run-flats "have a reinforced
sidewall".[15] In Great Britain, on this course's reading, regulation 27(2) is where the law provides for
them: paragraph (1) does not prohibit using a car with a tyre "which is deflated or not fully
inflated" and has a cut, bulge or exposed cord, "if the tyre and the wheel to which it is fitted are so
constructed as to make the tyre in that condition fit for the use to which the motor vehicle or trailer
is being put" and the tyre's sides are marked to show it.[11]

How far and how fast is the handbook's, and this course read no handbook for a car with run-flats. The
general figure read here comes from TyreSafe, the industry-backed charity: "Run flat tyres are designed only to be used for 50 miles at a maximum speed of 50 mph."[15]
Your handbook's figure is the one for your car. TyreSafe adds a warning of its
own: "It can be difficult to tell just by looking if your car's run-flat tyres are in a deflated
condition."[15] On this course's reading, that leaves the pressure warning and a gauge as the ways
you would know.

TyreSafe, the industry-backed charity, is clear about one case: driving on a punctured or deflated
run-flat "subjects the tyre structure to high stress", which it says makes the tyre "both unsuitable
and unsafe to repair".[15] Beyond that case the sources read don't give one answer, and this course
doesn't settle it. TyreSafe goes on: "A fully qualified tyre repairer may struggle to detect under
inflation in a run flat, which is why several associations do not recommend repairing a run-flat
tyre."[15] USTMA, the US tyre makers' trade body, says "some tire manufacturers may have repair limits
or restrictions for some tires, such as runflat tires and others."[14] Either way, it's a question for
the tyre fitter and your tyre maker's policy, not for the roadside.

## A proper repair, and why a plug from outside isn't one

Repairing a puncture is **Call someone**, because it's a tyre fitter's job, and this course
doesn't teach plugging a tyre. What it can teach is what to ask for.

In the UK, the standard is the British Standard BS AU 159, which this course read as summarised by the
[BTMA](https://btmauk.com/advice-about-tyres/tyre-industry-professionals/), the British tyre makers' trade body, not in the original. Its extracts are from the 1997 issue
(suffix f), and the BTMA says the standard is "currently suffix g", an issue this course did not read.
The summary: "Some minor damage to car
or light van tyres can be safely repaired; principally punctures to the central area of the tread that
are less than 6mm in diameter." The repairer must "Remove the tyre from the wheel and thoroughly
inspect, both internally and externally." Among methods "not recognised as acceptable" are "Liquid
sealants.", "Plugs applied externally to fitted tyres." and "Temporary repairs." For a repair it
accepts, it requires "Repair in area 'T' only" (a central band of the tread, set as a share of the tyre's
width), by a "Rubber only
combination plug patch." or a "Rubber only patch and penetration filling material.", and the
procedure marks the patch's outline "on the inner liner": a patch on the inside either way. And a tyre repaired to
the standard is "able to operate at their original speed and load capabilities."[13] The BTMA adds that
the standard "does not apply to T-type temporary use spare tyres" (space savers).

In the US, the page of [USTMA](https://www.ustires.org/tire-care-safety/tire-repair-basics), the US tyre makers' trade body, says to consider a repair "only if damage is limited to the tread area only
and the puncture injury is no greater than 1/4 inch (6mm) in diameter", that the tyre comes off the
wheel for inspection, that the hole is filled with a plug and the inside sealed with a patch, "(A plug
alone is an unacceptable repair.)", and: "NEVER perform an outside-in tire repair or on-the-wheel
repair."[14] The two industries' rules agree in substance: tread only, 6 mm, off the wheel, inside
sealed.

**Why off the wheel.** TyreSafe, the industry-backed charity, gives the reason: permanent repairs need the tyre off the wheel "to
allow a thorough inspection internally as well as externally to ensure there is no hidden damage which
could result in a catastrophic failure."[15] And the BTMA's summary, the British trade body's, lists what that inspection can
find that rules a repair out, including "Secondary effects, e.g. under-inflation/deflation damage".[13]
On this course's reading, that is the link back to Toyota's warning at the top: driving on a flat can
do damage that doesn't show from outside, and that damage is what turns a repairable puncture into a
new tyre.

TyreSafe, the industry-backed charity, says repairs "must only be carried out by a tyre specialist and in accordance with the
current British Standard AU159", and separately that "To stay compliant with the law" repairs can
only be made on the central three quarters of the tyre.[15] This course read no statute that makes
either a legal requirement, so it reports them as TyreSafe's.

## What people get wrong

**"A plug from outside is a repair."** The two trade bodies, the BTMA in the UK and USTMA in the US,
both reject it, above.

**"A space saver is just another wheel."** Toyota: with it fitted, the car's braking and stability
systems "may not operate correctly"; every handbook read limits the speed; and in Great Britain the law
excuses it only up to 50 mph.

**"A run-flat goes as far as you need."** TyreSafe, the industry-backed charity, gives a general figure of 50 miles at 50 mph, and your
handbook's may differ. Neither is "as far as you need".

**"Sealant fixes it."** Nissan: it "cannot permanently seal a punctured tire". The BTMA, the British
trade body, lists
liquid sealants among methods not accepted as a repair.

**"Stop at once, wherever you are."** Annex 6 says as soon as it is safe, and the RAC, which sells breakdown cover and repairs, says to
drive on only to "the nearest safe point". Stopping in a live lane or on a narrow verge to save a wheel rim gets
the trade the wrong way round.

:::checkpoint Four flats. For each, say whether what the car carries matters, and what the driver does. (a) Great Britain: a slow puncture noticed at home, a nail in the tread, a sealant kit in the boot. (b) England: a burst on a motorway, the car now on the hard shoulder, a full-size spare in the boot. (c) US: a flat found in the morning with the car on its own level concrete driveway, a compact spare in the trunk, daylight. (d) Great Britain: a flat on a narrow country lane with no verge, just past a blind bend, at dusk, with a space saver.

(a) The kit may be usable: go down the handbook's list (in date, one hole, in the tread, not driven flat), run it outdoors, drive to a tyre fitter within its speed and have it repaired properly. Or leave the kit alone and call a tyre fitter or a breakdown service to the house.

(b) What's carried does not matter. No wheel change on a motorway: the callout's steps, and call from behind the barrier.

(c) A wheel change is on the table: off the road, away from traffic, level and hard, daylight, and the handbook's method (lesson 19). The compact spare then carries its handbook's speed limit to a tyre shop.

(d) The spare doesn't help. The car cannot get off the road, the traffic comes round a blind bend, and it's getting dark: the conditions that matter here all fail. Hazard lights and sidelights, everyone well away from the car and the road, and call. If the car can be driven slowly to a proper place a short way on, the "nearest safe point" of the RAC, which sells breakdown cover and repairs, is the other option.
:::
