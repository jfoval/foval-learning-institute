A car can go its whole life in some US states without a stranger ever looking at its brakes. Drive
the same car across a state line and it may need a sticker on the windscreen (windshield) every
year, the state's proof that it passed, a check of its tread down to the thirty-second of an inch
(under a millimetre), and a look under the bonnet (hood) for a missing catalytic converter. Great
Britain has one test for the whole country, which lesson 21, "The MOT and Northern Ireland's vehicle
test", covered. The US has no national test at all. It has states, and they don't agree. Five of
them show the range here, and they lead to a real disagreement: whether periodic safety inspection
prevents crashes.

:::callout Before the car lets you down
This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. If your car fails on a motorway or other fast road in Great Britain, leave at the next exit or services if you can; if you can't, go left into an emergency area or onto the hard shoulder, switch on your hazard lights, get everyone out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and don't go back to the car. If you're stuck in a live lane or can't get out safely, stay in with seat belts and hazard lights on and call 999 and ask for the police. In the US, pull as far onto the right shoulder as you can, switch on your hazard lights and stay in with your seat belt on, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind, in which case get out on the side away from traffic; call 911 in an emergency. Never put any part of your body under a car held up only by a jack, and never touch the orange high-voltage cables on a hybrid or electric car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.

## No national test: the shape, and why it shrank

This is general information, not legal advice.

The main official account this course found is a [2015 report](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-15-705) by the Government Accountability
Office (GAO), the audit office of the US Congress. It counted the states: "As of July 2015, we found
16 states with periodic inspection programs." Of those, "11 of the 16 states with inspection
programs require an annual vehicle safety inspection, three states require a biennial inspection,
and two states require time frames other than annual or biennial."[1] That's the most recent official
national count this course found. It's eleven years old, and at least one state has left the list
since: Texas ended safety inspection for non-commercial vehicles from 1 January 2025, as below.
Whether any other state has started or stopped since 2015, this course didn't check, so it gives no
current total.

:::predict In the mid-1970s, how many states do you think required periodic safety inspection: fewer than 16, about the same, or about twice as many?

About twice as many. GAO: "the mid 1970’s was the high-water mark for these programs, with 31 states
and the District of Columbia implementing programs."[1]
:::

Why did the number fall? GAO ties the long decline to federal money. From 1967,
the Department of Transportation "could withhold a percentage of federal-aid highway funds
apportioned to a state that did not implement a periodic motor vehicle inspection program". Then:
"Since 1976, after legislation limited NHTSA’s authority to withhold federal-aid highway funding,
the number of states with programs has declined."[1] Since that change, in GAO's words, "states have
been able to choose whether or not to follow the guidelines". NHTSA's guideline, still recommending periodic inspection of all
registered vehicles, remained as of GAO's report.[1] Of the five states and the District of Columbia that dropped
programmes between 1990 and 2015, GAO says they did so "some citing a lack of evidence proving the
program’s effectiveness or saving financial resources as reasons".[1]

Two things are easy to confuse with the safety test: **emissions testing**, a separate programme
under the federal Clean Air Act, and tread rules, where the only federal rule that binds a driver
covers commercial vehicles. Each has its own section below.

## Five states, side by side

This is general information, not legal advice.

These five were chosen because between them they show the range: annual safety with emissions in some
counties (a county is a local area within a state), annual safety with emissions everywhere, annual
safety with emissions only in places the state's law names, no safety test since 2025, and
emissions only. They aren't a sample of the country. Your state may do something none of them does.
A US car is registered with its state, which renews the registration at intervals it sets, and a
state can make renewal wait on a passed test, as New York and California do below.

**Pennsylvania.** PennDOT, the state transport department, runs [its programme](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dmv/vehicle-services/inspection-and-safety-requirements/safety-inspection-program) through "official
PennDOT Inspection Stations (usually a repair garage or a service station with a repair shop)", and
"Inspection fees are posted at official inspection stations."[2] Its regulations are published as Pub 45, which is 67 Pa. Code Chapter 175; the
edition read is dated February 2026.[3]

**New York.** The state's Department of Motor Vehicles ([DMV](https://dmv.ny.gov/new-york-state-vehicle-safetyemissions-inspection-program)), the office that registers cars, runs
one visit for both tests: "The emissions inspection is done at the same time as the annual safety
inspection." Two consumer points on its page are New York's rules, and worth asking about wherever
you're inspected: "The inspection station must have your permission before beginning any repairs",
and "You are not required to have your vehicle repaired or re-inspected at the station that performed
the initial inspection."[4]

**Virginia.** The [State Police](https://vsp.virginia.gov/safety-and-enforcement/vehicle-safety-inspection/) run it. Its published inspection procedure runs from brakes and lights to
the hood latch, fluid levels ("Brake fluid" and "Power steering fluid"), the airbag and its readiness
light, and the emissions parts: "No parts removed, disconnected, or rendered inoperable."[5] The emissions test
itself is set by statute: § 46.2-1178 of the Code of Virginia applies it in localities it names:
five counties and five cities in one subsection, and six more in another, subject to regulations
and EPA approval. Blacksburg, the case below, is in none of them.[17]

**Texas.** The Department of Public Safety (DPS) [announced the change](https://www.dps.texas.gov/news/vehicle-safety-inspection-program-changes-now-effect) in January 2025: "as of Jan. 1,
2025, non-commercial vehicles no longer need a vehicle safety inspection prior to registration." It
is "due to House Bill 3297", passed by the 88th Legislature in 2023. Emissions tests continue in the
counties it names, and "All commercial vehicles in all counties are still required to obtain a
passing vehicle safety inspection."[7]

**California.** The state programme this course read for California is Smog Check, run by the Bureau
of Automotive Repair ([BAR](https://www.bar.ca.gov/consumer/smog-check-program)), and it tests emissions. "Inspection prices are not regulated by BAR."[8] GAO's 2015 appendix lists neither California
among the states then inspecting nor among those that had repealed a programme.[1] This course found no
California page that says in words that there's no periodic safety inspection.

## A car in Blacksburg, Virginia

This is general information, not legal advice.

A car registered in Blacksburg, Virginia, goes in for its annual inspection. Three things from the
state's manual and pages decide what happens.

The fee first. Virginia's regulation 19VAC30-70-40 sets maximums: "$20 for each inspection of any
other vehicle, including trailers and motor homes", which covers a car.[6] That's a ceiling, not a
price, the same shape as Great Britain's MOT maximum in lesson 21.

The tyres (tires) next. Lesson 8, "Tyres II: tread, damage, age, and when to replace", walked
through Virginia's measuring method on a tyre in Lynchburg, and it
applies here unchanged: two adjacent grooves where the tread is thinnest, more measurements only if
both are under 2/32 inch, and "Do not take measurements from the tread wear indicators."[6] One line
in the same regulation catches people out. The inspector rejects the car if it "has installed on any
axle a space saver emergency spare tire that is intended for temporary use".[6] The owner who put the
compact spare on after a puncture last month, and never swapped it back, fails on that alone.

Then what a fail means. The State Police page: "ALL DEFECTS MUST BE CORRECTED AND THE VEHICLE
REINSPECTED WITHIN 15 DAYS. THE DRIVER MAY BE SUBJECT TO RECEIVING A TRAFFIC SUMMONS FOR ANY DEFECT
STILL PRESENT WHEN THE VEHICLE IS OPERATED ON THE HIGHWAY."[5] Its FAQ adds that a rejection sticker
"in itself, places no travel restrictions on the vehicle; however, it does not provide any exception
to any statute governing equipment defects."[5] So fifteen days to fix it isn't fifteen days of
permission to drive a defect.

Replacing the spare is **Yours, on conditions**: the conditions are lesson 19's, "Changing a wheel
safely", and, as lesson 19 reads its sources, it's never on a motorway, a US freeway shoulder or any
other fast road, where the callout's steps apply instead. Repairing the punctured tyre is **Call someone**, a tyre fitter's
job, as lesson 18, "A flat tyre: the decision, and spare, space saver, sealant or run-flat", says.

:::checkpoint The Blacksburg car's airbag readiness light stays on through the whole inspection. On Virginia's list, does that matter, and would it matter the same way in New York?

In Virginia it's a reason to reject. The inspection covers "Any defects in the air bag system noted
by the air bag readiness light", and a light that "stays on continuously".[5] In New York the
station checks the airbag warning lamp too, but the DMV says it's "an advisement only and not
cause for rejection."[4] Lesson 11, "Dashboard warning lights", met the same contrast between New
York and the MOT. Same light,
three different consequences, because each is that place's rule. Either way the airbag itself is
**Call someone**.
:::

## From Waco, Texas, to Schenectady, New York

This is general information, not legal advice.

Now the wrinkle. A privately owned 2019 petrol (gasoline) car has been registered in Waco, Texas, since 2022. Waco
is in McLennan County, which isn't on DPS's list of emissions counties, so since 1 January 2025 the
car has had no inspection of any kind.[7] In 2026 its owner moves to Schenectady, New York, and
registers it there.

Two lines on the New York DMV's page decide it. "If your vehicle was registered in another state which
issues an inspection sticker, you do not need to have your vehicle inspected immediately. Your
out-of-state inspection remains valid until it expires or for one year after the registration date,
whichever comes first." And: "If the previous inspection was performed in a jurisdiction that does
not issue an inspection sticker, you must have your vehicle inspected in New York."[9]

:::checkpoint What does New York require of the car from Waco, and what will its inspection cover?

The second line, on this course's reading. The car has no current inspection from anywhere, and
Texas no longer inspects non-commercial cars, so there's no out-of-state sticker for New York to
honour. The DMV's page doesn't mention Texas by name; reading its rule onto a car from a state that
stopped inspecting is this course's application, and the DMV is the body to ask.

The inspection is both halves in one visit. The safety half is the DMV's list, from seat belts and
brakes to tyres at 2/32 inch. The
emissions half is the OBDII check, which the DMV applies to most 1996 and newer petrol cars with a
gross vehicle weight rating (the most the vehicle is rated to weigh loaded) under 8,501 pounds
(about 3,856 kg), and it "examines the Malfunction Indicator Lamp (MIL), also known as the "check engine"
light".[4] And at renewal the registration depends on it: "You
cannot renew a vehicle registration unless the DMV has a computer record that the vehicle passed the
required emissions inspection within the last 12 months or you can provide proof of the inspection
by providing a copy of the vehicle inspection report."[4]
:::

The point of the case is that nothing about the car changed at the state line. What changed is which
state's rule it lives under.

## Emissions testing: a separate programme

This is general information, not legal advice.

The emissions test has a different origin from the safety test. The
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA): "The 1990 Amendments to the Clean Air Act established I/M
requirements for several areas across the country."[10] I/M stands for inspection and maintenance,
which EPA defines as "the periodic inspection of the emissions control systems of motor vehicles".
The programmes are required mainly where air quality falls short of the national standards, and
"States are responsible for developing their own I/M program".[11] On this course's reading, that fits
Texas, where DPS says "Emissions tests are required in major metropolitan areas" and Waco's county
isn't on its list,[7] and it's why New York's DMV says "The 1990 Federal Clean Air Act requires New
York State to conduct an inspection program".[4]

:::predict An OBD test reads the car's own computer. Which of these would you expect it to catch: a failing oxygen sensor, worn brake pads, or a bald tyre?

The oxygen sensor. EPA says the OBD system "continuously monitors the emission control systems", and
names a fault "most commonly from the catalytic converter or an oxygen sensor".[11] Brakes and tyres
are the safety inspection's, where a state has one.
:::

EPA names three kinds of test. Visual inspections "deter tampering by checking for the presence of
certain required emission control parts (e.g., catalytic converter replaced with a straight pipe)."
Tailpipe testing is mainly "for vehicles that are model year 1995 and older". And the one most cars
now get: "Beginning with the 1996 model year, vehicles have been equipped with OBD computerized
systems. The OBD system continuously monitors the emission control systems and will activate the
‘‘Check Engine’’ light on the dashboard if a fault, also known as a Diagnostic Trouble Code (DTC), is
detected".[11] Lesson 11 explained the light; this is where the state's test meets it.

California shows the emissions test standing on its own. BAR: "A Smog Check is required every other
year to renew your vehicle registration", for petrol, hybrid and alternative-fuel cars of model year 1976
and newer and diesels of 1998 and newer (the model year is the one the maker gives the car's model),
and also on a change of
ownership and on first registering a car in California. Petrol, hybrid and alternative-fuel cars
"eight model years and newer" are exempt at renewal and pay a smog abatement fee instead. At a change of ownership the exemption is "four model years and newer",
with a smog transfer fee instead. Electric vehicles don't need one.[8] And BAR ties it to lesson 11: "A vehicle will not pass a Smog Check with the check
engine light on."[8]

### Removed parts: what three states check

The federal rule is in the Clean Air Act at 42 U.S.C. 7522(a)(3), which lesson 3, "Diesel exhaust: the
DPF and AdBlue", quoted: it prohibits any person "knowingly to remove or render inoperative" an
emission-control device after the car is sold.[12] Three of the states read here check for it at
inspection, and two of them in the safety inspection. Pennsylvania's regulation, in the 42 counties with no emissions test,
rejects a car if "The catalytic converter has been removed, disconnected or is the wrong type for the
certified vehicle configuration", with the same test for five other parts, "Provided that the make and
model year of the vehicle would have originally been equipped with the device".[3] Virginia's list
says "No parts removed, disconnected, or rendered inoperable", adding "This includes the catalytic
converter and the fuel tank filler pipe".[5] California's BAR says modified or non-approved software
"will cause a vehicle to fail a Smog Check".[8] A "delete" (lesson 3's word for an emissions part
removed or switched off) is not a maintenance choice anywhere this course read.

### A car in Clearfield, Pennsylvania

A car in Clearfield, Pennsylvania, has had its catalytic converter cut out and replaced with a
straight pipe. Clearfield County is one of the 42 on the state environmental department's Drive
Clean PA page where cars need no emissions test, though most "are required to pass a visual
anti-tampering check as part of a safety inspection".[16] So the safety inspection is where it's
caught, and § 175.80(d) rejects the car.[3] In a county that does test emissions, that test does the
checking instead; this course didn't read Pennsylvania's emissions test rules, so it says no more.
Putting the converter back is **Call someone**.

## Tread law, gathered

This is general information, not legal advice.

Lesson 8 set out the tread rules; this section gathers them. The federal figures in 49
CFR 393.75 are for commercial motor vehicles, and they're the only federal tread rule this course
found that binds a driver. For passenger cars, 49 CFR Part 570 sets "not less than two thirty-seconds
of an inch" as a criterion "for the inspection of motor vehicles by State inspection systems", and
says the part "does not in itself impose requirements on any person".[18] GAO says the same: these
standards "apply to all states that choose to implement a vehicle safety inspection program".[1] So
for driving a private car, tread law is set by states. The three inspection states read all use 2/32
of an inch at inspection, each measured its own way: Pennsylvania at any one point, New York in the
grooves "showing the most wear", Virginia at the thinnest spot, and at two more points only if that
one is under.[3][4][6]

NHTSA's advice, for any car in the US, is to replace tyres worn to 2/32 of an inch. That's advice,
not law; lesson 8 quotes it with its source, and the argument about replacing earlier.

## Between inspections: whose duty?

This is general information, not legal advice.

In Great Britain, lesson 20, "The law on a car's condition: Great Britain and Northern Ireland",
showed a driver's duty that applies every time the car is used, in the Road Traffic Act. Apart from
one Texas statute, below, this course read no US statute that does the same, and it doesn't borrow
Great Britain's. What it read first is what three state agencies say on their own pages.

New York's DMV: "Motor vehicles are required by law to be in safe operating condition whenever they
are driven on a public street or roadway in New York State."[4] Virginia's State Police, above: a
rejection sticker "does not provide any exception to any statute governing equipment defects".[5] And
Texas's DPS, in the same release that announced the end of inspection: "Regular maintenance
ensures the proper operation of your vehicle’s headlights, taillights, horn, mirrors, windshield
wipers, tires and other critical functions. Driving a vehicle without many of these properly working
safety features is against the law and puts everyone at risk."[7]

Those are each agency's statements about its own state's law. For Texas this course read the law
too. The same bill that ended the test, House Bill 3297, removed one offence from 1 January 2025:
driving a vehicle "in a mechanical condition that endangers a person, including the operator or an
occupant, or property" (Transportation Code § 548.604).[19] It left another. Section 547.004 makes
it a misdemeanor to operate or move a vehicle that "is unsafe so as to endanger a person" or isn't
equipped as that chapter requires.[20] On this course's reading, Texas removed a test and one of two
overlapping offences, and kept the offence of driving a car that is unsafe. The New York and
Virginia statutes behind their agencies' words weren't read.

The checks are **Yours** in every state: lessons 7 to 10 gave them. An inspection, where there is one,
is a snapshot, just as lesson 21 said of the MOT.

:::checkpoint Sort the five states: in which could a private car in 2026 go a whole year with no official check of its brakes, and in which could it go a whole year with no official check of anything?

No official brake check: Texas (the safety test ended for non-commercial vehicles on 1 January 2025)
and, on the pages read, California, whose programme tests emissions. Pennsylvania, New York and Virginia inspect
safety every year. No official check of anything: a Texas car outside the emissions counties, and a
California car in its exempt years (eight model years and newer at renewal) or in a year when
renewal doesn't call for a Smog Check, since it's every other year. None of this changes what the
three agencies above say about driving a car with defects, or Texas's statute.
:::

## Does periodic safety inspection reduce crashes?

Everything so far is established: each state's rule says what it says. This question is different.
It's a **contested empirical question**: serious, informed people disagree, and the evidence doesn't
settle it. Behind it sits a value question too, about how much cost and inconvenience a possible
safety gain is worth, and that one is for each state's voters and legislators. This course answers
neither. It gives the evidence it read, then each side in its own words, with who is speaking. Logic
and Argument lesson 7, "Reconstruction and charity: the strongest version first", is the rule for
reading both.

### The evidence GAO read

GAO reviewed 29 studies and judged six reliable enough to use.[1] Its summary: "research remains
inconclusive about the effect of safety inspection programs on crash rates." Of the three US studies,
"none were able to establish a statistically significant effect of safety inspection programs on
crashes involving either fatalities or injuries."[1] Abroad, two Norwegian studies found that inspection
improved the technical condition of cars but not their crash rates, and a New Zealand study found
that going from yearly to six-monthly inspection might cut crashes by about 8 per cent, with a
confidence interval from 0.4 to 15 per cent that GAO says "indicated considerable statistical
uncertainty".[1] GAO also compared crash rates before and after two states, New Jersey and Oklahoma,
dropped inspection, and found the rate "did not significantly change for either state", adding that
"this analysis does not provide sufficient evidence to conclude that inspection programs did not have
an effect on crash rates".[1]

And then the sentence that stops the evidence being read as a verdict: "While our literature review
did not yield any studies establishing that vehicle safety inspections reduce crashes, this does not
necessarily demonstrate that inspections do not have such an effect."[1]

Why is it so hard to tell? GAO gives the reasons. Vehicle component failure, on estimates from NHTSA
data, is "a factor in about 2 to 7 percent of crashes", so any effect is a small signal in a big number.
There is "no comprehensive database for all police reported crashes in the United States"; the
national sample "is not set up to be representative at the state level"; and, as some literature
and safety advocate organisations told GAO, police officers "often do not have the time and
resources to conduct a thorough vehicle check".[1] In the police-reported sample, GAO found
component failure noted in around 2 per cent of crashes from 2009 to 2013. When NHTSA's
investigators examined cars thoroughly, in a 2005 to 2007 survey of over 5,000 crashes, component
failures "were found to be present in 6.8% of crashes", present rather than proved to be the cause.
GAO reads that as a sign the police reports undercount.[1]

GAO also reports what running a programme is hard about. Eleven of the 15 inspecting states named
oversight, including making sure stations "do not intentionally pass vehicles that should have
failed", and one 2008 state study found inspections averaging 5 minutes against the 15 to 30 a
thorough one should take. Some states have responded, GAO says, by "implementing more stringent
program rules", and one now makes mechanics use fingerprint scanners before some inspections.[1]

:::predict Before you read the two cases: what do you think the case for inspection leans on hardest, and what does the case against lean on hardest? Write one line for each.

On the words quoted below, the case for leans on what inspections find: large numbers of cars failing
and being repaired, which officials see as defects that would otherwise stay on the road. The case
against leans on cost and on the missing crash evidence: time and money for every owner, for a
benefit the studies GAO reviewed could not establish.
:::

### The case for keeping inspection, in its holders' own words

**PennDOT**, which runs Pennsylvania's programme, states the purpose on its own page: "Pennsylvania
requires vehicle safety inspections to ensure that vehicles are maintained for safe operation. Safety
Inspections can prevent vehicle failure on the highways and crashes that may result in injuries or
death."[2] **New York's DMV** puts it as a minimum standard: its programme "helps make sure every vehicle
registered in this state meets the minimum standards for safe operation on public streets and
highways."[4]

The fullest statement of the case is the officials GAO interviewed in 15 inspecting states, as GAO
reports them. They "said their programs help identify vehicles with safety problems and remove these
unsafe vehicles from the roadways or compel owners to make repairs that otherwise might not be
performed." Their evidence is what inspections find. "Pennsylvania officials provided 2014 data showing
that more than 529,000 vehicles (about 20 percent of the state’s 2.7-million registered vehicles)
underwent repairs in order to pass inspection after initially failing." Virginia's officials "believed
that their state’s roadways were safer" because their programme found safety problems in over 1.4
million of the state's 7.5 million vehicles in 2014, which GAO puts at 19 per cent, and 700,000 of
them were rejected for brakes.[1]
And they argued the need is growing: Rhode Island's officials said road salt corrodes brake lines and
steering, and "vehicles are staying in service longer"; GAO adds DOT figures showing the average
passenger car's age rising from 8.4 years in 1995 to 11.4 in 2013.[1] Behind them stands the federal
guideline, which, as GAO reported it, recommends periodic inspection "to reduce the number of vehicles with existing
or potential conditions that may contribute to crashes".[1]

Who is speaking matters. These are the agencies that run the programmes.
In Texas, the groups on record against House Bill 3297 included an inspection company, an inspection
station and the Texas State Inspection Association, whose business is inspection, and also police
chiefs' and sheriffs' associations, a police officers' union, and carmakers' and dealers'
representatives, as the House Research Organization's digest lists them.[13] The digest lists names, not arguments;
this course read no statement of their reasons.

### The case for ending it, in its holders' own words

The **Texas House committee's [analysis](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/88R/analysis/html/HB03297H.htm)** of House Bill 3297 gives the reason for the bill in one line: "The
impact of the mandatory vehicle safety inspection program on road safety is not strong enough to
justify the program's existence."[14] The **author's and sponsor's statement of intent**, as the Texas
[Senate Research Center](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/88R/analysis/html/HB03297E.htm) recorded it, makes the fuller case: "Vehicle inspections are costly,
time-consuming, and often provide little benefit to public safety. The process requires Texans to take
time off work or out of their busy schedules to have their vehicles inspected at a designated location.
This can be especially difficult for those living in rural areas, where inspection stations may be few
and far between." It argues the test checks the wrong things: "Many of the items checked during an
inspection are not related to safety. For example, inspectors often check the tint on windows and the
condition of license plates, which have no bearing on whether a vehicle is safe to drive." It adds that
they "do little to improve road safety and serve only to generate revenue for the state". And it
argues the car now checks itself: "Modern vehicles are equipped with onboard diagnostic systems that
constantly monitor their performance and can detect any potential issues."[15]

GAO reports the same reasons from states that ended programmes earlier. "In 2001, an Oklahoma Senate
Press Release stated there was no evidence that vehicle safety inspection programs resulted in
decreased highway accidents or injuries statewide and that eliminating the program would save
Oklahomans $12 million." In 2010 New Jersey's Motor Vehicle Commission chief "announced that with a
lack of conclusive data on program effectiveness and with the current (2010) fiscal crisis, New Jersey
could not justify the program’s expense".[1]

Who is speaking, once. These are legislators and the states that chose to repeal. In Texas, those on
record in favour of the bill included the Texas Public Policy Foundation and the Texas Conservative
Coalition Research Institute, two policy research organisations; the True Texas Project, a political
group that began as a tea party; a county tax assessor-collector; an electric-car maker; and a
recreational-vehicle trade association, among others,
as the digest lists them.[13] Again, names only.

### Weighing one claim from each side

Take one claim from each case and ask the same three questions: what kind of claim is it, what does it
rest on, and what would test it?

Start with Pennsylvania's figure: more than 529,000 cars failed and were repaired in a year. It's a
count of defects found and fixed, and it rests on the state's own inspection records. It shows the
inspection does something to cars. It doesn't, by itself, show fewer crashes, because it can't tell
which of those defects would have caused one, or whether owners would have fixed them anyway. What would
test it is whether states like Pennsylvania have fewer component-failure crashes than comparable states
without inspection, other differences controlled for, and that is exactly the evidence GAO found missing.

:::checkpoint Your turn, with the other side. The Texas statement says modern cars' onboard diagnostic systems "constantly monitor their performance and can detect any potential issues." What kind of claim is it, what does it rest on, and what would test it?

A claim about what the car can see for itself, and it rests on what a modern car's own systems
watch. Read at its strongest, "onboard diagnostic systems" means more than the emissions computer,
and this course has shown several that watch safety items: the tyre pressure warning required on US
cars from model year 2008 (lesson 7), the brake, ABS and airbag lamps (lesson 11), and Virginia's
inspection acting on the airbag readiness light, above. The limit is in the words "any potential
issues". EPA describes the regulated OBD system as one that "continuously monitors the emission
control systems",[11] and GAO found the commonest component failures noted in crashes were tyres,
brakes and steering.[1] A pressure warning isn't a tread gauge, and on the sources this course read,
no dashboard lamp reports tread depth or a cracked windscreen. What would test the claim is a count
of the defects found at inspection that the car had already flagged on the dashboard.
Pennsylvania's figure needed crash evidence GAO found missing; this claim needs defect evidence nobody
quoted here has. Neither tells you which way your state should go. Each tells you what evidence to
ask for.
:::

### Where they meet, and what would settle it

On this course's reading, neither side disputes that a car with failed brakes or bald tyres is
dangerous, and neither, in the words quoted, cites a US study showing that inspection cuts crashes.
They disagree about what that absence means. GAO reads it partly as a problem of data; the case for
rests instead on what inspections find, and the case against reads it as a reason not to make every
owner pay. And they weigh the cost differently, which is the value half of the question.

Each side has a stake. The agencies that run inspections, and the businesses paid to do them, gain
from keeping them; the owners who pay for inspections gain from ending them (an Oklahoma Senate press
release put the saving there at $12 million), and the legislators who proposed repeal answer to those
owners as voters. An interest is a reason to check the evidence
harder, not a reason to call the claim false, and that applies to both sides equally.

Between the two, GAO reports states that kept their programmes by narrowing them: officials in two
"either relaxed or eliminated some non-safety related standards" or "exempted newer-model vehicles
from safety inspections as a compromise with state legislatures to continue their programs".[1]

What would settle it, from GAO's own account of the gaps: crash data that records component failure
reliably, from thorough vehicle examinations rather than roadside police reports, and that is
representative state by state, so that states with and without inspection, or one state before and
after a change, can be compared with other causes of crashes controlled for. And for the cost half, what a programme
costs to run, which officials from 8 of the 15 inspecting states told GAO they don't track.[1] On this course's reading,
Texas's change in 2025 is the kind of before-and-after a future study could use, if the data exists.
This course read no study of it. The course stops there.

## What people get wrong

**"Every US state inspects cars."** GAO counted 16 with periodic safety inspection in July 2015, and
Texas has stopped since ("No national test", above).

**"The US minimum tread is 2/32 inch."** It's 2/32 inch at inspection in the three states read, each
measured its own way, and the federal 2/32 standard for cars is for states that inspect, not a rule
on drivers ("Tread law, gathered", above).

**"The emissions test and the safety inspection are the same thing."** One comes from the Clean Air
Act and checks emissions; the other is a state safety programme. New York does both in one visit; a
Texas emissions county now does only the first.

**"GAO proved inspections don't work."** It found the research "inconclusive" and said the lack of
studies "does not necessarily demonstrate that inspections do not have such an effect."

**"A fifth of cars fail inspection, so inspection prevents crashes."** A failure count shows defects
found, not crashes prevented. The three US studies GAO read "did not find statistically significant
differences in crash rates in states with inspection programs compared to those without."

**"No inspection means nobody cares what state the car is in."** Not in Texas, where DPS says driving
without working safety features "is against the law" and the statute still makes driving an unsafe
car an offence ("Between inspections", above).

**"A rejection sticker gives me fifteen days to drive it."** Not in Virginia: it's fifteen days to
fix and reinspect (the Blacksburg case, above).
