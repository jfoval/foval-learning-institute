## What the tread is for

NHTSA, the US federal road safety regulator, says "Tire tread provides the gripping action and
traction that prevents your car or truck from slipping and sliding, especially when the road is icy
or wet."[13] [TyreSafe](https://www.tyresafe.org/the-checks/tread-depth/), a UK tyre safety charity
backed by tyre makers, carmakers and retailers, gives the account in terms of water: "The deeper the
tyre tread, the more water it can clear from the road surface and the better its grip. As the tread
wears down or water depth increases, grip is reduced."[15] When the water can't get
out of the way, TyreSafe says, the tyre aquaplanes (hydroplanes, in the US): "Aquaplaning occurs when
the water cannot be removed from between the tyre and the road."[15]

That's as far as the sources read here go. They describe what tread does and measure what happens as
it wears; none of them gives the physics of how a groove clears water, and this course won't supply
it.

How much tread there is to start with depends on the tyre. TyreSafe says "New tyres generally have a
tread depth of between 7-9 mm", varying with the brand, model and season.[15] The AA, a motoring organisation
that sells breakdown cover and repairs, says new tyres start "with about 8 mm".[17] Everything below is about what happens to that 7 to 9 mm on the way down.

## The tread rule in Great Britain

This is general information, not legal advice.

The rule for cars is regulation 27(4)(f) of the [Road Vehicles (Construction and Use) Regulations
1986](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1986/1078/regulation/27), as amended. Read it in full, because every word of it does work:

> the grooves of the tread pattern of every tyre fitted to the wheels of a vehicle mentioned in
> sub-paragraph (e) shall be of a depth of at least 1.6 mm throughout a continuous band comprising the
> central three-quarters of the breadth of tread and round the entire outer circumference of the
> tyre.[1]

The vehicles in sub-paragraph (e) include "passenger vehicles other than motor cycles constructed or
adapted to carry no more than 8 seated passengers in addition to the driver", which is a car.[1]
Take the words one at a time.

- **"the grooves of the tread pattern"**. Regulation 27(6) says the tread pattern excludes "(i) tie
  bars or tread wear indicators; (ii) features which are designed to wear out substantially before
  the rest of the pattern under normal conditions of use; and (iii) other minor features".[1] DVSA's
  MOT manual, the rulebook for Great Britain's periodic test, puts it simply: grooves with wear
  indicators in them, or cut as deep, "are considered to be primary grooves", and shallower grooves
  "are not to be considered when assessing tread depth".[2]
- **"a continuous band comprising the central three-quarters of the breadth of tread"**. The
  breadth of tread, defined in regulation 27(6), is "the breadth of that part of the tyre which can
  contact the road under normal conditions of use measured at 90 degrees to the peripheral line of
  the tread".[1] So the band is the middle three-quarters of the part that touches the road. The MOT
  manual spells out what that leaves: "Either side of the central three-quarters of the tyre can be
  devoid of tread ('bald')."[2]
- **"round the entire outer circumference"**. All the way round. One good point doesn't carry a
  worn one.

The Highway Code's Annex 6 gives the same rule as a MUST: "Cars, light vans and light trailers MUST
have a tread depth of at least 1.6 mm across the central three-quarters of the breadth of the tread
and around the entire circumference."[3] Bigger vehicles and motorcycles have a 1 mm rule in the same
regulation; this lesson is about cars.

### What follows from breaking it

Using a car whose tyres break a construction and use requirement is an offence under section 41A of
the Road Traffic Act 1988.[4] The Sentencing Council's guideline for England and Wales, "Tyres
defective", gives a Level 4 maximum fine for vehicles up to 3.5 tonnes, £2,500 on its own table, and "Must endorse and
impose 3 points", which means the offence and the points go on the driver's record.[5] DVLA's code
for it is CU30, "Using a vehicle with defective tyre(s)".[6] Then the line the advice pages read
here state differently:

> Fine is for each single tyre (but ordinarily only one set of points apply where several offences
> committed on the same occasion).[5]

So the fine can multiply by the tyre, and the points ordinarily don't. TyreSafe's tread page says
"three penalty points PER tyre"; the AA, which sells breakdown cover and repairs, says four
dangerous tyres could mean "a £10,000 fine and 12 points"; and the RAC, which sells breakdown cover
and tyres, lists "Three penalty points per illegal tyre". The Sentencing Council, which issues the
sentencing guidelines for England and Wales, says that ordinarily only one set of points
applies.[5][15][17][18] Scotland's penalties weren't read by this course. At the MOT,
"Tyre tread depth not in accordance with the requirements" is a Dangerous defect.[2]

One more piece of law makes the checks you'll do below count. Section 48 of the Road Traffic
Offenders Act 1988 says the court must not endorse or disqualify for a section 41A offence if the
driver proves "that he did not know, and had no reasonable cause to suspect, that the facts of the
case were such that the offence would be committed".[7] The fine still applies. On this course's
reading, the defence is narrow for a driver: a worn or damaged tyre that could be seen gave cause to
suspect, whether or not anyone looked. The checks below are how you find it first. Lesson 20, "The
law on a car's condition: Great Britain and Northern Ireland", has the rest. None of this section reaches Northern Ireland: Northern Ireland's own law on a car's condition
wasn't read here, so a reader there checks nidirect instead.

### A tyre in Hawes

A driver in Hawes checks her tyres with a tread depth gauge at three points round each one. On one
front tyre the grooves across the middle read 2.4 mm at every point, and the outer shoulder (the
edge of the tread, where it turns down to the sidewall) is worn smooth all the way round.

The law first. Every primary groove in the central band is above 1.6 mm, all the way round, and the
MOT manual says the edges outside that band can be bald. On the depth rule, as general information,
the tyre meets regulation 27(4)(f).

Then the advice, beside it. The AA, which sells breakdown cover and repairs, says "Aim to replace
tyres before the tread wears below 2 mm", and the same AA page says "You should consider replacing
them at 3 mm". TyreSafe, the industry-backed charity, says "Tyre and safety experts recommend
replacing tyres at 3mm".[15][17] So this lawful tyre sits under the 3 mm point both give, and
above the 2 mm point they also both give. Who says what, and why, including a tyre maker's case against
replacing by depth alone, is the last main section of this lesson.

And one more reading. Wear on one edge, on the account of AAA (which runs a repair network) in
lesson 7, "Tyres I: pressure, the placard and TPMS", typically means wheels out of alignment.[20] Measuring the depth is **Yours**.
The alignment is **Call someone**.

## Tread rules in the US

This is general information, not legal advice.

The federal tread figures, 4/32 of an inch on the front tyres of a bus, truck or truck tractor and
2/32 elsewhere, are in 49 CFR 393.75, which is part of the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations
and covers commercial motor vehicles.[11] This course found no federal tread minimum for private
cars. Federal tread rules apply to commercial vehicles; for private cars, tread law is set by states.
The three states read here all use 2/32 of an inch, which is about 1.6 mm (this course's arithmetic:
2/32 × 25.4 = 1.59). The number nearly matches Great Britain's, and the way it's measured doesn't.

| | The rule | How it's measured |
|---|---|---|
| **Great Britain** | 1.6 mm (reg 27(4)(f)) | A continuous band across the central three-quarters, all the way round[1] |
| **Pennsylvania** (inspection) | Reject if "A tire has two adjacent treads with less than 2/32-inch tread remaining at any point"[8] | Any one point; also reject if the wear indicators "contact the road in any two adjacent grooves"[8] |
| **New York** (inspection) | "must be at least 2/32 inch"[9] | "when measured in two adjacent major tread grooves showing the most wear"[9] |
| **Virginia** (inspection) | Reject "a tread depth measuring less than 2/32 of an inch"[10] | Two adjacent grooves "where tread is thinnest"; if both are under, measure at two more points round the tyre; reject only if under at all three. "Do not take measurements from the tread wear indicators."[10] |

(The rest of Pennsylvania's sentence sets 4/32 inch for the front tyres of vehicles over 10,000
pounds, which aren't cars.) Each of these is a rule for the state's inspection. What any of them
requires of a driver between inspections is a question this course didn't read, and lesson 22, "The
US: state inspections, emissions tests and tread law", keeps to what was read. Many states don't
inspect at all.

Beside the law sits the regulator's advice. NHTSA: "Tires are not safe and should be replaced when
the tread is worn down to 2/32 of an inch."[13] That's advice from the federal regulator, for any car in the US, and it isn't law.

### A tyre in Lynchburg, Virginia

A car goes in for its annual inspection in Lynchburg, Virginia. On the front left tyre the inspector
finds where the tread is thinnest and measures two adjacent grooves there: 1/32 inch and 1/32
inch. Both are under 2/32. So, following Virginia's manual, the inspector measures again "at two
additional equally spaced intervals around the circumference". At the second point the two grooves
read 3/32 and 3/32; at the third, 3/32 and 4/32.

Virginia's rule rejects the tyre only "If the tread depth is less than 2/32 of an inch in two
adjacent tread grooves at each of the equally spaced intervals".[10] It isn't at the second or third
point, so on depth, as general information, the tyre isn't rejected. The same tyre in Pennsylvania
would be rejected, because there one point with two adjacent grooves under 2/32 is enough.[8] And in
Great Britain, a spot at 1/32 inch (about 0.8 mm) in the central band breaks the "round the entire
outer circumference" requirement.[1] One tyre, three verdicts, on this course's reading of each rule.
NHTSA's advice, in the US, is that tyres "are not safe and should be replaced" at 2/32 inch, and
this one is past that at its thinnest spot.[13]

Now the gap. The rear right tyre on the same car: at its thinnest spot, two adjacent grooves read
1/32 inch and 3/32 inch, and nowhere on the tyre are two neighbouring grooves both under 2/32.

No further measurement, and not rejected on depth. Virginia's note: "If either of the grooves
measure 2/32 of an inch or more, no further measurements are necessary and tread depth is
satisfactory."[10] One of the pair reads 3/32, so the rule stops there. If you went on to the two
extra points, look again at the word "either". In Pennsylvania the answer is the same for a
different reason: its rule needs two adjacent grooves both under 2/32 at a point, and this tyre has
none. Passing Virginia's depth check says only that. A groove at 1/32 inch is under every figure in
this lesson, law or advice, and below where any position in the last section begins: Virginia's own
2/32 is the line it's measured against, and NHTSA's advice is that a tyre worn to 2/32 is "not
safe".

## Measuring: the gauge, the bars and the coins

**A tread depth gauge** measures the groove itself. The AA, which sells breakdown cover and
repairs, calls "a calibrated tyre gauge" the best
way: "To use, place the gauge into the tyre's groove."[17] Measure in the main grooves, the ones with
wear indicators in them, and not on the indicator bars themselves. That's Virginia's instruction to
its inspectors, and on this course's reading it follows from the GB regulation's exclusion of the
bars too.[1][10] Measure at several
points: TyreSafe, the industry-backed charity, says "check at least three locations around each
tyre", and AAA, which runs a repair network, says to measure in
"three locations across the tire's tread: (1)outer edge, (2) center, and (3) inside edge".[15][20]
Together that's three points round the tyre and three across it.

**The wear indicators** are raised bars across the bottom of the main grooves. In the US, FMVSS 139
requires new car tyres to have at least six, spaced round the tyre, that let you see "whether the tire
has worn to a tread depth of one sixteenth of an inch", which is 2/32.[12] The AA describes them as
"usually small bars, about 5 mm wide", and says "If the tyre tread is level with the top of the wear
indicator, it means your tyres have reached the legal limit of 1.6 mm."[17] The photograph below
shows where they are: look for the letters TWI near the edge of the tread.

:::figure https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/66/Autoreifen_mit_Verschlei%C3%9Fanzeige.JPG | A close-up of a car tyre's tread pattern, with the letters TWI moulded near the shoulder at the bottom of the picture

**The coins** are quick and rough, and each one measures one depth only.

| In the UK | In the US |
|---|---|
| **The 20p.** TyreSafe: "If the outer band of the 20p coin is obscured when it is inserted, then your tread is above the legal limit. If the outer band of the coin is visible, then your tyres may be illegal and unsafe and should be checked immediately by a qualified tyre professional."[15] | **The penny.** NHTSA: "placing a penny in the tread with Lincoln's head upside down and facing you. If you can see the top of Lincoln's head, replace your tires."[13] AAA: a visible head means "less than 2/32" of tread.[20] |
| The AA gives the width: the 20p's outer rim "is just under 3 mm wide", so a hidden rim means "comfortably above the legal minimum".[17] | **The quarter.** AAA: put a quarter in a groove with Washington's head down; "If the top of his head is not visible, your tires have at least 4/32” of tread and are fine for continued use."[20] |

:::predict The 20p's outer band shows at one point on a tyre in Britain. Is that tyre under the legal limit at that point?

Not necessarily. On the AA's figure the band is just under 3 mm wide, so a visible band means the
tread there is under about 3 mm, not under 1.6 mm. It's why TyreSafe says "may be illegal", and why
the AA says to check "more carefully using a proper tyre tread depth gauge". The coin's line sits
at nearly twice the legal depth, so it can warn early; it can't pass or fail a tyre.
:::

The 20p's band width rests on the AA alone; this course read no specification for the coin. Some
pages put the test more loosely: the RAC, which sells breakdown cover and tyres, says "If you can't see the outer band on the coin, your tyres
are above the legal limit", which says nothing about the other points round the tyre.[18] The penny
and the quarter are the US versions: the penny sits at the 2/32 figure, the quarter at AAA's 4/32.

Checking the tread is **Yours**. NHTSA says to do it "at least once a month when you're checking their
pressure", TyreSafe "at least once a month and before any long journeys", and the RAC suggests the
coin test "around every two weeks and before long journeys".[13][15][18]

## Damage that counts at any depth

This is general information, not legal advice.

A tyre can have plenty of tread and still be barred. In Great Britain, regulation 27(1) says a tyre
shall not be used on a road if it has any of these, among others:[1]

- "(c) the tyre has a cut in excess of 25 mm or 10% of the section width of the tyre, whichever is the
  greater, measured in any direction on the outside of the tyre and deep enough to reach the ply or
  cord;"
- "(d) the tyre has any lump, bulge or tear caused by separation or partial failure of its structure;"
- "(e) the tyre has any of the ply or cord exposed;"

A catch-all, 27(1)(h), covers a tyre "not maintained in such condition as to be fit for the use" or
with "a defect which might in any way cause damage to the surface of the road or damage to persons on
or in the vehicle or to other persons using the road".[1] At the MOT, a qualifying cut is a Major
defect, and a lump, bulge or tear, or exposed cords, is Dangerous.[2]

The states read here have their own lists. Pennsylvania rejects a tyre if "A part of ply or cord
is exposed" or there is "a bump, bulge or separation".[8] New York checks for "any fabric break or cut over the
length or width of 1 inch, visible bumps, bulges or knots".[9] Virginia rejects "Any tire has a cut
or puncture into the fabric", and allows plugs or patches "in the tread area only".[10] One inch is
25.4 mm, so New York's cut figure and Great Britain's 25 mm nearly match, on this course's arithmetic;
Great Britain also has the 10 per cent alternative and requires the cut to reach the ply or cord.

The advice read here points the same way. TyreSafe, the industry-backed charity: "If the tyre has lumps or bulges it must be examined by
a tyre specialist since these could indicate internal damage."[15] NHTSA says to stop using a tyre
for "signs of physical damage (cuts, cracks, bulges, etc.)".[13] Spotting damage is **Yours**. What
to do with a damaged tyre, and whether to drive on it at all, is lesson 18's, "A flat tyre: the
decision, and spare, space saver, sealant or run-flat"; any repair is **Call someone**.

:::checkpoint Sort these four tyres, as general information, against Great Britain's regulation 27. (a) 5 mm of tread and a cut 15 mm long that shows cord. (b) 2 mm across the central band all round, and a small scuff on the sidewall with no bulge or cord showing. (c) 6 mm of tread and a bulge on the sidewall. (d) 1.7 mm across the central band all round, and a cut 30 mm long, in a tyre whose section width is 205 mm, that doesn't reach the cord.

(a) Barred: 27(1)(e), any ply or cord exposed, whatever the cut's length. (b) Not barred by the rules
quoted here: the depth is above 1.6 mm all round and there's no cut to the ply, bulge or exposed
cord, though it's at the AA's 2 mm point. (c) Barred: 27(1)(d), a bulge. (d) Not barred
by 27(1)(c), because the cut must reach the ply or cord as well as exceed the length (25 mm, or 10 per
cent of 205 mm, which is 20.5 mm, whichever is greater, so 25 mm here; this course's arithmetic).
Depth meets 27(4)(f). If you barred (d) on length alone, reread the words "and deep enough to reach
the ply or cord".

## Age

This is general information, not legal advice.

:::predict A ten-year-old tyre with plenty of tread is on a family car in Great Britain. Does the law there set an age limit that it breaks? Decide before you read on.

Not on what this course read. Regulation 27 has a ten-year rule, but regulation 27(1A) limits it to
certain tyres on buses, minibuses and heavy goods vehicles (front axles, and a minibus's single
wheels). What exists for cars is advice, from NHTSA and the
RAC, and it's set out below.
:::

Tyres age whether or not they're used. NHTSA: "Tire aging occurs when the rubber and other components
in a tire change over time due to service, storage, and environmental conditions." Most drivers wear
the tread out first, it says, and the ones at risk from age are low-mileage cars and vehicles that
stand: "exposure to sunlight and warmer climate, poor storage and poor maintenance also contribute to
tire aging." And: "You cannot detect tire aging simply by looking at your tires."[13]

That ageing happens is common ground. How fast it matters is not, and the sources give ranges, not a
rule:

- NHTSA: "Some vehicle and tire manufacturers recommend replacing tires that are six to 10 years old,
  regardless of treadwear."[13] Its winter page: "Some vehicle manufacturers recommend replacing tires
  every six years regardless of use."[14]
- The RAC, a UK motoring organisation that sells breakdown cover and tyres: "Even if you drive limited
  miles each year, tyres should be replaced every five or six years."[18]

None of that is law. Great Britain's ten-year rule doesn't reach cars, as the predict above
showed, and the three states' tyre rules read here set no age limit either.[1][8][9][10] DVSA's manual does say "Tyres with a three-digit code will be more than 10
years old", which is useful for reading the date on any tyre.[2] Lesson 7 showed how to read the
four-digit code. Your handbook, and the tyre maker, are where a figure for your car and tyre comes
from; the ranges above are other people's.

NHTSA also warns about the spare: "Spare tires on all vehicles are prone to aging because they seldom
get replaced", and says a full-size spare should not be "used as a replacement for worn tires, except
in emergencies such as replacing a flat tire".[13]

## When to replace: the argument above the floor

Everyone read here agrees that the legal floor is where the law of each place puts it. And every
party that argues about replacing above it agrees that wet braking gets worse as a tyre wears: even
the tyre maker arguing for full use says so. Where they
part is where, above the floor, a tyre should come off, and that turns on facts that are disputed and
on a value question, how much safety margin is worth how much money and waste. This course takes no
side. The positions follow, each in its holder's words, with what each sells. Logic and Argument
lesson 7, "Reconstruction and charity: the strongest version first", is the rule for reading them,
and Personal Safety lesson 5, "Home, the door and the doorstep", has the same kind of argument about
burglar alarms, with its point that who paid for a study doesn't tell you its result is wrong; the
design does.

The chart puts the positions on one scale. Read it from the bottom: the two legal floors are almost
the same depth, and everything above them is somebody's advice. From the top, a new tyre has 7 to 9
mm to lose, and the argument is about where in the last 0.4 to 1.6 mm above the floor, on this
course's arithmetic, it should come off. The AA and TyreSafe each give two points, 2 mm and 3 mm,
in different words, and the quotations below keep both. NHTSA's advice sits at the US floor. One
position can't go on this scale: Michelin's, that the tyre's design matters more than its depth
alone.

### Replace earlier: 3 mm, or 4/32 inch

**TyreSafe**, the UK tyre safety charity backed by tyre makers, carmakers and retailers, says "Tyre
and safety experts recommend replacing tyres at 3mm", and on the same page: "You should have your tyre
replaced once it reaches 2mm and the minimum tread depth commonly advised by tyre and safety experts
is 3mm." Its reason is wet grip: "in general wet grip decreases as the tyre tread depth approaches the
legal minimum", and "motorists may wish to consider replacing tyres before reaching the tyre tread
limit."[15] It cites a test in which cars on 1.6 mm tread had wet braking distances up to 44 per
cent longer than on 3 mm. This course has not read that test's report, so the figure is TyreSafe's.

**The RAC**, a motoring organisation that sells breakdown cover and, on the same page, tyres ("RAC Tyres
where we sell everything from budget to premium brands"), says: "Tyre and safety experts believe the
1.6mm legal minimum is insufficient to guarantee safety". It goes on to say most recommend 3 mm, and
gives the same comparison: "The difference in wet braking distance between a tyre worn to 3mm and one
worn to 1.6mm can be as much as 44%."[18]

**The AA**, a motoring organisation that sells breakdown cover and repairs, says "You should consider
replacing them at 3 mm" and "Aim to replace tyres before the tread wears below 2 mm".[17]

**AAA**, the US motoring clubs that sell roadside assistance and run an approved repair network, ran
its own test with the Automobile Club of Southern California. Against new tyres, tyres worn to 4/32
inch showed "An average increased stopping distance of 87 feet for a passenger car and 86 feet for a
light truck", on wet pavement at highway speed, and it found that "worn tire performance deteriorated
significantly for all tires tested, including those at a higher price point." Its position:
"AAA maintains that tires should be replaced once the tread depth reaches 4/32”, when stopping
distances have already begun to deteriorate significantly." It also makes a claim about the other
side. Of the 2/32 guidance in industry guidelines and state rules, it says: "Not only does this
recommendation jeopardize a driver's safety, it minimizes manufacturer warranty costs and is often
paired with environmental concerns."[19] That is AAA's claim about who the guidance suits; it doesn't
say anyone set the figure for that reason. This course read no reply to it from a tyre maker, and
doesn't adopt it.

### Use the tyre fully: design over depth

**Michelin**, which makes and sells tyres, argues on its supporter page on TyreSafe's own site that
depth alone is the wrong test. Its words, including the part that concedes ground:

> The fact that wet braking performance, which is key to safety, decreases as tyres are used
> highlights the importance of evaluating worn tyres. Contrary to popular belief, tread depth does
> not guarantee safe tyres. The real difference lies in how well tyres are designed.[16]

Its reason for running tyres longer is cost and waste: its approach is "good for the environment and
consumers' budgets", because "having tyres that are still safe when worn means changing our tyres
less often. That's better for the planet since it saves resources and reduces CO2 emissions."[16] A
reader who holds this view would put it this way: every tyre taken off at 3 mm throws away tread that
was still lawful, and what makes a worn tyre safe or not is how it was designed, so judge the tyre and
not the depth alone. That summary is this course's; the words it rests on are Michelin's.

### At the floor: the US regulator

One voice in this argument sells nothing. NHTSA, the US federal regulator, puts its advice at the
2/32-inch mark: "Tires are not safe and should be replaced when the tread is worn down to 2/32 of an
inch."[13] That's advice, not law, and it sits at the same depth as the three states' inspection
rules. This course found no UK official body that gives a replacement point above Great
Britain's legal floor. The Highway Code's tread line is the law itself.

### The driver paying for them

Whoever is right, the driver pays for the choice, and TyreSafe (the industry-backed charity) and
the AA (which sells breakdown cover and repairs), both of which urge replacing before the floor,
give rough figures for what the last millimetres are worth. TyreSafe: "Generally, tyres with 3mm of tread depth may last
between 10,000 and 20,000 miles", with the caveat that "Actual lifespan can vary widely", and that
tyres at 2 mm "may last ~1000 miles".[15] The AA: "You can expect around 1,000 miles out of your
tyres if they're down to 2 mm of tread."[17] TyreSafe adds that "Manufacturors do not provide mileage
predictions for tread depth for this reason" (the spelling is TyreSafe's). Those are TyreSafe's and
the AA's estimates, not measurements, and this course has none of its own. No source read puts a
price on those miles, and Michelin's case on budgets and waste, above, gives no figure either.

### What the evidence read here can and can't settle

Look at what each test compared. AAA's (the clubs sell roadside assistance and run a repair
network), as its release describes it, compared new tyres with tyres at 4/32 inch, not 4/32 with
2/32.[19] The release also says "tire performance does vary by brand", with no figures by brand, and
its finding on tyres "at a higher price point" compares each tyre with itself new, so it doesn't
test Michelin's claim about design either way.[19] AAA's tyre-care page does report a comparison of
the disputed depths, from Tire Rack research this course didn't read: a pickup truck at 70 mph on
wet pavement stopping 122 feet shorter, a 24 per cent difference, on tyres that pass the quarter
test (4/32 inch) than on tyres that pass only the penny test (2/32).[20] TyreSafe (the
industry-backed charity) and the RAC (which sells tyres) cite a comparison of 3 mm with 1.6 mm, from
a report this course didn't read. Michelin, which makes and sells tyres, gives no test comparing
depths; its page does claim that one of its own tyres, after over 18,000 miles of use, brakes "below
the official level for a new tyre", with no test cited.[16] So what was read here is one release
comparing new tyres with worn ones, two comparisons of the disputed depths known only through
parties who cite them, and one maker's claim for its own tyre. None answers, from a report this
course read, the question in dispute, which is how much stopping distance, on which tyres, is bought by
replacing at 3 mm or 4/32 rather than at the legal floor. What would settle it is independent
wet-braking tests across tread depths and across tyre designs, published in full. This course read
none. Until then, each position is a reading of partial evidence plus a judgement about cost, and the
law in each place is the only fixed line.

## What people get wrong

**"1.6 mm anywhere on the tyre is legal."** In Great Britain it's a continuous band across the central
three-quarters, all the way round (the tread rule section above).

**"If the 20p band is hidden, the tyre's legal; if it shows, it's illegal."** On the AA's figure the band
is just under 3 mm. It says a spot has more or less than about 3 mm, and nothing about the rest.

**"Three points for every bald tyre."** The Sentencing Council: the fine is per tyre, but "ordinarily
only one set of points" for offences on the same occasion. TyreSafe, the AA and the RAC say otherwise
(the tread rule section above).

**"The US minimum is 2/32 inch."** It's 2/32 inch in the three states read, each measured its own
way; the federal figures are for commercial vehicles, and many states don't inspect at all (tread
rules in the US, above).

**"Measure on the wear bar."** Virginia's manual tells inspectors not to, and the GB regulation leaves
the bars out of the tread pattern. Measure in the groove (measuring, above).

**"Car tyres have a legal age limit in Great Britain."** Not on what this course read: the ten-year
rule is for buses, minibuses and heavy goods vehicles. Age advice exists, from NHTSA and the RAC, and
it's advice (age, above).

## Practice

:::exercise Three points, three rules, four opinions
This is general information, not legal advice. Take 20 minutes over these. You need a tread depth gauge (or, if you have none, a 20p or a penny and a quarter), the handbook, and a pen, for a car that is yours or whose keeper agrees. Park off the road on level ground, engine or motor off, parking brake on, key or fob away from the car. You don't lift the car or reach under it; measure only the tread you can reach from the side of the car. Don't pull out anything stuck in the tread: note it instead. None of this is done at a roadside.

1. **Measure and look.** On each tyre, measure the main grooves at three points round the tyre, and at each point across the tread (outer, centre, inner). Record every reading in mm or 32nds, and note any groove where you hit a wear bar instead of the groove floor. Then look along both sidewalls and the tread for cuts, cord showing, lumps, bulges or cracks, note anything stuck in the tread, and record the date code (lesson 7).
2. **Compare with the law where you drive.** For each tyre, write whether its lowest readings meet the rule for your place, as general information: Great Britain's continuous central band, or your state's rule and method if it has one (Pennsylvania, New York and Virginia are above; for another state, find its DMV or state police page and write down what it says and the date you read it, or "no rule found").
3. **Compare with the advice.** Next to each tyre's lowest central reading, write where it sits against each position in the last section: 3 mm (TyreSafe, the industry-backed charity; the RAC, which sells tyres; and the AA's "consider"), 2 mm (the AA, which sells repairs, and TyreSafe), 4/32 inch, about 3.2 mm (AAA, which runs a repair network), 2/32 inch (NHTSA, the regulator), and the legal floor with Michelin's case that the tyre's design matters more than depth alone (Michelin makes and sells tyres). Then write down which, if any, you'll follow, and why, in one line.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your record against these points.

Your three points round the tyre matter as much as the three across it: the GB rule is "round the
entire outer circumference", and Virginia's method depends on measuring the thinnest spot first. A
reading taken on a wear bar isn't a groove reading. If one edge of a tyre read much lower than the
other, that's AAA's usual alignment pattern, and **Call someone**; both edges low and the centre fine is
its under-inflation pattern from lesson 7. Anything showing cord, or any lump or bulge, is barred in
Great Britain whatever the depth, and rejected at inspection in the states read. For step 3, there's
no right answer to check against: the question is contested, and the point is that your line names
whose advice you're taking and what it rests on.
:::
