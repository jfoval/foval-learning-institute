In January 1996 a man in the north-eastern United States went out after a blizzard to check that his car
would still start. He made, in the words of the report the CDC published about that storm, "no
attempt to remove any snow surrounding the car before starting the engine."[1] He was found
unconscious in his car about an hour later. He lived, and when the report was written his short-term
memory was still affected.

Summer has its own version: a car standing still, an adult who forgot the child was there or meant to
be back in a minute, and a child inside. NHTSA, the US federal road safety regulator, gives the figure
for 2025: 31 children dead of heatstroke in vehicles.[2]

Neither of those cars was being driven. This lesson is about the two seasons in which a parked car
can kill the people sitting in it, and about the duller work that heads off some seasonal breakdowns:
getting the car ready for the cold and the heat, and clearing it before you drive.

:::callout Before the car lets you down
This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. If your car fails on a motorway or other fast road in Great Britain, leave at the next exit or services if you can; if you can't, go left into an emergency area or onto the hard shoulder, switch on your hazard lights, get everyone out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and don't go back to the car. If you're stuck in a live lane or can't get out safely, stay in with seat belts and hazard lights on and call 999 and ask for the police. In the US, pull as far onto the right shoulder as you can, switch on your hazard lights and stay in with your seat belt on, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind, in which case get out on the side away from traffic; call 911 in an emergency. Never put any part of your body under a car held up only by a jack, and never touch the orange high-voltage cables on a hybrid or electric car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

## Getting the car ready for winter

Four lists cover the same ground: two from the Highway Code, one from NHTSA, and the Met Office's
reproduction of the RAC's. It helps to see them side by side before deciding what's yours to do.

| Source | Whose, and for which car | What it asks |
|---|---|---|
| The Highway Code, Annex 6, "Before winter" | Advice, Great Britain, any car | "Ensure that the battery is well maintained and that there are appropriate anti-freeze agents in your radiator and windscreen bottle."[3] |
| The Met Office, reproducing the RAC's list (the RAC sells breakdown cover) | Advice, UK, any car | "FORCES": fuel, oil, rubber (tyres), coolant, electrics, screenwash[4] |
| NHTSA, "Winter Weather Driving Tips" | Advice, US federal regulator, any car | Tyre pressure, battery, cooling system, floor mats, "winter" washer fluid, wipers, snow tyres, a kit[5] |
| The Highway Code, Rule 228 | Advice, Great Britain | A kit: "de-icer and ice scraper, torch, warm clothing and boots, first aid kit, jump leads and a shovel, together with a warm drink and emergency food"[6] |

Most of what these lists ask for, you've already met as a check of its own. What winter adds is a
reason to do them before the first cold spell rather than after it.

**The battery.** The Met Office page, reproducing the RAC's advice, says "Battery problems are the
reason for more RAC call-outs in winter than anything else."[4] That's the RAC's count of its own
call-outs, and the RAC sells breakdown cover and repairs. NHTSA's version is a single line, "When the
temperature drops, so does battery power", and its advice is to "Have a mechanic check your battery,
charging system, belts, and for any other needed repairs or replacements."[5] Having the battery
tested is **Call someone**: a mechanic, in NHTSA's words. Lesson 5, "The 12 V
battery: what it does, and why short trips flatten it", explained why winter is when a battery fails
and not usually when it was damaged.

:::checkpoint Without looking back at lesson 5: a battery fails on the first frosty morning. What did AAA say about when the damage was done, and what does that mean for when to have a battery tested?

AAA's Mountain West club: "while winter weather is most likely to cause your battery to fail, it's actually hot weather that does most of the damage, which becomes evident when the temperature drops and more power is required to get the car started." So the frost shows up damage done earlier, often in summer heat. That's why the same club says "Get your battery tested annually before winter", and why a test belongs on the autumn list, not the morning it won't start.
:::

**Screenwash and coolant.** Lesson 9, "Under the bonnet: oil, coolant, brake fluid and screenwash",
covered both: the Met Office's screenwash "effective down to at least -15 degrees Celsius", the
handbook's coolant, and never a hot cap. NHTSA says the same for the US in its own words: "Make sure
your vehicle's reservoir is full of high-quality "winter" fluid with de-icer before winter weather
hits."[5] Topping up screenwash is **Yours**; topping up coolant is **Yours, on conditions**, cold and
with the handbook's coolant, as lesson 9 set out.

**Tyre pressure.** NHTSA: "As the outside temperature drops, so does tire inflation pressure."[5]
Lesson 7, "Tyres I: pressure, the placard and TPMS", said that a tyre set right in the autumn can read
low in midwinter without a leak. Winter is when that shows, so a cold check with a gauge against the
placard, which is **Yours**, belongs on the list.

**Wipers and floor mats.** Lesson 10 took the wipers. NHTSA adds one item that isn't on the UK lists:
thicker winter mats, which, NHTSA warns, are dangerous if fitted wrongly: "Improperly installed floor mats in your vehicle could interfere with
the operation of the accelerator or brake pedal, increasing the risk of a crash."[5] Its answer is the
maker's instructions and the retention clips.

**Electric cars.** NHTSA explains that a battery uses its own charge to warm itself in the cold: "The
battery drain due to heating can be minimized by keeping your electric vehicle as warm as possible
during freezing temperatures." And then: "A common way to do this is plugging your vehicle in at night
during the winter".[5] Lesson 4, "Hybrids and electric cars: how they work", gave the cold-weather range
figures.

**Fuel economy in the cold.** Lesson 12, "Fuel economy: what maintenance buys, and the figures that
were withdrawn", promised this. fueleconomy.gov, run by the US Department of Energy and the
Environmental Protection Agency, says that "Fuel economy tests show that, in city driving, a
conventional gasoline car's gas mileage is roughly 15% lower at 20°F than it would be at 77°F. It can
drop as much as 24% for short (3- to 4-mile) trips."[7] That's about −7 °C
against 25 °C, by this course's conversion. Here the source does give a mechanism: "Engine and
transmission friction increases in cold temperatures due to cold engine oil and other drive-line
fluids", and "It takes longer for your engine to reach its most fuel-efficient temperature."[7] Its view
of warming the car up before you set off fits in one line: "idling gets 0 miles per gallon".[7]

### Winter tyres

In the UK, the RAC says it plainly: "Winter tyres aren't mandatory in the UK."[8] It's advice. The Met Office page, reproducing the RAC, recommends "ideally
a minimum tread depth of 3mm in icy or very wet conditions".[4] That's the RAC's recommendation, above the
legal floor lesson 8, "Tyres II: tread, damage, age, and when to replace", set out, and the RAC sells

about the UK; NHTSA presents snow tyres as something to consider, and this course read no state's rule
on them. Whether your car can take snow chains is your handbook's to say, and where they're required is
the road authority's; this course doesn't teach them.

## Winter law: clearing the car in Great Britain

This is general information, not legal advice.

The Highway Code applies to England, Scotland and Wales, and its Introduction sets the convention:
rules with "MUST/MUST NOT" are legal requirements, and it names the law behind each.[9] Everything else
is advice, though the Code "may be used in evidence in any court proceedings under the Traffic Acts",
and that "includes rules which use advisory wording such as 'should/should not' or 'do/do not'."[9] Northern Ireland has its own Highway Code, which
this course did not read.

[Rule 229](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/the-highway-code/driving-in-adverse-weather-conditions-226-to-237), under the heading "Before you set off", says:[6]

- "you MUST be able to see, so clear all snow and ice from all your windows"
- "you MUST ensure that lights are clean and number plates are clearly visible and legible"
- "make sure the mirrors are clear and the windows are demisted thoroughly"
- "remove all snow that might fall off into the path of other road users"
- "check your planned route is clear of delays and that no further snowfalls or severe weather are
  predicted."

The first two are law. The rule cites "CUR reg 30, RVLR reg 23, VERA sect 43 & RV(DRM)R reg 11",[6]
and lesson 10, "Lights, wipers, glass and the walk-round", quoted regulation 30 of the Construction and
Use Regulations: fitted glass is to be kept so that it "does not obscure the vision of the driver".
Notice what the MUST says: all snow and ice, from all your windows. A clear patch in front of your
face does not meet it. The last three lines are advice, including the snow on the roof. This course
read no law on snow left on a roof, and makes no statement about one.

Clearing the car is **Yours**, and it takes time, which is where the second rule comes in. Lesson 12
quoted [Rule 123](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/the-highway-code/general-rules-techniques-and-advice-for-all-drivers-and-riders-103-to-158) in full. Its MUST line: "You MUST NOT leave a parked vehicle unattended with the engine running or leave a vehicle
engine running unnecessarily while that vehicle is stationary on a public road."[10] On this course's
reading, leaving the car on the street with the engine running to defrost while you go back inside is
what the first part of the rule forbids. The rule's words are about a public road; this course read
nothing on a private drive, and does not say what applies there. Annex 6's advice on security points the same way wherever the car
is: when you leave it, "remove the ignition key and engage the steering lock" and "lock the car, even
if you only leave it for a few minutes".[3]

One more thing about running an engine while you clear a car. Never do it inside a garage, even with
the door open. The CDC, the US public health agency, says: "Never run your car or truck inside a
garage that is attached to a house, even with the garage door open."[11] That sentence is about an
attached garage, and the same page tells readers to open a detached garage's door; keeping a running
engine out of every garage is this course's own precaution, stricter than the CDC's. Carbon monoxide in
the house, and a car in an attached garage, are Home Repair and Maintenance lesson 11's, "Carbon
monoxide and the alarms that warn you".

### A frosted car in Inverurie

It's 7 a.m. in Inverurie, in Scotland, where the Highway Code applies. The car has been out all night
on the street, and every window is white. The driver has twenty minutes.

**Step 1: the engine.** The tempting move is to start the car, turn the heater to full and go back in
for a coffee. On this course's reading, Rule 123's first part covers that: a parked car, unattended,
engine running, on a public road. Staying with the car answers only that first part, on the same
reading. The
regulation behind it, regulation 107 of the Construction and Use Regulations, which lesson 12
described, speaks of a car "not attended by a person licensed to drive it", and this course read
nothing on what counts as attending. The second part is about running an engine "unnecessarily" while
stationary on a public road. The Code names only standing in traffic and diagnosing faults as times it's
permissible, and this course read nothing that says whether defrosting is one. The rule's advice, its should line, is clear,
though: "Generally, if the vehicle is stationary and is likely to remain so for more than a couple of
minutes, you should apply the parking brake and switch off the engine".[10] So how the law's second part
applies to defrosting is a question this course can't answer, and the Code's advice is not in doubt. Twenty minutes of standing is a switched-off
engine and a scraper.

**Step 2: the glass.** Rule 229's first MUST is all snow and ice from all windows: the windscreen, the
side windows and the rear window, not a peephole. A scraper and de-icer are on Rule 228's kit list.

**Step 3: lights and plates.** The second MUST: lights clean, plates "clearly visible and legible".
Frost on a headlamp or a number plate counts.

**Step 4: the rest.** Mirrors clear, windows demisted, snow off the roof and bonnet where it could slide
onto the windscreen or onto someone else. These are advice, and good advice, since snow sliding off the
roof onto the windscreen at the first junction puts the driver back where Step 2 started.

**Step 5: the exhaust.** Before the engine runs at all in deep snow, the pipe is clear. The next section
explains why.

## A car stuck in snow: carbon monoxide

A petrol or diesel engine's exhaust carries carbon monoxide. Toyota's handbook for the 2023 Corolla
sold in the US puts it in one line: "Exhaust gases include harmful carbon monoxide (CO), which is
colorless and odorless."[13] Snow is what brings it inside.

**The mechanism.** The CDC's report on the January 1996 blizzard gives it: "CO can seep into the cabin
of a vehicle through leaks or cracks in the floorboard if the exhaust pipe is obstructed."[1] The CDC's
own advice page puts it for the public: "If the tailpipe is blocked, carbon monoxide, an odorless,
colorless, and deadly gas produced by your engine can build up quickly inside your vehicle, poisoning
anyone inside."[14] And Toyota describes the other route, snow piled round the car rather than in the
pipe: "If snowbanks build up around the vehicle while the engine is running, exhaust gases may collect
and enter the vehicle."[13] You can't smell it. The report says CO is "colorless, tasteless, odorless,
and nonirritating", so "its presence usually is not detected."[1]

**What it did in 1996.** The same report describes one city's two days after the storm: "a total of 21
persons were admitted directly to the Jacobi Medical Center or transferred from other hospitals for
hyperbaric oxygen therapy because of CO poisoning", and "In addition, one fatal case of CO poisoning was
reported in which the victim was not admitted to the hospital." All 21 "were found unconscious inside
automobiles with engines running." Eight were under 16 and twelve were over 50.[1] Those are one city's
figures from one storm, thirty years ago, and they're given here for what they show about how it
happens, not as a rate. This course found no UK official page on snow-blocked exhausts in a stranded
car, so the sources in this section are American, and one is a maker's handbook for a US car.

One of the report's cases is worth reading slowly. A family was digging their car out of a snowbank,
and a four-year-old girl got cold. "To warm the child, she was placed in the car with both the engine
and heater running while deep snow surrounded the rear of the automobile." Her grandmother and sister
sat in the car too, and got out after a few minutes "when the grandmother became dizzy and
lightheaded." The girl, thought to be napping, stayed about 30 minutes "before she was found to be
unconscious and could not be awakened." She was treated and discharged.[1] The family was trying to warm a cold child.

What the report asks of everyone is short: "Following heavy snowfalls, the public should be reminded to
inspect vehicles to ensure that exhaust pipes are cleared of snow before engines are started."[1]
Clearing snow from the exhaust pipe before starting is **Yours**. A leak in the exhaust itself is not.
Toyota's handbook for the Corolla: "If you smell exhaust gases in the vehicle even when the trunk lid is
closed, open the windows and have the vehicle inspected at your Toyota dealer as soon as possible."[13]
On this course's reading, that makes exhaust fumes inside any car **Call someone**: a garage.

What carbon monoxide does to a person, and what to do for someone it has affected, is First Aid and
CPR's: lesson 14, "Seizures, low blood sugar and poisoning", covers carbon monoxide, and lesson 15,
"Heat, cold, bites and stings", covers cold.

### When you're stranded in snow

These are US steps, for a car stuck in snow. In Great Britain, a car stopped on a motorway or other
fast road follows the callout's steps whatever the weather, and lesson 17, "Motorways and fast roads:
go left, the live lane, and the smart motorway question", teaches them in full: out on the side away
from traffic, behind the barrier if there is one, and, in the words of the Highway Code's Rule 277,
which is advice, "DO NOT return to your vehicle even if it's raining, cold or dark".[18] This course
read no UK official advice for a car stuck in snow anywhere else.

NHTSA gives the steps for a car that is "stopped or stalled in wintry weather":[5]

- "Stay with your car and don't overexert yourself."
- "Let your car be seen. Put bright markers on the antenna or windows and keep the interior dome light
  on."
- "Be mindful of carbon monoxide poisoning. Make sure your exhaust pipe is clear of any snow and run your
  car only sporadically", just long enough, the page goes on, to stay warm. "Don't run your car for long
  periods of time with the windows up or in an enclosed space."

Caltrans says much the same for California: "When stalled, stay with your vehicle and try to conserve
fuel while maintaining warmth. Be alert to any possible exhaust or monoxide problems."[12]

Toyota's handbook goes further. For the 2023 Corolla sold in the US, under "When parking":
"Do not leave the engine running in an area with snow build-up, or where it is snowing."[13] That's stricter than NHTSA, which allows
short spells with the exhaust clear. The two agree on the dangerous part, a blocked exhaust and long
running, and differ on whether to run the engine at all while it is snowing. On this course's
reading, the handbook governs its own car here too, as it does for every figure (lesson 1): if yours
says don't, don't. If it says nothing, NHTSA's short spells with the pipe checked each time are the
advice this course read, for the US.

NHTSA's kit list for the car is the practical side of staying put: "a snow shovel, broom, and ice
scraper;" "abrasive material (sand or kitty litter), in case your vehicle gets stuck in the snow;"
"blankets for protection from the cold; and" "a cell phone and charger, water, food, and any necessary
medicine."[5] National Highways, which runs England's motorways and major A roads, gives a similar
list for its roads, and its first advice is to "Check the weather forecast. If snow or ice is expected, consider
delaying your journey until it clears."[15]

## Getting the car ready for summer

The summer lists are shorter.

**The cooling system.** National Highways: "During particularly hot weather, make sure your engine stays
within the normal operating temperature range. If it begins to overheat, find somewhere safe to stop
and allow it cool down."[16] (The missing "to" is the page's.) "Somewhere safe" is lesson 16's subject.
Annex 6 adds the rule you met in lesson 9: "If your engine overheats, you should wait until it has
cooled naturally."[3] A hot cap is never opened, and the temperature light is one of lesson 11's
stop-now group.

**The people.** The Highway Code's Rule 237 says, as advice: "Keep your vehicle well ventilated to avoid
drowsiness."[6] National Highways' summer kit starts with "drinking water", and it asks you to take
enough "for you and your passengers".[16]

## A child or an animal in a hot car

NHTSA's campaign page puts the scale in two sentences. "More than 1,000 children have died of heatstroke,
because they were left or became trapped in a hot car over the past 25 years." And: "In 2025, 31
children died of heatstroke in vehicles."[2] Those are US figures. This course found no UK figure, and it
doesn't borrow the American one for the UK.

**Why a child.** NHTSA gives one reason: "A child's body temperature rises three to five times faster
than an adult's."[2] That is the whole of the mechanism the sources read here give. None of them explains
why a parked car heats up as it does, and this course doesn't supply an explanation of its own. A 2022 heat-wave news release from AAA's Auto Club
Group (AAA clubs sell roadside assistance) gives a size, for the US heat it was writing about: "Outside air temperatures in the nineties can rise to 125
degrees inside the vehicle very quickly".[17] In °C that's a day of about 32 to 37 °C becoming about 52 °C
inside, on this course's conversion.

:::predict Most people would feel better leaving a child for five minutes in a car parked in the shade with the windows open. Before reading on: what do you think NHTSA says about each of those three things (the time, the shade, the windows)?

NHTSA: "Never leave a child in a vehicle unattended for any length of time. Rolling windows down or parking in the shade does little to change the interior temperature of the vehicle." So none of the three makes it safe: there's no safe number of minutes, and the shade and the windows, in NHTSA's words, do little. AAA says the same about time: "not even for a short period of time".
:::

### How it happens

NHTSA's two figures point at two different ways, and they need two different habits.

The first is forgetting. "Over 50% of pediatric vehicular heatstroke deaths are a result of a parent or
caregiver forgetting a child in a car."[2] NHTSA's answers are
habits: "Make it a habit to check your entire vehicle", especially the back seat, "before
locking the doors and walking away"; "Place a personal item like a purse or briefcase in the back seat,
as another reminder to look before you lock"; and "Ask your childcare provider to call if your child
doesn't show up for care as expected."[2]

The second is children getting in by themselves. "Over 230 children have died from vehicular heatstroke
since 1998 because they gained access to a vehicle and became trapped." The answer is the car, not the
parent: "Always lock your car when you aren't using it", and "Store car keys and fobs out of a child's
reach and teach children that a vehicle is not a play area."[2] NHTSA addresses the first of these to
everyone, children or not, because the child who climbs in might be a neighbour's.

The Highway Code's version is one line in Annex 6's advice on security, under "When you leave your
vehicle you should": "never leave children or pets in an unventilated car".[3] It's a should, advice.
Its word is "unventilated". It doesn't say an open window makes a car safe, and NHTSA says rolling
windows down "does little" to the temperature inside. AAA, whose clubs sell roadside assistance, has a line that covers animals too: "Never leave children or animals unattended in a car, not even for a short
period of time."[17] This course did not read any law, in Great Britain, Northern Ireland or any US state,
on leaving a child or an animal in a car, or on breaking into a car to reach one, and it makes no
statement about either. The habits above are **Yours**, and they're what the sources give.

### If you find one

NHTSA, for the US: "If you see a child alone in a locked car, act immediately and call 911. A child in
distress due to heat should be removed from the vehicle as quickly as possible and rapidly cooled."[2]
NHTSA doesn't say who removes the child or how, and, as above, this course read no law on breaking into
a car and makes no statement about it.
This course read no UK page that sets out a bystander's steps for a child in a hot car. In the UK the
emergency number is 999. What happens after the child is out, the cooling and the signs of heatstroke, is
First Aid and CPR lesson 15, "Heat, cold, bites and stings", and it's worth reading before a summer, not
during one.

### A five-minute errand in Temecula

A parent in Temecula has a toddler asleep in the back and a prescription to collect. The pharmacy is
across the car park. The plan: park under a tree, windows down four inches, back in five minutes.

:::checkpoint Using only NHTSA's page: what's wrong with each part of that plan, and what does the parent do instead?

Five minutes: NHTSA says "for any length of time", and gives no safe time. The tree and the windows: "does little to change the interior temperature". The child: a child's temperature rises three to five times faster than an adult's. So the toddler comes in, asleep or not. The same parent on a different day, driving to work with the child asleep and forgotten in the back, is the other half of NHTSA's figures, and its back-seat habit (a bag in the back, a look before locking) is the guard against that.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"My battery was fine all summer, so the frost killed it."** The frost is when a tired battery gives
out, not usually what tired it. AAA's Mountain West club, which sells roadside assistance, puts most of the damage on hot weather, which
is why the autumn test in the checkpoint above comes before the cold rather than after it.

**"A window open makes a hot car safe."** NHTSA: rolling windows down or parking in the shade "does
little to change the interior temperature of the vehicle."

**"Idling in the snow to keep warm is fine."** Only with the exhaust clear and only in short spells, on
NHTSA's advice for the US, and not at all if your handbook says otherwise. The CDC's report is about people who
did exactly that with the pipe blocked.

**"A peephole in the ice will do."** In Great Britain, Rule 229's MUST covers all your windows. The
section on winter law has the words.

**"Winter tyres are required in the UK."** The RAC, which sells breakdown cover: "Winter tyres aren't mandatory in the UK."

## Practice

:::exercise Your seasonal checklist
Take 20 minutes over this. It's on paper, with your own car's handbook (or the maker's online handbook). Don't drive for the exercise. If you look at the car, it should be yours or its keeper should agree, parked and switched off, and you look without opening any cap or reservoir.

1. Make two lists, "Before winter" and "Before summer", for the country you drive in. For each item, write whose it is (law, handbook or advice, and whose), for which car, and the job label: Yours, Yours on conditions (name the condition), or Call someone. Use the tables and lists in this lesson, and the earlier lessons they point to.

2. From your handbook, find and copy: anything it says about running the engine in snow or in an enclosed space; the washer fluid it specifies for cold weather; anything it says about snow chains or winter tyres for your car; and the tyre pressures, cold, from the placard.

3. For a winter kit, write what you would carry, marking which list each item comes from (Rule 228, National Highways, NHTSA), and where in the car it would go. Don't buy anything for the exercise.

4. This is general information, not legal advice. If you drive in Great Britain, write Rule 229's two MUST lines in your own words, and one sentence on Rule 123 and defrosting. If you drive in a US state, write down which state agency's page you would check for winter rules such as chain controls, and one question you would ask it.

5. Write your two hot-car habits from NHTSA's list, in the words you'd actually use, and where the reminder item will go in your car.
:::

:::checkpoint Check yourself on part 1. Where should "clear the exhaust pipe of snow before starting the engine" sit, whose is it, and what label does it get? And "never leave a child in the car"?

Clearing the exhaust is advice from the CDC and NHTSA (and, in its stronger form, Toyota's handbook for the Corolla), for any petrol or diesel car in snow, and it's Yours. Never leaving a child in a car is advice: NHTSA's for the US, AAA's, and the Highway Code's Annex 6 as a should in Great Britain. It's Yours too, as a habit. Neither is a MUST in anything this course read.
:::
