Changing a wheel is the one job in this course that lifts a car off the ground, and the only one
where the car itself can fall on you. [Mazda's handbook for the Mazda3](https://owners-manual.mazda.com/gen/en/mazda3/mazda3_8gd7ee17h/contents/08020300.html) says it in two sentences:
"Changing a tyre is dangerous if not done properly. The vehicle can slip off the jack and seriously
injure someone."[3] Done by the handbook, on the right ground, it is a job an owner can do. This
lesson is that handbook order, what each step is for as far as the makers say, and the places and
conditions where the right answer is not to start.

:::callout Before the car lets you down
This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. If your car fails on a motorway or other fast road in Great Britain, leave at the next exit or services if you can; if you can't, go left into an emergency area or onto the hard shoulder, switch on your hazard lights, get everyone out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and don't go back to the car. If you're stuck in a live lane or can't get out safely, stay in with seat belts and hazard lights on and call 999 and ask for the police. In the US, pull as far onto the right shoulder as you can, switch on your hazard lights and stay in with your seat belt on, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind, in which case get out on the side away from traffic; call 911 in an emergency. Never put any part of your body under a car held up only by a jack, and never touch the orange high-voltage cables on a hybrid or electric car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

This is the lesson the callout's jack clause belongs to. Lesson 18, "A flat tyre: the decision, and
spare, space saver, sealant or run-flat", decided whether you change the wheel at all; this one assumes
the answer was yes. One section below reports what the Highway Code says, and which parts are advice.
This is general information, not legal advice. The rules differ between England and Wales, Scotland,
Northern Ireland and each US state, and they change.

The lesson uses UK words with the US one once: tyre (tire), wheel nuts (lug nuts), boot (trunk),
handbrake or parking brake, wheel brace (lug wrench: the spanner in the car's kit that turns the
nuts), space saver (compact spare). Torque is in N·m (newton metres) with lbf·ft
(pound-feet).

## First, whether the place will do

This is general information, not legal advice.

Changing a wheel is **Yours, on conditions**. Lesson 18 set them out; here they are as a check you
run before the boot is opened:

- **Not on a motorway or other fast road, and not on a hard shoulder or in an emergency area.** On a
  motorway, the Highway Code's Rule 277, which is advice rather than a MUST, says "DO NOT attempt
  repairs on your vehicle", and Green Flag, which sells breakdown cover and repairs, says not to
  change a tyre on a motorway "even if you're on the hard shoulder or in an emergency area".[7][9] On
  any other fast road in Great Britain the callout's steps apply (everyone out on the side away from
  traffic, and nobody goes back to the car), so on this course's reading there's no wheel change
  there either, lay-by or not; lesson 17 has those roads. On a US freeway shoulder, this course's
  reading is the same, and the callout's US steps apply: stay in, belted, unless the shoulder is too
  narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind (lesson 16).
- **Firm, level ground.** Toyota: "a hard, flat surface"; Honda: "a firm, level, non-slippery
  surface"; Mazda: "a hard, level surface off the right-of-way".[1][2][3] [HSE](https://www.hse.gov.uk/MVR/mechanical-repair/under-vehicles.htm), Great Britain's
  workplace safety regulator, in guidance written for garages, says the same for work away from the
  workshop: "the ground is firm and level before raising a vehicle".[5]
- **Off the road, and nobody between the car and the traffic.** Mazda's handbook says to drive "to a level spot that is well off the road and out of the way of traffic",
  and has everyone out and "away from the vehicle and traffic".[3] In Great Britain, Rule 276, again
  advice: "do not stand (or let anybody else stand) between your vehicle and oncoming traffic".[7]
- **The car's own jack, and a handbook with the method.** All three handbooks read here say to use
  the jack that came with the car; step 9 has their words.

The third condition is the one people miss. Kneeling at a wheel on the traffic side of a car parked at
the edge of a road puts you in the traffic Mazda says to keep away from, and in Great Britain exactly
where Rule 276 advises not to stand. On this course's reading, if the
flat is on the traffic side and the traffic passes close, the job is **Call someone**, however good the
ground. Lesson 13, "A flat battery and jump-starting", applied these same placement rules to a jump
start: off the road if you can, hazard lights on, and nobody standing between the car and traffic.

## The order, and what each step prevents

The three handbooks this lesson re-read give nearly the same order. [Toyota's](https://assets.sia.toyota.com/publications/en/om-s/OM02568U/pdf/OM02568U.pdf) is for the 2023 Corolla
sold in the US, Honda's for the 2024 Civic Sedan sold in the US, Mazda's for the Mazda3 in its European
English edition.[1][2][3] Where they differ, the difference is shown. Where a handbook says why, the
reason is given; where none does, this course says so and doesn't invent one.

**1. Stop on firm, level ground, off the road.** The first section's check.

**2. Parking brake on.** All three.

**3. P, or a gear.** Toyota: "Shift the shift lever to P." Honda: P for its CVT automatic, R for a manual.
Mazda: P for an automatic, "Reverse (R) or 1" for a manual.[1][2][3]

**4. Engine off.** Toyota: "Stop the engine." Honda: power mode to "VEHICLE OFF". On a hybrid or
electric car, off means switched off in the way its handbook describes. Such a car can be silent and
still on, and so can a petrol car whose stop-start has stopped the engine: READY isn't off, and
silence proves nothing. Lesson 14, "High voltage: hybrid and EV safety, charging at home, fire and
flood", has why. And while the car is on the jack, Toyota: "Do not start the engine or drive the vehicle while the vehicle is supported by
the jack." Mazda gives the reason: starting the engine or shaking the car "could cause the vehicle to
fall off the jack resulting in an accident."[1][2][3]

**5. Hazard lights on.** All three.

**6. Everyone out, and away.** Mazda: "Have everyone get out of the vehicle and away from the vehicle
and traffic." Toyota: "Do not raise the vehicle while someone is inside." Honda: "Do not use the jack
with people or luggage in the vehicle." Mazda again gives the reason: "The occupant could cause the
vehicle to fall resulting in serious injury."[1][2][3]

:::predict Step 7 is a chock: a block against one wheel so the car cannot roll. You have one chock and a flat front left tyre. Which wheel does it go against, and on which side of it?

The wheel diagonally opposite the flat: here, the rear right. Honda and Mazda put a block both in front of it and behind it. Toyota's handbook shows one chock, and for a front flat it goes behind the diagonal rear wheel; for a rear flat, in front of the diagonal front wheel.
:::

**7. Chock the diagonally opposite wheel.** Honda: "Place a wheel block or rock in front and rear of the
wheel diagonal to the flat tire." Mazda: "Block the wheel diagonally opposite the flat tyre. When
blocking a wheel, place a tyre block both in front and behind the tyre."[2][3] Toyota's diagram puts a
single chock on the same diagonal wheel, behind it for a front flat and in front of it for a rear
flat.[1] And the reason, from Mazda: "Changing a flat tyre without using wheel blocks is dangerous
because the vehicle may move and fall off the jack even with the shift lever in 1st or R, or the select
lever is in P".[3] So the parking brake and P aren't enough on their own. None of the handbooks says
why the diagonal wheel in particular, and this course doesn't supply a reason.

Honda adds a step the other two don't, before the nuts are loosened: "Place the compact spare tire
(wheel side up) under the vehicle body, near the tire that needs to be replaced."[2] Honda gives no
reason, and this course doesn't supply one. It goes there while the car is still on its wheels. On
this course's reading, step 12 still holds when the spare comes out again to be fitted: take hold of
it from the side, and don't reach under the car.

No chock in the boot? Honda says "a wheel block or rock"; Mazda, "use rocks or wood blocks of
sufficient size if possible".[2][3] The words that matter are "of sufficient size"; on this course's
reading, a small stone is not one.

**8. Loosen each nut about one turn, with the wheel still on the ground.** Toyota: "Slightly loosen the
wheel nuts (one turn)." Honda: "about one turn". Mazda: "Loosen the wheel nuts by turning them
anticlockwise one turn each, but do not remove any wheel nuts until the tyre has been raised off the
ground."[1][2][3] The handbooks don't say why the order is this way round. Green Flag, which sells
breakdown cover and repairs, gives the danger it avoids, about a wheel that won't come off a raised car: "pushing and pulling it could bring
the car off the jack."[9] On this course's reading, that's the reason for doing the hard pull on the
nuts while the tyre still carries the car.

If a nut won't turn with the car's own wheel brace, the job ends there, on this course's reading. No
handbook read offers a way to force a nut. Honda's warning against "extra torque using your foot or a
pipe" is about tightening, and on the same reading it's no better an idea for loosening. **Call
someone.** The same goes for a locking wheel nut (one nut on the wheel that needs its own key) when the
key isn't in the car; no handbook read covers them.

**9. The maker's jack, at the maker's jacking point.** Toyota: "Only use the tire jack that comes with
this vehicle for replacing a flat tire." Honda: "Use the jack provided in your vehicle", because other
jacks "may not support the weight" or "may not fit the jacking point"; and "Use only at the jacking
points." Mazda: "Use only the jack provided with your Mazda", and "Use only the front and rear jacking
positions recommended in this manual", because otherwise "The vehicle could slip off the jack and
seriously injure or even kill someone."[1][2][3] The jacking points (Toyota's "jack point", Mazda's
"jack-up position") are the places the handbook's drawing marks for the jack, and Honda and Mazda
have you use the one closest to the flat. They aren't simply wherever looks strong. HSE, for
garages, says the same: check that the lifting points are right for that vehicle and "consult the
vehicle manufacturer's handbook for guidance".[5]

**10. Nothing on or under the jack.** Toyota: "When raising the vehicle, do not put an object on or
under the jack." Honda: "Do not put anything on top of or underneath the jack." Mazda: "Never place
objects under the jack", because "The jack could slip and someone could be seriously injured by the
jack or the falling vehicle."[1][2][3]

**11. Raise it only as far as the job needs.** Toyota: "Raise the vehicle until the tire is slightly
raised off the ground", and "Do not raise the vehicle to a height greater than that required to replace
the tire." Honda: "until the tire is off the ground". Mazda sets the height by the spare, not the flat:
"raise the vehicle high enough so that the spare tyre can be installed", and "Jacking up the vehicle
higher than is necessary is dangerous as it could destabilize the vehicle resulting in an
accident."[1][2][3] So the makers put the height a little differently, and your handbook's words are
the ones to follow.

**12. Nothing of you under the car.** This is the callout's clause, and every handbook read says it.
Toyota: "Do not put any part of your body under the vehicle while it is supported by the jack." Mazda:
"No person should place any portion of their body under a vehicle that is supported by a jack." Honda:
"never get under the vehicle when it is supported only by the jack", because "The vehicle can easily
roll off the jack, seriously injuring anyone underneath."[1][2][3] Nissan, in its
handbook for the 2024 LEAF sold in the US: "Never get under the vehicle while it is supported only by a
jack."[4] On this course's reading, "any part of your body" includes a hand reaching under to steady
something, and a foot or a knee pushed under the sill.

**13. Mind the brakes.** Toyota: after driving, "the disc wheels and the area around the brakes will be
extremely hot", and touching them "may result in burns."[1]

**14. Clean surfaces, and nuts the right way round.** All three say to clean the wheel's mounting
surface first. Honda: "Wipe the mounting surfaces of the wheel with a clean cloth." Toyota: if dirt is
left there, "the wheel nuts may loosen while the vehicle is in motion". Mazda: "Do not apply oil or
grease to wheel nuts and bolts", because "The wheel nuts could loosen while driving and cause the
tyre to come off".[1][2][3] Toyota and Mazda have you fit each nut with its tapered or bevelled end
facing inward.

**15. Hand-tight, lower, then tighten in the handbook's order.** Toyota and Mazda have you run the nuts
on by hand, and Honda until they "touch the lips around the mounting holes"; all three then lower the
car and tighten the nuts in the order their illustration shows. Toyota:
"Firmly tighten each wheel nut two or three times in the order shown in the illustration." Honda: "Go
around, tightening the nuts, two to three times in this order."[1][2][3] Toyota also says, "When lowering
the vehicle, make sure that there is no-one near the vehicle."[1]

**16. Stow everything.** Honda: "Loose items can fly around the interior in a crash and can seriously
injure the occupants."[2]

## The torque, and why this course prints no single figure

After the nuts are tightened with the wheel brace, Toyota wants a torque wrench (a different tool from
the wheel brace, made to tighten a nut to a set figure), and soon: "Have the
wheel nuts tightened with a torque wrench to 76 ft•lbf (103 N•m, 10.5 kgf•m) as soon as possible after
changing wheels." It gives the reason in the same warning: failing to follow its precautions "could
cause the wheel nuts to loosen and the tire to fall off, resulting in death or serious injury."[1] Honda gives 80 lbf·ft (108 N·m) for the
Civic.[2] Mazda gives a range, 108 to 147 N·m (80 to 108 lbf·ft), and adds: "If you are unsure of how tight the nuts should
be, have them inspected at an expert repairer".[3]

Lesson 1, "What a car is made of, and why the handbook comes first", put these side by side with
Nissan's figure for the LEAF sold in the UK and made the point: several makers, several figures, and no
reason given for any of them. That's why this course prints no single figure. A figure from a forum
or a friend belongs to no car in particular.

Two things go wrong in the other direction. Honda: "Do not over tighten the wheel nuts by applying extra
torque using your foot or a pipe." Mazda: "do not tighten the wheel nuts beyond the recommended
tightening torque", because the nuts and bolts "could be damaged if tightened more than necessary".[2][3]

Toyota's words set the deadline, "as soon as possible after changing wheels". On this course's
reading, and for the reason lesson 1 gave (a page can't check a torque), the final torque is **Call
someone**: a tyre fitter or a garage, on the way to having the flat tyre repaired, which lesson 18 said
needs doing soon anyway. The RAC, which sells breakdown cover, suggests "giving the wheel nuts a final
check for tightness", and Green Flag, which sells breakdown cover too, says to "Check them all
twice".[8][9] Neither is a torque wrench.

## Under the car: where this course stops

Toyota's handbook goes one step further than this course: "Use a jack stand if it is necessary to get
under the vehicle."[1] Nissan's says, "If it is necessary to work under the vehicle, support it with
safety stands."[4] Those are the makers' steps. This course does not teach them, as its own scope
decision, not because the work is unlawful: a page can't check a stand's footing or a car's support
points. HSE's guidance for garages puts the principle in six words: "do not rely on jacks alone".[5] At the roadside, with the car's own jack, the answer is simpler.
Nothing of you goes under the car, at all.

## What the injury figures show, and don't

The only injury study this course found is American and old. In 1998, NHTSA's statisticians published
a [four-page research note](https://ntlrepository.blob.core.windows.net/lib/21000/21300/21317/PB99111775.pdf) on injuries from "jack failures", which it defined as situations where "the
vehicle is believed to have moved either as a result of the jack or car shifting, or as a result of the
jack collapsing or losing pressure needed to hold the vehicle."[6] It used the US Consumer Product
Safety Commission's emergency-room injury data.

Here's what it found, and how far it goes. For one year, November 1994 to October 1995, it had
"data from 102 cases", and from them it estimated that 4,822 people across the US were treated in
hospital emergency rooms for injuries from a jack failure, in that sense, during some activity involving
a motor vehicle. Of those, "Approximately three-fourths (74%) of the persons injured in motor vehicle jack
failures were injured as a result of being struck by the vehicle as it fell from the jack." And by
activity: "Another 913 (19%) persons were injured as they used the jack to lift the vehicle while
attempting to change a tire."[6]

:::predict Before reading on: does that note show that changing a tyre is the most dangerous way to use a jack?

No. The 74 per cent is about what hurt people (the falling car), not what they were doing. By activity, the largest group, 40 per cent, were repairing the vehicle, and 36 per cent were doing something unrecorded; tyre changes were 19 per cent. And even a bigger study of this kind couldn't rank the jobs, on this course's reading, because it counts people hurt, not jobs done: nobody counted how many tyre changes or repairs happened that year, so a larger share could just mean more people doing that job. The 102 cases, one year and emergency rooms only make even the counts rough, and the note doesn't try to rank anything.
:::

Its limits are these. It's an estimate scaled up from 102 cases. It counts people
treated in hospital emergency rooms, and nobody else. Its data are over thirty years old. And the usual follow-up interviews weren't done: "funding limitations precluded this
from being done in the present instance."[6] So it is a picture of one year in the mid-1990s in one
country, not a current rate. This course found no UK figure at all.

What it does show is where the harm comes from: the car coming down. HSE's page gives the workplace
version: "Vehicles falling or rolling off incorrectly positioned jacks or stands, particularly where they
are not chocked, are one of the main causes of fatal accidents in motor vehicle repair (MVR)." One of its
examples is a roadside recovery: a fitter "crushed to death under the air-lifted axle of a tractor unit
they were attempting to rescue from the roadside. No supplementary supports were used."[5] That was a
lorry, not a car, and a trade job, not an owner's. The mechanism is the same one every handbook's jack
warning is about.

## A wheel change in a Hornsea lay-by

Now a case worked through. A driver's Mazda3 gets a slow puncture in the nearside rear tyre (the left,
the side away from the traffic) on a quiet local road out of Hornsea on a dry afternoon. Hers carries the
temporary spare and a jack; the handbook says a Mazda3 "may or may not be equipped with a spare tyre, jack,
wheel brace, and tool bag".[3] A lay-by, set well back from the carriageway with a hard, level surface,
is a few hundred metres on.

**Whether.** Not a motorway or other fast road. The lay-by is off the road, hard and level. The flat is on the nearside,
so she won't be between the car and the traffic while she works. The
Mazda3's handbook gives the method. Every condition is met, so it's **Yours, on conditions**.

**The preparation.** Parking brake on; P, since it's an automatic; switched off as the handbook
describes; hazard lights on. Her
passenger gets out and waits well away from the car and the road. She takes the spare, jack and tools
from the boot.

**The chock.** The flat is rear left, so the diagonal wheel is front right. Mazda wants a block in front
of it and behind it. She has no chocks, so she uses two stones from the edge of the lay-by, each big
enough to hold the tyre, as Mazda allows.

**The nuts.** With the wheel on the ground, a turn each, anticlockwise. None comes off yet.

**The jack.** At the rear jack-up position the handbook's drawing shows, nothing under it, raised high
enough for the spare to go on, as Mazda puts it, and no higher. She keeps her hands and knees out from under the car throughout.

**The swap.** Nuts off, wheel off, the mounting surfaces wiped, spare on, nuts on by hand with the
bevelled edge inward. Car lowered with nobody beside it. Nuts tightened in the order the handbook's
drawing shows.

**After.** Stones moved, tools stowed. The spare's speed limit is 80 km/h (50 mph) in Mazda's words;
lesson 18 has what the law in Great Britain adds. And straight to a tyre fitter, both to have the
puncture seen to and to have the nuts checked with a torque wrench against Mazda's range.

### A harder one

Now one with a gap. A driver has a flat front tyre at dusk on a country road. The only place off the
road is a soft grass verge with a slight slope. There's no chock in the car and nothing solid in sight. When
she tries the first wheel nut with the brace, it won't move.

:::predict Before opening this: of the four problems (soft verge, slope, no chock, a nut that won't move), which ones end the job on their own? And what does she do instead?

All four, each on its own. The soft verge fails "hard" or "firm", which each handbook read asks for, and HSE for garages too. The slope fails "level": Honda says to use the jack only "where the ground is firm and level", and one of HSE's fatal examples was a coach "parked on a sloping car park", though that was a trade job with three wheels already off, and HSE doesn't say the slope caused it. The stuck nut ends it before the car is even lifted, on this course's reading, since no handbook read offers a way to free a nut that won't turn with the car's own brace, and Honda warns against extra force by "your foot or a pipe". The missing chock ends it too. Every handbook read chocks the car before it's raised, and Mazda says that without blocks it "may move and fall off the jack", even in P. It's the only one of the four a handbook gives a way round, since Honda allows a rock and Mazda rocks or wood blocks "of sufficient size", but there's nothing solid in sight. So she puts the hazard lights and sidelights on, gets everyone well away from the car and the road, and calls for help. Dusk, on this course's reading in lesson 18, tips a close call further the same way.
:::

### A Chico driveway, and the US handbook

One US case, briefly. A 2023 Corolla has a flat in a level concrete driveway in Chico. Everything in the
place check passes, and there is no traffic to stand between. The owner follows Toyota's pages 370 to
378 (the chock on the diagonal wheel, the nuts loosened a turn, the jack at its point, the tyre raised
"slightly" off the ground), and afterwards has the nuts set to Toyota's 76 lbf·ft (103 N·m) with a torque
wrench, as the handbook says, "as soon as possible". The compact spare then carries Toyota's own limit
of 50 mph (80 km/h), and its pressure isn't watched by the car's warning system, as lesson 18 showed.

## What people get wrong

**"A plank under the jack makes it steadier."** The RAC, which sells breakdown cover, says "Placing a
small plank of wood under the jack will help keep it stable."[8] All three handbooks re-read here forbid
anything under the jack, and Mazda says why: the jack could slip. For your car, the handbook governs.

**"A quick look underneath is fine."** Not while it's on the jack. "Any part of your body", in Toyota's
words, and Mazda's "any portion of their body".

**"Tighten them as hard as you can."** Honda forbids extra force by foot or pipe, and Mazda says too much
can damage the nuts and bolts. The handbook's figure, with a torque wrench.

**"Any strong-looking point will take the jack."** Honda: "Use only at the jacking points." Mazda: other
positions could let the car "slip off the jack", and could "deform the vehicle body".[3]

**"It's in P with the handbrake on, so it can't move."** Mazda: without wheel blocks the car "may move
and fall off the jack even with" the lever in P.

Take 20 minutes over this. It's done on paper and by looking. Do not lift the car, loosen a wheel nut or take out the spare for practice; nobody goes under the car, and nobody stands at a roadside for the exercise. The car should be yours or its keeper should agree, parked off the road with the engine or motor off and the parking brake on.

1. In the handbook, find the section on changing a wheel. Write its title and pages, and the order of steps in your own short words.

2. Copy, word for word, the handbook's warning about the jack and about going under the car.

3. From the handbook's drawing, write where the jacking points are, then find them on the car by looking from the side without reaching under it.

4. From lesson 18's wheel page, check where the jack, wheel brace and any locking wheel nut key are kept. Add where any chock is kept; if there's none, write what you'd use, as Honda and Mazda allow, and where you'd find it.

5. Copy the wheel-nut torque, with its units, and the handbook's page.

6. Write three places on roads you use where you would change a wheel, and one where you wouldn't, with the reason for each from this lesson.
