The car clicks, or does nothing, and the dashboard lights flicker. A neighbour has jump leads and is
happy to help. This is the one job in this course where you connect two cars' electrics together,
and [Nissan's handbook for the LEAF](https://www.nissanusa.com/content/dam/Nissan/us/manuals-and-guides/leaf/2024/2024-nissan-leaf-owner-manual.pdf) opens its jump-start pages with the reason to slow down: "If done
incorrectly, jump starting can lead to a 12-volt battery explosion, resulting in severe injury or
death."[1] Done the way the handbook says, in a safe place, with a battery that is fit to be jumped,
it gets you going in minutes. This lesson is about telling which of those you've got, and about the
order the leads go on and come off.

:::callout Before the car lets you down
This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. If your car fails on a motorway or other fast road in Great Britain, leave at the next exit or services if you can; if you can't, go left into an emergency area or onto the hard shoulder, switch on your hazard lights, get everyone out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and don't go back to the car. If you're stuck in a live lane or can't get out safely, stay in with seat belts and hazard lights on and call 999 and ask for the police. In the US, pull as far onto the right shoulder as you can, switch on your hazard lights and stay in with your seat belt on, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind, in which case get out on the side away from traffic; call 911 in an emergency. Never put any part of your body under a car held up only by a jack, and never touch the orange high-voltage cables on a hybrid or electric car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

The motorway clause matters here, because a jump start is never done there, as the next section
explains. Nothing in this lesson asks you to connect leads for practice: the exercise is done on
paper, from your handbook. It uses UK words with the US word once: bonnet (hood), jump leads (jumper
cables), and earth for what American handbooks call ground.

## Is it the battery?

Lesson 5, "The 12 V battery: what it does, and why short trips flatten it", gave the signs: a slow
crank, dim lights, and this line from the AA, a motoring organisation that sells breakdown cover and
repairs: "If the dash lights are flickering or don't come on, it's likely the battery is
discharged."[2] It also gave the AA's three free checks before blaming the battery (fuel,
a dead key fob, wheels jammed against the kerb). A jump start is the answer only to a flat 12 V
battery. It won't fix an empty tank, and it cannot help a battery that's damaged.

:::checkpoint Without looking back at lesson 5: name the three hazards a lead-acid battery poses to the person standing over it, and the one hazard HSE says it is unlikely to pose.

Hydrogen, which can ignite and explode; acid, which burns skin and can permanently damage eyes; and a short circuit, when a metal tool or a ring bridges the terminals and gets very hot very quickly. The one it is unlikely to pose is an electric shock: HSE says most batteries "produce quite low voltages, and so there is little risk of electric shock". That changes with the high-voltage battery in a hybrid or electric car, which is lesson 14's.
:::

## A jump start, or a call?

A jump start is **Yours, on conditions**, and the conditions come in the next sections. Before
those, two sources remind you it's a choice. [The AA](https://www.theaa.com/breakdown-cover/advice/using-jump-leads), which sells breakdown cover and repairs, is
candid about what it would rather you did: "For your safety, and our peace of mind, we'd always
prefer you to give us a call and leave the jump starting to our trained mechanics."[3] That is a
safety point and a sales point in one sentence, and the same page goes on to give its own steps.
Toyota's handbook for the 2023 Corolla sold in the US offers the other route plainly: "You can also
call your Toyota dealer or a qualified repair shop."[4]

What decides it, on this course's reading, is three things: where the car is, what state the
battery is in, and whether you have the handbook's method. Where comes first.

### Where a jump start is never done

On a motorway in Great Britain, the Highway Code's [Rule 277](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/the-highway-code/breakdowns-and-incidents-274-to-287) lists what to do after you have stopped,
and it includes "DO NOT attempt repairs on your vehicle".[7] That's advice (the rule says "you
should"), not a MUST rule. No source read mentions jump starts by name there, so this is this
course's reading: a jump start is a repair in the sense that matters, because it needs two cars
parked close with their bonnets up and people standing between them, next to traffic moving at
motorway speed. So on a motorway, a hard shoulder (in the US, the shoulder) or an emergency area,
nobody jumps a car. The
callout's steps apply instead, and lesson 17, "Motorways and fast roads: go left, the live lane, and
the smart motorway question", teaches them in full.

On a US freeway, this course found no official source on jump-starting at the roadside. Its reading
is the same: nobody jumps a car on a freeway shoulder, and the callout's US steps are the ones to
follow there.

Anywhere else, this course's reading is that a jump start follows the same placement rules as a
wheel change in lesson 19, "Changing a wheel safely", and the stopping place in lesson 16, "When the
car stops: where, who stands where, and the call": off the road, hazard lights on if the car is
causing an obstruction, and nobody standing between the cars and traffic. If the car can't be got
off the road, that's **Call someone**. For Great Britain, Rule 276 (advice again) says "do not stand
(or let anybody else stand) between your vehicle and oncoming traffic".[7] A drive or a car park is
the right kind of place. The verge of a busy road at night is not, and lesson 16 deals with that
decision.

## Why the last clamp goes where it does

The danger in a jump start is hydrogen. Nissan: "Explosive hydrogen gas is always present in the
vicinity of the 12-volt battery."[1] Lesson 5 showed from HSE, Great Britain's workplace safety
regulator, why: a battery gives off hydrogen and oxygen while it charges, and a spark can set them
off. [HSE's leaflet](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/indg139.pdf), written for workplaces in 2011, then says exactly when the risk peaks: "Many
explosions happen when batteries are being connected or disconnected. The sparks produced when this
is done incorrectly may cause the battery to explode, especially if it has just been charged."[6]

:::predict A friend clamps the last black lead onto the flat battery's own negative terminal, because it is closest and the clamp fits. Before reading on: what has he done that every source read tells him not to, and why?

He's made the final connection right at the battery, where the hydrogen is. Ford puts it on the engine "away from the battery and fuel injection system", Nissan says negative goes to body ground "not to the 12-volt battery", HSE says at least 15 cm from the battery, and the AA, which sells breakdown cover, says "well away from the flat battery and fuel system". If that last connection sparks, it sparks over the gas. It may work, which is what makes the habit stick.
:::

So the connection that matters most is the last one, made away from the gas. HSE, for the case
where no maker's instructions are available, has the black lead go from the good battery "to a
suitable unpainted metal part of the chassis or engine of the dead vehicle. The point of attachment
should be at least 15 cm from the battery and away from fuel and brake lines."[6] (15 cm is about 6
inches, this course's conversion.) Ford, Nissan and
the AA put the last clamp in the same kind of place, as the table below shows. None of the sources
read says in so many words why it is the last clamp in particular that goes there. This course's
reading of HSE's paragraphs is that the connection which ends the sequence is the one made at a
distance, so that any spark it makes is made 15 cm or more from the gas, and not over the battery
that has just been giving it off.

The same logic explains the other warnings. Toyota: "Do not allow the + and - clamps of the jumper
cables to come into contact with each other", and don't let the other end of the positive lead,
once one end is on, touch "any other parts or metal surfaces in the area, such as brackets or
unpainted metal".[4] The AA, which sells breakdown cover: rings, necklaces and watch straps off, and no metal objects touching the batteries, because
they "could cause a spark and possibly make the battery explode".[3] Nissan adds "remove rings,
metal bands, or any other jewelry", wear eye protection, and "Do not lean over the 12-volt battery
when jump starting."[1]

Two things stand out. Every source read puts the last clamp on metal away from the flat battery
(Toyota's in its drawing, which shows a metal point on the engine), and every one runs positive to
positive first. But they do not agree on which end of the red lead goes on first. Ford, Toyota and
Nissan start at the flat battery. HSE describes the lead without saying which end goes first, and
the AA's page gives both: its step 7 finishes "with the red lead from the other car", and its list
for taking the leads off finishes at "the positive (+) terminal on the flat battery".[3] Run
backwards, the first puts the red lead on the working car first and the second puts it on the flat
battery first. So there is no single "correct order" that you can carry from car to car. HSE's jump-starting section opens with the rule: "Whenever possible, follow the manufacturer's instructions
for jump-starting a vehicle."[6] The AA says the same of its own steps.[3]

### Portree, a Saturday morning

In Portree, a petrol hatchback on its owner's drive will not start: a click,
then nothing, and the dash lights flicker. It's been standing for a fortnight. A neighbour offers his
petrol estate and a set of leads. This course read no UK petrol car's handbook, so for the example,
say her handbook gives the same four steps as Toyota's for the 2023 Corolla.

**Is it the battery?** Standing a fortnight and flickering lights both fit the AA's account of a
flat battery.[2] She checks the fuel gauge first anyway.

**Can this battery be jumped?** She looks at it from where she stands, with the bonnet up, touching
nothing: no crack, no swelling, no powder on the terminals, and on a mild morning a frozen battery
isn't in question. The AA:
"Never try to jump start a battery that looks damaged or is leaking".[3] Both cars are 12 V; Ford's
handbook puts it as "Connect batteries with only the same nominal voltage", and HSE says that if
you are not sure, "do not proceed".[5][6] The leads have insulated clamps and no damage.

**Where?** Her drive, off the road. The neighbour parks close enough for the leads to reach, with
the two cars not touching (Ford: "making sure the two vehicles do not touch").[5] Handbrakes
(parking brakes) on, ignitions off, rings and watches off.

**The order, from her handbook.** Positive clamp to her flat battery's positive terminal; the other
end to the neighbour's positive; negative clamp to the neighbour's negative; and the last clamp on
the metal point her handbook's drawing shows, well away from her battery. Nobody leans over either
battery while the clamps go on.

**Starting.** Toyota's steps have the donor's engine run a little above idle for "approximately 5
minutes to recharge the battery of your vehicle" before trying the flat car.[4] Then she starts her
own car.

That leaves the leads coming off, which is where the sources part company.

## Taking the leads off: where the sources disagree

Every source read takes the leads off in the reverse of the order they went on. They disagree about
whether the engines are running while you do it.

| Source | Whose, and for which car | Engines while the leads come off | Anything else |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ford | Handbook, 2024 Maverick, US and Canada | Neither engine is switched off in its steps: "run both vehicle engines for an additional three minutes before disconnecting the jumper cables" (so, on this course's reading, both are running) | "Do not switch the headlamps on when disconnecting the cables. The peak voltage could blow the bulbs." |
| Toyota | Handbook, 2023 Corolla, US | Neither engine is switched off in its steps: with the second vehicle's engine speed maintained, "Once the vehicle's engine has started, remove the jumper cables in the exact reverse order from which they were connected" (so, on this course's reading, both are running) | Have the car inspected at a Toyota dealer as soon as possible |
| Nissan | Handbook, 2024 LEAF, US | The LEAF left in its ready-to-drive state: "carefully disconnect the negative cable and then the positive cable" | Keep it in READY "over 20 minutes to charge the 12-volt battery" |
| HSE | Workplace leaflet, Great Britain | "Stop the engine of the good vehicle and turn off the ignition. Leave the engine of the other vehicle running" | Keep the metal parts of the leads apart until both are off |
| The AA | Motoring organisation that sells breakdown cover, UK | "Turn off both cars' engines and carefully disconnect the leads", after about 10 minutes at a fast idle | Its safety list: "Don't remove the jump leads while the car engines are running. This can cause serious damage to the cars' electronics" |

Sources: [1][3][4][5][6].

On the engines, the AA's page is consistent with itself; it's the other sources it does not match.
Ford's and Toyota's steps never switch either engine off before the leads come off, which this
course reads as both running; Nissan keeps the LEAF in READY; and HSE says outright to leave the
dead car's engine running, and explains why: "the battery is still flat, so you will need to drive
several miles before it has enough energy to restart the engine."[6] The AA, which sells breakdown
cover, switches both off and then restarts, and it gives a reason for its rule: removing the leads
with the engines running "can cause serious damage to the cars' electronics".[3]

Each source gives its own reason and none of them answers the other, and this course won't invent a
reconciliation. What is established is narrower: the leads come off in reverse order, **the handbook
for the car you are rescuing governs** whether its engine runs, and the donor's handbook governs the
donor. If your handbook is silent, this course's reading is that it's a question for the maker's
dealer, not a gap to fill with whichever rule you heard last.

:::checkpoint Back to Portree. Her handbook reads like Toyota's. Once her engine has started, which clamp comes off first, and is her engine running?

The last one on comes off first: the clamp on the metal point on her engine, then the neighbour's negative, then the neighbour's positive, then her own positive. Toyota's steps don't switch her engine off before step 7 ("Once the vehicle's engine has started, remove the jumper cables in the exact reverse order"), so on this course's reading her engine is running while the leads come off. If her handbook said what the AA's page says, both engines would be off; hers is the one that counts.
:::

## After it starts

A jump starts the engine. It doesn't leave the battery charged: HSE says that afterwards "the
battery is still flat",[6] and lesson 5 explained why short drives don't fill it either. The advice
after a jump is the same shape everywhere, with each source's own numbers: the AA, which sells
breakdown cover, says "Charge the battery by driving normally (not in stop-start traffic) for at least 30
minutes";[3] Nissan says keep the LEAF in READY for over 20 minutes; Toyota says have the car
inspected at a dealer as soon as possible.[1][4] If the car will not start again, the AA's advice is
that "there's probably a more serious problem requiring professional help".[3] Nissan recommends a
visit to a certified LEAF dealer after a jump "to have the 12V battery tested".[1] A battery that
needs a second jump soon after the first is one to have tested too, and lesson 5's signs are the
ones to watch.

## Electric cars and hybrids

Lesson 4, "Hybrids and electric cars: how they work", set up the two batteries. For a jump start,
that has four consequences.

:::predict In Grand Rapids, a petrol car's battery is flat on a winter morning. The only neighbour at home drives a battery-electric car and offers it as the donor. The battery on the petrol car looks fine and is not frozen. Before reading on: what should the petrol car's owner say, and what are her other routes?

No to that car as the donor. The electric car's own handbook is the authority for it, and the one this course read, Nissan's for the LEAF, says it "cannot be used as a booster vehicle"; the AA, which sells breakdown cover, advises avoiding a hybrid or electric donor "as this could cause damage". Her other routes are another petrol or diesel donor with a 12 V battery, a booster pack (a portable battery with its own leads) used by its maker's instructions (Ford's handbook says to follow them; the AA's page gives its own booster steps and says to follow the car's handbook where they differ), or a call-out, which is the AA's own preference. Had the cars been the other way round, with her electric car flat and the neighbour's petrol car offered, Nissan says a petrol car can jump the LEAF's 12 V battery, by the LEAF handbook's sequence.
:::

**The small battery can be jumped; the big one cannot.** [NHTSA](https://www.nhtsa.gov/vehicle-safety/electric-and-hybrid-vehicles), the US road-safety regulator: "The
high-voltage battery found in EVs and HEVs cannot be jumped." It adds: "However, in most cases, the
12-volt battery, which powers things like lighting and instrumentation, can be jumped".[8]

**A jump doesn't fill the big battery.** Nissan: "Jump starting does not charge the Li-ion battery.
The Li-ion battery must be charged before the vehicle can be driven."[1] It also warns: "Do not
attempt to perform a jump start on the 12-volt battery at the same time that the Li-ion battery is
being charged."[1] Lesson 4's DC/DC converter is why the order matters on a LEAF too: Nissan warns
that not following its steps "could result in damage to the DC/DC converter and cause personal
injury".[1]

**An electric car shouldn't be the donor.** Nissan: "LEAF cannot be used as a booster vehicle because it
cannot supply enough power to start a gasoline engine." It goes on: "However, a gasoline engine
vehicle can be used to jump start LEAF's 12-volt battery."[1] The AA, which sells breakdown cover,
of the donor car in general:
"avoid a hybrid or electric car as this could cause damage".[3] Nissan's statement is about the
LEAF, and the AA's is general advice; this course read no maker that says the reverse.

**Nothing on the high-voltage side is touched.** The jump points on these cars are 12 V points the
handbook shows, and every clamp stays on them. Anything orange, anything blue on a 48 V mild hybrid
(which may use blue for its wiring), or anything labelled high voltage, is **Call someone**. Lesson 4
gave the MOT manual's caveat that some imported cars may use another colour, so the handbook's labels
and pictures are what tell you. Lesson 14, "High voltage: hybrid and EV safety,
charging at home, fire and flood", is about why.

### Push-starting

A push start isn't a jump start, but three handbooks say which cars can't have one. Ford: "Do not
attempt to push-start an automatic transmission vehicle." Toyota, for the 2023 Corolla: "The engine
cannot be started by push-starting." Nissan: "An EV cannot be push-started or tow-started."[1][4][5]
This course doesn't teach push-starting for any car, as its own scope decision: it means people
pushing a car on a road, which the placement rules above exist to avoid.

## The conditions, in one place

A jump start is **Yours, on conditions**. On the sources read, and this course's reading of where,
every condition has to hold:

- **The handbook allows it and gives its method**, and you follow that method's order and points.
- **The battery is fit to be jumped:** not frozen (Nissan: "It could explode"), not damaged or
  leaking (the AA, which sells breakdown cover).[1][3]
- **The same voltage:** Ford's "same nominal voltage", HSE's "do not proceed" if unsure, and
  Nissan's "The booster battery must be rated at 12 volt."[1][5][6]
- **The leads are fit:** Ford's "adequately sized cables with insulated clamps", the AA's "Don't use
  jump leads that are damaged" and "Stop using the jump leads if they get hot".[3][5]
- **The donor is not an electric car or a hybrid** (Nissan for the LEAF; the AA for both), unless its
  own handbook says it can be one.
- **The place is off the road**, and never a motorway, hard shoulder or emergency area (this
  course's reading of Rule 277).
- **No flame, no smoking, no jewellery, nobody leaning over a battery**, with eye protection as
  Nissan asks.

If any fails, it's **Call someone**. A booster pack changes the donor, not the conditions: Ford's
handbook says to follow the pack maker's own instructions,[5] the AA's page gives its own steps and
defers to the car's handbook,[3] and this course recommends no pack.

Battery acid on skin or in an eye is first aid, not car care. First Aid and CPR lesson 10, "Burns,
cuts and small injuries", teaches chemical burns and a splash in the eye.

## What people get wrong

**"The last clamp goes on the flat battery's negative terminal."** Every source read puts it on
metal away from the battery; Nissan says "not to the 12-volt battery".

**"An electric car can jump another car."** Nissan says the LEAF can't, and the AA, which sells
breakdown cover, advises against using a hybrid or electric donor. On the sources read, it can be
jumped but shouldn't be the donor.

**"A jump start charges an electric car."** It wakes the 12 V system. The big battery still has to
be charged before the car can be driven.

**"Any car can be push-started."** Not an automatic, on Ford's handbook; not the 2023 Corolla, on
Toyota's; not an EV, on Nissan's.

**"Switch the headlights on while you take the leads off, to protect the electrics."** Ford says the
opposite, for its car: the peak voltage could blow the bulbs.

**"There's one right order for the leads."** The sources agree on positive first and a last clamp
away from the battery, and differ on the rest. The handbook for the car is the order.

## Practice

:::exercise Your car's jump page
Take 20 minutes over this. It's done on paper, with your own car's handbook (or the maker's online handbook if the paper one is lost), for a car that's yours or whose keeper agrees. Don't connect leads or a booster pack for this exercise, and don't touch the battery, its terminals or anything near it.

1. Find the jump-start section in the handbook. Write down its page or section title, and whether it allows a jump start at all.

2. Write the connection order in the handbook's own words, numbered as it numbers them. Mark where the last clamp goes: a terminal, a jump post, or a point on the engine or body. If the handbook gives a drawing, copy it roughly and mark the last point.

3. Write down what the handbook says about taking the leads off: the order, whether either engine runs, and anything it says not to do (headlights, for instance).

4. List every condition it gives: voltage, frozen or damaged batteries, eye protection, jewellery, anything about the donor.

5. If your car is a hybrid or electric car, write down what the handbook says about acting as a donor and about the main battery after a jump.

6. Optional, and by looking only: if the handbook shows the jump points under the bonnet, and you want to see them, park on level ground, apply the parking brake, select P (or leave it in gear), switch off in the way the handbook describes, take the key or fob away from the car, and let the engine cool. For a hybrid or electric car, remember lesson 4: an engine can restart and a fan can start without warning, so switch off fully and keep your hands out. Open the bonnet only by the handbook's method, and look from where you stand. Touch nothing. Don't reach past or go near anything orange, anything blue on a 48 V mild hybrid, or anything labelled high voltage; on some imported cars the colour may differ, so go by the handbook's labels.
:::

:::checkpoint Check yourself on steps 2 and 4. Ford's handbook sends the last clamp "to a ground connection point if available". Suppose your own handbook says the same, but its drawing shows no ground point. Where do you look next, and what would make you stop and call instead?

Look back at your handbook's own words first: Ford's gives an alternative, "an exposed metal part of the disabled vehicle's engine", away from the battery and the fuel system, and yours may too. If your handbook names no point at all, or the drawing and the words don't match your car, you don't have the handbook's method, and that's the first condition failing: call someone rather than guess. The same goes if the battery looks cracked, swollen or leaking, if you aren't sure both cars are 12 V, or if the only donor is an electric car or a hybrid.
:::

whether it can be a donor and what the main battery needs after a jump. Lesson 16 adds a breakdown
