When a car stops on a road, the people around it are what's at risk, and much of the Highway Code's
breakdown advice is about where they go, not what happens to the car.[1] (The Highway Code covers
England, Scotland and Wales.) In the US, the AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety, AAA's research charity,
reports from the crash data of NHTSA, the US federal road safety regulator, that "more than
2,100 people were killed outside of disabled vehicles at the roadside in 2019-2023". Its count takes in
roadside rescue and emergency workers, people who stopped to help, and "motorists who exited their own
disabled vehicles".[2] The decisions that matter come early: where the car comes to rest, where the
people go, and who gets called.

:::callout Before the car lets you down
This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. If your car fails on a motorway or other fast road in Great Britain, leave at the next exit or services if you can; if you can't, go left into an emergency area or onto the hard shoulder, switch on your hazard lights, get everyone out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and don't go back to the car. If you're stuck in a live lane or can't get out safely, stay in with seat belts and hazard lights on and call 999 and ask for the police. In the US, pull as far onto the right shoulder as you can, switch on your hazard lights and stay in with your seat belt on, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind, in which case get out on the side away from traffic; call 911 in an emergency. Never put any part of your body under a car held up only by a jack, and never touch the orange high-voltage cables on a hybrid or electric car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

The callout carries the fast-road steps, and lesson 17, "Motorways and fast roads: go left, the live
lane, and the smart motorway question", teaches them in full. This lesson is every other road, and
what to do at an incident on any road. Where the two countries' advice differs it sits in a table,
and you follow the advice for the road you're on. Several sections below state law. This is general
information, not legal advice. The rules differ between England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland
and each US state, and they change.

The lesson uses UK road words, each with the US one: carriageway (roadway, the part traffic drives
on), single carriageway (an undivided road), dual carriageway (a divided highway), lay-by (a pull-off
beside the road), verge (the grass edge), hard shoulder (shoulder), boot (trunk), sidelights (parking
lights) and level crossing (railroad crossing). An A-road is a main road with an A number. Distances
are in metres with feet, as the Highway Code gives them, and in feet where a US source does.

## Where to stop: a place of relative safety

The Highway Code's [Rule 275](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/the-highway-code/breakdowns-and-incidents-274-to-287)
starts before the car has stopped. "If you need to stop your vehicle in the event of a breakdown or
incident, try to stop in a place of relative safety. A place of relative safety is where you, your
passengers and your vehicle are less likely to be at risk from moving traffic."[1] Then it ranks them:

- "The safest place to stop is a location which is designed for parking." (A car park, for instance.)
- "On motorways and other high-speed roads, the safest place to stop is a service area."
- "Other places of relative safety include" lay-bys, emergency areas and hard shoulders, and "Be aware
  that hard shoulders provide less protection than other places of relative safety because they are so
  close to high-speed traffic."[1]

"Relative" is the word to hold on to: each of these is less likely to be at risk from moving traffic,
not free of it. On an ordinary road the choice is usually between a lay-by, a car park, a side road or a
wide verge a little further on, and a stop where the car died. Choosing is **Yours**, and the next
sections are about making it well.

:::predict Rule 275 tells you to keep well away from moving traffic, which is obvious. It also tells you to keep well away from your own car. Before reading on: why would standing next to your own stopped car be dangerous, if you're off the road?

Because the car is the thing that gets hit. Rule 275: "You and your passengers should, where possible, keep well away from your vehicle and moving traffic. Otherwise moving traffic could collide with your vehicle, forcing it into you and your passengers." A person standing beside, behind or in front of a stopped car is inside the space it will move into if something runs into it.
:::

That's the mechanism behind most of this lesson: a stopped car can become a moving one without its
driver, so where people stand matters as much as where the car is.

**What causes the stops.** National Highways, the government-owned company that runs England's
motorways and major A roads, says its traffic officers deal with "more than 85,000 breakdowns" a year,
and "Over 40 per cent of these are caused by vehicles running out of fuel, poor tyre maintenance, power
loss and engine trouble."[3] Its advice for the first of those is "Always keep your tank at least one
quarter full to avoid running out of fuel."[3] The next section has what it says about the law.
The other three causes are the checks in lessons 7 to 11.

## Rule 276 on an ordinary road, and which of it is law

This is general information, not legal advice.

Lesson 1, "What a car is made of, and why the handbook comes first", met the Highway Code's convention;
here it decides a whole rule. The Code's Introduction: legal requirements are "identified by the use of
the words 'MUST/MUST NOT'", with a reference to the law behind them, and the other rules are advice,
which "may be used in evidence in any court proceedings under the Traffic Acts".[4] That includes "rules
which use advisory wording such as 'should/should not' or 'do/do not'".[4] Rule 276 is the
breakdown rule for every road. It opens "If your vehicle breaks down, think first of all other road
users and", and none of its seven lines uses MUST:[1]

| Rule 276 says | Law or advice |
|---|---|
| "get your vehicle off the road if possible" | Advice |
| "warn other traffic by using your hazard warning lights if your vehicle is causing an obstruction" | Advice |
| "help other road users see you by wearing light-coloured or fluorescent clothing in daylight and reflective clothing at night or in poor visibility" | Advice |
| "put a warning triangle on the road at least 45 metres (147 feet) behind your broken-down vehicle on the same side of the road, or use other permitted warning devices if you have them. Always take great care when placing or retrieving them, but never use them on motorways" | Advice |
| "if possible, keep your sidelights on if it is dark or visibility is poor" | Advice |
| "do not stand (or let anybody else stand) between your vehicle and oncoming traffic" | Advice |
| "at night or in poor visibility do not stand where you will prevent other road users seeing your lights" | Advice |

So the 45 metres and the hazard lights when stopped are advice, not legal duties, and the Code can
still be used in evidence about how you behaved. This course did not read whether the law requires a car
in Great Britain to carry a triangle at all, and says nothing about it either way. Northern Ireland has
its own Highway Code, on nidirect. Its breakdown rules are close to these but not the same, this
lesson doesn't teach them, and a reader there follows that Code; 999 is the emergency number there too.

**Hazard lights.** There's a MUST nearby, in Rule 116, and it runs the other way. "These may be used
when your vehicle is stationary, to warn that it is temporarily obstructing traffic. Never use them as
an excuse for dangerous or illegal parking. You MUST NOT use hazard warning lights while driving or
being towed unless you are on a motorway or unrestricted dual carriageway and you need to warn drivers
behind you of a hazard or obstruction ahead."[5] The rule cites regulation 27 of the Road Vehicles
Lighting Regulations 1989. So when you're stopped and in the way, hazards are advised; when you're
moving, they're against the law except in that one case.

**The triangle.** Placing one is **Yours, on conditions**. The Code's conditions are an ordinary road,
never a motorway, and "great care" in placing it. This course reads "great care" as a real condition,
not a formality: carrying a triangle 45 metres back means walking along the road towards the traffic
you're warning, and if there's no verge or pavement to walk on, the course's reading is that you leave
it in the boot. No source read tells you to put it out at any cost.

**Obstructions.** If anything falls from a vehicle, Rule 280 separates the roads. On a motorway or
other high-speed road, "DO NOT remove the obstruction yourself": stop in a place of relative safety and
call 999. "On other roads, you should only remove obstructions if it is safe to do so."[1] So on an
ordinary road, moving it is **Yours, on conditions**: not a high-speed road, and only if it's safe.

**Running out of fuel.** National Highways adds a line about the law: "You can be issued a Fixed
Penalty Notice in some locations if your breakdown was foreseeable, which includes running out of
fuel."[3] That's National Highways' statement, with its own "in some locations"; this course did not
read the law behind it and says no more about it.

### An overheating car near Berwick-upon-Tweed

Now a case worked through. It's dusk on a single-carriageway A-road near Berwick-upon-Tweed. A driver
and her teenage son are twenty minutes from home when the temperature warning light comes on, and a
sign for a picnic-site car park appears a few hundred metres ahead.

**Step 1: stop.** Lesson 11, "Dashboard warning lights", put the coolant temperature light in its
stop-now group: Ford's handbook and the RAC (which sells breakdown cover and repairs) both say stop. She has within sight the kind of place Rule
275 calls safest, one "designed for parking", so she pulls into the car park, well clear of the road,
and stops. With no car park in sight, the light's stop-now meaning and Rule 275's "try to stop in a place of
relative safety" would have pulled the same way: on this course's reading, the nearest place off the
carriageway she could reach.

**Step 2: lights.** Rule 276 advises hazard lights "if your vehicle is causing an obstruction". In a car
park, clear of the road, it is not, and the Code does not ask for them. It's dusk, and Rule 276's
sidelights are for a car other road users need to see. In a car park that means anyone driving in, so
on this course's reading they're worth having on, "if possible".

**Step 3: the triangle.** Rule 276 puts the triangle "on the road" behind the car, and says nothing
about a car that is off the road altogether, in a car park. On this course's reading, the triangle does
little here, and walking 45 metres back along an A-road at dusk has a cost of its own. It stays in the
boot.

**Step 4: people.** Both get out and stand well away from the car and from the road, as Rule 275
advises. In a car park that also means away from where other cars drive in.

**Step 5: the engine.** She switches the engine off. Ford's handbook for the Maverick, in lesson 11,
says it for that car's temperature light ("safely stop your vehicle and switch your vehicle off"), and
her own handbook governs hers. Lesson 9, "Under the bonnet: oil, coolant, brake fluid and
screenwash", covered the rest: the coolant cap stays shut until the engine "has cooled naturally", in Annex 6's words, and a
hot cap is never opened.

**Step 6: the call.** Nobody is hurt, nothing is on fire and the car is off the road, so on this
course's reading this is a call to a breakdown service, not to 999. What she tells them is in "The
call, and where you are", below.

:::checkpoint Four stops, all in Great Britain. For each, say whether Rule 276's triangle applies, and one thing about where people stand. (a) A car dies on a straight rural road in daylight, half on the verge, with a wide grass bank. (b) A car stops on a motorway hard shoulder. (c) A car pulls fully into a supermarket car park with a warning light on. (d) A car stops at night on a narrow lane with high hedges and no verge, just past a bend.

(a) The triangle is advised, 45 metres back on the same side, placed with great care; everyone on the bank, well away from the car, not between it and oncoming traffic.

(b) Never: Rule 276 says never on motorways, and Rule 277 says "DO NOT place a warning triangle on the carriageway". People out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier, as the callout says, and lesson 17 covers it.

(c) Off the road in the kind of place Rule 275 calls safest, so there's no road traffic to warn and, on this course's reading, no triangle to put out, as in the case near Berwick-upon-Tweed; stand away from the cars moving in the car park.

(d) The hard one. The Code still advises a triangle, but walking back round a blind bend with nowhere to step off is exactly what "great care" rules out, on this course's reading; sidelights on, hazards on because the car is obstructing, and nobody standing where they'd hide the lights. Everyone gets off the road if there's any gap or gateway, and nobody stands between the car and traffic coming round the bend (Rule 276). If there's nowhere off the road to stand, that's the reason, on this course's reading, to call for help at once.
:::

## In the US: stopping, warning devices and Move Over

This is general information, not legal advice.

**Stopping.** AAA Club Alliance (AAA clubs are US motoring clubs that sell roadside assistance and run
a repair network), in an interview with its fleet manager, gives its advice for "a highway or surface
street": "pull over as far to the right as you're able and turn on your hazard lights."[6] Then stay in,
unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind; in those two cases the callout
above says to get out on the side away from traffic, and that holds on every US road. AAA's reason for
staying in otherwise: "Vehicles have airbags and side curtain bags. That along with your seat belt will protect you
from serious injury if your disabled vehicle is struck by another vehicle". If you have to get out,
having pulled onto the right shoulder, "exit the vehicle from the passenger side and wait beyond the
protective guard rail, if one exists. A good rule of thumb is to remain at least 40 feet from your
disabled vehicle."[6] (40 feet is about 12 metres, on this course's conversion.) The California Driver
Handbook lists the same two exceptions, a shoulder without enough space and a guardrail, among the
situations in its freeway steps where it's safer to get out and stay away, and lesson 17 teaches them
in full.

**Warning devices.** The federal rules on carrying and placing warning devices are those of FMCSA (the
Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration, which regulates trucks and buses), and they're for
commercial motor vehicles only. Under its general rule, 49 CFR 392.22 has a driver of a stopped truck or bus place them "as soon as
possible, but in any event within 10 minutes": one about 10 feet (3 metres) and one about 100 feet
(30 metres) back towards the approaching traffic, and one about 100 feet the other way. 49 CFR
393.95(f) requires "Three bidirectional emergency reflective triangles" or "At least 6 fusees" (road
flares).[7] The rule has other placements for divided roads, curves and hills. They
don't apply to a private car, and this course found no federal rule for one and read no state's rule
on it. So a US private driver's question about triangles goes to their own state. AAA Club
Alliance's kit list includes "flares, orange triangles" among other things,[6] and that is advice from
a club that sells roadside assistance.

**Move Over.** This is the US law aimed at the drivers going past. NHTSA: "For the safety of everyone on and off the road, all 50 states have Move Over laws", and
"All states require drivers to move over and slow down for emergency vehicles with flashing lights."[8]
Some states go further, as California did from 1 January 2026. Its Vehicle Code § 21809 now covers
"any other stationary vehicle displaying flashing hazard lights or another warning device, including,
but not limited to, cones, flares, or retroreflective devices": passing drivers must change lanes "if
practicable" or "slow to a reasonable and prudent speed", and "A violation of subdivision (a) is an
infraction, punishable by a fine of not more than fifty dollars ($50)."[9] It does not apply where the
stopped vehicle "is not adjacent to the highway or is separated from the highway by a protective
physical barrier."[9] So in California, a stopped driver's hazard lights or triangle now put a legal
duty on the traffic coming up behind.

:::checkpoint In 2026, a driver in California comes up behind a car stopped on the shoulder of a four-lane divided highway with its hazard lights on. What does Vehicle Code § 21809 ask of her? And would it apply if the stopped car were in a parking lot set well back from the highway?

Change lanes if practicable, or slow to a reasonable and prudent speed; breaking that is an infraction with a fine of up to $50. A stopped car showing hazards is what the 2026 amendment brought in. The parking lot is outside it: the section doesn't apply where the stopped vehicle "is not adjacent to the highway".
:::

In Great Britain, the nearest rule this course read is Rule 281, for drivers approaching an incident:
"You should slow down and be prepared to move safely into another lane or stop", which is advice, and
"You MUST follow any directions given by police or traffic officers as to whether you can safely pass
the incident or obstruction."[1]

| On an ordinary road | In Great Britain (Highway Code) | In the US (AAA; federal and California law; the callout) |
|---|---|---|
| Where the people go | Well away from the car and the traffic; not between the car and oncoming traffic (advice) | Stay in, belted, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind (the callout); if out, on the side away from traffic, beyond a guard rail, 40 feet away (AAA's advice) |
| Warning triangle | 45 m (147 ft) behind, same side, never on a motorway (advice) | No federal rule for private cars; FMCSA's rule is for commercial vehicles; ask your state |
| Hazard lights | When stopped and obstructing (advice); not while moving, with one exception (MUST NOT) | Pull right and switch them on (AAA Club Alliance's advice); in California they now trigger Move Over |
| Emergency number | 999 | 911 |

No source read gives a reason why the two countries' advice on staying in the car differs, and this
course does not supply one. They agree on a live lane, which is lesson 17's.

## A car stopped on a level crossing

This is general information, not legal advice.

At a level crossing the danger is a train, not other cars, and the steps change. Each country's are
its own, and no source read gives a reason for the differences.

**In Great Britain.** The Highway Code's Rule 299 covers a breakdown or incident on a crossing. It says
"should" and has no MUST, so it's advice:[14]

- "get everyone out of the vehicle and clear of the crossing immediately"
- "use a railway telephone if available to tell the signal operator. Follow the instructions you are
  given"
- "move the vehicle clear of the crossing if there is time before a train arrives. If the alarm sounds,
  or the amber light comes on, leave the vehicle and get clear of the crossing immediately."

**In California.** The state's official driver handbook has a section headed "Disabled Vehicles on
Railroad Tracks", with two cases.[11] "If your vehicle stalls or stops while blocking part of a train
track with a train approaching and warning lights flashing": "Immediately exit your vehicle. Run away
from the tracks diagonally in the direction the train is coming from. Then call 911." And "If you do
not see a train approaching and warning lights are not flashing": "Exit your vehicle. Dial the number
located on the railroad crossing posts or metal control box near the tracks. Provide the crossing
number (if posted). Tell them a vehicle is on the tracks. Then call 911."[11]

They agree on the first thing: everyone out. After that, follow the steps for the country you're in.

### A stalled car on a level crossing near Modesto, California

Say your car stalls on a crossing near Modesto. There's no train in sight and the lights aren't
flashing. By the handbook's second case, you get out, find the number on the crossing post or the
metal box by the tracks, call it with the crossing number if one is posted, tell them a vehicle is on
the tracks, and then call 911. Now say the lights start flashing while you're still in the car. The
first case takes over: get out at once, go away from the tracks diagonally in the direction the train
is coming from, and call 911 from there. That is California's advice for California's crossings. The
handbook does not say why diagonally towards the train, and this course does not add a reason.

## The call, and where you are

**Which number.** In the US, 911.gov, the federal 911 programme's site: "An emergency is any situation
that requires immediate assistance from the police, fire department or ambulance."[10] In Great
Britain, Rule 283 says to "contact the emergency services on 999" when you're involved in an incident or
stop to help, and Rule 277 gives 999 for a car on a motorway whose people cannot get out of it safely,
as does Rule 279 for a disabled driver who cannot follow those steps.[1] On this course's reading, a
car that has simply stopped somewhere safe, with nobody hurt, is a call to a breakdown service. In the UK the AA, the RAC and Green Flag are breakdown providers that sell cover and
repairs; in the US, AAA clubs sell roadside assistance. None is recommended here. Some roads have public
services too, National Highways' traffic officers in England and the California Highway Patrol's
Freeway Service Patrol, which "provides free emergency roadside services in certain areas" at commute
times;[11] lesson 17 has both.

**Where you are.** An operator who can't find you can't send anything. The Highway Code's table, in
Rule 277, is headed "Communicating your location. How to identify your location to the emergency
services", and Rule 283 points to it for a motorway or other high-speed road:[1]

| Method | What to do |
|---|---|
| eCall | "Press the SOS button if your vehicle has one." |
| App | "Use a mobile telephone mapping application." |
| Marker post or driver location sign | "Quote the numbers and letters on marker posts or driver location signs which are located along the edge of the road." |

eCall is the SOS button some cars have, which Rule 283 also names.

On a smaller road there may be no posts. First Aid and CPR lesson 2, "Danger, the call, and recognising
cardiac arrest", gave the fallback for a place you don't know the name of: say what you can see, such as
a shop name, a road you came in on or a landmark. For a car, this course's reading adds the road number,
the direction you were heading and the last place you passed.

For the driver near Berwick-upon-Tweed, calling a breakdown service, that means the A-road's number,
which way she was heading, the picnic-site car park and the last place she passed; then the car's make
and model, that it isn't electric, and that the temperature light came on.

In the US, 911.gov lists what the call-taker may ask: "The location of the emergency, including the
street address", "The phone number you are calling from" and "The nature of the emergency". It adds:
"Be prepared to follow any instructions the call-taker gives you", and "Do not hang up until the
call-taker instructs you to do so."[10]

For anyone who can't make a voice call, Rule 279 says, as advice: "If you are deaf, hard of hearing or
speech impaired, it is recommended that you register for the 999 text service (emergencySMS.net) before
making a journey."[1] This course did not read an official page on texting 911, and says nothing about it.

A driver on their own at the roadside may also be thinking about the people who stop. Threat from
other people is Personal Safety's, in lesson 6, "Out and away: streets, travel and meeting people",
which is about being out in general rather than breakdowns.

## The recovery truck

Two things to tell a recovery service beyond where you are: the make and model, and whether it's
electric or hybrid. Lesson 14, "High voltage: hybrid and EV safety, charging at home, fire and flood",
said to tell them, and it changes the truck that comes.

The two makers read say their own electric cars mustn't be towed the ordinary way, and Tesla gives the
reason for its car: "The motor(s) in Model 3 generates power when the wheels spin."[13] Nissan, in its 2024 LEAF handbook for the US: "NISSAN recommends that your vehicle be
towed with the driving (front) wheels off the ground or that the vehicle be placed on a flatbed truck as
illustrated", and "Never tow with the front wheels on the ground or four wheels on the ground (forward
or backward), as this may cause serious and expensive damage to the motor."[12] Tesla's Model 3 manual,
as this course read it in an October 2024 archive copy: "Always transport Model 3 with all four tires
off the ground."[13] Those are two makers' instructions for their own cars. This course's reading is
that telling the service what the car is lets it bring the right equipment and follow that maker's
handbook. Towing an electric car is **Call someone**: Nissan's advice is to have "a service operator tow
your vehicle", and it adds: "Never ride in a
vehicle that is being towed."[12]

One more thing when the truck arrives, from the fleet manager of AAA Club Alliance, whose clubs sell
roadside assistance, for the US: "A common mistake we see is drivers placing the vehicle into neutral
to try and help the tow operator. This is highly dangerous because the vehicle isn't secured and can
move at any time".[6] No UK source read says it, but AAA's reason, that the car isn't secured and can
move, holds for any car, so this course's reading is to leave it as it is until the operator says what
they want.

:::checkpoint A driver's small electric hatchback has stopped with a fault on a busy urban road in England, and she's waiting in a side street for the recovery service. A US page she found while waiting says to stay in the car, belted, until the tow operator comes to you. What should she have told the service on the phone, where should she wait, and what does she leave alone when the truck arrives?

Where she is, in a form they can find (the road, the side street, a landmark); the make and model; and that the car is electric.

Where to wait: the US page's stay-in advice is US advice for a car at the roadside. She's in England, where Rule 275's advice is to keep well away from the car and the traffic, so she waits in the side street, away from the car.

Being electric matters because both handbooks read say not to tow their electric car with its driving wheels on the road: Nissan says flatbed or driving wheels off the ground for the LEAF, and Tesla all four wheels off the ground for the Model 3, because its motor generates power when the wheels spin. Her own maker's handbook has the instruction for her car, and it's worth reading the towing section before she ever needs it. When the truck comes, she leaves the car as it is until the operator says (this course's reading, from the US advice of AAA Club Alliance, which sells roadside assistance, that putting it in neutral is "A common mistake").
:::

## After a collision: making the scene safe

This is general information, not legal advice.

Rule 283 is the Highway Code's list for anyone "involved in an incident or collision or stop to give
assistance", and its vehicle-side steps are this course's to teach:[1]

- "if possible, stop in a place of relative safety (see Rule 275)"
- "use your hazard warning lights to warn other traffic"
- "put on high-visibility clothing if you have it"
- "ask drivers to switch off their engines"
- "ask drivers and passengers to stop smoking"
- "contact the emergency services on 999 and provide full details of the incident location and any
  casualties"
- "move uninjured people away from the vehicles to a place of relative safety (see Rule 275)"
- "stay at the scene until the emergency services arrive"
- "be prepared to exchange details (see Rule 286)."

Every line is advice. The same list says "DO NOT move injured people from their vehicles unless they are
in immediate danger". The Introduction marks a legal requirement with MUST or MUST NOT and a reference to
the law behind it, and calls "do/do not" advisory wording. Rule 283 has neither, so its capitalised "DO
NOT" is advice, not a MUST NOT.[1][4] First Aid and CPR lesson 11 starts from the same place and names
its own exceptions, for danger, the airway and CPR, and the casualty is First Aid's, not this course's. Rule 283 itself says "be prepared to give first aid".[1] That course has
it: lesson 2, "Danger, the call, and recognising cardiac arrest", for the danger step and the call;
lesson 7, "Breathing but not awake", for someone unresponsive who is breathing; lesson 9, "Severe
bleeding and shock"; lesson 10, "Burns, cuts and small injuries"; and lesson 11, "Bones, joints, head
and spine", for keeping an injured person still.

**What the law requires after a collision.** Rule 286 is a MUST. If you're in a collision "which causes
damage or injury to any other person, vehicle, animal or property, you MUST" stop, give your name and
address, the owner's, and the registration number "to anyone having reasonable grounds for requiring
them", and if you don't give them at the time, "report it to the police as soon as reasonably
practicable, and in any case within 24 hours."[1] The rule cites section 170 of the Road Traffic Act
1988. Insurance is Personal Finance Fundamentals lesson 8's, "Handing off what you cannot absorb".

**Dangerous goods.** Rule 284 says vehicles carrying dangerous goods in packages "will be marked with plain
orange reflective plates", and road tankers carry hazard warning plates. Rule 285, as advice: "switch off
engines and DO NOT SMOKE"; "keep well away from the vehicle and do not be tempted to try to rescue
casualties as you yourself could become one"; and "call the emergency services and give as much
information as possible about the labels and markings on the vehicle. DO NOT use a mobile phone close to
a vehicle carrying flammable loads."[1]

### A collision on the A66

Now one to work yourself before you open the answer. On the A66 on a wet afternoon, a van and a car have collided a few hundred metres
ahead of you. You stop in a lay-by. From there you can see the van has plain orange plates, something is
running from it onto the road, and one person is sitting on the verge beside the car holding an arm.

:::predict Before opening this: where do you stand, where do you make the call from, what do you tell the operator, and what do you do about the person on the verge?

You're already stopped somewhere Rule 275 would call relatively safe; hazards on, and high-visibility clothing if you have it. Orange plates mean dangerous goods, so Rule 285 takes over from Rule 283's ordinary list: keep well away from the van, engines off and no smoking (ask others too), and don't go close to rescue anyone ("do not be tempted to try to rescue casualties as you yourself could become one"). Make the call from where you are, well back, because the Code says "DO NOT use a mobile phone close to a vehicle carrying flammable loads". Tell the 999 operator the road, the direction, the nearest place you passed (and the code on a driver location sign or marker post if you can read one from where you are, or your place from a mapping app: Rule 277's table, which Rule 283 points to on high-speed roads), that a vehicle with orange plates is involved and leaking, what you can see of its labels and markings from where you stand, and that one person is hurt. The person on the verge is First Aid's, and here the first thing First Aid lesson 2 asks is whether it's safe to approach at all; with a leaking dangerous-goods van beside them, the operator's instructions come first.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"A warning triangle is a legal requirement."** Nothing this course read makes it one for a private
car in either country, and in Great Britain placing one is advice; see "Rule 276 on an ordinary road, and which of it is law" and the US section's "Warning devices", above.

**"A triangle goes behind any stopped car, motorway included."** Rule 276: "never use them on motorways".
Rule 277: "DO NOT place a warning triangle on the carriageway". Both are advice, and both say the same.

**"Hazard lights are fine while you drive slowly."** Not in Great Britain; see Rule 116 under "Rule 276
on an ordinary road, and which of it is law", above.

**"Put it in neutral to help the tow truck."** AAA Club Alliance's fleet manager (the club sells
roadside assistance) calls it "A common mistake": the car is not secured and can move.

**"Get the injured person out of the car."** Rule 283: "DO NOT move injured people from their vehicles
unless they are in immediate danger." First Aid lesson 11 has the rest, including its own exceptions.

## Practice

:::exercise Your breakdown plan for ordinary roads
Take 15 minutes over this, on paper. Don't stand at a roadside or drive for the exercise. If you look in the car, it should be yours or its keeper should agree, parked somewhere off the road, and you only look.

1. Write the numbers for the country you drive in: the emergency number, and your breakdown service's number if you have one, with the policy or membership number. If you have no cover, write whether a free public service covers roads you use (National Highways' traffic officers in England; California's Freeway Service Patrol in parts of California; lesson 17 has both), then how you'd find a recovery service and what you'd ask it.

2. From the handbook, or by looking in the car: is there a warning triangle, and where? A high-visibility vest or light-coloured clothing? A torch? Write where each is kept, and what's missing.

3. Write the three things you'd tell a recovery service about your car: make and model, whether it's electric or hybrid, and anything your handbook says about towing it (the section's title and page).

4. This is general information, not legal advice. If you drive in Great Britain, open the Highway Code's "Breakdowns and incidents" page and list every line in Rules 275 to 287 that says MUST, with its rule number. In a US state, write which agency's page you would check for your state's Move Over law and any rule on warning devices. In Northern Ireland, open its own Highway Code's "Breakdowns and incidents" page on nidirect and do the same.

5. Pick one ordinary road you use often. Write where on it you'd try to stop, how you'd describe your location to an operator, and where everyone would stand.
:::
