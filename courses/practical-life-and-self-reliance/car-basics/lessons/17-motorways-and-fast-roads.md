A car that stops on a motorway is sitting beside traffic at motorway speed. The danger is the
same one lesson 16 described on an ordinary road; what changes is how little time the drivers behind
have to react, and how few places there are to get out of the way. In England there's a second
complication. About a quarter of the motorway network is now "smart", and on parts of it the hard
shoulder has been turned into a lane, part-time or for good, so the places to stop are emergency areas
some distance apart.[4][7] This lesson is what to do when the car fails on a road like that, what's different in the US, and the
argument about whether taking the hard shoulder away was a safe thing to do.

:::callout Before the car lets you down
This course is education, not mechanical training, and not legal advice about your own car. If your car fails on a motorway or other fast road in Great Britain, leave at the next exit or services if you can; if you can't, go left into an emergency area or onto the hard shoulder, switch on your hazard lights, get everyone out on the side away from traffic and behind the barrier if there is one, and don't go back to the car. If you're stuck in a live lane or can't get out safely, stay in with seat belts and hazard lights on and call 999 and ask for the police. In the US, pull as far onto the right shoulder as you can, switch on your hazard lights and stay in with your seat belt on, unless the shoulder is too narrow or there's a guardrail to wait behind, in which case get out on the side away from traffic; call 911 in an emergency. Never put any part of your body under a car held up only by a jack, and never touch the orange high-voltage cables on a hybrid or electric car. The law on a car's condition differs between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

Lesson 16, "When the car stops: where, who stands where, and the call", covered ordinary roads and the call
itself; this one is the fast roads. **Follow the advice for the road you're on.** No source read gives a
reason why British and American advice differs, and this course doesn't supply one.

**Northern Ireland** has its own Highway Code, on nidirect, and its motorway breakdown rule is not the
same as Great Britain's Rule 277.[17] This lesson doesn't teach Northern Ireland's steps, and a reader

Several sections below state law. This is general information, not legal advice. The rules differ

## Four kinds of motorway, and the Red X

This is general information, not legal advice.

National Highways, the government-owned company that runs England's motorways and major A-roads,
describes three kinds of smart motorway in its fourth-year safety report. A **controlled motorway** uses
variable speed limits and keeps "a permanent hard shoulder". A **dynamic hard shoulder** motorway opens
the hard shoulder as a lane "at the busiest times". An **all-lane running** (ALR) motorway works "by
permanently converting the hard shoulder into a live lane".[7] A **conventional motorway** keeps its hard
shoulder and has none of these systems. A "live lane" is a lane with traffic moving in it. As of 2022,
the report says, England's 446 miles (about 718 km) of smart motorway carried "around a third of all
motorway traffic in England".[7]

Where the hard shoulder is a lane, the Highway Code's Rule 270 gives the places to stop: "Emergency areas
are located along motorways with no hard shoulder or where the hard shoulder can be used as an extra lane
(see Rule 269) and MUST only be used in an emergency." And: "They are marked by blue signs with an
orange SOS telephone symbol and may have orange surfacing."[2] National Highways adds that each is "around the same
length as a football pitch" with phones "linked directly to our control rooms", and that the government
committed £390m for "over 150 additional emergency areas", a commitment "met in Spring 2025", leaving
"around 50% more emergency areas across the ALR network, in comparison to January 2022".[6]

The Code's MUSTs here are law, and each cites the regulation behind it:[2]

| Rule | What it says | Law or advice |
|---|---|---|
| 269 | "You MUST NOT use a hard shoulder except in an emergency or if directed to do so by the police, traffic officers or a traffic sign." | Law |
| 270 | Emergency areas "MUST only be used in an emergency" | Law |
| 271 | "You MUST NOT stop on any carriageway, emergency area, hard shoulder, slip road, central reservation or verge except in an emergency, or when told to do so by the police, traffic officers, an emergency sign or by red flashing light signals." | Law |

**The Red X.** Rule 258: "Red flashing light signals and a red ‘X’ on a sign identify a closed lane in
which people, stopped vehicles or other hazards are present." You MUST "follow the instructions on signs
in advance of a closed lane to move safely to an open lane", and you MUST NOT "drive in a closed lane".
The Code says why: "emergency services and traffic authorities use closed lanes to reach incidents and
help people in need", and "Lane and road closures indicated by red flashing lights are enforced by the
police."[2] For a driver who has broken down, the Red X is the other half of the system, as the
live-lane section below shows.

## Go left: Rule 277, step by step

This is general information, not legal advice.

The Highway Code's [Rule 277](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/the-highway-code/breakdowns-and-incidents-274-to-287)
is headed "Go left, Get safe, Get help", and it starts before the car stops: "If your vehicle develops a
problem, leave the carriageway at the next exit or pull into a service area if possible (see Rule 275
for places of relative safety). If you cannot, you should".[1] Then the first list:

- "move into the left lane"
- "pull into an emergency area or onto a hard shoulder if you can"
- "stop as far to the left as possible, leaving space to exit your vehicle and with your wheels turned
  to the left"
- "if you can, stop just beyond an emergency telephone"
- "switch your hazard warning lights on"
- "if it’s dark or visibility is poor, use sidelights."[1]

Almost all of Rule 277 is "should": advice, which the Introduction says "may be used in evidence in any
court proceedings under the Traffic Acts" to establish liability.[3] Its one MUST is about animals, which "MUST be kept in the vehicle or, in an
emergency, under control on the verge".[1] Choosing the exit, the services or the left lane is
**Yours**: no one else is in the driver's seat.

[National Highways' version](https://nationalhighways.co.uk/safety/driving-on-our-roads/driving-on-motorways/)
follows the rule almost word for word, and adds two things. It adds lay-bys to the places to pull into,
and it moves the trigger earlier: "If your vehicle develops a problem, or if you see a red or amber
warning light on your vehicle’s dashboard, leave the carriageway at the next exit or pull into a service
area if possible." And: "Do not stop in a live lane unless absolutely necessary."[4] Lesson 11,
"Dashboard warning lights", sorted the lights by colour; on a motorway, National Highways' advice is to
act on an amber one too.

:::predict Rule 277 says "exit your vehicle by the side furthest from traffic". Why do you think it says that, rather than "by the left-hand door"?

On this course's reading, because the rule is about where the traffic is, not about which door is which. On a British motorway the side furthest from traffic is usually the left, but the Code names the traffic, and that's the thing to check before a door opens. The reason for keeping away from the traffic side at all is Rule 275's, which lesson 16 taught and the live-lane section below uses.
:::

### Power loss in lane 3

Here's the ordinary case, worked through. A driver is in lane 3 of an all-lane-running stretch of
motorway in England, in daylight, when the engine loses power and an amber light comes on. There's no
hard shoulder. A sign gives the distance to the next emergency area, a little way ahead.[5]

**Step 1: can she leave?** The rule's first choice is the next exit or services. If one is closer than
the emergency area and the car will reach it, that's where she goes.

**Step 2: go left.** Across to the left lane, then into the emergency area, as far left as it goes,
wheels turned left, just past the telephone if she can manage it. Hazard lights on.

**Step 3: get out.** If it's safe and possible, everyone gets out on the side furthest from traffic and
waits behind the barrier, well away from the car. Getting out is **Yours, on conditions**: the Code's
own conditions are "if it is safe and possible to do so".[1] Rule 277 also says not to go back to the car,
even in rain, cold or dark, and not to attempt repairs.[1] Any repair on a
motorway, the emergency area included, is **Call someone**.

**Step 4: get help.** She picks up the free emergency telephone, which goes straight to National
Highways' control room, or calls National Highways on 0300 123 5000 from a mobile, facing the traffic
while she speaks, as Rule 277 asks.[4][6][1] Then her breakdown service, if she has one.

**Step 5: leaving.** Rule 278 has a MUST here: "you MUST use the emergency telephone provided and follow
the operator’s advice for exiting the emergency area. A lane may need to be closed so that you can
rejoin the carriageway safely."[1] So even if the fault clears, she doesn't just pull out.

## Get safe, and the live lane

This is general information, not legal advice.

Rule 277's "Get safe" list is about people, not the car. It includes:[1]

- "exit your vehicle by the side furthest from traffic, if it is safe and possible to do so, and ensure
  passengers do the same"
- "put on high-visibility clothing if you have it and it is within easy reach"
- "get behind a safety barrier where there is one, but be aware of any unseen hazards such as sudden
  drops, uneven ground or debris"
- "DO NOT stand in a place where your vehicle could be forced into you if moving traffic collides with
  it"
- "DO NOT return to your vehicle even if it’s raining, cold or dark"
- "remain alert and aware of vehicles or debris coming towards you"
- "keep passengers away from the carriageway and children under control"
- "DO NOT attempt repairs on your vehicle"
- "DO NOT place a warning triangle on the carriageway"

Inside that list is a box for the case where none of it is possible. The Code's words: "If you can’t" /
"get your vehicle to the left lane or a place of relative safety (see Rule 275), and" / "exit your
vehicle safely to get well away from it and moving traffic," / "you should" then "stay in your vehicle",
"keep your seat belts and hazard warning lights on", and "call 999 immediately and ask for the police.
Alternatively, press your SOS button if your vehicle has one and ask for the police."[1]

**If you can't follow these steps.** Rule 279 adds a case: if you have a disability that stops you
following Rules 277 and 278, you should "switch on your hazard warning lights", "stay in your vehicle and
keep your seat belt on" and "call 999 immediately and ask for the police".[1] National Highways widens it
to anyone in the car: "If you or anyone in your vehicle is unable to follow our breakdowns advice for any
reason, stay in your vehicle, keep your seatbelts and hazard warning lights on and call 999
immediately."[4] On this course's reading, a passenger who can't get out and over a barrier is that
case.

**Why the live lane is the worst place.** Lesson 16 gave Rule 275's reason for keeping away from a
stopped car: traffic "could collide with your vehicle, forcing it into you".[1] In a live lane, the
traffic isn't passing beside the car; it's coming straight at it. The 999 call is what starts the
protection. National Highways: "The emergency services can alert us, so we can close motorway lanes and
send other help as required, such as a Traffic Officer."[4] A closed lane is a Red X, which Rule 258
makes law for everyone behind you.[2] Put together, on this course's reading: stay belted in the
car because stepping out puts a person in the lane, and call at once because the call is how the lane
gets closed.

The two countries agree on this case. AAA Club Alliance, whose clubs sell roadside assistance, on its own advice page for
the US: "You should never leave your vehicle if you haven’t pulled over, particularly if there is traffic
moving around it."[16]

### Lane 2, and no gap

Now one with a gap for you. A different driver, on a three-lane motorway in England with no hard shoulder,
is in lane 2 in heavy traffic when the car cuts out and rolls to a stop. Lorries are passing on both
sides. There's no gap to get left.

:::predict Before opening this: the live-lane steps are above. What does she tell the operator about where she is, and when, if ever, should anyone get out of the car?

The box's steps: hazards, belts on, stay in, and 999 for the police (or the SOS button), since nobody can get out safely "to get well away from it and moving traffic". For where she is, Rule 277's table gives the car's SOS button, a phone mapping app, or the numbers and letters on the marker posts and driver location signs along the edge of the road. As for getting out: no source read gives a moment for it. This course's reading is that she stays put until the police or traffic officers who come to the scene tell her what to do, since they can see whether the lane has been closed.
:::

:::checkpoint Three stops on English motorways. For each, stay in or get out? (a) The car coasts into an emergency area, but your passenger uses a wheelchair and can't get out and over the barrier. (b) The car stops on a hard shoulder at night with a small child in the back, and the passengers want to wait in the warm. (c) The car stops in lane 1, half on a strip of verge, with a barrier two metres (about 6.5 feet) away and traffic moving in lane 2.

(a) In. National Highways: "If you or anyone in your vehicle is unable to follow our breakdowns advice for any reason, stay in your vehicle, keep your seatbelts and hazard warning lights on and call 999 immediately." Rule 279 says the same for a driver with a disability.

(b) Out, everyone including the child, on the side furthest from traffic and behind the barrier, if it's safe and possible. Rule 277: "DO NOT return to your vehicle even if it’s raining, cold or dark".

(c) The hard one, and it turns on Rule 277's own test: can everyone get out safely "to get well away from it and moving traffic"? If they can reach the barrier from the side away from traffic, out and behind it. If not, stay in, belted, hazards on, and 999.
:::

## Get help, and rejoining

This is general information, not legal advice.

Rule 277's "Get help" list starts with "use the free emergency telephone to obtain advice and
assistance" and "contact a breakdown recovery service". It asks you to "always face the traffic when
you speak to remain aware of vehicles or debris coming towards you" and to "inform them if you are a
vulnerable motorist such as disabled, older or travelling alone".[1]

**The numbers**, re-read on National Highways' page on 26 September 2026. For England's motorways and
major A-roads, from an emergency area, lay-by or hard shoulder: "call National Highways on 0300 123 5000
to obtain advice and assistance". For anyone with "a hearing, speech or physical impairment": "text us on
0738 028 3600 for roadside assistance". Anyone who can't get out of the car, or hasn't stopped near a free emergency telephone: 999.[4] The
0300 number is National Highways', and it covers England's roads only.

**Rejoining.** Rule 278 splits it. From a hard shoulder, as advice: "build up speed, indicate and watch
for a safe gap in the traffic". From an emergency area, as law, the MUST in the worked example above.[1]
Rejoining from an emergency area is
**Yours, on conditions**, and the condition is that call.

## In the US: the freeway shoulder

This section is the US's, and a UK reader on a UK road doesn't act on it. The US has no single federal
breakdown rule for private cars that this course read. The two sources here are California's official
driver handbook, for California's freeways, and AAA Club Alliance, a US motoring club that sells roadside
assistance.

[California's handbook](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/handbook/california-driver-handbook/safe-driving-cont2/),
"If your vehicle stops working on the freeway":[15]

1. "Safely pull over to the right shoulder."
2. "Exit on the right side so you are away from traffic if you must get out of the vehicle."
3. "Dial 511 from your cell phone or locate call box for assistance.." (the double full stop is the
   handbook's; 511 and call boxes are California's services)
4. "Return to your vehicle as soon as you can. Get back into the vehicle from the right side (away from
   traffic)."
5. "Stay inside your vehicle with your seat belt on until help arrives."
6. "Use your emergency flashers at your discretion. They can help other vehicles see you at night and in
   different weather conditions."

Then the exceptions: "There are certain circumstances where it is safer to get out of your vehicle and
stay away. These include situations where there is:" "Not enough space on the shoulder.", "A guardrail.",
or "An area for you to safely stay away from freeway lanes."[15] And a free public service: "During
commute times, the CHP FSP provides free emergency roadside services in certain areas", the California
Highway Patrol's Freeway Service Patrol.[15]

Read in order, the list's "Return to your vehicle as soon as you can" is for the default case, after a
walk to a call box. Where one of the exceptions applies, the handbook says it is "safer to get out of
your vehicle and stay away", and on this course's reading that governs until help arrives.

Lesson 16 gave AAA's reason for staying in: the car's airbags and the seat belt. And if you can't stay
in, AAA Club Alliance's page says: "If the conditions of your situation make it impossible to stay in your
vehicle (and you've safely pulled over to the right shoulder), exit the vehicle from the passenger side
and wait beyond the protective guard rail, if one exists." Its rule of thumb is "to remain at least 40
feet from your disabled vehicle".[16] (40 feet is about 12 metres, on this course's conversion.) 911 is the emergency number; lesson 16 has what the call-taker needs.

| On a fast road | In Great Britain (Highway Code; National Highways) | In California (DMV handbook) and AAA |
|---|---|---|
| Where to stop | Next exit or services; if not, left lane, then an emergency area or hard shoulder | The right shoulder |
| Default | Out on the side furthest from traffic, behind the barrier; don't go back | In, belted, until help arrives, unless the shoulder is too narrow, there's a guardrail, or a safe area away from the lanes |
| When to do the other | Stay in if you can't reach safety or get out safely, or anyone can't follow the steps | In those cases, get out on the side away from traffic and stay away from the car |
| Hazard lights | Switch them on (advice) | "At your discretion" (California); switch them on (AAA) |
| Numbers | 999; 0300 123 5000 for National Highways (England) | 911; 511 and call boxes (California) |
| A live lane | Stay in, belts and hazards on, 999 | Never leave the car if you haven't pulled over (AAA) |

### A guardrail near Redding

A US case. A driver on Interstate 5 near Redding, California, loses power and pulls onto the right
shoulder. It's narrow, and a metal guardrail runs along its edge, with a grass bank behind.

California's handbook would have her stay in, belted, by default, and this is one of its named
exceptions: "A guardrail." So, on that handbook and on AAA's advice, she gets out on the right side,
away from traffic, and waits beyond the guardrail, well away from the car; AAA's rule of thumb is at
least 40 feet. She doesn't go back to the car to wait: the guardrail is the handbook's reason to stay
away from it. She calls for help, 911 if it's an emergency, and California's 511 or a call box for
assistance.

## The smart motorway question

This is the part of the lesson where informed people disagree, and it's not a question this course
settles. It has an empirical side (are all-lane running motorways as safe as ones with a hard shoulder?)
and a value side (how to weigh fewer collisions of one kind against more of another, and how much weight
drivers' confidence should carry). What follows is what's settled, then each side in its holders' own
words, then a committee of MPs whose conclusions cut across both, then the numbers, then what would
settle it. Logic and Argument lesson 7, "Reconstruction and
charity: the strongest version first", is the rule for reading them.

### What's settled

These are facts, dated, and the words matter. In **January 2022** the Department for Transport **paused**
new all-lane running schemes: "rollout of new all lane running smart motorway schemes will be paused
until 5 years of safety data is available".[8] In **April 2023** it **cancelled** all new smart
motorways, "in recognition of the current lack of public confidence felt by drivers and cost pressures",
and the same release says the cancellation delivered "on the Prime Minister’s summer campaign pledge".[9]
Existing smart motorways stay. National Highways says all the actions from the government's 2020
stocktake (its review of smart motorway safety, which set these actions) are complete, including "ensuring radar stopped vehicle detection (SVD) is in place on every
all lane running (ALR) motorway", and that "Around 25 per cent of the motorway network is now made up of
smart motorways".[5][4]

The government's stated reasons for cancelling were confidence and cost. The release makes no claim
that smart motorways had been found less safe.[9]

### The case that they're as safe or safer

**National Highways** runs the roads it reports on, and its report is the most detailed evidence this
course read. Its headline: "overall, all three types of smart motorway continue to be better than
conventional motorways for those safety metrics which consider deaths or serious injuries, and that no
one type of motorway, smart or conventional, is ranked best against every safety metric."[7] It explains
the pattern: "PIC are higher on all three types of smart motorways than on conventional motorways, but a
collision on a conventional motorway is more likely to involve a death or serious injury than a
collision on any of the three types of smart motorway." (PIC means personal injury collisions.)[7]

It points to where people are hurt. "The majority (96%) of collisions on our network involve only moving
vehicles and the risk of being killed or seriously injured in this type of collision continues to be
highest on A-roads followed by motorways with a permanent hard shoulder." The next sentence is the other
half: "The minority (4%) of collisions on our network involve stopped vehicles, and the risk of being
killed or seriously injured in this type of collision continues to be highest on A-roads followed by
motorways without a permanent hard shoulder. These types of collision happen on all roads whether there
is a hard shoulder or not."[7] And it argues the hard
shoulder was never the safe place people picture: "The hard shoulder is perceived to be a place of safety
but, in reality, it does not provide a completely safe place to stop. Between 2018 and 2022 one out of
every 18 motorway deaths resulted from a vehicle entering, leaving or being on a hard shoulder. There
have been no deaths resulting from a vehicle entering, leaving or being in an emergency area."[7]

**The Department for Transport**, which set the policy, said in January 2022 that "available data shows
smart motorways are comparatively the safest roads in the country in terms of fatality rates", and that
"it does not agree with the view that smart motorways were rolled out prematurely or unsafely."[8] The
Transport Secretary then, Grant Shapps: "While our initial data shows that smart motorways are among the
safest roads in the UK, it’s crucial that we go further to ensure people feel safer using them."[8]

Someone who holds this view would put it this way, in this course's summary: judge a road by how many
people are killed or badly hurt on it, and on that measure these roads do as well as or better than the
ones they replaced; the hard shoulder has its own deaths; and the fixes the critics asked for, detection
and more emergency areas, have been built.

### The case against all-lane running

**The AA**, which sells breakdown cover and repairs, is the critic this course read most. Edmund King,
its president, on the third-year report: "The fundamental problem remains that stopped vehicle incidents
are more frequent on All Lane Running (ALR) ‘smart’ motorways compared to motorways with hard shoulders.
To address this, the hard shoulder needs to be reinstated or at the very least the number of Emergency
Areas needs to be radically increased." The same statement opens by applauding National Highways "for
making good progress in implementing and improving the performance of Stopped Vehicle Detection".[11]
Its alternative: "We have called for more ‘controlled motorways’", and "These motorways have the
benefit of ‘smart’ motorway technology whilst also having a hard shoulder."[11]

**The RAC**, which also sells breakdown cover, makes the comparison directly. Simon Williams, its head
of policy, in April 2024: "The hard shoulder is by no means a safe location, but in the event of a
breakdown, it is far safer than being stranded in a live lane of traffic waiting for the ‘red X closed
lane’ sign to be turned on and then for other drivers to do the right thing and move into another lane."
Its proposal: convert all-lane running roads to dynamic hard shoulder ones "or repaint the white line and
reintroduce a permanent hard shoulder".[12]

**A coroner.** In January 2021 David Urpeth, Senior Coroner for South Yorkshire West, reported on two
drivers who stopped in the left lane of an all-lane running stretch after a minor collision "even though
a “refuge” was available a mile further up the road", and were killed by a goods vehicle about six
minutes later. The inquest's conclusion was unlawful killing. "Whilst the main cause of the tragedy was
the careless driving of the driver of Mercedes goods vehicle, the inquest heard and accepted evidence
that the lack of a hard shoulder due to the road being a smart motorway contributed to their deaths." His
first matter of concern was "The obvious and foreseeable risk posed by the absence of a hard shoulder on
smart motorways;". And he asked for more than an inquest could give: among his concerns was "The need
for a wider review / inquiry into Smart Motorways", because "An inquest cannot say what government
policy should be".[13]

**Families.** People who lost relatives on motorways have campaigned on this. The one this course read
in her own words is Meera Naran, described by the Department as an "Independent road safety campaigner",
whose eight-year-old son died in a motorway crash in 2018. Her words, as the Department's release gives
them: "Conventional and smart motorways both have their risks and benefits. I welcome this pause in the
rollout of smart motorways, which will give us all a positive opportunity to assess the future of our
motorway network." She went on: "I’ll continue to both challenge and work alongside the Department for
Transport to ensure even more is done".[8] When the new schemes were cancelled in 2023 she said: "Since
successfully campaigning for the 18-point action plan, £900 million commitment and the pause in the roll
out in January 2022, there has been a lot of joined up thinking in mutually coming to this
decision."[9] Her words ask for the pause and more safety work, not for the hard shoulder back. That's
one campaigner, in words two government releases chose to print; this course read no other family's
statement.

Someone who holds this view would put it this way, in this course's summary: a road where a broken-down
car has nowhere to go is a different kind of road, stopped-vehicle collisions are more frequent on it by
the operator's own figures, and a measure that averages them in with every other collision hides the
risk that falls on the people who break down; and a controlled motorway keeps the technology without
taking the hard shoulder away.

### A committee of MPs, 2021

The House of Commons Transport Committee's 2021 report, "Rollout and safety of smart motorways", made
recommendations to government, and its conclusions cut across both cases.[14] It found that governments "underestimated the scale of safety measures needed
effectively and reliably to mitigate the risks associated with the permanent removal of the hard
shoulder", that "The available data on the safety of all-lane running motorways are limited and
volatile", and: "In conclusion, we are not convinced that the benefits of all-lane running motorways are
sufficient to justify the risks to safety associated with permanently removing the hard shoulder." It
recommended the pause. It also said: "Examining the available evidence base, we are not convinced that
reinstating the hard shoulder on all all-lane running motorways will improve safety. The evidence
suggests that doing so could put more drivers and passengers at risk of death and serious injury,
especially if the reduction in capacity were to divert drivers on to less safe, local roads. Although the
hard shoulder offers a relative place of safety, it is not a safe place to stop."[14]

Among its figures: "Of the 1,489 deaths on England’s roads in 2019, 15 (1%) occurred on motorways without
a hard shoulder. Seventy (5%) occurred on motorways with a hard shoulder", and "Deaths on motorways without
a permanent hard shoulder, however, have increased from five in 2017 to 15 in 2019", in a period when
such roads grew "from 172 miles to 204 miles". It also reported that "Some 40% of breakdowns on all-lane
running motorways take place in a live lane."[14] The deaths and mileage are 2017 to 2019 figures,
before the retrofits; the part of the report read doesn't date the 40 per cent.

### The numbers, both halves

Dynamic hard shoulder, at 1.13. Conventional and all-lane running are almost level: 1.39 against 1.38 per hundred million vehicle miles, 2018 to 2022, a hundredth lower on all-lane running and a difference the report can't test. The stopped-vehicle table goes the other way: 0.29 injury collisions involving a stopped vehicle on all-lane running against 0.19 on conventional motorways, a difference the report says "we can be confident" about. Neither half makes sense without the other.

2022, per 100 million vehicle miles, based on police collision data (STATS19) "with minor
amendment".[7] KSI is killed or seriously injured, DHS dynamic hard shoulder. The stopped-vehicle
collisions in the second table are part of the all collisions in the first.

|---|---|---|
| Conventional | 5.26 | 1.39 |
| Controlled | 7.44 | 1.35 |
| DHS | 6.88 | 1.13 |
| ALR | 5.62 | 1.38 |

| Stopped-vehicle collisions | Injury (PIC) | KSI |
|---|---|---|

What the report says about its own figures matters as much as the figures. On all injury collisions,
conventional 5.26 against ALR 5.62: "there is strong evidence that these two figures are statistically
different to each other", with conventional motorways lower. On the stopped-vehicle rates: "The
statistical testing suggests that we can be confident that the stopped vehicle PIC rates for
conventional motorways (0.19) and controlled motorways (0.19) are lower than that of ALR (0.29). However,
the evidence for statistical differences between other motorway types is not as strong." And on the
killed-or-seriously-injured rates, which carry its headline: "The FWI and KSI rates do not meet the
criteria and cannot be tested at this time."[7] (FWI is its weighted measure of deaths and injuries.)
The reason is technical: testing "is only viable where the measure being tested is an observed whole
number data point", and these rates are estimates, adjusted because police forces have recorded injury severity
in different ways, not whole-number counts. The report says "all police forces will eventually move to
injury-based reporting systems", and expects to test KSI rates from then.[7] So, on this course's reading, the
headline rests on measures the report can't yet test for significance, and the stopped-vehicle KSI rate
on all-lane running, 0.19 against 0.10, can't be tested either.

### What would settle it

Partly, time. The extra emergency areas were finished in spring 2025.[6] National Highways reports that the
ORR announced in December 2023 that, "following fixes we implemented by June 2023, the targets for
detection rates were now being met".[7] The report says it has "started the
process to assess and understand the impact of the actions on safety outcomes" with 2023, the first full year, and
that "We will also need further years’ data".[7] So the empirical half would be settled by
stopped-vehicle collision and casualty rates on all-lane running roads over several years after those
changes, compared with the other types, with the killed-or-seriously-injured rates tested for
significance once police injury reporting allows it, and, on this course's reading, checked by someone
other than the operator. The Office of Rail and Road, which is "responsible for monitoring and enforcing the performance and
efficiency of National Highways" on this, has assured past reports.[10] This course read none of its
reports.

The value half no data settles: whether a road that, on the operator's figures, does as well or better
on deaths and serious injuries overall but has more injury collisions with stopped cars is the better
road, and how much drivers' confidence should count. This
course takes no side on either.

## What people get wrong

**"The hard shoulder is a safe place."** Rule 275: hard shoulders "provide less protection than other
places of relative safety".[1] National Highways, which runs the roads: it "does not provide a completely safe place to stop",
and the RAC, which sells breakdown cover and argues for bringing it back: "by no means a safe location",
though "far safer than being stranded in a live lane".[7][12]

**"Wait in the car on the hard shoulder, where it's warm."** Rule 277: "DO NOT return to your vehicle
even if it’s raining, cold or dark".[1]

**"Stop and help another driver on the motorway."** National Highways: "Don't stop to assist another
vehicle".[4] Call instead.

**"Smart motorways were cancelled because they were shown to be less safe."** The Department's stated
reasons were public confidence and cost; see "What's settled" above.

**"British and American advice can't both be right."** Each is written for its own roads, and they agree
on the live lane. Follow the advice for the road you're on.

## Practice

:::exercise Your plan for the fast roads you use
This is general information, not legal advice. Take 15 minutes over this, on paper or with a map app at home. Don't stand at a roadside, on a hard shoulder or in an emergency area for this, and don't drive for it.

1. Pick the motorway or fast road you use most. Write down the exits and services along the stretch you drive, in order.

2. For your country only. In Great Britain, find out whether it has a hard shoulder all the way, part of the way or not at all (National Highways' "Driving on motorways" page and its emergency-area page are the place to start in England); if you can't find it on an official page, write "not known" and plan for both, a hard shoulder and emergency areas some distance apart. In Scotland and Wales this course read no road operator's page, so plan for both there too. In Northern Ireland, open the Highway Code for Northern Ireland's "Breakdowns and incidents" page on nidirect and copy its motorway steps, not Great Britain's. In the US, find your state's own official driver handbook and its section on a disabled vehicle, and copy its steps.

3. Write the steps for your country in your own words, in order, with the numbers your own country's page gives: in Great Britain, 999 and, in England, 0300 123 5000 (and, if you have a hearing, speech or physical impairment, 0738 028 3600 by text); in the US, 911 and any assistance number your state's handbook gives. In Great Britain, mark which of your steps come from a Highway Code MUST (Rules 258, 269, 270, 271, 277's line on animals, and 278) and which are should.

4. Write the one sentence you'd say to a 999 or 911 operator if you were stuck in a lane, including how you'd tell them where you are.
:::
