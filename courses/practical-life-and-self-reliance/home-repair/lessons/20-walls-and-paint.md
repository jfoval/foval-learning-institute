A coat of paint is the job most people try first, and it's the one that shows every shortcut. A
filled hole that wasn't sanded, a new wall painted before it dried, a stain painted over without
asking what made it: each comes back through the new paint within weeks. And two things about a
wall decide whether it is safe to prepare at all, before the question of how it will look: what's in
the old paint, and what made the mark you want to cover. This lesson puts the work in order, from
reading the wall to the last coat, with a maker's methods labelled as a maker's, and it sends you to
lessons 18 and 19 at the two points where the job may have to stop.

:::callout Before you pick up a tool
This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

Painting a room opens nothing up, but it does bring you close to the switches and sockets, which

Consumer Product Safety Commission, says panel boxes "are usually electrically live, even with the
main circuit breakers turned off",[12] and HSE's guidance for people who work on electrical systems
does not recommend "non-contact devices such as 'volt sticks'" for proving anything dead.[13]

## First, read the wall

Before a filler or a brush, the wall gets two questions. Neither is about paint.

**What made the mark?** A brown ring on a ceiling, a grey bloom in a corner, a patch that bubbles
every winter: these are marks made by water, and paint doesn't fix water. For the US, Purdue
University's extension bulletin on paint failure, written in 1988 about paint on the outside of
wooden houses, says "The most common cause of premature paint failure on wood is moisture", lists
"Plumbing leaks, overflow of sinks, bathtubs or shower spray and improperly sealed walls" among the
sources of water from inside, and puts the rule plainly: "If a new paint coat is to be successful
over a problem area, any moisture problems must be eliminated."[6] It is old and about outside
woodwork, but it is the only painting source this course read that says what to do about a wet
patch, and its rule matches the order lesson 18, "Damp inside: condensation, mould, ventilation and
rising damp", teaches for the inside of a house: find and fix the cause first. That lesson also has
EPA's line on mould, "Do not paint or caulk moldy surfaces."[7] So a stain goes to lesson 18 before
it goes anywhere near a tin, and a stain in a rented home goes to the landlord.

**What is in the old paint, and what's on the ceiling?** Filling and preparing a wall means sanding,
and sometimes scraping. In an older house, the layers underneath may contain lead, and a textured
ceiling or wall coating may contain asbestos. Lesson 19, "Asbestos, lead paint and radon: what not to
disturb", teaches both: how to tell whether your home is old enough to matter, what the law and the
advice say, and what to do instead. This lesson does not reteach any of it. What it does is mark the
points where you stop and go there: before you sand or scrape any paintwork that might be old enough,
and before you fill, sand or scrape a textured coating at all. For the US, EPA's page for people doing
their own renovation puts the first of those in one sentence: "If your home was built before 1978, it
may have lead-based paint; the older your home, the more likely it has lead-based paint."[10]

:::predict A small grey patch of mould has come back three times on the corner of a bedroom ceiling. Someone suggests a stain-blocking paint to cover it for good. Before you read on: what do the sources this course read say about that plan?

It's the wrong order. EPA says "Do not paint or caulk moldy surfaces", and lesson 18 teaches the
cleaning, the small-patch limit and, first, finding the water.[7] Purdue's bulletin says the same of
paint over a moisture problem: the moisture has to go first.[6] A mark that keeps coming back is
telling you the cause is still there. Paint over it and you have hidden the evidence, not the damp.
:::

## Finishes, and the two countries' names

Dulux describes the finishes by how much light they reflect: "To hide imperfections, use velvety
matt emulsion. Satin and silk are mid-sheen finishes that look slightly polished and reflect some
light. Eggshell sits somewhere between the two, with a surface that looks as its name suggests.
Choose a gloss finish for a shiny look that reflects lots of light."[1] Dulux's reason for the
choice, as far as it gives one, is that matt hides imperfections. That a shinier finish shows them
more is this course's reading of "reflects lots of light", not Dulux's words.

"Emulsion" is the British name for ordinary wall paint thinned with water. In American shops the
name you will see for water-based wall paint is "latex", and Purdue's bulletin uses it ("acrylic
latex").[6] This course read no source that defines either word against the other. Treating them as
the two countries' names for the same kind of paint is this course's reading, and the tin decides
what's in it. Dulux also says synthetic brushes suit water-based paints, because the bristles
"don't swell up and cause tramlines to appear in the paint".[1]

Where you see "solvent-based" or "oil-based", the paint is thinned with a solvent instead of water.
The UK law in the section on labels below calls them "water-borne" and "solvent-borne", and defines
them by how the paint's thickness is adjusted: with water, or with an organic solvent.[3] Both kinds
give off some VOCs (the limits in the table below are above zero for both, and EPA says paints
contain organic solvents). What you breathe while it dries is the section after that.

## Filling holes and cracks

Dulux's preparation page tells you to buy "a filler that’s up to the task", and describes fillers
made for particular jobs: hairline cracks, screw holes, deeper holes, and a flexible filler for
"around the tops of skirtings, windows and door frames".[1] (A skirting is the board along the foot of
a wall; in the US it is a baseboard.) Dulux gives no reason why those gaps want a flexible filler, and
this course supplies none. Its method, for filling:[1]

1. Clean and check. Wipe the wall down, wash it with sugar soap and rinse it (more on that below),
   then look it over for cracks and holes.
2. Fill with a filling knife, using the filler for that surface.
3. Deep holes in layers: "For any holes that are more than 10mm-deep, build up the filler in
   layers." (10 mm is about 3/8 inch.)
4. Dry, sand, dust: "Allow to dry for one to two hours, sand until smooth, and give the wall a final
   once-over with a duster."

The drying time is for the fillers Dulux sells; the filler's own instructions give yours. Dulux says
sanding is needed because it "ensures your fresh, new paint will adhere to the surface properly".[1]

Filling screw holes and small cracks in a plain plastered wall is **Yours**. Two things change that.
The first is step 4, which sands. On a newer wall that makes plaster and filler dust. Lesson 13,
"Tools, dust and fumes: a small kit used safely", gave HSE's kinds of dust (gypsum, in plasterboard or drywall,
among the "general" ones), and its advice on masks and clean-up applies. On old paintwork it's lesson 19's question, and on a textured coating the answer is
no. The second is the crack itself. This course read no source on reading cracks: which are
shrinkage, which are movement, which mean the building is moving. Structural work is one of the jobs
lesson 1 named as needing a professional, and a crack you can't account for, or one that comes back
wider, is a question for a surveyor or builder, not for a filler. That is this course's line, not a
source's.

Sticking doors, sealant round a bath and wallpaper are left out: this course read no maker's or
official method for any of them, and it does not teach from general knowledge.

## Preparing the room, in Dulux's order

Dulux's advice is to "Set aside half a day to complete all your prep", and its warning is that
skipping it can leave "an uneven finish, unsightly paint bubbles and obvious imperfections, such as
holes and cracks".[1] Its steps, in its order:[1]

1. **Clear the room.** Furniture, pictures, curtains and blinds out; a dust sheet over the floor and
   whatever can't move.
2. **Switches and sockets.** Dulux says: "Loosen or tape around electrical cover plates on switches
   and outlets." This course teaches the second half only. Tape around the plate. Loosening the plate
   is the first step of opening a fitting, and this course teaches no work on fittings, anywhere
   (lesson 5 set out where the householder's hands stop). That is this course's scope decision, not a
   claim about the law.
3. **Hardware.** Unscrew, or tape over, handles and locks on doors and windows.
4. **Clean.** "Wash the walls thoroughly with sugar soap and rinse them with clean water to remove any
   residue." Sugar soap is a British name for a wall-cleaning compound; this course read no US source
   that names an equivalent, so in a US home the cleaner's own label is the guide.
5. **Fill, then sand** the filled spots and any woodwork you will paint, and wipe the dust off.
6. **Mask** the edges you want sharp, such as the tops of skirtings, with masking tape.
7. **Prime** a surface that has never been painted. (New plaster has its own section, next.)
8. **Paint from the top down.** Dulux: "start at the top of the room and work your way down". Ceiling
   first, "Apply two coats and move onto the walls, working away from natural light." Then: "Paint
   your windows in the morning so they have a full day to dry before you close them at night. Lastly,
   finish off with doors, door frames and skirting."

Two of those steps have a stop in them. Step 5 sands, which in an older house is the point to check
with lesson 19 first. And step 8 starts on the ceiling, which means a stepladder. Lesson 16,
"Stepladders, and what the ladder injury figures count", worked through painting a hall ceiling from
a stepladder, with HSE's rules and the choice of a roller on a long handle from the floor, and it
applies here unchanged.

:::checkpoint A friend's plan for a Saturday: paint the walls first "while I'm fresh", then the ceiling, then sand and fill the holes she notices along the way, and finish with the skirting. What would Dulux change, and why does the order matter?

Fill and sand before any paint goes on, as part of the preparation, so the dust from sanding doesn't
land on wet paint and the filled spots get painted with the rest (Dulux's steps 5 and 8).[1] Then
top down: ceiling, then walls, then woodwork, with the skirting last. Painting the ceiling after the
walls drops paint on finished walls, which is the reason top-down makes sense; Dulux gives the order
rather than the reason, so that part is this course's reading. And before sanding anything, the
lesson 19 check if the house is old enough.
:::

## New plaster

A wall that has just been plastered, or skimmed (a thin new coat over an old one), is the one wall
where waiting is part of the job. Dulux, answering a reader in its "Dr Dulux" column, gives the
reason in one line: "Plaster is extremely permeable, which means it’ll soak up any moisture quick."[2]
Its steps:[2]

1. **Let it dry.** "As a rule of thumb, plaster takes up to 4 weeks to dry completely." What sets
   the time: "There are different factors that influence how soon can you paint on new plaster, such
   as the type of material and weather." Dulux adds: "You can speed things up with a heater or by
   opening a window." And the cost of hurrying: "If you don’t let the plaster dry and apply emulsion
   paint to wet plaster, it’s likely to crack and peel off."
2. **Mist coat.** "Mist coat paint is simply watered-down emulsion paint, which acts as your primer."
   White emulsion, thinned: "Ten parts paint to one part water should work."
3. **Why it works**, in Dulux's words: "The mixture will soak into the wall and seal the plaster."
   Dulux says this is what helps the top coat stick. Any flaws can be sanded or filled once it is dry.
4. **Top coat** as normal.

Dulux's "up to 4 weeks" is a rule of thumb from one maker. So: drying time depends on the plaster, the thickness and the weather, and the
plasterer who did the wall and the maker of the paint you are using are the people to ask. Dulux's
ratio is for its emulsions; another maker's tin may give its own. And its method is written for
British plastered walls. Nothing this course read covers a new plaster skim in an American house,
or drywall, so applying Dulux's wait and mist coat there is this course's reading, and the paint
maker's own instructions come first.

:::predict New plaster, dry and pale after three weeks. Someone decides to skip the mist coat and put on two full-strength coats of their colour instead, reasoning that two thick coats will do the same job. On Dulux's account, what's the problem?

Dulux's point is that plaster is "extremely permeable" and soaks up moisture, and that the thinned mist
coat "will soak into the wall and seal the plaster", which is what helps the top coat stick.[2] A
full-strength coat is not what Dulux describes as the sealer; the thinned one is. So on Dulux's
account, the first thick coat is being asked to do a job Dulux gives to a thinned coat. Dulux
doesn't explain the chemistry of why thinning helps, and this course does not either. Its page also
mentions a ready-made sealer product as an alternative; that's a product, and the tin's instructions
would decide.
:::

## The label on the tin

This section states law. This is general information, not legal advice.

In the UK, paints and varnishes for the walls, woodwork and fittings of buildings (aerosols
excepted) come under the Volatile Organic Compounds in Paints, Varnishes and
Vehicle Refinishing Products Regulations 2012. A volatile organic compound, or VOC, is defined there
as an organic compound with an initial boiling point at or below 250°C (482°F).[3] The Regulations apply to
products that "are to be used in any part of the United Kingdom" (with a further condition for
products in Northern Ireland that refers to the EU's customs territory).[3] This course read the revised text on
legislation.gov.uk, which shows the 2020 change to that scope and says there are "no known
outstanding effects" for the Regulations.

What they do: under regulation 4, a person "must not place on the market" a paint covered by them
unless its VOC content is within the limit for its kind, and it carries the label regulation 5
requires. That label must show "the sub-category of the product and the relevant VOC limit values,
measured in g/l", and "the maximum content of VOC in g/l of the product in a ready to use
condition".[3] "Ready to use" matters: the Regulations define VOC content as the mass of VOCs "in the
formulation of the product in its ready to use condition", and regulation 4 applies the limits to a
product that has to have solvent added before use in its ready-to-use state.

Notice who the law binds. It's the person placing the tin on the market. Nothing in it tells you how
to use the paint, and nothing in it binds a householder. What it gives you is a number to read.

A few of the limits in Schedule 2, for the kinds of paint in this lesson:[3]

| Sub-category | Water-borne limit | Solvent-borne limit |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Interior matt walls and ceilings | 30 g/l | 30 g/l |
| (b) Interior glossy walls and ceilings | 100 g/l | 100 g/l |
| (d) Interior and exterior trim and cladding paints for wood and metal | 130 g/l | 300 g/l |
| (g) Primers | 30 g/l | 350 g/l |

The table is this course's selection; the Schedule has twelve sub-categories for paints and varnishes.
Read across the trim row and you see the difference the last section described: the law sets a
solvent-based trim paint's limit more than twice as high as a water-based one's. The figure on your tin is the
product's own maximum, which may sit well under its limit.

In the US, this course read no federal rule on labelling a consumer paint's VOC content, so it says
nothing about what an American tin must show. What it read is EPA's advice, in the next section. Its
list of steps opens: "Increase ventilation when using products that emit VOCs." "Meet or exceed any
label precautions."[4]

:::checkpoint Two tins in a British shop. One says "Interior matt walls and ceilings", limit 30 g/l, max 2 g/l. The other says "Interior/exterior trim and cladding paints", solvent-based, limit 300 g/l, max 290 g/l. Which gives off more solvent as it dries, and what doesn't the label tell you?

The trim paint holds far more VOC per litre, so it has far more to give off (the Regulations measure
VOC content as grams per litre of the paint as used).[3] That it gives off more is this course's
reading of what "volatile" content means, not a line from the Regulations. What the label doesn't tell
you: how much will be in your room's air, how long to air the room, or whether a mask helps. Those
come from the tin's own instructions and from ventilation advice, next.
:::

## Air, leftovers and strippers

Part of this section states law. This is general information, not legal advice.

[EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/volatile-organic-compounds-impact-indoor-air-quality),
the US Environmental Protection Agency, says "Paints, varnishes and wax all contain organic
solvents", and lesson 13 gave its figures for how far indoor levels rise, especially while stripping
paint. EPA is also frank about how much is not known: "At present, not much is known about what health
effects occur from the levels of organics usually found in homes."[4] Its advice doesn't wait on that.
Its general step is "Increase ventilation when using products that emit VOCs", and among its steps
against benzene, which it says comes partly from paint supplies, it lists "providing for maximum
ventilation during painting". Where a label says to
use a product in a well-ventilated area, lesson 13 gave EPA's order: outdoors or with an exhaust fan,
otherwise open windows.[4]

A dust mask is no help with any of this. Lesson 13 showed why: the masks this course read about filter
particles, and a solvent vapour is not one.

Then the leftovers. EPA:[4]

- Buy small. For paints and strippers used now and then, "buy only as much as you will use right
  away".
- Don't keep what you won't use. "Because gases can leak even from closed containers, this single
  step could help lower concentrations of organic chemicals in your home." What you do keep, EPA
  says, goes somewhere well ventilated and out of children's reach.
- Don't put it in the bin (trash). "Do not simply toss these unwanted products in the garbage can." EPA sends you to a
  local collection day for toxic household wastes, where one exists.

That is US advice. In England, Thames Water, a water company, says: "Take chemicals, solvents, engine
oil and paint to your local refuse or recycling site", the same line lesson 4, "Water out: traps,
vents and blocked drains", gave for keeping them out of the drain.[5] Thames Water's page is about its own
area; this course read no other UK body on leftover paint, so your council's waste service is the one
to ask.

**Strippers.** Lesson 13 covered methylene chloride, a solvent some old paint strippers contain, which
EPA says "is converted to carbon monoxide in the body".[4] In the US, EPA's rules since 2019 have
prohibited making and distributing it for consumer paint removal, and for a tin someone already has,
"EPA is encouraging all consumers to stop using methylene chloride products that they may have
already purchased for paint and coating removal."[8] EPA's indoor air page still says to "Use
products that contain methylene chloride outdoors when possible; use indoors only if the area is well
ventilated."[4] Lesson 13 set the two pages side by side; for paint removal, this course follows the
one about the rules. This course read no UK rule on it. Any other
stripper, in either country, is used by its own label.

## Old paint: overcoat or strip?

This is lesson 19's decision, and this lesson only marks where it comes. If the paintwork you are
preparing is old enough to fall inside lesson 19's dates, the question isn't how to sand it but
whether to disturb it at all. The two countries' advice differs, so here it is one country at a
time.

**In the UK**, the British Coatings Federation says in its guidance for the public not to remove lead
paint that is in sound condition, especially if the lead paint is not the top layer: "overcoating is
the safest option to prevent exposure".[9] Where sound paint needs preparing for the new coat, it says
to rub it down wet, so no dust is made. Lesson 19 labels that **Yours, on conditions**: the paint is
sound, and any rubbing down is wet.

**In the US**, EPA's page for do-it-yourselfers says: "DIY projects can easily create dangerous lead
dust, so EPA recommends homeowners hire lead-safe certified contractors for all RRP projects in
pre-1978 homes." (RRP is renovation, repair and painting.) If you can't find out the home's age, it
says "it’s safest to assume the area you are renovating has lead-based paint and use the DIY lead-safe
work practices described below".[10] Those are seven practices, which lesson 19 sets out, and doing
the job yourself on those terms is lesson 19's **Yours, on conditions**.

Everything after that, the methods that are allowed, the ones that are not, the heat limits and the
law, is in lesson 19.

So, for this lesson's purposes: in the UK, sound old paint that only needs a fresh colour is painted
over without disturbing it, which the BCF calls the safest option, and any rubbing down is done wet.
In the US, EPA's first advice for any renovation, repair or painting job in a pre-1978 home is a
lead-safe certified contractor, and doing it yourself means EPA's seven practices in lesson 19. In
either country, anything that needs stripping, or flaking paint in an older home, is a lesson 19
question before it's a painting one.

## A living room in the Forest of Dean

Take the simple case first. In a house in the Forest of Dean built in 2006, the owner wants to
repaint the living room walls. There are six screw holes where a shelf came down, a hairline crack
over the door, and the walls are the original matt emulsion, a bit grubby near the light switch.

1. **Read the wall.** No stains, no mould, no bubbling paint. Built after 2000, so on HSE's line
   "Buildings constructed after 2000 are unlikely to contain asbestos", and well after the dates the
   UK sources give for lead paint (the British Coatings Federation's cut-off for testing is
   "Pre-1995").[11][9] No textured coating. Nothing sends this job to lesson 18 or 19.
2. **Choose.** Matt emulsion again, since Dulux says matt hides imperfections, and the filled spots
   are imperfections.[1] A filler for screw holes and fine cracks, used by its own instructions.
3. **Clear, protect, tape.** Furniture out, dust sheet down, masking tape round the switch and socket
   plates (not loosened, the course's line).[1]
4. **Clean.** Sugar soap, then clean water, and let it dry.[1]
5. **Fill and sand.** Fill the holes with a filling knife; none is deeper than 10 mm, so one layer.
   Let it dry for the time the filler gives, then sand smooth and dust off.[1] It is plaster and filler dust from a
   newer wall; lesson 13's clean-up advice (no dry sweeping) applies.
6. **Paint.** Window open while painting and afterwards: EPA's "Increase ventilation when using
   products that emit VOCs", applied in England as this course's reading, plus whatever the tin
   adds.[4] The walls in
   two coats, working away from the window, then the woodwork if it's being done.[1]

This is **Yours**. What made it simple is step 1: a house new enough that neither lesson 18 nor
lesson 19 had anything to say.

## A renovation in Youngstown

Now one with a wrinkle, and a gap for you.

In a 1920s house in Youngstown, the owners have had a back bedroom's walls skimmed with a thin coat
of new plaster. It has been ten days, the walls look dry to them, and they want to paint this
weekend, full-strength, because their guests arrive in two weeks. The same room has the original
door, with thick layers of paint that have begun to crack. They want to strip it back to the wood and
varnish it, and one of them has already bought a tin of paint stripper.

Two questions sit in this room, and they go to different places.

**The walls.** Dulux's rule of thumb is "up to 4 weeks", and ten days is inside it.[2] Dulux gives
no way of telling by eye that a wall is dry, and says the time depends on the material and the
weather. This is a US wall, and nothing this course read covers a US skim coat, so Dulux's wait and
mist coat are one British maker's method, not the answer here. The people who know are the plasterer
who did the skim (what was used, how thick) and the maker of the paint they plan to use, and the
question to ask both is when it can be painted and what goes on first. What Dulux does say about
rushing, for its own paint: emulsion on wet plaster is "likely to crack and peel off". **Yours, on
conditions**: the plasterer's and the paint maker's say-so on timing and first coat.

:::checkpoint Now the door. Before you open this: what is the first question about it, where does the answer come from, and what does EPA recommend?

The first question is its age, not how to strip it. The house was built in the 1920s, and EPA says
"If your home was built before 1978, it may have lead-based paint; the older your home, the more
likely it has lead-based paint."[10] Thick old layers on an original door are the kind of paint that
may hold it. So this is lesson 19's question before it is a decorating one, whenever the guests
arrive: whether the door's paint should be disturbed at all, and, if it must be stripped, which methods lesson 19's sources allow, before the tin is opened. (The stripper itself is
used by its own label, and if it contains methylene chloride, EPA's lines in the section above
apply.) EPA recommends homeowners hire "lead-safe certified contractors for all RRP projects in
pre-1978 homes", or, if they do it themselves, the seven practices on the same page, which lesson 19
sets out.[10] Those are lesson 19's labels: in the US, EPA's first advice is a certified contractor;
doing it yourself is **Yours, on conditions**, the conditions being EPA's seven practices. Lesson
23, "Hiring a tradesperson, and when to stop", is where hiring is covered.
:::

The two questions in that house look alike, since both are about waiting, but they're different
kinds of answer. The plaster wait is a maker's rule of thumb about how paint sticks: get it wrong and
you repaint. The door is about what is in the paint, and that's the one lesson 19 was written for.

## What people get wrong

**"Paint over the stain and it's gone."** A mark made by water comes back until the water's fixed
(Purdue; lesson 18), and EPA says not to paint mouldy surfaces.[6][7]

**"New plaster that looks dry is ready."** Dulux's rule of thumb is up to four weeks, and it depends
on the plaster and the weather; paint on wet plaster is "likely to crack and peel off".[2]

**"The mist coat is optional if you use more coats."** Dulux gives the sealing job to the thinned
coat.[2]

**"A low VOC number means no need to open a window."** The label is a legal requirement on
whoever puts the tin on the market, about what is in the tin; EPA's advice, to increase ventilation when using products that emit VOCs, makes no exception for a low number.[3][4]

**"Leftover paint is fine in the cupboard with the lid on."** EPA: gases can leak even from closed
containers.[4]

**"Sanding old paintwork is just preparation."** In an older home, it's lesson 19's question first.
(And for the stripper: lesson 13.)

## Practice

:::exercise One room, planned
Take 30 minutes over this, with a notebook and, if you have one, a tin of paint. It is reading and
planning. Don't sand, scrape, fill or strip anything for this exercise, and in particular nothing
that might be old paint or a textured coating. Do not loosen or take off any switch or socket plate,
and don't climb a stepladder for it. A renter plans the room on paper and leaves the real work until
the landlord agrees, or until it is their own home to repair.

This exercise includes reading what the law makes a label say. As in the lesson, this is general
information, not legal advice.

1. **Read a tin.** In the UK, find the VOC panel: the sub-category, the limit and the product's
   maximum, and check the limit against the table in this lesson. In the US, find the ventilation
   instruction and anything the label says about use indoors. Note the drying time between coats.
2. **Read the wall.** Pick one room. Note any stains, mould, bubbling or flaking paint (lesson 18 if
   so), the house's age, any textured coating, and any old woodwork (lesson 19 if so).
3. **Plan the job in order,** with how long each step waits: clearing, taping, cleaning, filling
   and its drying time, sanding (or the reason not to), new plaster if any, and the painting order
   from the ceiling down. Mark where you'd need a stepladder and look back at lesson 16.
4. **Plan the leftovers.** How much paint the job needs, where what's left will go, and where your
   area's household hazardous waste goes.
:::

:::checkpoint Three surfaces, overcoat or strip, or stop. (1) An inside door in England, fitted and painted in 2015, paint sound but faded. (2) A 1930s baseboard (skirting) in a US house, sound but chipped at one corner. (3) A stippled textured coating on the landing wall of a 1980s house in Scotland, to be painted a new colour. Decide for each, and say which lesson decides it.

(1) Not a lesson 19 question: painted in 2015, long after the UK dates the sources give (the British
Coatings Federation's cut-off for testing is "Pre-1995").[9] So it's cleaned, prepared and repainted
with this lesson's steps and the tin's own instructions. **Yours.**

(2) A 1930s house is pre-1978, so EPA says it may have lead paint.[10] Whether and how to deal with
the chipped corner is lesson 19's question, and nothing gets sanded before it is answered. In the US,
EPA's first advice is a certified contractor; doing it yourself is **Yours, on conditions**, the
conditions being EPA's seven practices.

(3) A textured coating in a building from before 2000 is on HSE's list of places asbestos may be, and
anything that would fill, sand or scrape it stops. Nothing this course read says whether a textured
coating that might contain asbestos can be painted over. What HSE does say, for a home where you
suspect asbestos, is that "it is often best to leave them where they are", especially if they are in
good condition and unlikely to get damaged, and that you can ask an environmental health officer at
your council.[14] So the coating is left alone, and the question of painting it goes to someone
trained in asbestos work, or to the council's environmental health officer. **Call someone.**
:::

## For your plan

To the **stop list**: sanding or scraping any paintwork old enough for lesson 19, and filling, sanding
or scraping a textured coating; painting over a stain whose cause isn't fixed; loosening switch or
socket plates. To the **calendar**: nothing seasonal of its own, but a note to paint in weather that
lets you keep the windows open. And for the **repair write-up**: if your one repair is a patch and a
repaint, this lesson's order, from reading the wall to the last coat, is its plan.
