Electrical Safety First (ESF), a UK charity that campaigns on electrical safety, asked 1,000
electricians about DIY work they'd been called to put right. It reports: "The most common mistakes
people make when attempting to undertake DIY were cutting through power leads, drilling into wiring,
or trying to repair an electrical item while it is switched on."[1] ESF does not give the survey's
date, and the figure counts what electricians reported, not how often DIY goes wrong. But a wall
hides what's in it, and this lesson is about the second of those three: what to do before the bit
goes in.

:::callout Before you pick up a tool
This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

"Isolate the power before you open anything up" has a second meaning here: before you drill a
wall, because a wall has cables in it that you cannot see. The box stays shut, as it did in lessons 5
and 6, and the two facts that go with it still hold. CPSC, the US Consumer Product Safety Commission,
says panel boxes "are usually electrically live, even with the main circuit breakers turned
off".[5] And HSE's guidance for people who work on electrical systems does not recommend "non-contact
devices such as 'volt sticks'" for proving anything dead.[6]

## The detector, and what it cannot see

A cable and pipe detector is sold for this. This course found no official UK or US consumer page on
detectors, so it uses a maker's guidance for its own devices. [Bosch](https://www.bosch-diy.com/gb/en/all-about-diy/drill-more-safely),
about its own digital detectors, gives these steps:[2]

1. Take off rings, bracelets and watches, and put your phone out of reach, or they can confuse the
   reading.
2. Hold the whole device flat on the wall.
3. Put your other hand flat on the wall, about 20 to 30 cm away, for grounding. Bosch warns that "If
   there is insufficient grounding, for example, due to insulating footwear, or if you are standing
   on a conductor, it won’t be possible to detect live cables."
4. Move it across the wall with even pressure, along one line at a time.
5. "The device needs to be moved over the same place several times to detect whether there is
   something behind the wall."
6. Switch something on in the circuit, such as a light or a vacuum cleaner, while you scan. "A cable
   must have a current in order to be picked up by the device."
7. Then: "for your own safety, after detection you should switch off the power supply completely
   before you start drilling."

:::predict Before reading on: you scan a wall with every light in the room off and nothing plugged in nearby, and the detector shows green everywhere. On step 6, what has the scan told you about cables?

Nothing about the cables on circuits that had nothing switched on. Bosch says a cable needs current
to be picked up, so a cable with nothing drawing on it gives the detector nothing to find.[2] The
scan has not cleared the wall of cables.
:::

Step 6 is the one people miss, and it is the mechanism of the whole thing. On Bosch's account, a
detector of this kind needs current in a cable to find it. A cable carrying no current, because
nothing on it is switched on or because its breaker is off, gives it nothing to find. Bosch's own
summary of a green light is "Nothing was detected and you’re good to go".[2] Read that beside step 6:
green means nothing carrying current was found where you scanned, not that there's no cable. Your
detector may work differently; its own instructions decide.

## Where cables run

Cables often run straight up, down or across from sockets and switches. ESF states it as a
requirement for how buried cables are installed.[1] This course did not read the wiring standard ESF
is referring to, so it cannot tell you whether every cable in every older house follows it, and a
detector is still needed. Treat the wall above, below and beside any socket or switch as the likeliest
place for a cable, and not as the only one.

## Power off, and the check that it is

ESF: "For any DIY near electrical wiring or power supplies, switch off the power at your fuseboard
and, where possible, only use battery-powered tools." And: "To test that the power is off before
beginning non-electrical DIY, plug an appliance in or try switching on lights."[1] That is ESF's test
for non-electrical work such as drilling, and it is the one this course gives you. It is not a way of
proving a circuit dead for work on the wiring, which lesson 5 left to electricians: that needs a
proper tester and training, as HSE's guidance for electrical workers describes.[6] ESF is a British
charity, and this course read no American page on checking the power before non-electrical work; in
a US home, trying the lights is the course's reading of ESF's test, not an American body's advice.

The main switch, which lesson 1 found and lesson 5 explained, is the front of the box and it is
yours to switch. Everything behind the cover still is not, and CPSC's warning that a panel is usually
live with the main breakers off still stands.[5]

### If you hit something

If you hit a cable anyway, stop. A damaged cable in a wall is wiring, and this course's line is
**Call someone**, an electrician, whatever the drill did or did not trip. ESF's advice on electrical
work is the same: "Any electrical work should be left to competent professionals."[1]

If you hit a gas pipe, lesson 9, "Gas: the smell and the valve", has what National Gas says to do in
Great Britain: call the emergency number day or night, even if no gas seems to be escaping. If you
hit a water pipe, the stop tap or main shut-off valve that lesson 1 found is the first move.

## A material that might be asbestos

In Great Britain, HSE lists "drilling into a wall" among the activities that can disturb asbestos,
and says any building "built or refurbished before the year 2000" may contain it, while "Buildings
constructed after 2000 are unlikely to contain asbestos."[3] Its guide says that if you come across
any material you think may contain asbestos, "stop work immediately".[3] That guide is written for
people at work; stopping is also what this course tells a householder. In the US, EPA says "you
can't tell whether a material contains asbestos simply by looking at it, unless it is labeled. If in
doubt, treat the material as if it contains asbestos and leave it alone", and on its list of
don'ts: "Don't saw, sand, scrape, or drill holes in asbestos-containing materials."[4]

A textured ceiling, an old board that looks like plasterboard, anything in a pre-2000 British home
or an older American one that you're not sure of: don't drill it. That is a stop, not a judgement
call, and lesson 19, "Asbestos, lead paint and radon: what not to disturb", is where the course
teaches what comes next.

## A shelf on the Isle of Wight

The whole routine, on one job. A couple want a shelf on the living room wall of their house on
the Isle of Wight, built in 2004. The wall is plastered, there's a double socket about a metre to the
left of where the shelf will go, and they've borrowed a digital detector and have a cordless drill.

1. **The date.** Built in 2004, so on HSE's line the house is unlikely to contain asbestos.[3] The
   wall is plain plaster, with no textured coating. No reason to stop here.
2. **Scan with the power on.** Rings and watches off, the detector flat, a hand on the wall, the lamp
   on that socket and the room's lights switched on, several passes over the shelf's position,
   following the detector's own instructions.[2] The line straight above the socket gets extra care;
   the shelf's fixings are well to the right of it. They mark what the detector finds.
3. **Power off.** The main switch at the consumer unit, then ESF's check: the lights don't come on.[1]
   With the mains off, a corded drill would have nothing to plug into, so ESF's "battery-powered
   tools" is also the practical choice.
4. **Eyes on.** Eye protection, as lesson 13, "Tools, dust and fumes: a small kit used safely", set
   out.

:::predict Step 5 is the dust. The wall's surface is plaster, and HSE's construction dust sheet puts gypsum, the stuff of plasterboard, under "general" dust, not silica. Does HSE's silica advice have anything to say about this job? Think about what the drill goes through.

Very likely, yes. Plaster is only the surface. Behind it there may be brick or concrete block, and
HSE lists those among the silica materials ("bricks, tiles, concrete and mortar").[8] If so, the
drill makes silica dust once it is through the plaster. Four holes is the "occasional short-duration
drilling" row of HSE's table: a dust collector at the bit or an extractor on the drill where
possible, and otherwise a respirator with a factor of 20, which in the UK means FFP3.[7] The couple
don't know what's behind the plaster, so they plan for the silica case.
:::

5. **Dust.** An FFP3 on, with HSE's pre-use check, and a dust collector or an extractor on the drill
   if they have one. Without either, HSE's table leaves the job to the respirator.[7][10] No dry
   sweeping afterwards; HSE's wood dust sheet names vacuum equipment of at least dust class M for
   clean-up at work.[9]
6. **Fixings.** The fixings for that wall, by the fixing maker's instructions. This course read no
   source on fixings, so it gives none.
7. **Power back on**, and the lamp works.

Putting up a shelf this way is **Yours, on conditions**: a scan with the power on, the power off
before drilling, eyes and lungs covered, and a stop at anything that might be asbestos.

## A curtain rail in an older American house

This one has a wrinkle, and you're going to find it before you read it.

In a 1950s house in the American Southwest, a homeowner wants a curtain rail above a bedroom window.
There's a light switch beside the window. The wall above the window has the same swirled texture as
the ceiling. It is a bright afternoon, so the room's lights are off, and nothing is plugged in on
that wall. She runs a borrowed detector along the line above the window, gets green all the way,
switches off the main breaker, and picks up the drill.

:::checkpoint Two things are wrong. Find both, and say which source's words answer each, before you open this.

**1. The scan.** With the room's lights off and nothing plugged in, any cable to that switch, or
running past the window, had no current in it. Bosch: "A cable must have a current in order to be
picked up by the device."[2] So green told her nothing about cables. The light switch beside the
window also puts the line above it among the likeliest places for one.[1] The order is lights on,
scan, then power off.

**2. The texture.** It is a 1950s house and a textured finish she cannot identify. EPA: "you can't
tell whether a material contains asbestos simply by looking at it, unless it is labeled. If in
doubt, treat the material as if it contains asbestos and leave it alone", and "Don't saw, sand,
scrape, or drill holes in asbestos-containing materials."[4] This is the stop, and it comes before
the scan matters at all. The rail waits until she knows what the texture is, which is lesson 19's
subject.
:::

The order of the checks is the lesson: what the wall is made of first, because some walls mean stop
before anything else; then the scan, with current in the cables; then power off; then the drill.

## What people get wrong

**"A detector that shows nothing means there's no cable."** It means nothing carrying current was
found. Bosch: a cable needs current to be picked up.[2]

**"Cables always run in straight lines from sockets."** Often, and ESF says that is how buried cables
are required to go. This course did not read the standard behind that, and a detector is still the
check.[1]

**"Main switch off, so everything's dead."** CPSC: a panel is usually live with the main breakers
off. ESF's lights test is for drilling, not for opening anything.[1][5]

**"It's only a small hole."** EPA's don'ts include drilling holes in materials that may contain
asbestos, whatever the size.[4]

## Practice

:::exercise One wall, on paper
Take 20 minutes over this, with a notebook. It is planning only. Don't drill anything for this
exercise. Don't open a consumer unit, panel, socket or fitting, and don't use a volt stick or tester
on anything. If you rent, any real drilling waits until it is your home to repair or your landlord
agrees.

Pick one real job that means drilling a wall. Write the plan in this lesson's order:

1. The house's age and the wall's surface, and whether anything about it means stop.
2. Where the nearest sockets and switches are, and the lines above, below and beside them.
3. How you'll scan: what you'll switch on first, and how many passes.
4. How you'll switch off, and ESF's check that it is off.
5. What dust the wall will make, and how you'll deal with it in HSE's order.
6. What you'll do if you hit a cable, a gas pipe or a water pipe, with the number or the valve.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your plan. Did you decide about the wall's material before the scan, or after? Did you scan before switching off, or after?

The material first: a stop at anything that might be asbestos comes before any scan (HSE, EPA).[3][4]
Then scan with something on the circuit switched on, and only then switch off before drilling.
The other way round, the detector has nothing to find (Bosch).[2]
:::

## For your plan

To the **stop list**: any material you think might be asbestos when you're about to drill, and a
cable or pipe hit while drilling. To the **shut-off map**: where you've found cables and pipes in
walls you've scanned. And for the **repair write-up**: if your one repair involves drilling, this
lesson's order is the plan.
