[Electrical Safety First](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/guidance/advice-for-you/diyers/)
(ESF), a UK charity that campaigns on electrical safety, asked 1,000 electricians about DIY work
they'd been called to put right. It reports: "The most common mistakes people make when attempting
to undertake DIY were cutting through power leads, drilling into wiring, or trying to repair an
electrical item while it is switched on."[1] ESF does not give the survey's date, and the list is
what electricians reported, not a measure of how often DIY goes wrong. But a wall hides what's in
it, and this lesson is about the second of those three: what to do before the bit goes in.

:::callout Before you pick up a tool
This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

The box at the top says to isolate the water or power before you open anything up. A wall you are
about to drill is the nearest this lesson comes to that, because it has cables in it that you can't
see. Drilling is the one job in this course where the wiring is right in front of you, and your
hands still stop at the front of the consumer unit or panel, as lesson 5 set out. Two facts from
there matter more here than anywhere. [CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/5133.pdf), the US
Consumer Product Safety Commission, says panel boxes "are usually electrically live, even with the
main circuit breakers turned off".[5] And HSE's guidance for people who work on electrical systems
does not recommend "non-contact devices such as 'volt sticks'" for proving anything dead.[6]

## The detector, and what it cannot see

A cable and pipe detector is sold for this. This course found no official UK or US consumer page on
detectors, so it uses a maker's guidance for its own devices.

:::predict Before the steps: a detector scanned over a wall on a bright afternoon, with every light off and nothing plugged in, shows green everywhere. Is the wall clear of cables?

No. Step 6 below is why: Bosch says a cable needs current to be picked up, so a cable with nothing
drawing on it gives the detector nothing to find.[2] The scan has told you nothing about cables on
circuits that had nothing switched on, and it has not cleared the wall.
:::

[Bosch](https://www.bosch-diy.com/gb/en/all-about-diy/drill-more-safely), about its own digital
detectors, gives these steps:[2]

1. Take off rings, bracelets and watches, and put your phone out of reach, or they can confuse the
   reading.
2. Hold the whole device flat on the wall.
3. Put your other hand flat on the wall, about 20 to 30 cm away, for grounding. Bosch says "This is
   especially important when looking for power lines", and warns that "If there is insufficient
   grounding, for example, due to insulating footwear, or if you are standing on a conductor, it
   won’t be possible to detect live cables."
4. Move it across the wall with even pressure, along one line at a time.
5. "The device needs to be moved over the same place several times to detect whether there is
   something behind the wall."
6. Switch on electrical items, such as a light or a vacuum cleaner, while you scan. "A cable must
   have a current in order to be picked up by the device."
7. Then: "for your own safety, after detection you should switch off the power supply completely
   before you start drilling."

Bosch doesn't say which items. Since a cable needs current to be found, this course's reading is to
switch on things on the circuits likely to be in that wall: the room's lights, and a lamp in the
nearest socket.

Step 6 carries the mechanism of the whole thing. On Bosch's account, a detector of this kind needs
current in a cable to find it. A cable carrying no current, because nothing on it is switched on or
because its breaker is off, gives it nothing to find. Bosch's own summary of a green light is
"Nothing was detected and you’re good to go", and of a yellow one, "If the light turns yellow, the
result is inconclusive and you need to check the spot again."[2] Read the green line beside step 6:
green means nothing carrying current was found where you scanned, not that there's no cable. Your
detector may work differently; its own instructions decide.

## Where cables run

Cables often run straight up, down or across from sockets and switches. For UK homes, Electrical
Safety First, a UK charity, puts it as a requirement: buried cables are to run horizontally or
vertically from sockets and switches.[1] This course didn't read the wiring standard behind that
line, so it can't tell you that every cable in a given wall follows it, and ESF's page says, just
before it, "A quality cable detector can help track buried cables before you start work."[1] This
course read nothing on where cables run in American walls. So in either country, treat the wall
above, below and beside any socket or switch as the likeliest place for a cable, but not as the only
one.

## Power off, and the check that it is

ESF: "For any DIY near electrical wiring or power supplies, switch off the power at your fuseboard
and, where possible, only use battery-powered tools." And: "To test that the power is off before
beginning non-electrical DIY, plug an appliance in or try switching on lights."[1] Elsewhere the same
page writes "fuseboard (consumer unit)", so the two words mean the same box. That is ESF's test for
non-electrical work such as drilling, and it is the one this course gives you. It is not a way of
proving a circuit dead for work on the wiring, which lesson 5 left to electricians: that needs a
proper tester and training, as HSE's guidance for electrical workers describes.[6] ESF is a British
charity, and this course read no American page on checking the power before non-electrical work; in
a US home, trying the lights is the course's reading of ESF's test, not an American body's advice.

The main switch, which lesson 1 found and lesson 5 explained, is the front of the box and it is
yours to switch. Everything behind the cover still is not, and CPSC's warning that a panel is usually
live with the main breakers off still stands.[5]

### If you hit something

If you hit a cable anyway, stop, and leave that circuit off. A damaged cable in a wall is wiring,
and this course's line is **Call someone**, an electrician, whether or not anything tripped. ESF's
advice on electrical work is the same: "Any electrical work should be left to competent
professionals."[1] If anyone has had a shock, First Aid and CPR lesson 2, "Danger, the call, and
recognising cardiac arrest", has what comes first: the power off before you touch them, then the
call.

If you hit a gas pipe, lesson 9, "Gas: the smell and the valve", has what National Gas says to do in
Great Britain: call the emergency number day or night, even if no gas seems to be escaping. In
Northern Ireland the number is the one in the box at the top of this lesson, 0800 002 001. This
course read no Northern Ireland page on a struck pipe, so its reading is to follow lesson 9's
Northern Ireland steps, which put that call first. In the US, this course read no utility page on a
pipe hit inside a house either. Its reading is to treat it as a leak: leave, and call 911 or your gas
utility once you're outside, as the box at the top says. Don't try to deal with the pipe: SoCalGas,
a US utility, says "DO NOT attempt to control the leak or repair a damaged pipe or meter."[11]
Lesson 9 has the rest of each country's steps.

If you hit a water pipe, the stop tap or main shut-off valve that lesson 1 found is the first move,
and if the water is reaching sockets, switches or the consumer unit, lesson 1's "When the water
reaches the electrics" has the course's rule for going near the box.

## A material that might be asbestos

In Great Britain, [HSE](https://www.hse.gov.uk/asbestos/introduction/index.htm) lists "drilling into
a wall" among the activities that can disturb asbestos, and says any building "built or refurbished
before the year 2000" may contain it, while "Buildings constructed after 2000 are unlikely to contain
asbestos."[3] Its guide says that if you come across any material you think may contain asbestos,
"stop work immediately".[3] That guide is written for people at work; stopping is also what this
course tells a householder. In the US,
[EPA](https://www.epa.gov/asbestos/protect-your-family-exposures-asbestos) says: "Generally, you
can't tell whether a material contains asbestos simply by looking at it, unless it is labeled. If in
doubt, treat the material as if it contains asbestos and leave it alone", and on its list of don'ts:
"Don't saw, sand, scrape, or drill holes in asbestos-containing materials."[4]

A textured ceiling, an old board that looks like plasterboard (drywall), anything in a pre-2000 British home
or an older American one that you are not sure of: don't drill it. That is a stop, not a judgement
call, and lesson 19, "Asbestos, lead paint and radon: what not to disturb", is where the course
teaches what comes next. That stop is this course's line, built on HSE's and EPA's advice. What the
law says about asbestos in your own home, and who may work on it, is lesson 19's, with the legal
label it needs.

## A shelf on the Isle of Wight

The whole routine, on one job. A couple want a shelf on the living room wall of their house on
the Isle of Wight, built in 2004. The wall is plastered, there's a double socket about a metre to the
left of where the shelf will go, the shelf needs three fixings, and they've borrowed a digital
detector and have a cordless drill.

1. **The date.** Built in 2004, so on HSE's line the house is unlikely to contain asbestos.[3] The
   wall is plain plaster, with no textured coating. No reason to stop here.
2. **Scan with the power on.** Rings and watches off, the detector flat, a hand on the wall, the lamp
   on that socket and the room's lights switched on, several passes over the shelf's position,
   following the detector's own instructions.[2] The line straight above the socket gets extra care;
   the shelf's fixings are well to the right of it. They mark what the detector finds.
3. **Power off.** The main switch at the consumer unit, then ESF's check: the lights don't come on.[1]
   With the mains off, a corded drill would have nothing to plug into, so ESF's "battery-powered
   tools" is also the practical choice.
4. **Eyes on.** Eye protection, as lesson 13, "Tools, dust and fumes: a small kit used safely", set
   out.

:::predict Step 5 is the dust. The wall's surface is plaster, and HSE's construction dust sheet puts gypsum, the stuff of plasterboard, under "general" dust, not silica. Does HSE's silica advice have anything to say about this job? Think about what the drill goes through.

It may well. Plaster is only the surface. Behind it there may be brick or concrete block, and HSE
lists those among the silica materials ("bricks, tiles, concrete and mortar").[8] If so, the drill
makes silica dust once it is through the plaster. Three holes is the "occasional short-duration
drilling" row of HSE's table: a dust collector at the bit or an extractor on the drill where
possible, and otherwise a respirator with a factor of 20, which in the UK means FFP3.[7] The couple
do not know what's behind the plaster, so they plan for the silica case.
:::

5. **Dust.** An FFP3 on, with HSE's pre-use check, and a dust collector or an extractor on the drill
   if they have one. Without either, HSE's table leaves the job to the respirator.[7][10] No dry
   sweeping afterwards. HSE's wood dust sheet says not to dry sweep, and to clean up with a vacuum
   rated at least dust class M ("medium hazard"). That sheet is written for wood dust at work, and
   this course applies it to plaster and brick dust as its own reading.[9]
6. **Fixings.** The fixings for that wall, by the fixing maker's instructions. This course read no
   source on fixings, so it gives none.
7. **Power back on**, and the lamp works.

Putting up a shelf this way is **Yours, on conditions**: a scan with the power on, the power off
before drilling, eyes and lungs covered, and a stop at anything that might be asbestos.

## A blind in an older American house

This one has a wrinkle, and you are going to find it before you read it.

In a 1950s house in the American Southwest, a homeowner wants to put up a blind above a bedroom
window. There's a light switch beside the window. The wall above the window has the same swirled
texture as the ceiling. It is a bright afternoon, so the room's lights are off, and nothing is
plugged in on that wall. She runs a borrowed detector along the line above the window, gets green
all the way, switches off the main breaker, and picks up the drill.

:::checkpoint At least two things are wrong. Find the two that matter most, say which source's words answer each, and see if you spot anything else, before you open this.

**1. The scan.** With the room's lights off and nothing plugged in, any cable to that switch, or
running past the window, had no current in it. Bosch: "A cable must have a current in order to be
picked up by the device."[2] So green told her nothing about cables. A switch beside the window is
also a reason to take extra care along the line above it, though the rule ESF describes is British
and this course read nothing on how American walls are wired. The order is lights on, scan, then
power off.

**2. The texture.** It is a 1950s house and a textured finish she cannot identify. EPA: "Generally,
you can't tell whether a material contains asbestos simply by looking at it, unless it is labeled.
If in doubt, treat the material as if it contains asbestos and leave it alone", and "Don't saw,
sand, scrape, or drill holes in asbestos-containing materials."[4] This is the stop, and it comes
before the scan matters at all. The blind waits until she knows what the texture is, which is
lesson 19's subject.

**Also:** she went straight from the breaker to the drill, with no check that the power is off
(ESF's lights test, which in a US home is the course's reading), and nothing for her eyes or the
dust (lesson 13). But the texture stops the job before either matters.
:::

The order of the checks is the lesson: what the wall is made of first, because some walls mean stop
before anything else; then the scan, with current in the cables; then power off; then the drill.

## What people get wrong

**"A detector that shows nothing means there's no cable."** It means nothing carrying current was
found. Bosch: a cable needs current to be picked up.[2]

**"Cables always run in straight lines from sockets."** Often, and for UK homes ESF says that is how
buried cables are required to go. This course did not read the standard behind that, read nothing
on American walls, and a detector is still the check.[1]

**"Main switch off, so everything's dead."** CPSC: a panel is usually live with the main breakers
off. ESF's lights test is for drilling, not for opening anything.[1][5]

**"It's only a small hole."** EPA's don'ts include drilling holes in materials that may contain
asbestos, whatever the size.[4]

## Practice

:::exercise One wall, on paper
Take 20 minutes over this, with a notebook. It is planning only. Don't drill anything for this
exercise. The only thing you touch is paper. You can look at the front of your consumer unit or
panel to see where the main switch is, but no cover, faceplate or fitting comes off, and no tester
or volt stick goes near anything. If you rent, any real drilling waits until it is your home to
repair or your landlord agrees.

Pick one real job that means drilling a wall. Write the plan in this lesson's order:

1. The house's age and the wall's surface, and whether anything about it means stop.
2. Where the nearest sockets and switches are, and the lines above, below and beside them.
3. How you will scan: what you will switch on first, and how many passes.
4. How you will switch off, and ESF's check that it is off.
5. What dust the wall will make, and how you will deal with it in HSE's order.
6. What you will do if you hit a cable, a gas pipe or a water pipe, with the number or the valve.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your plan. Did you decide about the wall's material before the scan, or after? Did you scan before switching off, or after?

The material first: a stop at anything that might be asbestos comes before any scan (HSE, EPA).[3][4]
Then scan with something on the circuit switched on, and only then switch off before drilling.
The other way round, the detector has nothing to find (Bosch).[2]
:::

## For your plan

To the **stop list**: any material you think might be asbestos when you're about to drill, and a
cable or pipe hit while drilling. To the **shut-off map**: where you've found cables and pipes in
walls you've scanned. And for the **repair write-up**: if your one repair involves drilling, this
lesson's order is the plan.
