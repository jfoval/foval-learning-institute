Almost every lesson in this course has ended somewhere in the words "call someone". The pipe that
keeps weeping after a new washer, the breaker that trips again with everything unplugged, the
ceiling that might be asbestos, the gutter you can't reach safely: each one hands the job to a
person you pay. That handover is where the money in home repair goes, and it's where much of the
harm is too, because the person at the door may not be qualified, registered, insured or honest,
and the contract you sign decides what you can do about it. This last lesson gathers the course's
stop points in one place, then goes through choosing, checking, agreeing, paying and complaining,
in the UK and in the US, with the law stated only as far as this course read it.

:::callout Before you pick up a tool
This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

## When to stop: the course's stop points in one place

Every job this course has named carries one of three labels: **Yours**, **Yours, on conditions**,
or **Call someone**. Here are the main Call someone jobs from lessons 1 to 21, with the reason
each lesson gave. Read the middle column before the first: the reason is what tells you whether a job
you've never met belongs on the list.

| The job | Why it's Call someone | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Any gas work beyond what the appliance's user instructions give the user | In Great Britain the law requires competence of anyone and registration of businesses; HSE and the Gas Safe Register advise a registered engineer, and the label follows the advice | 10 |
| Anything behind the cover of a consumer unit or panel, and any wiring | This course teaches no wiring; CPSC says panel boxes "are usually electrically live, even with the main circuit breakers turned off"[17] | 5, 22 |
| A breaker, RCD or GFCI that keeps tripping | ESF (UK) and SCE (US) send it to an electrician | 5 |
| A cable hit while drilling, whether or not anything tripped | It is wiring now | 14 |

| Mould beyond "very small amounts" (GOV.UK) or about 10 square feet (EPA), behind wallpaper or panelling, or from sewage | GOV.UK: "qualified professionals"; EPA: its larger-job guide and experienced help, a professional for sewage | 18 |

Look down the middle column and three kinds of reason turn up. Some stops are the law's: in Great

## Finding and checking a tradesperson

This section includes a legal point about insurance. This is general information, not legal advice.

### In the UK

For finding a trader in England, [Citizens Advice](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/get-more-help/find-a-trader-and-check-you-can-trust-them/) says the best place to start is your local
council: search its website for "approved traders" or "Trading Standards".[2] It lists TrustMark as
"the Government’s trader scheme".[2] TrustMark says "TrustMark is the Government Endorsed Quality
Scheme that covers work a consumer chooses to have carried out in or around their home."[3] This
course read no government page saying so, and reports both as their words.

Then the checks, from its page ["Before you get work done on your home"](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/getting-home-improvements-done/before-you-get-building-work-done/), which applies to England:[1]

- **References.** Get "2 or 3 recent examples of similar work" and the customers' contact details,
  and ring them, because "written references aren’t always genuine".
- **Is the business real?** Ask for a business card or letterhead and full contact details, and
  ring the business to check it exists and the person works for them. Be wary of a mobile number and
  nothing else.
- **Membership.** Check any scheme, competent person scheme or trade association the trader
  names on that body's own website. The Federation of Master Builders, a trade association, puts the
  reason bluntly: "some places claim to be a member when they're not".[4]
- **Insurance.** Ask to see the trader's policies and check they don't run out before the job
  ends. Citizens Advice says "If a trader which has employees doesn’t have employer’s liability
  insurance, they are breaking the law", and to tell your own buildings insurer about the work.
  Insurance itself is Personal Finance lesson 8's subject.

For the trades this course has sent you to, the register is the check:

- **Gas.** Citizens Advice: "If you're getting gas work done, you must use a Gas Safe registered
  engineer".[1] Lesson 10, "Gas: the law and the engineer", showed the whole check: the card, the
  categories on its back, and the Register's Check an Engineer service.
- **Electrical work.** Citizens Advice says "you should use a properly qualified or registered
  electrician".[1] GOV.UK lists the competent person schemes MHCLG has authorised: for electrical
  work in homes they include "Certsure LLP trading as NICEIC" and "NAPIT Registration Limited", among
  others, and GOV.UK says members of an electrical scheme go onto a single register where
  householders can find registered electricians.[5] Check the scheme's own list, reached by your own
  route. What the rules require in Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland is lesson 22's.
- **Plumbing, chimneys, asbestos.** WaterSafe's approved plumbers (lessons 3 and 4); a sweep from HETAS's
  scheme, whose ID you ask to see (lesson 11); and for sprayed coatings, lagging and asbestos insulating board, HSE says removal
  is for "a contractor licensed by HSE" (lesson 19).[16] HSE also says to tell any trade about
  asbestos you know of before they start. This course did not read how to check an HSE licence, so
  its line is to ask to see it, and to ask your council's environmental health officer, whom HSE
  names for asbestos questions at home.

One rule runs under all of these. Digital Literacy lesson 11, "Recognising a con, and the ten
minutes after you clicked", put it as "Do the thing. But by your own route." Here that means you
find the register yourself, and never follow a link or number the trader gave you.

### In the US

The FTC's page ["How To Avoid a Home Improvement Scam"](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-avoid-home-improvement-scam) is the federal starting point:[6]

> Consider only contractors who are licensed and insured. Check with your state or county
> government to confirm a contractor’s license, and ask the contractor for proof of insurance.

Licensing differs by state and city, which is why the check goes to the state or county. This
course read one state's board: California's Contractors State License Board (CSLB), whose guide asks "Did you contact the Contractors State License Board
(CSLB) to check the status of the contractor’s license?", and says workers' compensation insurance
"is required by law if the contractor has employees or workers".[8] Lesson 22 has the licensing
rules this course read for five places.

Two hazards from lesson 19 come with their own checks, both from EPA:

- **Paint in a home built before 1978.** EPA's lead rule requires "anyone paid to perform work that
  disturbs painted surfaces in homes, childcare facilities and preschools built before 1978" to be
  certified, and EPA's page links a search to find a lead-safe certified contractor near you.[14]
- **Asbestos.** Federal law does not require training for work in detached single-family homes,
  though some states and localities do; EPA says homeowners "should ensure that workers they hire to
  handle asbestos are trained and accredited", that each should give proof of accreditation, and
  that it is better to use one firm to inspect and a different one to remove, "so there is no
  conflict of interest".[15]

:::checkpoint A roofer's van has a trade association's logo on the side and a phone number. He hands you a card with a web address where, he says, you can "verify my membership". What do you do with the logo, the card and the address?

Nothing with any of them, as evidence. A logo is a claim, and the Federation of Master Builders says
some firms claim membership they don't have.[4] Find the association's website yourself, by
searching for its name or from a list you trust, and look him up in its own members' list. The
card's web address and phone number are his, so checking him through them checks him against
himself. That is Digital Literacy lesson 11's own route, applied to a van.
:::

## Quotes, estimates and the written contract

This section states law. This is general information, not legal advice.

Citizens Advice draws the line that people blur most often: "A quote is a fixed price, so you’ll know
what you’re getting and how much it will cost. An estimate is just a rough guess, so you might end
up paying more."[1] On its account, a trader can't charge more than a quote unless you ask for extra
work, you agree to extra work they tell you is needed, or they made a genuine mistake writing down or
working out the price. It advises: "Try to get written quotes from at least 3 different traders
before you decide on one."[1] Be cautious, it says, of a price far below the others, which might mean
the trader lacks the skills, is not being honest, or is not quoting for the same work. A good quote, on
its list, gives a fixed total price rather than a daily rate, a breakdown of the work and materials,
how long the price is valid for, and whether it includes VAT.

The FTC says the same about the cheapest bid in the US: get several written estimates, "Don’t
automatically choose the lowest bidder", and ask why if the prices differ a lot.[6] Its contract
list asks for the contractor's name, address, phone and licence number, estimated start and
completion dates, any promises made in conversation, the written right to cancel where it applies,
and no blank spaces.[6][7]

The UK's Consumer Rights Act 2015 puts a floor under every contract in which a trader supplies a
service to a consumer.[10]

- Section 49: the trader "must perform the service with reasonable care and skill".
- Section 50: anything "said or written to the consumer" by or for the trader about itself or the
  service, if you took it into account in deciding, is treated as a term of the contract. Whether a
  claim painted on a van was said to you, this course did not read, so get it into the written quote.
- Section 51: where no price was fixed, "the consumer must pay a reasonable price for the service,
  and no more". Section 52 does the same for time.
- Section 57: a term that tries to exclude the section 49 duty is not binding on you.

In California, a home improvement contract over $500 has to be in writing and carry a list of
notices, set out in Business and Professions Code section 7159.[9] Two of them do most of the
protecting, and the next section is about them.

## Paying

This section states law. This is general information, not legal advice.

Both countries' sources come back to one idea: **your money should not run ahead of the work**.
Citizens Advice gives the reason for paying in stages: "Paying in stages is a good idea, because it
means problems can be put right before you make the final payment."[1] California writes the same
idea into its law, below.

**In England**, Citizens Advice's advice (guidance, not law) is to pay by debit or credit card if you
can, otherwise by bank transfer, and "Don’t pay with cash."[1] Avoid deposits; if the trader wants one for materials, offer to buy the
materials yourself; and on a long job where you can't avoid one, "don’t agree to more than 25%". For
work costing between £100.01 and £30,000 paid by credit card, Citizens Advice says you may be able to
make a "section 75 claim" against the card company if something goes wrong, including for a deposit,
even one under £100. Which ways of paying can be clawed back, and why, is Personal Finance lesson 10.

**In California**, the cap is law, and [section 7159](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=7159) makes a contract that charges a down payment print it in bold type:[9]

> "THE DOWNPAYMENT MAY NOT EXCEED $1,000 OR 10 PERCENT OF THE CONTRACT PRICE, WHICHEVER IS LESS."

The licence board's guide adds that there are "no exceptions for special order materials"; the only
exception is a contractor with a blanket performance and payment bond on file with the board,
which the guide says only "a handful of licensees" have, noted on the board's website.[8] And a contract with
progress payments must carry this: "IT IS AGAINST THE LAW FOR A CONTRACTOR TO COLLECT PAYMENT
FOR WORK NOT YET COMPLETED, OR FOR MATERIALS NOT YET DELIVERED. HOWEVER, A CONTRACTOR MAY REQUIRE A
DOWNPAYMENT."[9]

Keep the two apart: England's 25% is advice, California's cap is law, and neither is the other
country's rule. Elsewhere in the US, the FTC says "Some states actually limit the amount of money a
contractor can ask for as a down payment", and to ask your state or local consumer agency.[6] And the
last payment waits: the FTC says never make it "until the work is done and you’re
satisfied".[7]

### Liens: why paying in full doesn't end it, in California

A lien is a claim against your home. California's statute makes every home improvement contract
carry a "Mechanics Lien Warning", and the [licence board's guide](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/Resources/GuidesAndPublications/HomeImprovementContractsConsumerGuide.pdf) explains it: "Even if the contractor
is paid in full, unpaid subcontractors, suppliers, and laborers involved in the project may record a
mechanics lien and sue the property owner in court to foreclose the lien."[8] And: "A property owner
could be forced to pay twice or have the court sell the home to pay the lien."[8]

The guide's reason is its first sentence on the subject: "Anyone who helps improve property, but is
not paid, may place what is called a mechanics lien on the property."[8] The claim goes with the
unpaid work to the house, whoever you paid. The protections follow from that. Suppliers and
subcontractors must send you a "Preliminary Notice" within 20 days of starting work or delivering
materials; ask the contractor for a list of them; have each sign a lien release when their part is
paid; or pay with a joint check made out to the contractor and the supplier together.[8][9] The
statute also makes the contract promise that, once a portion of the work is paid for, the contractor
gives you a full and unconditional release for that portion before any further payment.[9]

This course read no other state's lien law and no UK source on liens, so it says nothing about
either.

## Cancelling

This section states law. This is general information, not legal advice.

| | UK (Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013) | US federal (FTC Cooling-Off Rule) | California (on top of the federal rule) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Which contracts | Off-premises, such as one made at your home with the trader there, over £42 | Sales at your home of $25 or more, or elsewhere away from the seller's premises of $130 or more, including when you invited the seller | Home improvement contracts over $500; the three- and five-day rights not if negotiated at the contractor's place of business |
| How long | 14 days from the contract for a service; where goods are supplied, even fitted, from the day you get them | Until midnight of the third business day; Saturday counts, Sunday and federal holidays do not | Three business days; five if you are 65 or older; seven for repairing damage from a declared emergency |
| If you were not told | Extended by up to 12 months | The seller must tell you and give you two copies of a cancellation form | The contract must carry the notice |
| Repairs you called for | No right to cancel an urgent repair you asked for, but extra work or goods sold on that visit keep it | Excludes an emergency where you made the contact, with your handwritten waiver, and repairs to your personal property you asked for; extras keep it | The licence board's guide: the right ends once work starts on emergency repairs you asked for |
| Money back | Pay a proportion for work you asked to start in the 14 days | Refund within 10 business days | Refund within 10 days |

Sources: the UK column, regulations 5, 27(3), 28 to 31 and 36;[11] the federal column, 16 CFR Part 429
and the FTC's page;[12][13] California, section 7159, Civil Code section 1689.5 (a "senior citizen" is
65 or older) and the board's guide.[9][8]

Three points in that table are the ones people miss.

**The 14 days and early work.** For the work itself, [the Regulations](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3134/part/3) do not stop it starting in the 14 days, but the
trader "must not begin" unless you have made an express request, and for an off-premises contract the
request must be "on a durable medium", which the Regulations define to include paper or email.[11]
If you cancel, you pay for what was done, in proportion. And once a service-only job is finished, if you
asked for the early start and acknowledged you'd lose the right, the right is gone (regulation
36(2)). If the trader never gave you the cancellation information, you pay nothing for it.

**Urgent repairs.** Regulation 28(1)(e) takes away the right to cancel "contracts where the consumer
has specifically requested a visit from the trader for the purpose of carrying out urgent repairs or
maintenance". Regulation 28(2) then keeps the right for extra services, and for goods other than the
replacement parts the repair needed, sold on that same visit.[11] The FTC's rule has the same shape
for repairs you asked for, and says "personal property"; this course did not read whether a repair to
the house itself counts as that, so it says nothing either way.[12]

**States can give more.** The FTC's rule leaves a state law in force unless it is "directly
inconsistent", and a state right to cancel that isn't "substantially the same or greater" counts as
that.[12] On this course's reading of the two texts, California's longer periods are not
inconsistent, which is why the California column sits on top of the federal one.

:::checkpoint A homeowner on Humberside rings a plumber at 7am because a pipe under the bath has burst. He fixes it for £180, and while he's there sells her a new shower mixer from his van for £260 and fits it before he leaves. She signs for both at her kitchen table. The next day she has second thoughts about both. Which one does she have a right to cancel, on the Regulations as this lesson read them?

The shower mixer, not the pipe repair. She specifically asked him to come for an urgent repair, so
regulation 28(1)(e) removes the right to cancel that. The mixer is a good other than a part the
repair needed, supplied on the same visit, so regulation 28(2) keeps the right for it: it's an
off-premises contract, over £42, and she's well inside 14 days.[11] What cancelling something
already fitted costs her, and what happens to it, is set by regulations this course did not read, so
the answer stops at the right itself.
:::

## When the work is wrong

This section states law. This is general information, not legal advice.

**In the UK**, the [Consumer Rights Act](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/part/1/chapter/4) gives you a sequence.[10] If the service wasn't done with
reasonable care and skill, you can require the trader to do it again, "to the extent necessary to
complete its performance in conformity with the contract" (section 55). The trader must do that
within a reasonable time, without significant inconvenience to you, and "must bear any necessary
costs incurred in doing so (including in particular the cost of any labour or materials)". Only if
putting it right is impossible, or the trader fails to do it within a reasonable time, do you move to
a price reduction, which can, where appropriate, be the full price, refunded within 14 days of the trader agreeing
(section 56). The Act doesn't stop you seeking other remedies too, but this course did not read how
those work. Citizens Advice's practical order:[1] as soon as something's wrong, ask the trader to put
it right, agree how, and get that agreement in writing.

**In the US**, the FTC's advice is to try the contractor first, then to follow any phone call with a
letter sent by certified mail with a return receipt, and keep copies.[6] If that fails, it points to
your state attorney general or local consumer protection office. In California, the licence board's
guide says you have four years to file a complaint with it about a faulty project.[8]

## The pattern at the door

Personal Safety lesson 5 taught the pressure pattern and the call-back rule, and Personal Finance
lesson 10 taught why the way you pay decides whether you can get money back. This lesson adds only the
signs the FTC lists for contractors.

From its home improvement page: scammers knock on your door saying they're in the area, say "they
have materials left over from a previous job", push for an immediate decision, want everything up
front or cash only, and suggest a lender they know.[6] One sign runs against what you'd expect:
"Scammers ask you to get any required building permits."[6] The FTC gives the sign without a reason,
and this course supplies none.

From [its page on scams after storms and disasters](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-avoid-scams-after-weather-emergencies-and-natural-disasters), the extra signs are a claim not to need a licence,
a discount "only if you sign a contract right away", asking you to sign over your insurance check,
insisting on payment by wire transfer, gift card, payment app, cryptocurrency or cash, a "blank
contract", and a fee to help you qualify for FEMA relief. The FTC's answers: "FEMA doesn’t charge
application fees", and "never sign your insurance check over to a contractor"; instead, arrange for
your bank to pay the contractor stage by stage once you've approved each stage.[7] Never sign papers
with blanks in them, and never transfer your deed to anyone without advice.[6]

## A boiler in Nottinghamshire

This worked example is set in England, and uses the law and advice read for England and for Great
Britain. This is general information, not legal advice.

Megan owns a 1970s semi in Nottinghamshire. Her combi boiler is eighteen years old and has lost
pressure twice this winter, and her engineer's last service note said it was near the end of its
life.

1. **Who.** A boiler is a gas appliance, so in Great Britain whoever fits it as a business must be
   competent and on the Gas Safe Register, and lesson 10's check applies to each engineer who
   quotes: the card, the boiler category on its back, and the Register's own site.
2. **Three quotes.** Engineer A quotes a fixed £2,850 including VAT, broken down into the boiler, the
   flue, removing the old one and flushing the system, valid for 30 days. Engineer B gives "an
   estimate of about £2,400, depending on what we find". Engineer C quotes £1,650, total only.
3. **Comparing.** B is an estimate, so on Citizens Advice's account it is a rough guess and the final
   bill may be higher. C is far below the others with no breakdown, which is the case Citizens Advice
   says to be cautious about; it may not cover the same work. She asks C what the price includes. The
   answer leaves out removing the old boiler and flushing the system. She chooses A.[1]
4. **Signing.** Engineer A comes round to measure up, and she signs his contract at her kitchen
   table. That makes it an off-premises contract, over £42, so she can cancel. Because it includes the
   boiler itself, the Regulations treat it as a sales contract, and her 14 days run from the day the
   boiler is delivered, not the day she signed.[11]
5. **Early start.** She wants it in next week. So she emails him asking him to start then; an email is
   a durable medium. On this course's reading, if she cancelled after he'd started, she'd pay for the
   fitting done, in proportion; what happens to a boiler already fitted is set by regulations this
   course did not read.[11]
6. **Paying.** He asks for £285 up front for the boiler. Citizens Advice's advice is to avoid a
   deposit where she can; she pays it by credit card, which Citizens Advice says may give her a
   section 75 claim, and she pays the rest when it is fitted and working.[1] Her last step, from
   Citizens Advice's list, is telling her buildings insurer about the work.

## A hailstorm in Riverside County, with a gap for you

This worked example is set in California, and uses federal rules and California's. This is general
information, not legal advice.

Dolores is 67 and owns a single-storey house in Riverside County, California. The morning after a
hailstorm, a man knocks. He's "working on a roof in the area", has "materials left over from that
job", and can fix her damaged shingles for $16,000, with a discount if she signs today and pays half
in cash. He adds that she can sign her insurance check over to him when it comes, "to save you the
hassle". No state of emergency has been declared for the storm.

Run him through the FTC's lists first. "In the area", leftover materials, a discount only if she
signs right away, half up front in cash, and signing over the insurance check: that is five signs from
the FTC's two pages before a ladder has come off his van.[6][7] She says no and closes the door. If
he'd offered a number to ring for someone to vouch for him, Personal Safety lesson 5's call-back rule
is why she wouldn't use it.

A week later she's found a roofer herself and checked his licence on the CSLB's website. He comes to
the house, and she signs his contract at her kitchen table. It's also for $16,000.

:::predict Before you read on: what is the most this second roofer can ask her for as a down payment, and how many days does she have to cancel?

The cap is $1,000 or 10 percent of the price, whichever is less.[9] Ten percent of $16,000 is $1,600,
so the cap is $1,000. For cancelling: she signed at home and she's 65 or over, so California gives her
five business days rather than three.[9] The federal rule's three business days also apply, and the
FTC's rule lets a state give more.[12] If a state of emergency had been declared for the storm, the
contract would carry California's seven-day notice instead, because it's for repairing damage from a
declared emergency.[9]
:::

Now the lien. The roofer will buy shingles from a supplier and may bring in a subcontractor for the
gutters. What should Dolores ask for, and when does she pay each stage? Work it out before you open
the answer.

:::checkpoint Her protections against a lien, in order.

Ask the roofer for a list of every subcontractor and supplier, and when each starts or delivers. Watch
for their Preliminary Notices, which can arrive up to 20 days after they start or deliver. Pay each
stage only for work done, as the contract's payment schedule says, and at each payment get a lien
release for that portion, which the contract must promise. For a supplier who has sent a notice,
she can pay with a joint check made out to the roofer and the supplier.[8][9] Paying the roofer in
full, on time, does not protect her by itself: that is the point of the warning.
:::

The insurance check stays in her name. The FTC's answer is to arrange with her bank or credit union
for a certificate of completion, so the bank pays the contractor for each stage after she has
approved it.[7] What her policy covers is Personal Finance lesson 8's question.

## What people get wrong

**"The logo on the van proves membership."** Check the body's own members' list, found by your own
route (FMB; Citizens Advice).[4][1]

**"TrustMark is a government body."** TrustMark describes itself as "the Government Endorsed Quality
Scheme"; this course read no government page saying so.[3]

**"I called them in, so I can't cancel anything."** In the UK, only the urgent repair you asked for
loses the right; extras sold on the same visit keep it (regulation 28).[11]

**"Paying in full protects me from liens."** Not in California, on the licence board's account.[8]

**"It's a good sign when the contractor asks me to get the permit."** The FTC lists it as a sign of a
scam.[6]

**"Everything on the stop list is there because it's illegal."** Some stops are law and many are
advice or this course's own line; each belongs on the list, but say which (lesson 22).
