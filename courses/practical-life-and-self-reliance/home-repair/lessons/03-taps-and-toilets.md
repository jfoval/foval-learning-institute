A dripping tap and a toilet that won't stop running are the commonest repairs in a house, and
they're the ones where people most often buy the wrong part. A washer from the hardware shop does
nothing for a tap that has no washer in it, and a part made for an American toilet is a guess in a
British one. Lesson 2 taught you to prove a leak and find it. This lesson fixes the ones a
householder can fix, and it names, for every method, the source and the kind of fitting it was
written for, because the method that suits one tap can do nothing for the next.

:::callout Before you pick up a tool
This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

Most repairs here open something, so the callout's "isolate the water" is the first step of each.
It means the stop tap (stopcock) in a UK home or the main shut-off valve in a US one, from lesson 1,
How a house works, and where it switches off, or a small isolation valve on the pipe to one tap, where
the installer fitted one. Isolating is half the step. The other half is checking that
it worked, and one of the sources below builds that check into its method.

## Two kinds of tap

This course read a method for each of two kinds of tap, and the two fail differently. That is the
whole reason to diagnose before you buy anything.

**A washer tap** has a washer inside it, and washers wear. Southern Water, an English water
company, says that while a dripping tap can have a few causes, most of the time the washer has
become degraded over time, and replacing it can be a simple fix.[1] EPA's WaterSense programme says
the same of American faucets (the US word for taps): "Old and worn faucet washers and gaskets
frequently cause leaks in faucets."[3] Neither source describes how a washer seals or exactly what
wears, so this course does not either.

**A ceramic-disc tap** has a cartridge instead. Bristan, a UK tap maker, puts continued dripping from
one of its own kitchen mixer taps down to what it calls the ceramic disc valves or cartridge, and
its leaflet's maintenance panel shows the fix as a new cartridge, not a washer.[2] The replacement
cartridge is usually specific to the make and model of the tap.[2]

How do you tell which you have? This course read no source that gives a test from the outside of a
tap. Two things do tell you. The first is the paperwork: if you have the maker's leaflet, or the
maker's name is on the tap, the maker says what is inside. The second is what you find when you take
the cover off, with the water isolated. A hexagonal nut and a tap top that lifts out whole, as
Southern Water describes, is a washer tap. A shroud and a cartridge, as Bristan shows, is a ceramic
one.[1][2]

:::predict Before you read the method: a washer tap drips, and you've just fitted a new washer and put it back together. What's the one mistake in the reassembly that the source warns can undo the repair?
Screwing it back together too tightly. Southern Water says to be careful not to, because you may
damage the new washer.[1] The instinct after a drip is to tighten everything, and here it works
against you.
:::

## A washer tap, step by step

This is [Southern Water's method](https://www.southernwater.co.uk/help-and-support/how-to-fix-a-dripping-tap/),
written by an English water company for a UK washer tap: the kind with a cover, a hexagonal nut, a
jumper plate and a washer.[1] It is **Yours**, on Southern Water's advice.

1. Turn off the water at your stop tap. Southern Water says to do this always, before taking the
   tap apart, to avoid a potential mess.
2. Turn the dripping tap on full. A few drops may come out before it stops completely.
3. Unscrew the tap's cover to reveal the hexagonal nut. Loosen the nut with a spanner and lift out
   the whole tap top. Lay the parts out in the order you took them off, so they go back together
   the same way.
4. Lift out the jumper plate, unscrew the nut that holds the washer (Southern Water says to use
   pliers), and replace the old washer with a new one.
5. Put the tap back together, not too tightly, because you may damage the new washer. Turn the
   water back on at the stop tap. The drip should have stopped.
6. If it hasn't, Southern Water says to hire a plumber "qualified to meet the regulations for
   working safely with drinking water".[1]

Step 2 is easy to skip, and it is your check. If the tap doesn't stop running after you've
turned the stop tap off, the water to that tap is not off, and you do not open it. WaterSafe does say
a stop tap may take a few minutes to stop the water altogether, so give it that.[5] Southern Water does not say why a tap might keep running beyond that, and this course will not guess. It can say that
some UK homes have water tanks in the loft: the Energy Saving Trust says a regular boiler has two
extra header tanks, usually installed there.[6] Lesson 6, Heating and hot water, covers how those
systems are laid out. Until you know how your hot water reaches the tap, a hot tap that won't stop
is a reason to stop there and ask a plumber.

:::checkpoint A friend in Devon has a pair of old separate taps on a bathroom basin. The hot one drips. She turns off the stop tap under the kitchen sink, turns the hot tap on full, and ten minutes later it's still running as hard as ever. What should she do, on this lesson's reading?
Stop. Southern Water's second step exists to show the water is off, and here it shows it isn't.
Ten minutes at full flow is past the few minutes WaterSafe allows for a stop tap. She should not unscrew anything. She should put the stop tap back on slowly, and then either find out how the hot
water in that house is supplied (lesson 6) or call a plumber. Opening a tap that still has water
behind it is the mess Southern Water's first step is there to prevent.
:::

## A ceramic-disc tap

[Bristan's leaflet](https://www.bristan.com/product-files/576945/fitting-instructions.pdf) is for
one of its own kitchen mixer taps, and its layout only survives as text well enough to be read for
meaning, so nothing from it is quoted here. Its maintenance panel shows, for that tap, turning off
the isolation valve on the flexible pipe that feeds it, taking off the shroud, taking out the
cartridge and fitting a replacement.[2] The same leaflet says isolation valves should be fitted on
the tap's supply pipes, so that it can be maintained later.[2]

That is one maker's method for one maker's tap. For any other ceramic-disc tap, the job is **Yours,
on conditions**: that you have the maker's instructions for that tap and the cartridge made for it.
Without both, it is a job for a plumber.

## American faucets, and showerheads

[EPA](https://www.epa.gov/watersense/fix-leak-week) gives no step-by-step for a faucet. It says
many tutorials exist online for a wide variety of faucets, and adds one tip: "Don't forget to turn
off the water line before you start!"[3] This course read none of the tutorials EPA links to, so it
has no US faucet method to give. For a US faucet the job is **Yours, on conditions**: the maker's
instructions for your model.

A leaking showerhead is simpler. EPA says some can be fixed by making sure the showerhead is tightly
connected to the pipe stem and sealing the thread with pipe tape (thread-seal tape).[3] That is
**Yours**, on EPA's advice. A shower that drips when it isn't in use is different: EPA calls that a
valve leak and sends it to an experienced handyperson or a licensed plumber, so it is **Call someone**.[3]

## The running toilet

Here the two countries' toilets differ, and the advice for one doesn't fix the other. Lesson 2's
dye test tells you whether a toilet leaks from the tank into the bowl in either country.[3] What
comes next depends on the toilet.

**In a US toilet,** the part EPA names is the flapper, a simple rubber valve seal that holds water
in the tank and lets it into the bowl when you flush. Because the rubber wears, the flapper "should
be checked periodically and replaced at least every five years".[3] A worn one can make the toilet
flush on its own or leak silently. EPA calls replacing it a quick fix. Its tip is to take the old
flapper, or a picture of it, to the shop so that the new one fits, and to check the width and
whether it has a foam float. The owner's manual or the maker's website lists the right part.[3] EPA
gives no step-by-step on the page, and this course read none. It is **Yours, on conditions**: the
flapper made for your toilet, and the water off.

**In a UK toilet,** [United Utilities](https://www.unitedutilities.com/help-and-support/your-water-supply/your-pipes/leakage/inside-leaks/)
names "modern push button toilet cisterns" among the biggest causes of leaks in homes and businesses, and says "Nearly 80% of all leaks reported to us last year were leaky loos!"[4] On an older cistern, the sign
to look for is the overflow pipe that goes through the outside wall. If it drips, United Utilities
says the cistern is faulty and needs repairing.[4] This course read no source on the parts of a UK
cistern or how they fail, so it gives no parts diagram and no method. Prove the leak with the dye
test and the overflow check, then follow the cistern maker's instructions if you have them, or use
a WaterSafe approved plumber, which is where United Utilities sends its customers.[4] With the
maker's instructions, it's **Yours, on conditions**; without them, **Call someone**.

United Utilities also tells metered customers that if they repair a leak within six weeks they may
be able to claim for the water lost.[4] That is its own scheme for its own customers. This course did not read other companies' allowances, so ask yours.

:::checkpoint A reader in Gwynedd has been reading a US website about toilets. Her push-button cistern leaks into the bowl on the dye test, and she's about to buy a flapper. What would you tell her?
That the flapper advice is EPA's, written for American toilets whose part is a flapper, and a UK
push-button cistern is the design United Utilities singles out. This course read no source on
what's inside it, so it cannot say which part has failed, and a flapper is a guess. The dye test
has done its job: she knows it leaks. The next step is the cistern maker's instructions, or a
WaterSafe approved plumber.
:::

## A kitchen tap in a Victorian terrace

This is the simpler of the lesson's two worked cases.

In a Victorian terrace in West Yorkshire, the cold tap on the kitchen sink drips about once a
second. It is an old pillar tap, one of a separate pair. Lesson 2's arithmetic puts that at more than
3,000 US gallons, over 11,000 litres, a year on EPA's figure.[3]

**Which kind of tap?** There is no leaflet, and the maker's name has worn off. So the owner isolates
it first, then looks. She turns off the stop tap under the sink, turns the cold tap on full, and
waits: a few drops, then nothing. The water is off.[1] She unscrews the cover and finds a hexagonal
nut. That is Southern Water's kind of tap, a washer tap.[1]

**The fix.** She follows Southern Water's steps: nut loosened with a spanner, tap top lifted out,
parts laid out in order, jumper plate lifted, nut undone, washer replaced, tap reassembled without
overtightening.[1] Then the stop tap goes back on, slowly.

**The check.** The drip has stopped. Had it not, Southern Water's advice would be a plumber
qualified for drinking-water work, not a harder turn of the handle.[1]

## A newer bathroom with two faults

This is the harder case, and it stops partway for you.

A couple buy a 1990s semi in Essex. In the bathroom, the single-lever mixer tap on the basin drips,
and outside, the overflow pipe from the upstairs toilet drips onto the path. In a kitchen drawer
they find a maker's leaflet for the basin tap.

**The tap.** The leaflet says the tap has a ceramic disc cartridge, and under the basin there are
two small isolation valves on the flexible pipes that feed it.

:::predict Before reading on: do Southern Water's washer steps apply to this tap? If not, what does, and what label does the job get?
No. Southern Water's steps are for a washer tap, with a hexagonal nut, a jumper plate and a washer,
and the leaflet says this one has a cartridge.[1] The route is the one Bristan's leaflet shows for
its own tap: isolate at the valves under the basin, check the tap has stopped, then take off the
shroud and change the cartridge for the one made for that model.[2] Because the couple have the
maker's instructions, it's **Yours, on conditions**: they follow those instructions and buy that
maker's cartridge. If the leaflet had been missing, it would be a job for a plumber.
:::

**The toilet.** The overflow drip is United Utilities' sign of a faulty cistern.[4] The couple do the
dye test anyway, to see whether the cistern is also leaking into the bowl, and the bowl stays
clear. So the cistern is losing water out of the overflow, not into the pan. Neither fact tells
them which part inside has failed, because this course read nothing on the parts of a UK cistern.
There is no leaflet for the cistern. So it is **Call someone**: a WaterSafe approved plumber.[4]

What the case teaches is the order. Name the fitting before you buy anything, use the method
written for that fitting, and where no method was written for it, don't borrow one.

## What people get wrong

**"If it drips, turn it off harder."** Neither source read here suggests forcing a tap shut. Their
fix is the washer in a washer tap and the cartridge in a ceramic one.[1][2] Force comes up in the
sources only as a warning: Southern Water's, against screwing a repaired tap back together too
tightly, and WaterSafe's, against forcing a stop tap.[1][5]

**"A drip means a new washer."** Only in a washer tap. Bristan's ceramic tap has no washer, and its
leaflet puts a continuing drip down to the cartridge.[2]

**"Toilet advice is toilet advice."** The flapper is the part EPA names in American toilets. United
Utilities blames modern push-button cisterns in the UK.[3][4] This course read nothing on how a UK
cistern works, and buying a US part for one is a guess.

**"The stop tap's off, so the tap is safe to open."** Only once the tap has stopped running.
Southern Water's second step is the check, and it is there because the answer is sometimes no.[1]

**"A dripping shower head just needs tape."** Only if it leaks at the joint. EPA sends a shower that
drips when it's off, a valve leak, to a handyperson or plumber.[3]

## Practice

:::exercise Name every tap and toilet
Take 15 minutes over this. It opens nothing, so it suits a renter too.

1. List every tap in your home. Next to each, write what you know about its kind: the maker's name
   if it is on the tap, whether you have a leaflet for it, and "unknown" if you don't. Don't take
   any tap apart for this exercise. If you later do, isolate it first and check the check, and only
   if it's your home to repair or your landlord agrees.

2. Look under each basin and sink for small isolation valves on the pipes feeding the taps, and
   note which taps have them.

3. For each toilet, note whether it is a US toilet with a tank or a UK cistern, and, in a UK home,
   find where its overflow pipe comes out through the wall, if it has one, and whether it drips.
:::

## For your plan

This lesson adds a line to your map: which taps are washer taps, which are ceramic and whose they
are, and where the isolation valves sit. It adds a line to your calendar: in a US home, the flapper
checked and changed at least every five years (EPA); in a UK home, a look at each overflow pipe
when you check the stop tap. And it adds a drawer for the leaflets, because the leaflet decides
which label a job gets.
