Dripping taps and worn toilet flappers are among the common household leaks that the US
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) names,[3] and both are easy to get wrong in one particular
way: by buying a part before you know what's inside. A washer from the hardware shop does nothing
for a tap that has no washer in it, and a part made for an American toilet is a guess in a British
one. Lesson 2 taught you to prove a leak and find it. This lesson fixes the ones a householder can
fix, and it names, for every method, the source and the kind of fitting it was written for, because
the method that suits one tap can do nothing for the next.

:::callout Before you pick up a tool
This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

Most repairs here open something, so the callout's "isolate the water" is the first step of each.
It means the stop tap (stopcock) in a UK home or the main shut-off valve in a US one, from lesson 1,
How a house works, and where it switches off, or a small isolation valve on the pipe to one tap, where
the installer fitted one. Isolating is half the step. The other half is checking that
it worked, and one of the sources below has a step that lets you check it.

## Two kinds of tap

This course read a method for each of two kinds of tap, and the two fail differently. That is the
whole reason to diagnose before you buy anything.

**A washer tap** has a washer inside it, and washers wear. Southern Water, an English water
company, says that while a dripping tap can have a few causes, most of the time the washer has
become degraded over time, and replacing it can be a simple fix.[1] EPA's WaterSense programme says
the same of American faucets (the US word for taps): "Old and worn faucet washers and gaskets
frequently cause leaks in faucets."[3] Neither source describes how a washer seals or exactly what
wears, so this course does not either.

**A ceramic-disc tap** has a cartridge instead. Bristan, a UK tap maker, says that for one of its
own single-lever sink taps a drip for a short time after use is normal, from water left in the tap
body, and that a drip that continues is possibly due to what it calls the ceramic disc valves or
cartridge.[2] Its leaflet's maintenance panel shows cleaning the cartridge, not changing a
washer.[2]

How do you tell which you have? This course read no source that gives a test from the outside of a
tap. Two things do tell you. The first is the paperwork: if you have the maker's leaflet, or the
maker's name is on the tap, the maker says what is inside. The second is what you find inside, with
the water isolated. Here is this course's own reading of the two sources: if the tap top comes out
and under it you find a small plate, which Southern Water calls the jumper plate, and a washer,
it's a washer tap; if you find a cartridge under the shroud (the cover over the cartridge), as
Bristan shows, it's a ceramic one.[1][2] A nut on its own does not tell you, because Bristan's tap
has one too. Getting inside is a method in itself, and the only one this course read that doesn't
need the maker's leaflet is Southern Water's, for a tap whose cover unscrews to show a hexagonal
nut. A tap that doesn't open that way, such as a lever held on by a small screw in its handle, stays
shut until you have its maker's instructions. That rule is this course's.

:::predict Before you read the method: a washer tap drips, and you've just fitted a new washer and put it back together. What's the one mistake in the reassembly that the source warns can undo the repair?
Screwing it back together too tightly. Southern Water says to be careful not to, because you may
damage the new washer.[1] The instinct after a drip is to tighten everything, and here it works
against you.
:::

## A washer tap, step by step

This is [Southern Water's method](https://web.archive.org/web/20260515022127/https://www.southernwater.co.uk/help-and-support/how-to-fix-a-dripping-tap/),
written by an English water company for a UK washer tap: the kind with a cover, a hexagonal nut, a
jumper plate and a washer.[1] It is **Yours**, on Southern Water's advice.

1. Turn off the water at your stop tap. Southern Water says to do this always, before taking the
   tap apart, to avoid a potential mess.
2. Turn the dripping tap on full. A few drops may come out before it stops completely.
3. Unscrew the tap's cover to reveal the hexagonal nut. Loosen the nut with a spanner and lift out
   the whole tap top. Lay the parts out in the order you took them off, so they go back together
   the same way.
4. Lift out the jumper plate, unscrew the nut with pliers (Southern Water's tool), and replace the
   old washer with a new one.
5. Put the tap back together, not too tightly, because you may damage the new washer. Turn the
   water back on at the stop tap. The drip should have stopped.
6. If it hasn't, Southern Water says to hire a plumber "qualified to meet the regulations for
   working safely with drinking water".[1]

Step 2 is easy to skip. Southern Water gives it without a reason, and this course uses it as your
check: if the tap does not stop running after you've turned the stop tap off, the water to that tap
is not off, and you do not open it. That rule is this course's. WaterSafe does say a stop tap may
take a few minutes to stop the water altogether, so give it that.[5] Southern Water does not say
why a tap might keep running beyond that, and this course will not guess. The Energy Saving Trust
says a regular boiler has two extra header tanks to store cold water, usually installed in the
loft,[6] but no source read here says whether those tanks feed a tap that the stop tap doesn't
control. Lesson 7, Heating: boilers, radiators and furnaces, covers how those systems are laid out. Until you know how
your hot water reaches the tap, a hot tap that won't stop is a reason to stop there and ask a
plumber.

:::checkpoint A friend in Devon has a pair of old separate taps on a bathroom basin. The hot one drips. She turns off the stop tap under the kitchen sink, turns the hot tap on full, and ten minutes later it's still running as hard as ever. What should she do, on this lesson's reading?
Stop. Southern Water's second step, as this course uses it, shows whether the water is off, and
here it shows it is not. Ten minutes at full flow is past the few minutes WaterSafe says a stop tap
may take.[5] She should not unscrew anything.

She should turn the hot tap off, then put the stop tap back on slowly, since WaterSafe says to turn
a stop tap on and off slowly and never force it.[5] Then she can either find out how the hot water
in that house is supplied (lesson 7) or call a plumber. Opening a tap that still has water behind
it is the mess Southern Water's first step is there to prevent.
:::

## A ceramic-disc tap

[Bristan's leaflet](https://www.bristan.com/product-files/576945/fitting-instructions.pdf) is for
one of its own single-lever sink taps. It is laid out in picture panels, so this lesson paraphrases
it rather than quoting it. Its maintenance panel shows, for that tap: turn off the isolation valve
on the flexible pipe that feeds it, undo the small grub screw that holds the handle (the leaflet
shows a hex key) and take the handle off, take off the shroud and the retaining nut, and lift out
the cartridge. Then soak the cartridge in a solution until the scale is gone (the leaflet does not
name one), put it back with the nut and shroud, refit the handle, and turn the water on and let it
run for a few minutes.[2] The leaflet doesn't show fitting a new cartridge. For spare parts it
sends you to Bristan's website to search for your product.[2] The same leaflet tells the installer
to fit isolation valves on the tap's supply pipes, so that it can be maintained later.[2]

That is one maker's method for one maker's tap. For any other ceramic-disc tap, the job is **Yours,
on conditions**: that you have the maker's instructions for that tap, and any part they name.
Without the instructions, it is a job for a plumber.

## American faucets, and showerheads

[EPA](https://www.epa.gov/watersense/fix-leak-week) gives no step-by-step for a faucet. It says
many tutorials exist online for a wide variety of faucets, and adds one tip: "Don't forget to turn
off the water line before you start!"[3] This course read none of the tutorials EPA links to, so it
has no US faucet method to give. For a US faucet the job is **Yours, on conditions**: the maker's
instructions for your model.

A leaking showerhead is simpler. EPA says some can be fixed by making sure the showerhead is tightly
connected to the pipe stem and using pipe tape to secure it.[3] That is **Yours**, on EPA's advice.
A shower that drips when it isn't in use is different: EPA calls that a valve leak and sends it to
an experienced handyperson or a licensed plumber, so it is **Call someone**.[3]

## The running toilet

Here the two countries' advice differs, and this course read nothing that says one country's part
fits the other's toilet. EPA wrote
lesson 2's dye test for American toilets.[3] Using it on a UK cistern is this course's own step, as
lesson 2 said: the test only asks whether water gets from the cistern into the bowl without a
flush. What comes next depends on the toilet.

**In a US toilet,** the part EPA names is the flapper, a simple rubber valve seal that holds water
in the tank and lets it into the bowl when you flush. EPA says the flapper "should be checked
periodically and replaced at least every five years".[3] A worn one can make the toilet flush on
its own or leak silently. EPA calls replacing it a quick fix. Its tip is to take the old flapper, or
a picture of it, to the shop so that the new one fits, and to check the width and whether it has a
foam float. The owner's manual, if you have it, or the maker's website has information on
replacement parts.[3] EPA gives no step-by-step on the page, and this course read none. It is
**Yours, on conditions**: the flapper made for your toilet, and the water isolated first, which is
this course's rule from the callout. EPA adds that a hardware store or a licensed plumber can help
if you need it.[3]

**In a UK toilet,** [United Utilities](https://www.unitedutilities.com/help-and-support/your-water-supply/your-pipes/leakage/inside-leaks/)
names "modern push button toilet cisterns" among the biggest causes of leaks in homes and
businesses, and says "Nearly 80% of all leaks reported to us last year were leaky loos!" (the page
does not say which year).[4] On an older cistern, the sign to look for is the overflow pipe that goes
through the outside wall. If it drips, United Utilities says the cistern is faulty and needs
repairing.[4] This course read no source on the parts of a UK cistern or how they fail, so it gives
no parts diagram and no method. Prove the leak with the overflow check and, as this course's own
step, the dye test, then follow the cistern maker's instructions if you have them, or use a
WaterSafe approved plumber, which is where United Utilities sends its customers.[4] With the
maker's instructions, it's **Yours, on conditions**; without them, **Call someone**.

United Utilities also tells metered customers that if they repair a leak within six weeks they may
be able to claim for the water lost.[4] That is its own scheme for its own customers. This course did not read other companies' allowances, so ask yours.

:::checkpoint A reader in Gwynedd has been reading a US website about toilets. Her push-button cistern leaks into the bowl on the dye test, and she's about to buy a flapper. What would you tell her?
That the flapper advice is EPA's, written for American toilets whose part is a flapper, and a UK
push-button cistern is the design United Utilities singles out. This course read no source on
what's inside it, so it cannot say which part has failed, and a flapper is a guess. The dye test,
used on a UK cistern as this course's own step, has done its job: she knows it leaks. The next step
is the cistern maker's instructions, or a WaterSafe approved plumber.
:::

## A kitchen tap in a Victorian terrace

This is the simpler of the lesson's two worked cases.

In a Victorian terrace in West Yorkshire, the cold tap on the kitchen sink drips about once a
second. It is an old pillar tap (the upright kind with its own handle), one of a separate pair. EPA
puts one drip a second at more than 3,000 US gallons a year, which lesson 2 converted to over 11,000
litres.[3]

**Which kind of tap?** There is no leaflet, and the maker's name has worn off. So the owner isolates
it first, then looks. She turns off the stop tap under the sink, turns the cold tap on full, and
waits: a few drops, then nothing, so the check passes.[1] She unscrews the cover and finds a
hexagonal nut. That alone does not settle it, so she loosens the nut with a spanner, lifts out the
tap top, lays the parts out in order, and finds a jumper plate and a washer. That is Southern
Water's kind of tap, a washer tap.[1]

**The fix.** She follows the rest of Southern Water's steps: jumper plate lifted, nut undone with
pliers, washer replaced, tap reassembled without overtightening.[1] She closes the tap, and the stop
tap goes back on, slowly.[5]

**The check.** The drip has stopped. Had it not, Southern Water's advice would be a plumber
qualified for drinking-water work.[1]

## A bathroom with two faults

This is the harder case, and it stops partway for you.

A couple buy a 1960s semi in Essex. The basin and its single-lever mixer tap are newer than the
house, but the toilet is an older one, with an overflow pipe through the outside wall. The basin tap
drips, and outside, the overflow drips onto the path. In a kitchen drawer they find a maker's
leaflet for the basin tap.

**The tap.** The leaflet says the tap has a ceramic disc cartridge, and under the basin there are
two small isolation valves on the flexible pipes that feed it.

:::predict Before reading on: do Southern Water's washer steps apply to this tap? If not, what does, and what label does the job get?
No. Southern Water's steps are for a washer tap, with a hexagonal nut, a jumper plate and a washer,
and the leaflet says this one has a cartridge.[1] The method is the one in the couple's own leaflet,
for this tap. Bristan's leaflet shows what such a method looks like for one of its taps (isolate,
handle off, shroud off, cartridge out and cleaned), but it isn't this tap's.[2]

Before opening it, they turn the tap on to check the isolation valves have worked, which is this
course's step borrowed from Southern Water's. With the maker's instructions it's **Yours, on
conditions**: they follow those instructions and use any part they name. Without the leaflet, it
would be a plumber's job.
:::

**The toilet.** United Utilities gives a dripping overflow as the sign of a faulty cistern on an
older toilet, and this one fits that description.[4] The couple do the dye test anyway, as this
course's own step, to see whether the cistern is also leaking into the bowl, and the bowl stays
clear. So the cistern is losing water out of the overflow, not into the bowl. Neither fact tells
them which part inside has failed, because this course read nothing on the parts of a UK cistern.
There is no leaflet for the cistern. So it is **Call someone**: a WaterSafe approved plumber.[4]

What the case teaches is the order. Name the fitting before you buy anything, use the method
written for that fitting, and where no method was written for it, don't borrow one.

## What people get wrong

**"If it drips, turn it off harder."** Neither source read here suggests forcing a tap shut. Their
fix is a new washer in a washer tap and a cleaned cartridge in Bristan's ceramic one.[1][2] Force
comes up in the sources only as a warning: Southern Water's, against screwing a repaired tap back
together too tightly, and WaterSafe's, against forcing a stop tap.[1][5]

**"A drip means a new washer."** Only in a washer tap. Bristan's leaflet for its ceramic tap
does not mention a washer. It puts a continuing drip possibly down to the ceramic disc valves or
cartridge, and its fix is to clean the cartridge.[2]

**"A ceramic tap that drips once after use is broken."** Not on Bristan's account. For its own tap,
it says a drip for a short time after use is normal, from water left in the tap body. It's a drip
that goes on that it puts, possibly, down to the cartridge.[2]

**"Toilet advice is toilet advice."** The flapper is the part EPA names in American toilets. United
Utilities names modern push-button cisterns among the biggest causes of leaks in the UK.[3][4] This
course read nothing on how a UK cistern works, and buying a US part for one is a guess.

**"The stop tap's off, so the tap is safe to open."** Only once the tap has stopped running.
Southern Water's second step, read as a check, is how you find out.[1]

**"A dripping shower head just needs tape."** Only if it leaks at the joint. EPA sends a shower that
drips when it's off, a valve leak, to a handyperson or plumber.[3]

## Practice

:::exercise Name every tap and toilet
Take 15 minutes over this. It opens nothing, so it suits a renter too.

1. List every tap in your home. Next to each, write what you know about its kind: the maker's name
   if it is on the tap, whether you have a leaflet for it, and "unknown" if you don't. Don't take
   any tap apart for this exercise. If you later do, isolate it first and run the check, and only
   if it's your home to repair or your landlord agrees.

2. Look under each basin and sink for small isolation valves on the pipes feeding the taps, and
   note which taps have them.

3. For each toilet, note whether it is a US toilet with a tank or a UK cistern, and, in a UK home,
   find where its overflow pipe comes out through the wall, if it has one, and whether it drips.

## For your plan

This lesson adds a line to your map: which taps are washer taps, which are ceramic and whose they
are, and where the isolation valves sit. It adds a line to your calendar: in a US home, the flapper
checked periodically and replaced at least every five years (EPA); in a UK home, a look at each
overflow pipe when you check the stop tap. And it adds a drawer for the leaflets, because the
leaflet decides which label a job gets.
