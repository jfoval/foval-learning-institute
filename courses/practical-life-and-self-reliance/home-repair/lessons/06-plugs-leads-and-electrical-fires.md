Lesson 5, "Electricity: what trips, and why", stopped at the box: what trips, and what it's telling
you. Most of the electricity a
household actually handles never gets near the box. It goes through a plug, often through an
extension lead or an adaptor, into something that heats or spins. That's where the small faults
start, and it's where a householder can do the most without opening anything.

:::callout Before you pick up a tool
This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

"Isolate the power before you open anything up" has one everyday meaning in this lesson: a plug is
opened only when it's out of the socket. Electrical Safety First says so in those terms.

## The fuse in a British plug

Plugs in the UK carry their own fuse, and US plugs don't; the course read no US source on plug
protection, so this section is British. [Electrical Safety First](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/safety-advice/home-and-people/house-maintenance/plugs-and-fuses/)
(ESF), a UK electrical safety charity: "The fuse inside a UK plug is a safety device to protect you. It will 'blow' if an
electrical appliance or extension lead draws too much current, either because of an overload or a
fault." And: "The blown fuse cuts off the electricity, stopping the cable and appliance from
overheating and causing a fire."[1] It's the breaker's job from lesson 5, done again, closer to
the appliance.

The rule that matters is one sentence: "If you have to replace a fuse, replace it only with another
of the same rating. A standard UK plug is usually fitted with a 3A or 13A fuse."[1] ESF adds that
"5A fuses are still used in some older equipment", and that the fuse should meet BS 1362, marked
on its body; the plug itself should be marked BS 1363.[1]

Which appliance takes which? ESF gives examples, and GOV.UK's fire safety guide gives others, and
they don't agree about everything. ESF lists a vacuum cleaner under 13A, and GOV.UK gives a vacuum
cleaner a 5A fuse as its example.[1][2] Both are examples, not rules. ESF's own instruction settles
it: "Check manufacturer's instructions if you are not sure which fuse to use."[1] So the right fuse
is the one the maker specifies.

Changing a plug fuse for one of the same rating is **Yours**, on one condition ESF states plainly:
"Only remove the cover when the plug is out of the socket first."[1] This course doesn't teach
wiring a plug, and on a moulded plug there's nothing else to open.

### A lamp that goes out

Here's the case, worked with a gap. In a rented flat in the north of England, a bedside lamp goes
out. The bulb is fine. The plug is a moulded one with a small fuse carrier, and the fuse inside
reads 3A.

1. **Out of the socket first.** The lamp is unplugged before anything is opened, ESF's condition.
2. **The same rating.** ESF lists a table lamp under 3A, and the fuse already in the plug is 3A. A
   new 3A fuse, marked BS 1362, goes in.
3. **Plugged back in, the lamp works.** For a week.

:::checkpoint Then the new fuse blows too. What does that tell you, and what's next? Decide before you open this.

One blown fuse can be a one-off. A second is the lamp telling you something draws too much current
"either because of an overload or a fault", ESF's words, and a table lamp isn't overloading
anything. So the fault is in the lamp or its flex. It's no longer a fuse job: stop using the lamp
and follow ESF's advice for a faulty product, "Unplug and contact the retailer, manufacturer, or a
qualified repair technician."[1] What it never means is a 13A fuse "to stop it blowing": ESF's rule
is the same rating. ESF says the fuse is there to stop the cable and appliance overheating, and
on this course's reading of that, a bigger fuse could let the flex overheat before anything cuts off.
:::

## Leads, adaptors and cable reels

[ESF on extension leads](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/safety-advice/home-and-people/house-maintenance/overloading-sockets/): "Most extension leads are rated at 13A but some older ones and many cable
reels may be only 10A or less."[3] The total of everything plugged into a lead must stay within its
rating, which ESF says is marked on the front of a cable reel and on the back or underside of a
lead. Two more of its rules. Don't plug an extension lead into another, "as this could cause the
plug in the wall socket to overheat."[3] And cable reels: "It's important they are fitted with a
thermal-cutout", "Make sure they are fully unwound if you need the full current rating", because
"The current rating is typically as low as 3 or 4A when wound."[3]

GOV.UK's fire safety guide adds the habits: one plug per socket where you can, high-powered
appliances such as washing machines on a socket of their own, and knowing the amp limit of any
adaptor or lead you use.[2]

:::predict A fan heater drawing close to 13A and a hair dryer are both plugged into a 10A extension lead on a cold morning. Nothing trips straight away. Before reading on: is that evidence it's fine?

No. The lead's rating is the limit ESF tells you to stay within, and this is over it. Nothing
tripping only tells you the circuit's breaker, which protects the house wiring, hasn't been asked
to do more than it can. It says nothing about the lead, whose own limit is lower. That's why ESF
puts the rating on the lead and tells you to read it.[3]
:::

## The warning signs

[GOV.UK's fire safety guide](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/make-your-home-safe-from-fire/fire-safety-in-the-home-accessible-version)
for England lists them: "scorch marks, hot plugs and sockets, fuses that
blow or circuit-breakers that trip for no obvious reasons, or flickering lights."[2] ESF's FAQ adds
the sound: "If you notice anything unusual - for example, burn marks on plugs and sockets, sounds of
‘arcing’ (buzzing or crackling), fuses blowing or circuit-breakers tripping - get a registered
electrician to check your electrics as soon as possible."[4]

Those two lists split the same way lesson 5's did. A hot plug on one appliance is that
appliance's problem, and ESF's line for it is to unplug it and contact the retailer, maker or a
repair technician.[1] A socket, a switch or a fitting that scorches, buzzes or crackles is inside
the wiring, and ESF sends it to a registered electrician.[4] Both are **Call someone**; they're
different someones.

:::checkpoint Two finds in one kitchen: the kettle's plug is hot to the touch after every boil, and the double socket behind the toaster has a brown mark and crackles now and then. Whose is each, on the sources here?

The kettle's plug is the appliance's side of the split: stop using the kettle and follow ESF's
advice for a faulty product, which is to unplug it and contact the retailer, the maker or a
qualified repair technician.[1] The socket is the wiring's side. A scorch mark is on GOV.UK's list
and crackling is on ESF's, and ESF's advice for either is a registered electrician, as soon as
possible.[2][4] Both are **Call someone**, and only the first call is to a shop.
:::

## Inspection: the check nobody sees

ESF recommends that the electrics of a home be inspected and tested "at least every 10 years for
an owner-occupied home", and "for tenanted properties, every 5 years or at each change of
occupancy, whichever is sooner".[1] The result is a report, an Electrical Installation Condition
Report. That's a charity's recommendation for an owner-occupier, not a law.

For landlords in England it is the law. This is general information, not legal advice. The rules
differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and between US states and cities,
and they change. [The Electrical Safety Standards in the Private Rented Sector and Social Rented
Sector (England) Regulations 2020](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2020/312/regulation/3)
require a landlord to have every electrical installation inspected and tested by a qualified
person before the tenancy starts and "at intervals of no more than 5 years", and to give "each
existing tenant of the residential premises" a copy of the report "within 28 days of the inspection
and test".[5] If you rent in England, that report is yours to ask for; this course teaches no
tenancy law beyond that fact.

Buying a home? ESF: "Don’t rely on the standard home survey as this will not cover electrical
safety."[6]

## What the fire figures say, and what they count

:::predict Before reading on: of the accidental fires in homes that England's fire and rescue services attended in the year to March 2026, what share do you think started in wiring or in electrical appliances other than cookers? Make a guess.

About a quarter, on this course's arithmetic from the Home Office's published counts, which the
next paragraph sets out. Cookers are left out of that share because the table doesn't split them by
fuel.[7] If you guessed nearer a half, the section after the figures is about where that number
comes from.
:::

**In England.** Fire and rescue services attended 23,795 accidental dwelling fires in the year to
March 2026.[7] In the [Home Office's detailed table](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/detailed-analysis-of-fires-england-year-ending-march-2026), "electrical distribution" (wiring, cabling,
plugs and the like) was the source of 3,410 and "other electrical appliances" of 2,739. Together
that's 6,149, about 26%, or roughly a quarter: this course's arithmetic from the published counts.
That total leaves out cooking appliances, 9,928 fires, because the table doesn't split cookers by
fuel.[7] GOV.UK's fire safety guide, updated in 2022, says "Faulty electrics (appliances, wiring and
overloaded sockets) cause around 4,000 fires in the home across the country every year", which
counts differently again.[2]

**In the US.** The National Fire Protection Association (NFPA), a US fire safety body, estimated
that US fire departments attended an average of "32,620 home fires involving electrical
distribution and lighting equipment per year in 2015–2019", with an average of 430 civilian deaths
a year. Wiring and related equipment accounted for 68% of those fires, and 23% happened between
midnight and 8 a.m. but caused 52% of the deaths.[8] Those are averages for 2015 to 2019, the
latest this course read, and they count equipment involved, not what caused the fault; the report
attributes none of them to DIY.

You'll sometimes read that almost half of house fires are electrical. Nothing this course read
from the Home Office shows that. A share that large depends on counting electric cooking, and the
table this course read can't separate electric cooking from gas.[7] The honest version is the one
with its counting rule attached.

## What people get wrong

**"A bigger fuse will stop it blowing."** Same rating only (ESF). A fuse that keeps blowing is a
fault, not an undersized fuse.[1]

**"Every vacuum cleaner takes a 13A fuse"** (or a 5A one). The two official lists disagree; the
maker's instructions decide.[1][2]

**"If nothing trips, the extension lead's fine."** The lead has its own, lower rating (ESF).[3]

**"The survey will have checked the electrics."** It won't (ESF).[6]

**"Almost half of house fires are electrical."** Not on the Home Office counts, which give about a
quarter for wiring and non-cooking appliances, with cooking uncounted by fuel.[7]

:::exercise One room's plugs and leads
Take 20 minutes over this, in one room, with a torch and a notebook. Don't open anything that's
plugged in, and if you rent, this is all looking and noting.

1. **Every plug.** For each appliance, note what it is, and in the UK the fuse rating printed on
   the plug or its fuse carrier, where you can read it without opening anything.
2. **Every lead and adaptor.** Note each extension lead's rating (on the back or underside) and add
   up roughly what's plugged into it. Note any lead plugged into another lead, and any cable reel
   used wound up.
3. **The warning signs.** Look and feel (from the outside, with the appliance off) for scorch marks,
   hot plugs or sockets, cracked casings, and listen for buzzing or crackling. Note what you find.
4. **Sort what you found** into: fine; change the habit (unplug, spread the load, unwind the reel);
   take the appliance out of use; or call an electrician. Say whose advice sits behind each.
:::

## For your plan

To the **calendar**: the one-room audit, repeated room by room over a season, and in England, if
you rent, the date your landlord's five-yearly report is due. To the **stop list**: a fuse that
blows twice, any scorching or crackling at a socket or switch, and any lead that is hot to the
touch.
