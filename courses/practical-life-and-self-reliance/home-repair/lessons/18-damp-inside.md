A black bloom behind a wardrobe, a grey line along the bottom of a bathroom ceiling, a wall that
feels cold and smells musty: damp inside a house is common, and it is easy to get wrong. It goes
wrong in two directions. People scrub the mould and never find the water, so it comes back. Or they
pay for a treatment aimed at a cause the house does not have; GOV.UK calls rising damp "often
misdiagnosed".[1] The government's guidance on damp and
mould in England was written after a death: "In December 2020, 2-year-old Awaab Ishak died from a
severe respiratory infection caused by prolonged exposure to damp and mould in his home."[1] This
lesson sorts damp into its kinds, explains where condensation comes from, shows how much mould is
yours to clean and how, and sets out the biggest argument in this part of the subject, over rising
damp, in the words of the people having it.

If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. ## Four kinds of damp

The UK guidance this lesson leans on most is a document on [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/damp-and-mould-understanding-and-addressing-the-health-risks-for-rented-housing-providers/understanding-and-addressing-the-health-risks-of-damp-and-mould-in-the-home--2),
developed by the Office for Health Improvement and Disparities, part of the Department of Health and
Social Care, with the housing department and the UK Health Security Agency.
It's written for landlords and housing professionals in England, and it was last updated on 1 April
2026. It sorts damp into four kinds:[1]

- **Condensation damp** "happens when moisture generated inside the home cools and condenses onto
  colder parts of the buildings (for example window frames, corners and low points on walls behind
  sofas or wardrobes)." The guidance adds: "This is the most common form of damp."
- **Penetrating damp** is water getting in from outside through defects in walls, roofs, windows
  or floors. Lesson 17, "Keeping water out: roof, gutters, ground and walls", gave its definition
  and its checks, and this lesson does not repeat them.
- **Rising damp** "is moisture from the ground that rises up through parts of the buildings in
  contact with the ground (walls and floors); it is usually found in older properties and is often
  misdiagnosed." It's the last section of this lesson.
- **Traumatic damp** "can be caused by leaking water from waste and heating pipes, overflowing baths
  or sinks, burst pipes or defective water storage vessels inside the building." That is the water
  from lesson 2, "Water in: supply, leaks and frozen pipes", and lesson 4, "Water out: traps, vents
  and blocked drains", ending up in the fabric of the house.

They don't stay in separate boxes. As lesson 17 showed, GOV.UK says water from a leak or from
outside can make condensation worse, so one house can have two kinds at once, and fixing one leaves
the other.[1] And you don't always see it. "The smell of mould without visible evidence of mould may indicate that
there is mould behind a surface", the guidance says, such as the back of wallpaper or panelling or
the underside of a carpet.[1]

:::predict A damp, mouldy patch appears in January, low down on the outside wall of a bedroom, behind a chest of drawers. It is dry in summer. There's no pipe in that wall. Before reading on: which of the four kinds would you suspect first, and why is "low down" not enough to call it rising damp?

Condensation first. GOV.UK's own definition names "low points on walls behind sofas or wardrobes"
as a place condensation forms, so height on the wall does not sort condensation from rising damp.[1]
A patch that comes in winter and goes in summer, behind furniture, on a cold outside wall, fits the
condensation definition well. That is a first suspicion, not a diagnosis: the outside of the same
wall still gets lesson 17's checks, because the kinds combine.
:::

## Condensation: where the water comes from

The water in condensation comes from living in the house. GOV.UK: "Moisture from normal occupant
activities, such as showering, drying laundry and cooking, builds up in the air around a home if it
cannot be removed."[1] The US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) puts it the same way round:
"Condensation can be a sign of high humidity."[2]

Two things decide whether that moisture ends up on a wall. One is whether it can get out: "If a
dwelling is inadequately ventilated, moisture in the air cannot escape the building and is therefore
more likely to cause condensation."[1] The other is how cold the surfaces are: "Homes that are
insufficiently insulated and heated are more prone to the build-up of moisture and condensation on
the inside of external walls and windows."[1] Put those beside the definition's examples (window
frames, corners, the low points of walls behind furniture) and you have the places to look first.
The sources give the mechanism in those words and no further, so this lesson stops there too.

### Humidity, and why the winter number is lower

The amount of moisture in the air is measured as relative humidity. The University of Minnesota
Extension, a US university's advice service, defines it plainly: "Relative humidity is the amount of
moisture in the air at a given temperature."[4] You measure it with a humidity meter, or hygrometer; EPA calls it "a
small, inexpensive ($10-$50) instrument available at many hardware stores" (a US price, on a page
updated in February 2026).[2]

The targets the sources give are all American. The GOV.UK guidance read here lists "consistently high
relative humidity" among its warning signs but gives no figure.[1]

| Source | What it says |
|---|---|
| EPA (US federal agency) | "keep indoor humidity below 60 percent (ideally between 30 and 50 percent) relative humidity"[2] |
| CDC (US federal agency) | "no higher than 50%"[3] |
| University of Minnesota Extension | "25% (winter) to 50% (summer)", which it calls "generally considered healthy and comfortable for people, and better for the house"[4] |

They look like they disagree, and on this course's reading mostly they do not. Minnesota gives the reason its winter figure is
so low: "Generally, in cold winter conditions, the relative humidity levels need to be low to prevent
condensation and potential damage to the house’s structure, as well as to discourage mold growth,
which can cause allergies and respiratory problems."[4] So on Minnesota's account, a figure that
suits a summer can be too high for a cold January. This course reads the three as a range and a direction,
not three rival answers.

:::checkpoint A hygrometer in a Wearside flat reads 70% in the kitchen after cooking and 45% in the bedroom the next morning. Using the table, which reading is the concern, and what would you want to know before calling it a problem?

The 70% is above all three sources' upper figures, but it was taken just after cooking, when
GOV.UK's own list says moisture is produced. The question is whether it comes down, which is what a
second reading an hour or two later would tell you. The 45% sits inside EPA's ideal band and under
CDC's 50%. Minnesota ties its low winter figure to "cold winter conditions", and none of the three sources is
British; the course found no UK figure. One reading is a snapshot, which is why the practice at the
end of this lesson asks for two weeks of them.
:::

## Before any tip: whose fault it isn't

Every piece of advice for cutting indoor moisture risks sounding like an accusation, and a lot of
damp is lived with by people who did not cause it and can't fix it. The GOV.UK guidance is blunt
about this, and this lesson puts it before any tip: "We are absolutely clear that it is totally
unreasonable to blame damp and mould in the home on ‘lifestyle choices’." It goes on: "It is
unavoidable that everyday tasks, such as cooking, bathing, washing and drying laundry will
contribute to the production of indoor moisture." Lesson 17 gave the next sentence, which puts the
fundamental cause in the building, its ventilation, heating and energy efficiency, "not tenants’
normal domestic activities". The guidance calls approaches
that blame occupants "unjust and counterproductive".[1]

It also says landlords can help tenants make small, reasonable changes, as long as that sits
alongside fixing the building and not instead of it. Its ventilation checklist has landlords talk to
tenants about "leaving a gap between furniture and
external walls, using extractor fans in kitchens and bathrooms, closing internal doors when they cook
or shower, covering pans when cooking, drying laundry outdoors (if possible) and opening windows
periodically".[1] Every one of those is **Yours**, as far as the home allows. But the guidance's own limit comes with them: "Tenants cannot be expected to reduce moisture
levels if their home does not enable them to do so."[1] A kitchen with no working extractor, or a
flat that can't be kept warm, is a building problem.

## Ventilation: build tight, ventilate right

This is general information, not legal advice.

It's easy to assume a draughty house airs itself. The US Department of Energy's Energy Saver
pages, which have since been taken down and are read here as archived, disagree: "Relying on air
leakage for natural ventilation is not recommended."[5] They give the reason: "During cold or windy
weather, too much air may enter the house. When it's warmer and less windy, not enough air may enter,
which can result in poor indoor air quality."[5] The recommendation is "to reduce air leakage as much as possible and to
provide controlled ventilation as needed", and the same pages say energy-efficient homes "require
mechanical ventilation to maintain indoor air quality".[5][6]

:::predict A couple replace every window in their 1960s house and draught-proof the doors. The house is warmer. Before reading on: would you expect condensation to get better or worse, and what does it depend on?

It depends on the ventilation. GOV.UK says energy-efficient homes are "typically less prone to
developing damp and mould, provided that ventilation is adequate", and warns that "some energy
efficiency interventions may reduce overall ventilation rates".[1] So a warmer house can have less
condensation, or more, if the new windows took away the air that used to carry the moisture out and
nothing replaced it.
:::

On the British side, GOV.UK's guidance says of windows: "Under building regulations, all replacement
windows are required to have trickle vents".[1] That is GOV.UK's statement in guidance for England;
this course didn't read the building regulations on ventilation themselves. Trickle vents are what
the Energy Saving Trust describes as "small vents above windows that let fresh air trickle in".[7]
The GOV.UK guidance adds that trickle
vents "should be controllable by the tenant, rather than permanently open, as this may encourage
tenants to block them", and that air bricks, which it calls "a cost-effective and easy means of
addressing inadequate under-floor ventilation", "should be kept clear and free of debris and obstruction".[1]

The Energy Saving Trust, the UK's government-funded energy advice body, says the same from the
draught-proofing side. When you draught-proof, it says, don't block extractor fans, underfloor
airbricks, wall vents or trickle vents, and it adds: "Good ventilation is still essential, so you
shouldn’t block vents, chimneys or airflow needed for safety and air quality."[7] Some of those
openings are there for a fuel-burning appliance, not for damp. Lesson 11, "Carbon monoxide and the
alarms that warn you", gave the Trust's warning about the fixed vents a gas heater needs, and it
holds here without exception.

The US pages add a warning about whole-house systems. They list the drawbacks of each kind, and say
exhaust-only systems "Can cause backdrafting in combustion appliances",
while supply systems "Can cause moisture problems in cold climates".[6] Of the energy-recovery kind, the pages say
only that "Only some HVAC contractors have enough technical expertise and experience to install
them"; they do not say whether a householder may fit any of them.[6] This course reads the backdrafting warning as a reason to leave whole-house
ventilation in a home with a boiler, furnace or fire to a professional.

Here's how the jobs sort. Keeping trickle vents open, air bricks and wall vents clear, and running
the extractor fans you have is **Yours**, in a rented home too. Replacing windows is **Call
someone**: in England, GOV.UK calls installing windows "controlled building work" that "should be undertaken by someone
competent to do so".[1] Fans and mechanical ventilation systems, the guidance says, "should be
regularly inspected and maintained by qualified professionals".[1] So a fan that doesn't run is,
on this course's label, **Call someone**, reported or booked and not opened up. Lesson 5,
"Electricity: what trips, and why", set out where a householder's hands stop with electricity: at
the front of the consumer unit or panel.

## Mould: moisture first, then a small patch

EPA's guide on mould, [*A Brief Guide to Mold, Moisture and Your Home*](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home),
opens with its whole argument in one sentence: "The key to mold control is moisture control."[2] It
explains why you can't win the other way: "It is impossible to get rid of all mold and mold spores
indoors", and spores don't grow without moisture.[2] So cleaning is half the job, and the other half
is finding the water. EPA: "If you clean up the mold, but don't fix the water problem, then, most
likely, the mold problem will come back."[2] GOV.UK says the same: "Simply removing surface mould
will not prevent the damp and mould from reappearing."[1] After a leak or flood, EPA's rule is to
"dry water-damaged areas and items within 24-48 hours to prevent mold growth", and CDC gives the same
window after a flood.[2][3]

Why bother at all? GOV.UK's key message is that "Damp and mould primarily affect the airways and
lungs, but they can also affect the eyes and skin", and that in the most severe cases the respiratory
effects can cause death.[1] The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reports that the
Institute of Medicine found in 2004 "sufficient evidence" linking indoor mould with upper
respiratory symptoms, cough and wheeze, asthma symptoms in people with asthma, and hypersensitivity
pneumonitis, an immune-mediated condition, in people susceptible to it; this course read CDC's summary, not
the report.[3] CDC also says exposure "may cause a variety of health effects, or none at all", and
EPA says "Research on mold and health effects is ongoing."[2][3] Symptoms and treatment are for a
doctor, not this course.

### How much is yours

This is where the two countries' guidance is worded differently.

- **In the US**, EPA gives a size: if the mouldy area is "less than about 10 square feet (less than
  roughly a 3 ft. by 3 ft. patch), in most cases, you can handle the job yourself".[2] Ten square feet
  is a little under a square metre (0.93 m², this course's conversion). Over that, or after a lot of
  water damage, EPA sends you to its longer remediation guide, and says any contractor you hire
  should have experience cleaning up mould. If the water was sewage or other contaminated water, it
  says to "call in a professional".[2]
- **In the UK**, the GOV.UK guidance gives no measurement. It says "most tenants could reasonably be
  expected to remove condensation and very small amounts of mould using an appropriate mould and
  mildew cleaner, larger areas of mould should only be addressed by qualified professionals."[1]

So a small patch on a hard surface is **Yours, on conditions**: small enough (under about 10 square
feet on EPA's figure, and "very small amounts" on GOV.UK's), on a surface you can clean without
pulling anything apart, with the moisture found, and in your own home or one where the landlord
agrees. Anything larger, anything soaked with sewage, and anything hidden behind wallpaper,
panelling or a carpet is **Call someone**. The hidden kind is on that list for a reason both
countries give: GOV.UK warns that "removal of carpets or wallpaper can lead to a significant release
of mould spores", and EPA that "removal of wallpaper can lead to a massive release of spores if there
is mold growing on the underside of the paper".[1][2] EPA also says: "If you have health concerns,
consult a health professional before starting cleanup."[2]

### Cleaning a small patch

The sources' steps, put together:

1. **Record it.** GOV.UK: "it is advisable for tenants or the landlord to photograph the area
   affected by mould and (if possible) draw around it to preserve a record".[1] If the patch comes
   back, the pencil line tells you whether it's growing.
2. **Protect yourself.** GOV.UK: "Regardless of the extent of mould, the person removing the mould
   should wear protective equipment, such as a mask, gloves and goggles".[1] EPA is more specific:
   "Gloves, goggles, N-95 respirator", long gloves to mid-forearm, and "Goggles that do not have
   ventilation holes are recommended"; it says you "may want to" wear the respirator.[2] Lesson 13,
   "Tools, dust and fumes: a small kit used safely", covered how a disposable respirator has to fit
   to work, and what a beard does to that.
3. **Air the room the right way.** GOV.UK: "Doors should be shut to help prevent mould spores from
   being spread to other areas of the house, but windows should be left open during and after the
   clean up activity."[1]
4. **Clean and dry.** EPA: "Scrub mold off hard surfaces with detergent and water, and dry
   completely." GOV.UK says to use an appropriate cleaning product, follow the product's guidance,
   and leave the surface "to dry completely".[1][2]
5. **Throw out what can't be cleaned.** Both say absorbent things such as carpets, soft furnishings
   and ceiling tiles may have to go.[1][2]
6. **Don't cover it up.** EPA: "Do not paint or caulk moldy surfaces."[2]
7. **Find the water.** EPA won't call the job finished until the moisture problem is "completely
   fixed".[2]

Those steps assume a small patch, on a hard surface, where nobody has already put a product on it.
If something has been sprayed on it already, read that product's label before you add anything.

### Bleach: three bodies, three answers

This is a real disagreement between official bodies, if a small one, and each says it differently.

| Source | On bleach |
|---|---|
| CDC (US) | Mould can be removed from hard surfaces with household products, soap and water, "or a bleach solution of no more than 1 cup of household laundry bleach in 1 gallon of water."[3] |
| EPA (US) | Using a biocide such as chlorine bleach "is not recommended as a routine practice during mold cleanup." Then: "There may be instances, however, when professional judgment may indicate its use (for example, when immune-compromised individuals are present)."[2] |
| GOV.UK (England) | "Mould and mildew products should be used in preference to bleach, for health and safety reasons."[1] |

In metric, CDC's limit is about 240 ml of bleach in 3.8 litres of water, roughly one part bleach
to sixteen parts water (this course's conversion from US measures). EPA adds a reason killing isn't enough: "Dead
mold may still cause allergic reactions in some people, so it is not enough to simply kill the mold,
it must also be removed."[2]

Where they agree matters more. All three put fixing the moisture first. And the two that discuss
mixing say never mix bleach with ammonia: CDC, "Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners", and
EPA, because "toxic fumes could be produced".[2][3] Lesson 4 gave Poison Control's wider rule, never
mix household cleaning products at all.

### A flat on Wearside, in January

A woman renting a first-floor flat on Wearside moves her wardrobe to hoover behind it and finds black
mould on the wall, about 50 cm by 30 cm, at skirting height. It is an outside wall. The wall above the
wardrobe line is clean.

**Which kind?** It's behind furniture, low on a cold outside wall, in winter, which is almost word for

the obvious alternatives. Is there a pipe in or near that wall (traumatic damp)? Is there a gutter
overflowing or a crack in the render on the outside of that wall (penetrating damp, lesson 17)? If
not, condensation is the lead suspect.

**Whose job?** At about 0.15 m² (1.6 square feet, on this course's conversion), it is well under EPA's
figure and fits GOV.UK's "very small amounts". It is on painted plaster. So cleaning it is hers, on
conditions: she photographs it and draws round it, and because she rents, she reports it to her
landlord as well. GOV.UK says tenant cleaning "should not be a substitute for assessing and addressing
the underlying issue, which should always be the priority".[1]

**The clean**, by the steps above: mask, gloves and goggles, door shut, window open, a mould and
mildew product by its label (GOV.UK's preference over bleach), dried completely.

**The water.** She leaves a gap between the wardrobe and the wall, which is the first item on
GOV.UK's list, uses the extractor when she cooks and showers, and starts the condensation diary from
the practice section. If the patch comes back past its pencil line, the diary goes to the landlord
with the photographs.

## Rising damp: what's agreed, and what isn't

Apart from bleach and the exact humidity figures, the sources so far have agreed with each other. On rising damp they don't. How often it happens, and whether the
usual treatment works, are disputed among surveyors, damp-proofing firms and people who look after
old buildings, and the dispute has gone on for years. This is a contested empirical question, so the
lesson gives each side in its own words and does not decide it. Logic and Argument lesson 7, the
strongest version first, is the rule for reading it.

### The official line in England

GOV.UK's full paragraph is the place to start, because it holds both halves. Rising damp "is usually
found in older properties and is often misdiagnosed. It can be identified through visual inspection;
however chemical testing is the most appropriate way of confirming it. Often it is due to defective
damp proof courses and membranes."[1] A damp-proof course (DPC), in the Property Care Association's
description, is a waterproof barrier "installed roughly at about 6 inches from the ground" (about 15 cm)
to stop water climbing the wall.[8] So the guidance treats rising damp as real, says it's
often misdiagnosed, and says how to confirm it.

### The case that it's real and treatable

The [Property Care Association](https://www.property-care.org/homeowners/advice/rising-damp/) (PCA)
describes itself as "the trade body representing specialists across the UK who can be trusted to help
resolve problems affecting buildings such as rising damp".[8] Its account: "Rising damp is simply due
to the process of external ground water being drawn up through brickwork by what is known as
capillary action." The signs it gives are moisture at the base of a wall that reduces with height,
often a "‘tide-mark’", and salts: "Hygroscopic salts (particularly chlorides/nitrates) are almost
invariably present in groundwater".[8] It explains what normally stops it: "Most types of masonry
used in the walls of buildings will allow some water movement by capillary action; however, this is
usually controlled by a physical barrier or damp proof course. If this physical barrier is absent,
has broken down or is damaged then it is often possible to install a remedial damp proof course (DPC)
to control water rising from the ground."[8]

Its first remedy isn't a treatment. Where the DPC has been bridged, by raised ground levels, a patio or
planting area, bridging render or debris in the cavity, it says "removing the ‘bridge’ should hopefully be sufficient
to control the capillary rise of moisture in the wall." Only where the DPC "has failed in some way"
does it suggest a PCA specialist and a survey, and it says: "Without a proper investigation (ideally
with a qualified damp specialist) it is impossible to advise what the exact cause of your issue is."[8]
Where salts have got into the plaster, it says, the plaster may need replacing with salt-resistant
plaster.[8]

The research case is reported in the journal of the Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors (RICS).
Two chartered surveyors, Michael Cooper and Andrew Thompson, wrote there in 2020 that rising damp "could benefit
from greater academic investigation", and summarised a 2017 University of Salford doctoral thesis by
Leslie Sellers as concluding: "Rising damp is a real phenomenon that warrants treatment and the
contemporary method of damp-proofing, installed as it would be on a real construction site, does
provide effective control."[9] This course read the article's summary, not the thesis.

### The case that it's rare, and the treatment wasted

The sceptics' words here come from one report in [The Architects' Journal](https://www.architectsjournal.co.uk/home/-rising-damp-is-a-myth-says-former-rics-chief/5204095.article),
from June 2009, eight years before that thesis. Stephen Boniface, former chairman of the
construction arm of RICS, said that "‘true rising damp’ is a myth" and that chemically injected
damp-proof courses are "‘a complete waste of money’". His account of what's really going on:
"‘The most likely causes of damp are moisture penetration and, most commonly, condensation’".[10]
Elaine Blackett-Ord, then chair of the Register of Architects Accredited in Building Conservation,
said of injected DPCs that "they are ineffective and grossly expensive", and, of what she called
"damaging impermeable cement based internal renders", that they "serve simply to conceal the problem in the wall behind. For most
historic buildings this is extremely damaging and irreversible."[10] Jeff Howell, a qualified
bricklayer and the author of *The Rising Damp Myth* (2008), said, in the article's words, that
"trials in the laboratory confirm the falsehood": "If you build a brick pillar and stand it in a tray
of water, the bricks in the water will get wet, but the water doesn’t rise by capillary action". And
he gave his reason: "Cement-based and most lime-based mortars will not allow water to go
through."[10] This course read the
article, not his book.

The same article carried a reply. Terry Brown, of GMW Architects, said: "It’s right to question the
diagnostic skills of commercial damp proofing firms, but to state categorically there is no such
thing as rising damp undermines a whole litany of rules of brickwork detailing I’ve adhered to all my
professional life." And: "Of course there is no reason why inherited conventional wisdom shouldn’t be
challenged." But "the challenge has to be scientific and not anecdotal."[10]

Two things to hold while you weigh this. The sceptics' quotations are from 2009, and this course
did not read any later statement of their case. The thesis is known here only through the RICS
authors' summary, and this course did not read any assessment of it by the other side. The next document is one that the trade body and the conservation bodies
both put their names to.

### The common ground: the 2022 joint statement

In September 2022, RICS, Historic England and the PCA wrote and produced a joint position
statement, [*Investigation of moisture and its effects on traditional buildings*](https://ihbc.org.uk/toolbox/docs/JPS2022%20Investigation%20of%20moisture%20v3.pdf).
Cadw, Historic Environment Scotland, Northern Ireland's Historic Environment Division, the Institute of Historic Building Conservation (IHBC) and the Society for the Protection of
Ancient Buildings adopted it too.[11] So the trade body, the surveyors' institution and the
conservation bodies are behind the same text. It is written for surveyors, "aimed at those providing consultancy advice or surveys to owners and buyers of a
building", and it never uses the words "rising damp".[11] It sets out how any damp in an old building
should be investigated.

By "traditional" it means "buildings with solid walls built from permeable materials such as brick,
stone, earth, timber and lime-based mortars, plasters and renders. Traditional construction absorbs
moisture but allows it to evaporate when conditions become drier. This is in contrast to modern
construction, which relies on impermeable barriers to prevent moisture entering the fabric."[11] On
this course's reading, that difference is what the conservation voices above were defending when
they objected to impermeable renders. What the statement asks of a surveyor:

- Recognise that in old solid-walled buildings "moisture presence in such buildings may not be down to a
  construction failure."[11]
- Understand salts and "how this might influence a resistance meter". The statement lists
  resistance meters among many measuring methods, alongside "gravimetric
  moisture analysis" and "gas-carbide meters".[11] It does not say how any of them works, and nor does
  this course.
- Deal with damp in stages: "Recognise how to deal with the obvious defects first (including, for
  example, matters such as ventilation, ground levels and lifestyle). Then, allow a period for
  monitoring and natural evaporation, and reassess before moving on to other treatments."[11]
  The statement does not define "lifestyle". Elsewhere its list for surveyors includes "Understand
  that occupation patterns can influence the moisture within the building."[11] It says nothing
  about blame, and GOV.UK's guidance, earlier in this lesson, calls blaming damp and mould on
  lifestyle choices "totally unreasonable".[1]
- Make recommendations that "must be proportionate to the defect and the significance of the
  building."[11]
- Where a surveyor or contractor also quotes for the work, "report objectively on the facts of the
  problem, only suggest remedial works that are proportionate to any defects discovered", and not
  specify "a product, treatment or process that will benefit the surveyor or their firm financially
  without declaring this to the client."[11]

That's the standard you can hold any damp survey to, whichever side of the argument the surveyor is
on.

### What would settle it

For one wall, GOV.UK's answer is testing: chemical testing is, in its words, "the most appropriate
way of confirming it".[1] For the general question, how common rising damp is and how well injected
DPCs work, the sources read here disagree about what the evidence already shows. The RICS authors
take Sellers' thesis to show that rising damp is real and that the treatment works, and also say the
subject "could benefit from greater academic investigation".[9] Jeff Howell, on the other
side, pointed to laboratory trials that he said already settle it.[10] This course did not find a
study that both sides accept. Until then, a householder does not need to pick a side to act well:
the cheap outside causes first, a diagnosis that does not rest on a meter alone, and a remedy in
proportion.

:::checkpoint Write one sentence for each side that its own holders would sign: the PCA's, and Boniface's. Then say what the 2022 statement adds, which the trade body and the conservation bodies both put their names to.

The PCA: rising damp is ground water drawn up a wall by capillary action where the damp-proof course
is missing, failed or bridged; remove any bridge first, and if the DPC has failed, a proper
investigation and often a remedial DPC can control it.[8] Boniface, in 2009: true rising damp is a
myth, injected DPCs are a waste of money, and most damp is penetration and, above all,
condensation.[10] If your sentence for either side used the other side's word for it ("a scam",
"denial"), it fails the test. The statement, produced by the PCA, RICS and Historic England and
adopted by the conservation bodies: moisture in an old solid wall may not be a failure; salts can
influence a resistance meter; obvious defects first, then monitoring; proportionate remedies; and
any financial interest declared.[11]
:::

### An 1890s terrace in the Cynon Valley

A man has bought an 1890s terraced house in the Cynon Valley, in south Wales, with solid walls. A damp
company's surveyor walks round with a resistance meter, gets high readings along the bottom of the
front room's walls, points to a stained band on the plaster, and quotes for an injected DPC and
replastering with salt-resistant plaster.

**What the sources let him check before he decides.**

- **Outside first.** The PCA's own first remedy is removing a bridge, such as raised ground or a
  path against the wall.[8] The joint statement's first stage is the obvious defects, ground levels
  and ventilation among them.[11] So the gutters, downpipes and ground level along that front wall,
  lesson 17's walk-round, come before anything is injected. Cadw, in Wales, is one of
  the bodies that adopted the statement.[11]
- **The meter.** The joint statement says salts can influence a resistance meter, and the PCA says
  salts are almost always present where groundwater has risen.[8][11] Put together, which is this
  course's reading of the two, a high reading at the foot of the wall does not by itself tell him what
  put the moisture there. GOV.UK's guidance for England names chemical testing as the most
  appropriate way to confirm rising damp; none of the sources this course read describes how that
  testing is done, so ask the surveyor what they would test and how.[1]
- **The quote.** The surveyor's firm would also do the work, which the statement expects and allows, on its
  conditions: objective reporting, proportionate work, and any financial interest in the product or
  treatment declared.[11]

:::checkpoint Before reading on, write the three questions he should put to the surveyor.

Yours may be worded differently; these are the ones the sources support. **One:** "What did you check
outside, and is the damp-proof course bridged by the ground, a path or render?" (the PCA's first
remedy, and the statement's first stage). **Two:** "Is this diagnosis from the meter readings alone,
or from another test, such as the chemical testing GOV.UK names, and what did the salts tell you?"
(the statement on salts and meters; GOV.UK on chemical testing). **Three:** "Would you deal with the obvious defects and monitor
before treating, and do you or your firm benefit from the treatment you've specified?" (the statement's
staged process and its declared-interest standard). None of the three accuses anyone, and none tells
him whether his wall has rising damp. That is the honest place for this example to stop: on the PCA's
own account, the cause can't be known without a proper investigation, and nobody can do one from a
page.[8]
:::

Getting the survey and any treatment is **Call someone**. What's yours is the outside walk-round, the
three questions, and, if the answers don't satisfy you, a second opinion (this course's suggestion; the
statement itself allows a surveyor who also quotes).

## If you rent

This is general information, not legal advice.

**In England**, GOV.UK's guidance, written for landlords, says: "Irrespective of the type of damp
(condensation, penetrating, rising or traumatic), landlords are legally responsible for addressing
damp and mould".[1] That's GOV.UK's summary; this course did not read the laws it lists, and doesn't
teach landlord and tenant law, including how quickly a landlord has to act. **In the US**, EPA tells
renters to report leaks and moisture problems to the building owner or manager straight away, as
lesson 17 quoted, and adds: "In cases where persistent water problems are not addressed, you may want
to contact local, state, or federal health or housing authorities."[2]

For a renter in either country, the practical part is the same: photograph and mark the patch
(GOV.UK), report it (EPA), and keep your own dated notes of what you reported and when, which is this
course's suggestion rather than either source's. Cleaning a very small patch is yours on GOV.UK's
account. Anything that changes the building, from a new vent to a new window, is something to agree
with the landlord first, on this course's reading.

## What people get wrong

**"A damp meter reading means rising damp."** GOV.UK's guidance for England says rising damp is often misdiagnosed and names
chemical testing as the way to confirm it, and the joint statement says salts can influence a
resistance meter.[1][11]

**"Mould at skirting height behind furniture means rising damp."** GOV.UK's definition of
condensation names "low points on walls behind sofas or wardrobes".[1]

**"Bleach kills it, so bleach fixes it."** EPA says dead mould can still cause allergic reactions and
has to be removed, and that the job is not finished until the moisture is fixed; GOV.UK prefers mould
and mildew products to bleach.[1][2]

**"Clean it well enough and it's gone for good."** EPA: "It is impossible to get rid of all mold and
mold spores indoors."[2] What stops regrowth is keeping things dry.

**"An old draughty house ventilates itself."** The Energy Saver pages: relying on leakage isn't
recommended, because it lets in too much air in cold, windy weather and too little when it's warmer
and calmer.[5]

**"Damp comes from the way people live."** GOV.UK calls blaming damp on lifestyle choices "totally
unreasonable".[1]

**"Draught-proof everything and the damp will go."** The Energy Saving Trust says not to block
extractor fans, airbricks, wall vents or trickle vents, and GOV.UK warns that energy efficiency work
can reduce ventilation.[1][7]

## Practice

:::exercise Vents, patches and a condensation diary
Take 15 minutes over steps 1 and 2. Step 3 runs for two weeks, a minute or two a day. Nothing here
opens, unscrews or disturbs anything, so it suits a renter too.

1. **List every vent.** Walk round and write down each extractor fan (kitchen, bathroom, toilet),
   trickle vent, wall vent and air brick you can see, and for each: does the fan run when switched
   on, and is the vent open and clear? Don't take any cover off. Note any vent that's blocked or
   taped, and anything near a gas fire or boiler that looks like a fixed vent: leave those alone and
   note them.

2. **Measure any mould you can see.** Measure with a tape held just off the surface, without touching
   the mould, and work out the area in square metres and square feet. Photograph it. Don't clean,
   scrape or peel anything for this exercise, and don't move heavy furniture on your own to look.

3. **Keep a condensation diary for two weeks.** Each morning and evening, note where there's
   condensation (which windows, which walls), and what had been happening (cooking, a shower, washing
   drying indoors, the heating on or off). If you have a hygrometer, note the room's reading at the
   same times.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your notes against this. What does each finding tell you, and whose job is the next step?

**A fan that doesn't run, or a vent blocked or taped:** clearing a vent is **Yours** (if it's your
home, or your landlord agrees); a dead fan is **Call someone**, the landlord if you rent. **A vent near
a fuel-burning appliance:** it stays open, always (lesson 11). **A mould patch under about 10 square
feet (0.93 m²) on a hard surface:** yours to clean by the steps in this lesson, if it's your home to
repair or the landlord agrees, and reported if you rent. **Over that, or behind wallpaper or
panelling:** **Call someone**. **The diary**, on this course's reading of the sources: condensation that
tracks cooking, showers or indoor drying points to ventilation first; condensation on one cold wall regardless of what's happening
points to that wall, and that's worth showing a surveyor or the landlord, with the diary.
:::

## For your plan

This lesson adds to the **map**: every extractor fan, trickle vent, wall vent and air brick, and
whether each works. It adds to the **calendar**: a look behind furniture on outside walls each winter,
and the vents checked clear each autumn (this course's suggestion; no source gives an interval). It
adds to the **stop list**: mould over about 10 square feet, mould behind wallpaper or panelling, any
water from sewage, bleach mixed with anything, any fixed vent near a fuel-burning appliance blocked,
and a damp treatment quoted on a meter reading alone.
