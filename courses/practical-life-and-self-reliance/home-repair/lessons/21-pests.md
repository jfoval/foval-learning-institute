A mouse in the kitchen, a cockroach under the sink, holes in a floorboard: the first thing many
people reach for is a can of something, and the regulators and researchers this lesson read put
that last, or rule it out. Pests come for food, water and shelter, and they get in through gaps. So the
approach the regulators and extension services describe starts with the cause, not the poison.
This lesson teaches that approach, then applies it to mice and rats, cockroaches, bed bugs, clothes
moths, woodworm and, in the US, termites, with the law on poisons and glue traps set out
nation by nation.

:::callout Before you pick up a tool
This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

Pest work takes you to where pipes and cables come into a house, because that's where the gaps
are. This course's line: note those gaps, and don't fill or pack round a gas pipe, a meter or a
cable yourself; ask the landlord, a Gas Safe registered engineer or an electrician. The box stays
shut too, as lessons 5 and 6 said:
CPSC, the US Consumer Product Safety Commission, says panel boxes "are usually electrically live,
even with the main circuit breakers turned off",[23] and HSE's guidance for people who work on
electrical systems does not recommend "non-contact devices such as 'volt sticks'" for proving
anything dead.[24]

Two sections of this lesson state law: the one on poisons and the one on glue traps. This is general
information, not legal advice. The rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern
Ireland, and between US states and cities, and they change.

Some pests are left out on purpose: this course read no official source on wasp nests, ants, flies,
fleas or silverfish, so it says nothing about them.

## Find the cause first: integrated pest management

The US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) calls the approach **integrated pest management**, or
IPM. Its page is written mainly for growers, but it says IPM "can be applied to both agricultural
and non-agricultural settings, such as the home, garden, and workplace".[1] It has four steps:[1]

1. **Set a threshold.** Decide what level of problem calls for action. "Sighting a single pest does
   not always mean control is needed."
2. **Monitor and identify.** Find out what the pest actually is, so you don't treat the wrong thing
   or treat when there's nothing to treat.
3. **Prevent.** Manage the space so pests don't become a problem in the first place.
4. **Control**, and only when the first three say so. "Effective, less risky pest controls are
   chosen first", such as traps. "Broadcast spraying of non-specific pesticides is a last resort."

EPA writes the threshold about pests as an "economic threat" to crops, so using it in a house is
this course's reading: one mouse is a reason to find its way in, not yet a reason for poison.

Great Britain's regulator starts from the same place. The Health and Safety Executive (HSE), in its guidance
on using biocides at home and at work, says "Everyone who uses biocides is responsible for using
them correctly", and before you buy one, asks "Is there a problem that needs to be controlled?" and
whether there's an alternative: traps, sealing doors and windows, storing things differently, and
keeping the area "clean, dry and well maintained".[2] (HSE's pages use "biocide" for products such
as rodent poisons; the label examples it gives include products for use against ants, woodlice and
cockroaches.)

:::predict A kitchen has had a few ants and one mouse in a month. Before reading on: of IPM's four steps, which does reaching straight for a spray skip, and why might that matter more than the spray itself?

It skips the first three. There's no threshold (is this a problem yet?), no identification (a spray
chosen for ants does nothing about a mouse), and no prevention (the gap under the back door and the
open packet in the cupboard are still there). EPA's order puts control last, and even then chooses
"less risky" controls first.[1] A spray that kills today's ants leaves tomorrow's the same way in.
:::

## Mice and rats: seal up, trap up, clean up

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the US public health agency, puts rodent
control in three words, in that order.[3][4][5][6] It lists the diseases rodents can spread, with
hantavirus among them, and its clean-up rules are built round breathing them in; this course read
nothing on hantavirus in the UK. Applying CDC's order and clean-up anywhere is this course's reading, not a UK body's advice.

### The signs

CDC names two: droppings and gnaw marks. "Rodent droppings are pointed at one end and often contain
hair." They turn up in cupboards, drawers and places out of sight. Old droppings don't prove mice
are still there, so CDC's test is to clean the area safely and look again: "If droppings are present
after cleaning, this indicates an active rodent presence."[3]

### Seal up

"Mice can fit through a hole the width of a pencil (1/4 inch or 6 millimeters in diameter)."[4] CDC's
list of places to look runs from under and behind kitchen units and appliances to the pipes under
sinks, cupboard corners and where floor meets wall; outside, doors, windows, eaves, vents and the
foot of the walls. Its fixes: steel wool held in with caulk (sealant) or spray foam for small holes,
and metal sheeting, wire mesh or cement for larger ones. Food goes in sealed containers.[4]

CDC's list includes holes round electrical, water, gas and sewer lines.[4] Look there, but by this
course's line above, a gap round a gas pipe, a meter or a cable goes to the landlord or the right
trade. Sealing ordinary small gaps is **Yours**, if it's your home to repair or your landlord agrees.

### Trap up

CDC: "Traditional snap traps are recommended to reduce rodent populations around the home. Only use
poison or bait stations for mouse and rat infestations that persist."[5] And a warning with its
reason: "Do not use glue traps and live traps. These traps can scare the rodents, causing them to
urinate, which can increase your chance of getting sick." Its method:[5]

- Set a lightly baited trap on the floor with the baited end against the wall, so it makes a T.
  "Rodents prefer to run next to walls or other objects for safety."
- Put traps where you've seen signs, and in closed places: behind the cooker and fridge, at the back
  of cupboards. Check them daily, out of reach of children and pets.
- Rats are wary of anything new, so pre-bait: bait the traps unset until the bait is being taken.
- CDC's all-clear: "If no rodents are captured for a week and there are no new signs of rodents, the
  rats are gone."

Setting snap traps this way is **Yours**, by CDC's advice and the trap's own instructions.

### Clean up

This is where most people get it wrong, because the natural thing is to sweep. CDC: "Don't vacuum or
sweep rodent urine, droppings, or nesting materials." Its reason: it "can cause tiny droplets
containing viruses to get into the air". Its steps for a home:[6]

1. **Air it.** "Open all doors and windows for 30 minutes before cleaning to ventilate the space.
   Leave the area during this time."
2. **Gloves.** Rubber or plastic.
3. **Soak.** Spray the droppings and urine with a household disinfectant, or a bleach solution, "1
   part bleach to 9 parts water", until very wet. "Let it soak for 5 minutes or according to
   instructions on the disinfectant label."
4. **Wipe** with paper towels into a covered bin, then disinfect the surfaces round it.
5. **Dead mice and nests:** spray, soak, and bag twice, each bag knotted.
6. **Wash** gloved hands, take the gloves off, and wash your hands.

A small clean-up this way is **Yours, on conditions**: CDC's precautions, every time. CDC says
"Additional precautions should be used for cleaning homes or buildings with heavy rodent
infestation", and sends rodents in heating and cooling ducts, or in places you can't reach to clean,
to a professional.[6][4] Those are **Call someone**.

## Poisons: the label, and who may use what

This section states law. This is general information, not legal advice.

### In the US

EPA says every pesticide label carries the statement "It is a violation of Federal law to use this
product in a manner inconsistent with its labeling." "In other words, the label is the law." EPA says the label sets out conditions that "define who may
use a pesticide, as well as where, how, how much, and how often it may be used".[8]

For rodent poisons, EPA's restrictions page says the consumer products are "ready-to-use bait
stations" with bait in block or paste form, and "Pelleted baits no longer are permitted to be used
in rodenticide products targeted for consumer markets." The second-generation anticoagulants "no
longer are registered for use in products geared toward consumers", because they stay in animal
tissues longer and pose greater risks to other animals; they're for professional pest control. A
consumer station is labelled for indoors, or for indoors and outdoors within 50 feet of buildings,
depending on whether it has been shown to resist tampering by young children and by dogs, so EPA
says to read the label before you buy.[7]

### In Great Britain and Northern Ireland

Rodent poisons come under the GB Biocidal Products Regulation in Great Britain and the EU's in
Northern Ireland, HSE's page says. On the commonest kind, HSE is blunt: anticoagulants "present a
higher risk to people and non-target animals than is normally acceptable for authorisation in Great
Britain and Northern Ireland", and are authorised only because the harm to society of not allowing
them would outweigh that, with strict controls. HSE's advice is to "consider other available control
methods, such as those listed above, before reaching for anticoagulant products". Products for the
public may be restricted in strength, in pack size, and in where they can be used, for example "only
within a bait box".[9]

On who may use what, HSE's biocides guidance says the label tells you who is allowed to use the
product: the general public, professional users, or professionals with demonstrated competence.
"Members of the general public should never use biocidal products that are only intended for
professional users", because professionals must have had training, and some need certification.
For a product meant for the public, "Following the label instructions carefully should be enough to
allow the product to be used safely and effectively." And a product authorised for one purpose:
"the biocide must not be used for any other purpose".[2]

### The law, the advice and the label together

In both countries, then, the label is the rule you meet. The advice sits on top of it: CDC says
poison only for infestations that persist after trapping, and HSE says consider other methods
before anticoagulants.[5][9]

A consumer bait station used by its label, after trapping has failed, is **Yours, on conditions**: the
label's, and CDC's order. A product marked for professionals is another matter: in the US its label
says who may use it and the label is the law, and in Great Britain and Northern Ireland HSE says the
public must never use one. A rodent problem that needs one is **Call someone**. This course's
exercises never have you set poison. If a child may have eaten bait, First Aid and CPR lesson 14,
"Seizures, low blood sugar and poisoning", has the response and each country's number.

## Glue traps: the law in each nation

This section states law. This is general information, not legal advice.

Three UK nations have legislated, differently; this course read the Acts on legislation.gov.uk:

| Where | The law read | What it makes an offence | In force |
|---|---|---|---|
| England | Glue Traps (Offences) Act 2022, section 1 | Setting a glue trap in England to catch a rodent, or in a way that risks catching one; a licence can allow a pest controller | 31 July 2024 |
| Wales | Agriculture (Wales) Act 2023, section 46, amending the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 | Setting in Wales a glue trap likely to catch any animal, or using one to kill or take any animal ("animal" meaning a vertebrate) | 17 October 2023 |
| Scotland | Wildlife Management and Muirburn (Scotland) Act 2024, sections 1 to 3 | Using, supplying or possessing a glue trap without reasonable excuse, unless authorised | 1 July 2026 |
| Northern Ireland | Not read | This course read no Northern Ireland law on glue traps | |

The details matter.[10][11][12]

- **England.** "A person who sets a glue trap in England for the purpose of catching a rodent
  commits an offence." So does someone who finds a set trap that risks catching one and, without
  reasonable excuse, leaves it that way. Licences go only to pest controllers, "for the purpose of
  preserving public health or public safety", and only if there is "no other satisfactory solution".
  The Act extends to England and Wales, but its offences are about traps set in England.
- **Wales.** Wider than England's: any vertebrate, not only rodents. The course read section 46, not
  section 11 of the 1981 Act as amended, so it can't tell you what exceptions that carries.
- **Scotland.** The widest: possession and supply are offences too, and authorisation, under a
  scheme the course did not read, is only for protecting public health where "no other method of
  rodent control is practicable".
- **The US.** This course read no US law on glue traps, only CDC's advice against them.[5]

One wrinkle. The cockroach guide in the next section recommends insect sticky traps for
monitoring.[13] Scotland's Act covers a trap that "is designed, or is capable of being used, to catch
an animal other than an invertebrate" with an adhesive.[12] Whether a small insect trap falls inside
that, this course cannot say from what it read; in Scotland, its line is to ask your council first.

Whether glue traps are humane is a value question. This course read no statement of the case for
them, or of the welfare case against them, in their holders' own words, so it reports the laws and
CDC's health reason and stops there.

## Cockroaches: why the bomb fails

The University of California Statewide IPM Program's guide, written for California homes, puts the
whole approach in two sentences: "Good sanitation and
exclusion are important for effective control; pesticides alone will not solve cockroach problems.
When using insecticides, baits provide better control than sprays."[13] UC calls the German
cockroach "the most common indoor species in California", living near food and water in kitchens and
bathrooms, and "Adult German cockroaches can hide in a crack 1/16 inch wide" (under 2 mm).[13] They
matter for health: UC says indoor infestations are "important sources of allergens and have been identified as risk factors for development of asthma
in children, especially within multi-unit housing environments."[13]

### The two studies

Z. C. DeVries and colleagues at North Carolina State University published two studies in 2019. This
course read the abstracts only. The first tested total release foggers, the "bug bombs" sold in
shops, in 20 infested homes, five for each of four products, against gel baits in 10 homes. Its result, in the authors' words:
"TRFs failed to reduce cockroach populations, whereas similarly priced gel baits caused significant
declines in the cockroach populations." After fogging, pesticide residues on horizontal kitchen
surfaces were 603 times the starting level on average, with a median of 85 times.[14] The authors
note why people use foggers at all: professional pest control "is often prohibitively expensive",
which pushes people on low incomes to over-the-counter products. Their conclusion, a judgement
rather than a measurement, is that the risks and the failure "call into question their utility in
the marketplace".[14]

The second asked why. Cockroaches collected from apartments where foggers had failed were about
202 times more resistant to cypermethrin, a common fogger chemical, than a laboratory strain, and
more than 96% carried at least one copy of a gene mutation known to give resistance to pyrethroids,
the insecticide family the paper's title names. Foggers killed insecticide-susceptible "sentinel"
cockroaches, but not the ones from the apartments. The authors say these results "suggest that pyrethroid resistance is a major factor
contributing to the failure of TRFs".[15] The same abstract found resistance to fipronil, a common
bait ingredient, too, but far lower: about 14 times.

So there are two reasons, from two sources. Resistance is the studies', hedged with "suggest".
Dispersal is UC's: foggers, bombs and sprays "may repel and disperse cockroaches to other areas
without actually killing them", which in a block of flats means next door.[13] That the two work
together is this course's reading.

### What works

UC's guide:[13]

- **Sanitation and exclusion first.** No food left out, leaks fixed, cracks sealed.
- **Baits**, gel or stations, placed near where cockroaches hide. They're slow: "It may take 7 days
  or longer before you see fewer cockroaches."
- **Monitor with sticky traps**, which UC calls "the best way to detect and monitor cockroach
  populations", placed along wall edges and under appliances (and see the Scottish question above).
- **A vacuum with a HEPA (fine-particle) filter**, because shed skins and droppings in the air can
  cause allergies.
- **No foggers, bombs or aerosol sprays.**

A bait used by its label in your own home is **Yours, on conditions**. In a flat, UC's point that
infestations spread through buildings makes it the landlord's problem too, so telling them in
writing comes first (this course's reading; no tenancy law was read).

## Bed bugs: confirm, then be patient

EPA's pages are the only source this course read on bed bugs, and they're American; no UK body was
read, though HSE's biocides guidance above applies to any product used in the UK.[16][17][18]

EPA's tips, in its order:[16]

1. Make sure it's bed bugs, not fleas or ticks: compare with EPA's pictures or show one to an
   extension agent.
2. Don't throw everything out: "Don’t throw out all of your things because most of them can be
   treated and saved." And don't reach first for the spray can; if pesticides are needed, "always
   follow label directions or hire a professional".
3. Cut clutter; put covers (encasements) on the mattress and box spring. "Leave the encasements on
   for a year."
4. Wash and heat-dry bedding. Home freezing "may not be a reliable method".
5. Heat works, but not the thermostat: "Raising the indoor temperature with the thermostat or space
   heaters won’t do the job." "Bed bugs die when their body temperatures reaches 45°C (113°F)", and
   the room or container has to be hotter still.
6. Vacuum, and seal the bag and put it in an outside bin. Slash or destroy a mattress you throw out.
7. For a professional, ask for IPM, and in the US ask your state pesticide agency.

EPA's safety page adds: "Never use a pesticide indoors that is intended for outdoor use."[18]

EPA's myths page corrects four ideas:[17]

- Dirt: "Bed bugs are not attracted to dirt and grime; they are attracted to warmth, blood and carbon
  dioxide." Clutter gives them more hiding places.
- Disease: "There have been no cases or studies that indicate bed bugs transmit diseases between
  humans." EPA still classes them as a public health pest.
- Light: keeping the light on "won’t deter these pests from biting you".
- Pesticide alone: it "will not by itself eliminate bed bugs", partly because of resistance.

Laundry, covers, clutter and vacuuming are **Yours**. A persistent infestation is **Call someone**,
and in a rented home the landlord first.

## Clothes moths

The Natural History Museum in London, writing about UK homes: "It is the immature larvae, rather
than the adult moths, that cause the damage", feeding on keratin, the protein in wool, fur and other
natural fibres.[19] Its controls: "Freezing to -20°C for a week or heating fabric to temperatures of
49°C for over 30 minutes kills all life stages", which it says a home freezer or washing machine can
do (-4°F; about 120°F). Dry cleaning, airtight storage and vacuuming help, and pheromone traps are
for monitoring.[19] All of it is **Yours**. (The page also says one class of insecticide spray leaves
no toxic residues; this course hasn't checked that and doesn't repeat it as advice.)

## Woodworm: old holes, live holes and damp

"Woodworm" is the everyday name for wood-boring insects. The clearest official page this course
read is a heritage page from Babergh and Mid Suffolk District Councils in Suffolk, about older and
listed buildings in their area.[20] Its first point is that active and old attacks are hard to tell
apart: "It can be difficult to prove whether you have an active infestation of wood-boring insects,
at most times of the year. Adult insects mainly emerge in the spring."

The council's signs:[20]

- **Active:** "An active outbreak can sometimes be identified by holes with sharp (rather than
  rounded) edges, alongside bore dust. The interiors of the holes ('frass') are not dark, but the
  colour of freshly-cut timber."
- **Old:** "Otherwise, holes may indicate an old infestation, that's no longer active. In these
  cases, no action is normally needed."
- **The common furniture beetle** makes holes 1 to 2 mm wide.
- **Not sure? Watch for a year.** "Water-soluble glue can be used to cover a group of holes with
  acid-free tissue paper. If there are beetles, they will punch their way out."

Then the cause, and the mechanism as far as the council gives one: "Wood-boring insects are a
symptom of excess damp in a building", because a house at normal temperature and humidity is hostile
to timber pests, and an infestation "is likely to continue until humidity levels are reduced". In old
buildings it blames the damp most often on impermeable finishes such as cement render or modern
paints.[20] That's the ground of lessons 17 and 18: find the water before you treat the wood.

On treatment, the council says an active infestation "might need" treatment straight away, prefers a
paste to a spray, and says: "For a comprehensive spray treatment, you will need listed building
consent." That rule is about listed buildings, on a page about one council's district, not a rule
for every house. The commercial view, that active woodworm needs chemical treatment as a rule, this
course saw only in search snippets from treatment firms, so it does not state it, and it does not
settle the difference.

The tissue-paper test, on furniture or timber that's yours (or with your landlord's agreement), is
**Yours**. An active attack in the fabric of a house is **Call someone**: on the council's advice for
older buildings, a surveyor first, to find the damp. That is this course's reading of a heritage page.

## Termites, in the US

Termites are a US pest in this course's sources; it read nothing on them in the UK. EPA says they
cause "billions of dollars in structural damage" a year.[21] The signs: hollow spots in exposed wood
when you probe it with a flathead screwdriver, and swarms. Ants get mistaken for them; EPA's test is
the wings (a termite's roughly equal in length, an ant's front pair longer) and the antennae
(straight on a termite, bent at a right angle on an ant).[21] Prevention is lesson 17's: dry soil at
the foundation, gutters and downspouts working, leaks fixed, no firewood against the house. EPA
doesn't say why moisture draws termites, and this course doesn't add a reason.

Treatment isn't a householder's job on EPA's account: "In most cases, termiticide application can
only be properly performed by a trained pest management professional", and soil treatments done
badly "can cause contamination of the home and surrounding drinking water wells". And, as EPA states
it: "Firms offering termite services must be licensed by your state."[21] Termites are **Call
someone**.

## Checking a pest controller

- **In the UK**, the British Pest Control Association, a trade association, describes its members as
  having "at least £2M public liability insurance" and being audited to "the British standard in pest
  management BS EN 16636". That's the association on its own members; check a company on its own
  site, not by a logo.[22] HSE, which recommends no companies, suggests your local council.[2]
- **In the US**, EPA says to ask to see a termite firm's state licence and to take concerns to your
  state pesticide regulatory agency, and, for bed bugs, to ask for an IPM approach.[21][16]

## Droppings in a flat on Merseyside

The simple case first. In a 1960s flat on Merseyside, the tenant finds droppings in the cupboard
under the sink and a chewed corner on a packet of rice. It's the first sign in two years.

1. **Identify.** Small, dark, pointed at one end: mouse droppings, on CDC's description.[3]
2. **Threshold.** Fresh signs in one cupboard: enough to act, not a reason for poison.[1]
3. **Tell the landlord**, in writing; anything that changes the building needs their agreement
   (this course's line).
4. **Look for the way in.** Round the pipes under the sink is on CDC's list; anything pencil-width
   counts. A gap round a gas pipe goes to the landlord, not to the steel wool.[4]
5. **Food.** Rice and cereals into sealed containers.[4]
6. **Clean up by CDC's method**: window open 30 minutes, out of the room, gloves, soak with
   disinfectant or 1 part bleach to 9 of water for 5 minutes, paper towels, wipe the cupboard.[6]
7. **Trap.** Snap traps, baited end to the wall, in the cupboard and behind the fridge, checked
   daily.[5] Not a glue board: in England, setting one is an offence under the 2022 Act unless it's
   done under a licence (general information, not legal advice).[10]
8. **The all-clear.** A week with no catches and no new signs, and no fresh droppings after the
   clean-up.[5][3]

Poison comes in only if trapping fails, and then a public product by its label, or a pest
controller.[9] Everything here is **Yours**, except the building work, which is the landlord's.

## Cockroaches in a Philadelphia apartment

In an apartment building in Philadelphia, a tenant has German cockroaches in the kitchen. She's set
off two foggers in the last month, and the numbers seem worse. Her neighbours across the hall have
them too. She's thinking of a third fogger, and a stronger one.

:::checkpoint Before you read on: what did the two foggers most likely do, on the studies and UC's guide, and what should she do instead, in order?

On DeVries and colleagues' first study, foggers "failed to reduce cockroach populations", and they
left kitchen surfaces with pesticide residues that averaged 603 times the level before.[14] On the
second, apartment cockroaches were highly resistant to a common fogger chemical, and the authors say
their results "suggest" that resistance is a major reason.[15] On UC's guide, the foggers may also
have pushed them elsewhere, which fits the neighbours having them.[13] A third fogger would, on this
course's reading of those studies, meet the same resistance.

Instead: (1) tell the landlord in writing, since it is a building-wide problem (this course's
reading); (2) sanitation, food sealed away, leaks reported; (3) gel baits near where they hide,
used by the label, and patience, since UC says 7 days or longer; (4) sticky traps to see where they
are and whether the numbers fall; (5) a professional who uses IPM, through the landlord.[13]
:::

The wrinkle is the building: the cause isn't only in her flat. And nothing in either study blames
the tenant.

## What people get wrong

**"A bug bomb will clear cockroaches."** In the DeVries study foggers didn't reduce them, and left
heavy residues; baits did.[14]

**"Sweep up the droppings, then disinfect."** CDC says never sweep or vacuum them; air the room, soak
them, then wipe.[6]

**"A glue board is fine anywhere."** CDC advises against them for health reasons, and
England, Wales and Scotland each now make setting or using them an offence in their own way (see the
glue traps section).[5][10][11][12]

**"Bed bugs mean a dirty home."** EPA: they're attracted to warmth, blood and carbon dioxide, not dirt.
Nor do they spread disease between people, on EPA's account.[17]

**"Holes in the wood mean live woodworm."** Babergh and Mid Suffolk's page: they may be an old attack,
needing no action; if unsure, watch for a year.[20]

**"An outdoor pesticide will be stronger indoors."** EPA: "Never use a pesticide indoors that is
intended for outdoor use."[18]

## Practice

:::exercise A pest walk-round
Take 30 minutes over this, with a torch and a notebook. It is looking and listing. Don't touch or
sweep any droppings, don't set poison or a fogger, and don't fill, pack or disturb anything round a
gas pipe, meter or electricity cable. Don't climb a ladder for it. If you rent, you list; changing
anything waits for your landlord's agreement.

1. **Gaps.** Walk CDC's list: under and behind the kitchen units and appliances, round the pipes
   under the sink, cupboard corners, doors and windows; outside, from the ground, the foot of the
   walls, vents, and where pipes and cables go in. Note every gap pencil-width or bigger, and who
   deals with each.
2. **Food and water.** Anything in open packets, pet food left down, a dripping tap or a damp
   cupboard base (lesson 18 if so).
3. **Signs.** Droppings, gnaw marks, holes in timber, damage to wool, anything on a mattress
   seam. For each, name what it might be and what would confirm it.
4. **Woodworm, if you have old holes** in furniture or timber that's yours to mark: start the
   council's tissue-paper test on one group of holes, with water-soluble glue and acid-free tissue,
   and write the date in your calendar a year ahead.
5. **Decide.** For each finding, the IPM step it belongs to, and its label: Yours, Yours on
   conditions, or Call someone.
:::

:::checkpoint Three finds. Decide each, with its source and its label. (1) In a rented flat in Stornoway, a few droppings under the sink and a glue board a previous tenant left behind a cupboard. (2) In a house in Charleston, winged insects swarming from a baseboard in spring, and a door frame with hollow spots when probed. (3) In a house in Kirkcaldy, a wool rug with bare trails and small white larvae underneath.

(1) Mice, probably. CDC's order: seal up, snap traps, clean up by its method, and the landlord told
in writing.[4][5][6] The glue board is a legal question in Scotland, where the 2024 Act makes using
and possessing one without reasonable excuse an offence unless authorised; tell the landlord it's
there and ask the council how to dispose of it, which is this course's line (general information,
not legal advice).[12] Label: Yours for the clean-up and traps, the landlord's for the building.

(2) Possibly termites; EPA says
swarms and hollow wood are the signs, and to check the wings and antennae against ants. Treatment
needs a state-licensed professional on EPA's account. Label: Call someone.[21]

(3) Clothes moth
larvae, on the NHM's description of the damage. Freezing at -20°C for a week or heating to 49°C for
over 30 minutes kills all stages, and vacuuming helps; pheromone traps then show whether it worked.
Label: Yours.[19]
:::

## For your plan

To the **calendar**: the pest walk-round from the exercise, once in spring and once in autumn, and the
tissue-paper test's check date a year from now. To the **stop list**: no foggers, no glue traps, no
poison marked for professionals, no sweeping droppings, no filling round gas pipes, meters or
cables. To the **call list**: a pest controller, checked on the trade association's or your state's
own site.
