Lesson 1 gave you the gas law in two lines: registration binds businesses, and competence binds
everyone. This lesson reads the words those two lines come from, and uses them on the cases people
actually meet: a relative's favour, a favour that turns into a sideline, a card held up at the door,
and an appliance you've stopped trusting. Then it looks at the US, where this course found no single
rule at all.

:::callout Before you pick up a tool
This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

## The law in Great Britain and Northern Ireland

This is general information, not legal advice.

:::predict Before you read the guidance: a friend fits your new hob for nothing, as a favour. Is that "work" in the law's sense at all, or does the law only reach people doing gas work for money?

It's work. HSE's guidance says "work" includes do-it-yourself activities, favours for friends and
relatives, and work with no expectation of reward. If you said the law only reaches paid work,
you've mixed up the two duties: payment matters to registration, not to competence. Paragraph 52,
quoted below, is where that comes from.
:::

### Two duties in one regulation

[Regulation 3](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1998/2451/regulation/3) of the Gas Safety
(Installation and Use) Regulations 1998 applies in England, Wales and Scotland. Its first
paragraph: "No person shall carry out any work in relation to a gas fitting or gas storage vessel
unless he is competent to do so."[1] "No person" means anyone.

Its third paragraph is the one people mean when they talk about the Register. In plain words: an
employer can't let staff do gas work, and a self-employed person can't do it, unless they belong
to the class of people HSE has approved. The paragraph itself reads: "no employer shall allow any of
his employees to carry out any work in relation to a gas fitting or service pipework and no
self-employed person shall carry out any such work, unless the employer or self-employed person, as
the case may be, is a member of a class of persons approved for the time being by the Health and
Safety Executive".[1] HSE's page on the Register says a gas business must be on the Gas
Safe Register.[9] Read who the paragraph names:
employers and the self-employed. A householder working on their own home is neither.

Three kinds of text are in play from here on. The Regulations are the law. HSE's Approved Code of
Practice sits inside [L56](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/l56.pdf), HSE's book on the
Regulations, and has a special legal status, below. The rest of L56 is HSE's guidance, which
explains both. The guidance says, in paragraph 52: "For the purposes of GSIUR, ‘work’ includes
do-it-yourself activities, work undertaken as a favour for friends and relatives, and work for which
there is no expectation of reward or gain, eg voluntary activity for charities. This means that
anyone carrying out such work must have the necessary competence, as required by regulation 3(1).
However, membership of an HSE-approved class of persons (under regulation 3(3)) is required only by
businesses carrying out gas-fitting work."[4] (GSIUR is L56's short name for the 1998 Regulations.)

So there are two duties. **Registration** binds those who do gas work as a business.
**Competence** binds everyone, householders included.

### What "competent" means

The Code isn't the Regulations, but it isn't only advice either. L56 says: "You may use alternative
methods to those set out in the Code in order to comply with the law." But the Code "has a
special legal status. If you are prosecuted for breach of health and safety law, and it is proved
that you did not follow the relevant provisions of the Code, you will need to show that you have
complied with the law in some other way or a Court will find you at fault."[4] So there are other
ways to comply, but someone who didn't follow the Code has to prove theirs.

Paragraph 81 is part of the Code, and it says: "Gas work should only be undertaken: (a) by a person
who has successfully completed an industry-recognised training course followed by assessment of
competence." Its other two limbs are for people who are or were registered and have proved
competence through a certification scheme, and for premises outside the Regulations.[4] Paragraph
85, which is guidance, defines the word: "Competence is a combination of practical skill, training,
knowledge and experience to carry out the job in hand safely, and ensuring the installation is left
in a safe condition for use."[4]

And paragraph 83 applies the duty to everyone: "do-it-yourself gas engineers and those performing
favours for friends and relatives all need to have the required competence." It adds that the
competence needed "should match the full extent of work done, but needs only to be sufficient for
and relevant to that work".[4] For gas work beyond the user instructions, the Code's measure is
still the course and the assessment in paragraph 81.

Regulation 3 makes two narrow exceptions, from the registration duty only and not from competence:
replacing the hose or the regulator fitted to a portable or mobile space heater, and replacing a
hose that connects a refillable cylinder to the pipework.[1] HSE's guidance says the same: people
doing those like-for-like replacements "need to have the required competence and ensure that they
follow instructions, eg given by the gas supplier/appliance manufacturer, as appropriate."[4]

So, under the gas safety regulations, "it's illegal to do any gas work yourself" overstates the law,
because the registration duty doesn't reach a householder, and "it's legal, so it's fine" misleads,
because the competence duty does, measured by a recognised course and an assessment. Other law can
apply as well. In England and Wales, the Building Regulations 2010 list installing a heat-producing
gas appliance, such as a boiler, among the jobs a member of the Gas Safe class can self-certify;
lesson 22 comes back to that.

### One rule that binds you in a leak

Regulation 6(5) is a single line: "No person searching for an escape of gas shall use any source of
ignition."[2] L56 says this prohibition "extends to householders and other members of the general
public."[4] So in Great Britain, part of lesson 9's no-flame step is law as well as advice: nobody
looking for a leak may use a flame or any other source of ignition. A lighter held to a pipe to find
a leak is what it forbids.

### What the advice says

The advice is the one lesson 1 gave, and it hasn't moved.
[HSE](https://www.hse.gov.uk/gas/domestic/faqownerocc.htm) "strongly advises that all gas
appliances, flues and pipework should be installed, regularly maintained and serviced at least
annually by a Gas Safe registered engineer", and [the
Register](https://web.archive.org/web/20251023004454/https://www.gassaferegister.co.uk/gas-safety/home-gas-safety/home-improvements/)
says "Don’t DIY with gas appliances, always use a Gas Safe registered engineer".[5][6] So gas work
in Great Britain is **Call someone**.

### Northern Ireland

Northern Ireland has its own instrument, the [Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations
(Northern Ireland) 2004](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisr/2004/63/regulation/3). Its regulation
3 has the same shape: "A person shall not carry out any work in relation to a gas fitting or gas
storage vessel unless he is competent to do so", and the same registration duty on employers and
the self-employed, with the approval given by "the Executive" rather than by HSE.[8] HSE says the Gas
Safe Register covers "Great Britain, Northern Ireland, the Isle of Man and Guernsey".[9] L56 is
written for the 1998 Regulations, and this course did not read the Northern Ireland guidance.

## A free hob from a brother-in-law

This is general information, not legal advice.

Now the harder case, in England. Sam lives in a 1970s townhouse in Rutland and has bought a new gas
hob. His brother-in-law, who is handy, has fitted kitchens and is not registered, offers to put it in
on Saturday for nothing. Sam wants to know where he stands. Read it with him, a step at a time.

**Is it gas work?** Yes. Fitting an appliance is installation, and L56 says installing a gas
appliance counts as work "even where the connection is made by a bayonet fitting or other
self-sealing connector".[4] Paragraph 52 says a favour for a relative is work.

**Does the registration duty reach the brother-in-law?** Regulation 3(3) names employers and the
self-employed, and paragraph 52 says membership "is required only by businesses carrying out
gas-fitting work". An unpaid favour isn't a business, so on those words, no.[1][4]

:::checkpoint Your turn for the next step. Does the competence duty reach the brother-in-law, and what would the Code of Practice want to see before it did?

Yes. Regulation 3(1) says "No person", and paragraph 83 names people "performing favours for
friends and relatives". The Code's measure is a recognised training course followed by an
assessment, and having fitted kitchens isn't that.[1][4]
:::

**What about having it checked afterwards?** The brother-in-law suggests a registered engineer could
look it over when he's done. The Gas Safe Register says of this: "It is illegal for someone who is
not Gas Safe registered to fit a gas appliance or do other gas work and then have the work checked
by a Gas Safe registered engineer. Both parties would be breaking the law."[6] That is the
Register's statement. If the first person does gas work as a business, regulation 3(3) covers them.
For an unpaid helper who does have the competence, this course read regulation 3 and HSE's
guidance and couldn't find the provision the Register has in mind, so it reports the
sentence as Gas Safe's, not as the law. Either way, a check afterwards doesn't supply the competence
the law asks of the person doing the work.

**And the advice?** HSE and the Register both say to use a Gas Safe registered engineer.[5][6] The
Register adds that using an illegal gas worker "could invalidate the warranty on your boiler and may
invalidate your home insurance".[7] What home insurance does and does not cover is Personal Finance
Fundamentals lesson 8, "Handing off what you cannot absorb".

So Sam's hob is **Call someone**: the law asks for a competence neither of them has, and the
advice is a registered engineer.

:::checkpoint Change one fact. The brother-in-law says he'll do it for £60 "to cover his time", and he does the odd job like this for other people too. Which part of regulation 3 now matters that didn't before? And does it help him that gas is only a sideline to his kitchen work?

Regulation 3(3). The Regulations were made under the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, and a
general rule of interpretation gives words in them that Act's meaning unless they say otherwise.
[Section 53](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1974/37/section/53) of the Act defines a
"self-employed person" as "an individual who works for gain or reward otherwise than under a
contract of employment". £60 for his time is reward, so on this course's reading he'd be doing the
job as a self-employed person, and the registration duty would reach him as well as the competence
duty.[14][15] The sideline doesn't help him: L56 paragraph 91 says gas engineering businesses,
"including self-employed gas engineers", must be members of the approved class "whether they carry
out such work as their main or part activity".[4] That reading of regulation 3(3) and HSE's
guidance is this course's, not a ruling on anyone's case.
:::

## What you may do yourself

This is general information, not legal advice.

If gas work is for an engineer, what is left for you? The Register answers on the same archived
page. You "can perform the tasks set out in the user’s instructions, (provided by the appliance
manufacturer) that are intended for the user to carry out. This would not be a breach of the law."
And: "The law allows you to replace or adjust any component or control that is designed to be
operated or replaced by the consumer e.g., a cooker tap control knob." It then draws the line: do
nothing that disturbs the gas-carrying parts, such as the supply pipe, or that could affect
combustion.[6]

The same page deals with the case on an appliance. A decorative case, it says, can usually be
removed by the consumer, such as ones that "can be lifted off or are hinged panels and do not need
to be unscrewed". But many cases also form part of the combustion circuit, and "If removing the case
involves undoing a number of screws, this normally means it is a functional case and it should not
be removed by the consumer".[6] In doubt, it sends you to the maker's instructions, helpline or
website.

HSE, in its guidance for landlords, gives one everyday case. A free-standing cooker connected by a
flexible connector (a bayonet fitting) can be moved temporarily, for instance to clean the space it
stands in, and that is not "work" under the Regulations. Anything more is, and HSE says it "must be
carried out by a Gas Safe registered engineer". For a householder working on their own home that
is advice, since the legal duty there is the competence one above.[10] The Register's own advice is stricter: "never try to fix, fit
or move appliances like your boiler or cooker yourself".[6] So even the temporary move is **Yours, on
conditions**: the cooker's user instructions allow it and you don't disturb the connection.
Otherwise, Call someone.

Two more lines from the Register belong on your list. Know where your gas pipes run, so that you
"don’t accidentally hit them during DIY work", and don't block or cover air vents and flues.[6] And
some work inside a boiler's casing doesn't need a Gas Safe engineer at all: the Register says a
non-gas part such as a water circulating pump or a central heating control valve can be replaced by
another competent tradesperson, a plumber or an electrician, provided nobody breaks a combustion
chamber seal or disturbs a gas-carrying part or the module that controls combustion.[6] That's a
trade's job, not yours; it's here because a plumber may be the right person for some boiler faults.

So the user tasks are **Yours, on conditions**, and the condition is that the appliance's own user
instructions give the job to the user.

## Checking the engineer

:::predict Before you read what's on the card: an engineer arrives to fit a gas fire, and his Gas Safe card is in date and the photograph is his. Is that enough to let him start?

Not yet. The back of the card lists the kinds of work he's qualified for, and a gas fire has to be
among them. Then there's one more check, against the Register itself, which the next paragraphs
set out.
:::

A Gas Safe registered engineer carries an ID card. HSE describes it: "The front of the card has a
photograph, a registration number and an expiry date and the reverse shows the different categories
of work that the engineer is qualified to undertake, eg cookers, boilers, gas fires."[9]

The back is the part people skip. Registration is not a licence for every gas job: the card lists
what this engineer is qualified for, and HSE's home-owner page says "The gas engineer's competencies
are clearly marked on the back of the engineer's Gas Safe Register ID card."[5]

To check that the person at your door is the engineer on the Register, HSE's page for home owners
sends you to the Register's Check an Engineer service on [its
website](https://www.gassaferegister.co.uk/). You enter "the licence number from the engineers ID
card", and it shows pictures of registered engineers and their qualifications.[5] HSE's page on the
card calls the number on its front a registration number, and this course didn't see a card, so if
you can't tell which number is the licence number, ring the Register. HSE gives its freephone
helpline as 0800 408 5500, and says the helpline can also send the engineer's picture to a
mobile phone as a picture message.[5] For a question about gas safety rather than about one
engineer, HSE runs a free Gas Safety Advice Line on 0800 300 363, open 9.00am to 5.30pm Monday to
Thursday and 9.00am to 5.00pm on Friday, bank holidays excepted.[5]

Here is the check done once, in the simple case. Priya lives in a semi outside Chelmsford, and her
combi boiler is being replaced. The engineer arrives and she asks for his card, as the Register's page
says to.[6] On the front: his photograph, which matches him, a registration number, and an expiry
date next spring. On the back, among the categories, is one for boilers. She types the licence number
into Check an Engineer on the Register's website, and the name, photograph and qualifications match
the card.[5] The job is in his categories, the card is in date and the Register agrees, so she lets
him start. Had the back listed only cookers and fires, she would have asked for an engineer whose card
covers boilers.

The reason to bother is the Register's own figure: "Approximately two thirds of illegal gas work
inspected by the Register has been found to be unsafe."[7] That is the Register's figure for the work
it inspected, with no year or count given on the page; it describes work that reached an inspection,
not all unregistered work. How to check a trader more generally, and what to agree before work
starts, is lesson 23, "Hiring a tradesperson, and when to stop". The doorstep pressure pattern is
Personal Safety lesson 5, "Home, the door and the doorstep".

## An appliance you don't trust, and the annual service

This is general information, not legal advice.

HSE is plain about the first: "It is illegal for anyone to use a gas appliance if they suspect it is
unsafe. Turn the appliance off and do not touch it until it has been checked by a Gas Safe registered
engineer."[5] The regulation behind it, [regulation
34](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1998/2451/regulation/34), puts the duty on "the responsible
person", which it defines as the occupier, the owner and anyone with authority to act on the gas
fittings there, so it covers most readers of this course.[16]

The signs that should make you suspect one are set out differently in each country. In Great
Britain, HSE's warning signs at the appliance are lesson 11's, with carbon monoxide. In the US,
Columbia Gas of Ohio, about its own customers, tells them to contact "a qualified professional" if
they notice a change such as no heat or overheating from a furnace or boiler, a yellow flame or soot
around a burner on a gas stove, or a furnace or water heater fan that keeps kicking on and off.[11]

The annual service is HSE's advice, quoted above: installed, maintained and "serviced at least
annually by a Gas Safe registered engineer".[5] HSE's reason is on the same page: "Every year around
7 people die from gas-related carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning." The page gives no year for the
figure.[5] HSE also says a carbon monoxide alarm "should not be regarded as a replacement for regular
maintenance".[5] Lesson 11, "Carbon monoxide and the alarms that warn you", sets out that
order in full.

If you rent in Great Britain, the law puts the check on your landlord. Regulation 36 requires a
landlord to have each gas appliance and flue they provide "checked for safety within 12 months of
being installed and at intervals of not more than 12 months since it was last checked", to keep a
record, and to give a copy to each existing tenant "within 28 days of the date of the check".[3]
(Where no gas appliance is in any room the tenant occupies, the landlord may display the record
instead.) The duty covers homes let on a lease of less than seven years or a periodic tenancy, and
homes or rooms let under a licence. HSE's domestic gas FAQs, in the section for tenants, include a
specimen letter asking to see the record.[10] That is the whole of the landlord law this course
teaches; the rest of tenancy law is not taught here.

## Who may work on gas in the US

This is general information, not legal advice.

This course read no general US rule on who may do gas work. What it read is one utility's advice and
two places' law.

The advice: [Columbia Gas of Ohio](https://www.columbiagasohio.com/safety/home-safety/appliance-safety/safe-appliance-installation)
says "For your safety, a qualified professional should perform all work on natural gas lines and
equipment inside the home", and of fitting an appliance, "Never attempt this yourself!"[11] It says
"qualified", not "licensed".

New York City: the city's Department of Buildings says that ordinary repair work needing no permit,
including "allowable replacement/relocation of plumbing, gas piping, equipment and components", still
"must be performed by a Licensed Master Plumber."[12] No permit is not the same as no licence.

Texas: the state's plumbing licence law says "A property owner is not required to be licensed under
this chapter to perform plumbing in the property owner's homestead" (homestead is the statute's word,
and this course didn't read where Texas law defines it). It defines "plumbing" to include piping used
to "supply, distribute, circulate, or recirculate water, other liquid, or gas".[13] So the state
licence law does not require a homestead owner to be a licensed plumber for gas piping. That is a
statement about the licensing statute, read in the state board's unofficial 2023 text. The exemption
says nothing about permits and inspections, which are set locally, and this course did not read any
Texas city's rules.

The Texas exemption is another case of the law allowing more than the advice read, which is a
utility's "qualified professional". Wherever you are in the US, ask your city or county building
department what a permit needs, and your gas utility what it advises, before anyone touches a gas
line. On the advice read, the label is **Call someone**.

## What people get wrong

**"It's illegal to do any gas work yourself."** Not in those words. In Great Britain the
registration duty binds businesses, and the competence duty binds everyone (see "The law in Great
Britain and Northern Ireland" above).

**"It's legal for me to do it, so it's fine."** The competence duty is law too; see what
"competent" means, above. HSE and the Register both advise a registered engineer.

**"If a registered engineer checks it afterwards, it's all right."** The Register says that
arrangement puts both parties in breach (a statement this course couldn't match to a provision for
a competent unpaid helper; see "A free hob from a brother-in-law"). Either way, a check afterwards does not
make the first person competent.[6]

**"A Gas Safe card means the engineer can do any gas job."** The back lists the categories they are
qualified for (HSE).[9]

**"Texas lets homeowners do their own gas work, so America does."** Texas's exemption is from one
state's licence law, and says nothing about permits there or about any other state (see "Who may work on gas in the
US" above).[13]

## Practice

:::exercise A card to read, and eight jobs to sort

Take 10 minutes over these. Don't move, open or unscrew any gas appliance for this exercise. Nothing
here needs you near an appliance.

1. **Read a card.** An engineer shows you a card: a photograph, a registration number, an expiry
   date two months away, and on the back "Cookers", "Central heating boilers" and "Water heaters".
   Which of these jobs does the card cover: a new gas fire in the lounge; a service of a combi
   boiler; moving a hob to a new worktop? Write what you would check on the Register's website
   before letting each go ahead.
2. **Sort eight jobs.** For each, write "user task" (Yours, on conditions), "gas work" (Call
   someone), "not gas work at all", or "another trade", and say whose statement puts it there. In
   Great Britain unless marked. This is general information, not legal advice.
   1. Unscrewing a gas fire's front case, held by six screws, to look at a rattle
   2. Pulling a free-standing bayonet-fitted cooker out to clean behind it, then pushing it back
   3. Moving that same cooker to the other side of the kitchen, on a new pipe
   4. Lifting off a hinged decorative panel on a fire, as the maker's guide shows
   5. Replacing the hose on a portable cabinet heater run from a gas bottle
   6. Replacing a boiler's water pump, with no gas part or seal disturbed
   7. Taking off a boiler's bottom panel that its user guide tells you to remove to reach the
      filling controls
   8. In New York City, replacing a length of gas pipe under the kitchen sink yourself

Write your answers before you open the checkpoint.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your card reading and your eight jobs.

**The card.** The gas fire isn't covered: gas fires aren't among its categories. The boiler service
looks covered by "Central heating boilers". Whether "Cookers" covers a hob, check the engineer's
qualifications on the Register's Check an Engineer service; this course didn't read the Register's
own list of categories. HSE is the source for what's on the card and for the checking service.[5][9]

**The eight jobs.**

1. **Gas work**: a case held by a number of screws is normally a functional case (the Register).[6]
2. **Not gas work at all** under the Regulations, for a temporary move to clean (HSE, in its
   guidance for landlords).[10] The Register advises never moving a cooker yourself, so it's Yours,
   on conditions: only if the cooker's user instructions give it to you.[6]
3. **Gas work**: reinstallation, which HSE says is for a Gas Safe registered engineer.[10]
4. **User task**, following the maker's instructions and isolating any electrical parts (the
   Register).[6]
5. **Gas work, and the trap in the list.** Regulation 3(4) takes this job out of the registration
   duty, not out of the competence duty, and HSE's guidance says people doing it need the required
   competence and must follow the supplier's or maker's instructions. On the advice read, it's Call
   someone unless the heater's user instructions give it to the user.[1][4]
6. **Another trade**: the Register says another competent tradesperson, such as a plumber, can
   replace a non-gas part inside the casing on those conditions. For you, it's still Call someone,
   and the someone is a plumber.[6]
7. **User task**, because the maker's user guide gives it to the user; lesson 7 met this panel in
   Worcester Bosch's filling guide. The rule is the Register's.[6]
8. **Gas work**, and in New York City a Licensed Master Plumber's by the city's rules, even
   without a permit (NYC Department of Buildings).[12]

If you put 5 as a user task because the Regulations exempt it, you read one duty and missed the
other, which is the mistake this whole lesson is about.
:::

## For your plan

This lesson adds to the **calendar**: every gas appliance serviced once a year by a Gas Safe
registered engineer (HSE), or by a qualified professional in the US; and, if you rent in Great
Britain, the landlord's check record within 28 days of each check. It adds to the **call list**: how
to check an engineer on the Register's site, the Register's helpline, and HSE's Gas Safety Advice
Line. It adds to the **stop list**: any gas job beyond the user instructions, any case held on with
screws, and any appliance you suspect is unsafe, which is turned off and not used until an engineer
has checked it.
