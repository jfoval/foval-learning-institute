You may have heard that it's illegal to do your own gas work. In Great Britain that is wrong in a
way that matters. The real rule is looser than that in one
direction and stricter in the other, and it takes about three sentences of the regulation to see
how. This lesson reads those sentences with you, then sets out what the law and the gas companies
leave to you, how to check the engineer you let in, what to do about an appliance you don't trust,
and where the US stands. By the end you should be able to answer a friend who says either "you
can't touch it" or "it's your house, you can do what you like", and tell them which part each has
wrong.

:::callout Before you pick up a tool
This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

## The law in Great Britain and Northern Ireland

This is general information, not legal advice.

:::predict Before you read the regulation: a householder fits her own gas hob, unpaid; a self-employed kitchen fitter fits one for a customer and charges for it. Which of them does Great Britain's law require to be on the Gas Safe Register, and which does it require to be competent?

Registration: only the fitter. Competence: both. That is the whole shape of regulation 3, and the
rest of this section reads it in its own words. If you said "both must be registered", you have
the version that overstates it. If you said "neither, it's her house", you
missed the duty that binds everyone.
:::

### Two duties in one regulation

[Regulation 3](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1998/2451/regulation/3) of the Gas Safety
(Installation and Use) Regulations 1998 extends to England, Wales and Scotland. Its first paragraph:
"No person shall carry out any work in relation to a gas fitting or gas storage vessel unless he is
competent to do so."[1] "No person" means anyone.

Its third paragraph is the one people mean when they talk about the Register. It says "no employer
shall allow any of his employees to carry out any work in relation to a gas fitting or service
pipework and no self-employed person shall carry out any such work, unless the employer or
self-employed person, as the case may be, is a member of a class of persons approved for the time
being by the Health and Safety Executive".[1] The only body with that approval is the Gas Safe
Register.[4] Read who it names: employers and the self-employed. A householder working on their own
home is neither.

HSE's guidance on the Regulations, known as L56, spells out what that means. In paragraph 52: "For
the purposes of GSIUR, ‘work’ includes do-it-yourself activities, work undertaken as a favour for
friends and relatives, and work for which there is no expectation of reward or gain, eg voluntary
activity for charities. This means that anyone carrying out such work must have the necessary
competence, as required by regulation 3(1). However, membership of an HSE-approved class of persons
(under regulation 3(3)) is required only by businesses carrying out gas-fitting work."[4]

So there are two duties. **Registration binds those who do gas work as a business. Competence binds
everyone**, householders included.

### What "competent" means

L56 answers that in two kinds of text. Paragraph 81 is part of HSE's Approved Code of Practice, and
it says: "Gas work should only be undertaken: (a) by a person who has successfully completed an
industry-recognised training course followed by assessment of competence." Its other two limbs are
for people who are or were registered and have proved competence through a certification scheme,
and for premises outside the Regulations.[4] Paragraph 85, which is guidance, defines the word:
"Competence is a combination of practical skill, training, knowledge and experience to carry out the
job in hand safely, and ensuring the installation is left in a safe condition for use."[4]

The Code isn't the Regulations, but it isn't only advice either. L56 says the Code "has a special
legal status. If you are prosecuted for breach of health and safety law, and it is proved that you
did not follow the relevant provisions of the Code, you will need to show that you have complied
with the law in some other way or a Court will find you at fault."[4] And paragraph 83 leaves no
room for the gifted amateur: "do-it-yourself gas engineers and those performing favours for friends
and relatives all need to have the required competence."[4]

Regulation 3 makes two narrow exceptions, from the registration duty only and not from competence:
replacing a hose or regulator on a portable or mobile space heater, and replacing a hose that
connects a refillable cylinder to the pipework.[1]

That gives you the exact position. "It's illegal to do any gas work yourself" overstates the law,
because the registration duty does not reach a householder. "It's legal, so it's fine" misleads,
because the competence duty does, and HSE's Code measures competence by a recognised course and an
assessment, which a householder who has never trained in gas work will not have.

### One rule that binds you in a leak

Regulation 6(5) is a single line: "No person searching for an escape of gas shall use any source of
ignition."[2] L56 says this prohibition "extends to householders and other members of the general
public."[4] So in Great Britain, lesson 9's no-flame step is law as well as advice. A
lighter held to a pipe to find a leak is exactly what it forbids.

### What the advice says

The law allows a householder more than the advice does, and this course gives both, law first. The
advice is not in doubt. HSE: "HSE strongly advises that all gas appliances, flues and pipework should
be installed, regularly maintained and serviced at least annually by a Gas Safe registered
engineer."[5] The [Gas Safe
Register](https://web.archive.org/web/20251023004454/https://www.gassaferegister.co.uk/gas-safety/home-gas-safety/home-improvements/),
on its own page: "Don’t DIY with gas appliances, always use a Gas Safe registered engineer".[6] So gas
work in Great Britain is **Call someone**, although the registration duty does not bind you. The
label follows the advice.

### Northern Ireland

Northern Ireland has its own instrument, the [Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations
(Northern Ireland) 2004](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisr/2004/63/regulation/3). Its regulation
3 has the same shape: "A person shall not carry out any work in relation to a gas fitting or gas
storage vessel unless he is competent to do so", and the same registration duty on employers and
the self-employed, with the approval given by "the Executive" rather than by HSE.[8] HSE says the Gas
Safe Register covers "Great Britain, Northern Ireland, the Isle of Man and Guernsey".[9] L56 is
written for the 1998 Regulations, and this course did not read the Northern Ireland guidance.

## A free hob from a brother-in-law

Now the harder case, in England. Sam lives in a 1970s townhouse in Rutland and has bought a new gas
hob. His brother-in-law, who is handy, has fitted kitchens and is not registered, offers to put it in
on Saturday for nothing. Sam wants to know where he stands. Read it with him, a step at a time.

**Is it gas work?** Yes. Fitting an appliance is installation, and L56 says installing a gas
appliance counts as work "even where the connection is made by a bayonet fitting or other
self-sealing connector".[4] Paragraph 52 says a favour for a relative is work.

**Does the registration duty reach the brother-in-law?** Regulation 3(3) names employers and the
self-employed, and paragraph 52 says membership "is required only by businesses carrying out
gas-fitting work". An unpaid favour isn't a business, so on those words, no.[1][4]

**Does the competence duty?** Yes. Regulation 3(1) says "No person", and paragraph 83 names people
"performing favours for friends and relatives". The Code's measure is a recognised training course
followed by an assessment, and having fitted kitchens isn't that.[1][4]

**What about having it checked afterwards?** The brother-in-law suggests a registered engineer could
look it over when he's done. The Gas Safe Register says of exactly this: "It is illegal for someone
who is not Gas Safe registered to fit a gas appliance or do other gas work and then have the work
checked by a Gas Safe registered engineer. Both parties would be breaking the law."[6] That is the
Register's statement. This course read regulation 3 and HSE's guidance on it and could not match the
sentence to a particular provision, so it reports it as Gas Safe's, not as the law. Either way, a
check afterwards doesn't supply the competence the law asks of the person doing the work.

**And the advice?** HSE and the Register both say to use a Gas Safe registered engineer.[5][6] The
Register adds that using an illegal gas worker "could invalidate the warranty on your boiler and may
invalidate your home insurance".[7] What home insurance does and does not cover is Personal Finance
Fundamentals lesson 8, "Handing off what you cannot absorb".

So Sam's hob is **Call someone**. Not because the law forbids every householder from touching gas,
which it doesn't, but because the law asks for a competence that neither of them has, and every
official source read says to use a registered engineer.

:::checkpoint Change one fact. The brother-in-law says he'll do it for £60 "to cover his time", and he does the odd job like this for other people too. Which part of regulation 3 now matters that didn't before, and what does L56 say about it?

Regulation 3(3). Doing gas work for payment, as something he offers other people, is doing it as a
self-employed person, which is the case 3(3) names, so the registration duty now reaches him as
well as the competence duty. L56 paragraph 91 says all gas engineering businesses, "including
self-employed gas engineers", must be members of the approved class "whether they carry out such
work as their main or part activity".[4] So it doesn't help that gas isn't his main trade. That
reading of how the guidance applies to him is this course's, not a ruling on anyone's case.
:::

## What you may do yourself

If gas work is for an engineer, what is left for you? The Register answers on the same archived
page. You "can perform the tasks set out in the user’s instructions, (provided by the appliance
manufacturer) that are intended for the user to carry out. This would not be a breach of the law."
And: "The law allows you to replace or adjust any component or control that is designed to be
operated or replaced by the consumer e.g., a cooker tap control knob." It then draws the line: do
nothing that disturbs the gas-carrying parts, such as the supply pipe, or that could affect
combustion.[6]

The same page deals with the case on an appliance. A decorative case, it says, can usually be
removed by the consumer, such as ones that "can be lifted off or are hinged panels and do not need
to be unscrewed". But many cases also form part of the combustion circuit, and "If removing the case
involves undoing a number of screws, this normally means it is a functional case and it should not
be removed by the consumer".[6] In doubt, it sends you to the maker's instructions, helpline or
website.

HSE adds one everyday case. A free-standing cooker connected by a bayonet fitting can be moved
temporarily, for instance to clean the space it stands in, and that is not "work" under the
Regulations. HSE goes on: "Any other type of installation/reinstallation is regarded as gas work and
must be carried out by a Gas Safe registered engineer".[10]

Two more lines from the Register belong on your list. Know where your gas pipes run, so that you
"don’t accidentally hit them during DIY work", and don't block or cover air vents and flues.[6] And
some work inside a boiler's casing doesn't need a Gas Safe engineer at all: the Register says a
non-gas part such as a water circulating pump or a central heating control valve can be replaced by
another competent tradesperson, a plumber or an electrician, provided nobody breaks a combustion
chamber seal or disturbs a gas-carrying part or the module that controls combustion.[6] That is for a
trade, not a householder. It is here so you know why a plumber may be the right person for some
boiler faults.

So the user tasks are **Yours, on conditions**, and the condition is that the appliance's own user
instructions give the job to the user.

## Checking the engineer

A Gas Safe registered engineer carries an ID card. HSE describes it: "The front of the card has a
photograph, a registration number and an expiry date and the reverse shows the different categories
of work that the engineer is qualified to undertake, eg cookers, boilers, gas fires."[9]

The back is the part people skip. Registration is not a licence for every gas job: the card lists
what this engineer is qualified for, and HSE's home-owner page says "The gas engineer's competencies
are clearly marked on the back of the engineer's Gas Safe Register ID card."[5] L56 says the same
from the trade's side: the scope of what a registered engineer is competent to do can be had from
their certificate of competence or from the Register.[4]

To check a card, HSE points to the Register's Check an Engineer service on its website, which takes
the licence number from the card and shows the engineer's photograph and qualifications, and to a
text service.[5] For a question about gas safety rather than about one engineer, HSE runs a free Gas
Safety Advice Line on 0800 300 363, open 9.00am to 5.30pm Monday to Thursday and 9.00am to 5.00pm on
Friday, bank holidays excepted.[5]

Here is the check done once, in the simple case. Priya lives in a semi outside Chelmsford, and her
combi boiler is being replaced. The engineer arrives and she asks for his card, as the Register's page
says to.[6] On the front: his photograph, which matches him, a registration number, and an expiry
date next spring. On the back, among the categories, is one for boilers. She types the licence number
into Check an Engineer on the Register's website, and the name, photograph and qualifications match
the card.[5] The job is in his categories, the card is in date and the Register agrees, so she lets
him start. Had the back listed only cookers and fires, she would have asked for an engineer whose card
covers boilers.

Why bother? The Register says: "Approximately two thirds of illegal gas work inspected by the
Register has been found to be unsafe."[7] That is the Register's figure for the work it inspected,
with no year or count given on the page; it describes work that reached an inspection, not all
unregistered work. How to check a trader more generally, and what to agree before work starts, is
lesson 23, "Hiring a tradesperson, and when to stop". The doorstep pressure pattern is Personal
Safety lesson 5, "Home, the door and the doorstep".

## An appliance you don't trust, and the annual service

This is general information, not legal advice.

HSE is plain about the first: "It is illegal for anyone to use a gas appliance if they suspect it is
unsafe. Turn the appliance off and do not touch it until it has been checked by a Gas Safe registered
engineer."[5] This course gives that as HSE states it; it did not read the regulation behind it.

What makes you suspect one? HSE's warning signs at the appliance are lesson 11's, with carbon
monoxide. In the US, Columbia Gas of Ohio tells its customers to contact "a qualified professional"
if they notice a change such as no heat or overheating from a furnace or boiler, a yellow flame or
soot around a burner on a gas stove, or a furnace or water heater fan that keeps kicking on and
off.[11]

The annual service is HSE's advice, quoted above: installed, maintained and "serviced at least
annually by a Gas Safe registered engineer".[5] HSE's reason is on the same page: "Every year around
7 people die from gas-related carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning." The page gives no year for the
figure.[5] HSE also says a carbon monoxide alarm "should not be regarded as a replacement for regular
maintenance".[5] Lesson 11, "Carbon monoxide and the alarms that warn you", sets out that
order in full.

If you rent in Great Britain, the law puts the check on your landlord. Regulation 36 requires a
landlord to have each gas appliance and flue they provide "checked for safety within 12 months of
being installed and at intervals of not more than 12 months since it was last checked", to keep a
record, and to give a copy to each existing tenant "within 28 days of the date of the check".[3]
("Lease" there means one for a term of less than seven years, or a periodic tenancy.) HSE's page for
tenants includes a specimen letter asking to see the record.[10] That is the whole of the landlord law
this course teaches; the rest of tenancy law is not taught here.

## Who may work on gas in the US

This is general information, not legal advice.

This course read no general US rule on who may do gas work. What it read is one utility's advice and
two places' law.

The advice: [Columbia Gas of Ohio](https://www.columbiagasohio.com/safety/home-safety/appliance-safety/safe-appliance-installation)
says "For your safety, a qualified professional should perform all work on natural gas lines and
equipment inside the home", and of fitting an appliance, "Never attempt this yourself!"[11] It says
"qualified", not "licensed".

New York City: the city's Department of Buildings says that ordinary repair work needing no permit,
including "allowable replacement/relocation of plumbing, gas piping, equipment and components", still
"must be performed by a Licensed Master Plumber."[12] No permit is not the same as no licence.

Texas: the state's plumbing licence law says "A property owner is not required to be licensed under
this chapter to perform plumbing in the property owner's homestead", and it defines "plumbing" to
include piping used to "supply, distribute, circulate, or recirculate water, other liquid, or
gas".[13] So the state licence law does not require a homestead owner to be a licensed plumber for
gas piping. That is a statement about the licensing statute, read in the state board's unofficial
2023 text. It says nothing about permits and inspections, which are set locally, and this course did
not read any Texas city's rules.

The Texas exemption is another case of the law allowing more than the advice read, which is a
utility's "qualified professional". Wherever you are in the US, ask your city or county building
department what a permit needs, and your gas utility what it advises, before anyone touches a gas
line. On the advice read, the label is **Call someone**.

:::checkpoint A homeowner in a house she owns and lives in outside San Antonio, Texas, wants to replace the gas line to her water heater herself. What does the state plumbing licence law say, what doesn't it settle, and what does the one utility page read advise?

The licence law exempts a property owner doing plumbing in her homestead from the state licence,
and its definition of plumbing covers gas piping, so the state licence is not required of her (the
board's unofficial 2023 text).[13] It does not settle permits and inspections, which are local, so
she asks her city's building department. And Columbia Gas of Ohio, the one utility page read, says a
qualified professional should do all work on gas lines inside the home; she asks her own utility
too. On that advice the label is Call someone.[11]
:::

## What people get wrong

**"It's illegal to do any gas work yourself."** Not in those words. In Great Britain the
registration duty binds businesses, and the competence duty binds everyone (see "The law in Great
Britain and Northern Ireland" above).

**"It's legal for me to do it, so it's fine."** The competence duty is law too, and HSE's Code
measures it by a recognised course and an assessment. HSE and the Register both advise a registered
engineer.

**"If a registered engineer checks it afterwards, it's all right."** The Register says that
arrangement puts both parties in breach, and whatever one makes of that, a check afterwards does not
make the first person competent.[6]

**"A Gas Safe card means the engineer can do any gas job."** The back lists the categories they are
qualified for (HSE).[9]

**"My CO alarm means the boiler can skip its service."** HSE says an alarm is not a replacement for
maintenance; lesson 11 has the rest.[5]

**"Texas lets homeowners do their own gas work, so America does."** Texas's exemption is from one
state's licence law, and says nothing about permits there or about any other state.[13]

**The card.** The gas fire isn't covered: gas fires aren't among its categories. The boiler service
looks covered by "Central heating boilers". Whether "Cookers" covers a hob, check the engineer's
qualifications on the Register's Check an Engineer service; this course didn't read the Register's
own list of categories. HSE is the source for what's on the card and for the checking service.[5][9]

**The ten jobs.**

1. **User task**, if the user instructions give it to the user (the Register).[6]
2. **User task**, the Register's own example.[6]
3. **Gas work**: a case held by a number of screws is normally a functional case (the Register).[6]
4. **Not work** under the Regulations, for a temporary move to clean (HSE).[10]
5. **Gas work**: reinstallation, which HSE says is for a Gas Safe registered engineer.[10]
6. **User task**, following the maker's instructions and isolating any electrical parts (the
   Register).[6]
7. **Gas work, and the trap in the list.** Regulation 3(4) takes this job out of the registration
   duty, not out of the competence duty, so the law still asks for competence. On the advice read,
   it's Call someone unless the heater's user instructions give it to the user.[1]
8. **Another trade**: the Register says another competent tradesperson, such as a plumber, can
   replace a non-gas part inside the casing on those conditions. For you, it's still Call someone,
   and the someone is a plumber.[6]
9. **User task**, because the maker's user guide gives it to the user; lesson 7 met this panel in
   Worcester Bosch's filling guide. The rule is the Register's.[6]
10. **Gas work**, and in New York City a Licensed Master Plumber's by the city's rules, even
    without a permit (NYC Department of Buildings).[12]

If you put 7 as a user task because the Regulations exempt it, you read one duty and missed the
other, which is the mistake this whole lesson is about.
:::

## For your plan

This lesson adds to the **calendar**: every gas appliance serviced once a year by a Gas Safe
registered engineer (HSE), or by a qualified professional in the US; and, if you rent in Great
Britain, the landlord's check record within 28 days of each check. It adds to the **call list**: how
to check an engineer's card on the Register's site, and HSE's Gas Safety Advice Line. It adds to the
**stop list**: any gas job beyond the user instructions, any case held on with screws, and any
appliance you suspect is unsafe, which is turned off and not used until an engineer has checked it.
