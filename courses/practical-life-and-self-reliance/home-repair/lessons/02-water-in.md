Water comes into a house under pressure, through one pipe, and most of what goes wrong with it
happens where nobody looks: under a lawn, in a crawl space (the low space under a raised ground
floor), inside a toilet, in a pipe along an
outside wall on the coldest night of the year. This lesson follows the water from the street to
the taps. You'll learn who is responsible for which length of pipe, how to tell whether your
pressure is normal, how to prove a leak exists before you hunt for it and how much a drip really
wastes, and what to do on the morning a pipe freezes, which is where the UK and US advice part
company.

:::callout Before you pick up a tool
This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

Nothing in this lesson opens a pipe, so the callout's "isolate the water" matters here mainly on
the morning a pipe freezes. Then it means the stop tap (stopcock) in a UK home, or the main shut-off
valve in a US one, which you found in lesson 1, How a house works, and where it switches off.
[WaterSafe's rule](https://www.watersafe.org.uk/advice/general_plumbing_advice/winter_advice/locate-internal-stop-tap/)
for turning it is short: "Turn it on and off slowly, never force it."[7]

## Whose pipe is it?

Before anyone pays to fix a leak, find out whose leak it is. In England and Wales the answer comes
from the [Consumer Council for Water](https://www.ccw.org.uk/faq/who-is-responsible-for-my-water-pipes/)
(CCW), the consumer body for water there, and it splits the pipe at your property boundary.[1]

The water main in the street is owned by the water company. CCW says the company is also
responsible for the pipe that joins the main to your boundary, for "The external stop tap usually
located in the public footpath", and for the water meter, if you have one.[1] From the boundary
inwards the pipe is the homeowner's responsibility: "The homeowner is responsible for maintaining
any water pipes located on private property up to the boundary of the road where the company’s water main is laid."[1] If one
pipe serves several properties, CCW says it becomes the joint responsibility of all the owners
connected to it, and a leak in the pipes and fittings inside the house is the homeowner's to have
repaired.[1]

This is general information, not legal advice. The rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland
and Northern Ireland, and between US states and cities, and they change. CCW's split is for England
and Wales. This course did not research who is responsible for which pipe in Scotland or Northern
Ireland, and in the US it varies by utility, which the course did not research either, so there
your water company or utility is the one to ask. The rule behind the split comes from an official
consumer body describing responsibility in England and Wales. The job that follows from it, a leak
on the homeowner's side of the boundary, is **Call someone**: a plumber, and CCW says it's the
homeowner's responsibility to arrange the repair.[1] CCW's page is written to homeowners. If you
rent, the person to tell is your landlord or whoever owns the home; this course didn't research
what a tenancy says about who arranges it.

:::checkpoint A wet patch appears in the pavement right outside a house in Hertfordshire, a metre (about 3 feet) short of the garden wall, and it smells of nothing but water. Whose job is it on CCW's account, and who should the household ring?
Most likely the water company's. The pavement is public ground, and CCW puts the pipe up to the
property boundary, and the external stop tap in the footpath, with the company.[1] Report it to
them. If the same wet patch were inside the garden wall, the same page would put it with the
homeowner.
:::

## Is your pressure normal?

Weak flow is the other complaint that sends people looking for a plumber, and
[WaterSafe](https://www.watersafe.org.uk/advice/common_plumbing_questions1/pressure/what_is_the_minimum_water_pressure_that_a_water_supplier_must_supply/),
the register of approved plumbers run for the UK water industry, gives a test you can do at the
sink.[2] With every other tap and appliance turned off, the first tap in the house, usually the
kitchen tap, "should be able to fill a 4.5 litre (one gallon) bucket in 30 seconds".[2] That comes
to 9 litres a minute, by this course's arithmetic. WaterSafe offers it only as a guide, and only
for a home with a suitable single service pipe, so a slow bucket on its own does not prove the
company is at fault. WaterSafe doesn't define a single service pipe. This course reads it as a
supply pipe that serves your home alone, not one shared with the neighbours.

The figures behind the guide belong to the companies. Pressure is measured where the company's
pipe meets yours, "usually the outside stop valve or property boundary", and WaterSafe says: "Most
water companies will aim to provide at least 10 metres head (approximately 14 psi or 1.0 bar) at
the end of their communication pipes. They are committed to a guaranteed standard of service to
provide a minimum of 7 metres static head (0.7 bar)."[2] That is WaterSafe's account, and it
doesn't say which nations it describes.

**In England and Wales, the 7 metres is law.** The Water Supply and Sewerage Services (Customer
Service Standards) Regulations 2008 set it for each country separately. For England,
[regulation 17G](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2008/594/regulation/17G) says: "An English
water wholesaler must maintain, in a communication pipe serving premises supplied with water, a
minimum water pressure of seven metres static head". For Wales,
[regulation 10](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2008/594/regulation/10) puts the same duty on
"A Welsh water undertaker".[11] The wholesaler and the undertaker are the regulations' names for
the water company that runs the mains. A communication pipe, in the regulations' definition, is "the part of
a service pipe between an English wholesaler’s water main and the boundary of the street in which
the water main is laid" (the Welsh definition is the same, and both have a variant for a house that
fronts straight onto the street).[11] In other words, the standard is measured on the company's
side of the boundary, not at your tap. If the pressure there falls below 7 metres for an hour or
more, and does so again within 28 days, other than during necessary works or a drought, the
regulations give the customer a payment: £50 in England, up to £250 in a financial year, and £25 in
Wales, once in a financial year. There are further exceptions in the text.[11] This is general
information, not legal advice. The rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern
Ireland, and between US states and cities, and they change. This course didn't research Scotland
or Northern Ireland.

"Metres head" sounds odd until you see how WaterSafe explains it in plain words. It says water
companies are required to supply water at a pressure that will reach the top storey of most homes,
with some exceptions, and it describes the guide flow as enough to fill a cold-water cistern in the
roof space of a two-storey house.[2] WaterSafe does not define "head". This course's reading of
those two examples is that head is pressure described as a height the water can be pushed to. Ten
metres is about 1 bar or 14 psi (pounds per square inch), in WaterSafe's own conversion; a bar is
a unit of pressure, and 0.7 bar is about 10 psi, which is this course's conversion.

In the US this course read no national pressure figure and no home test from an official source.
Your utility is the place to ask.

### A slow bucket in a shared house

A converted Edwardian house in Hampshire has been split into three flats. The owner of the top
flat times her kitchen tap into a 4.5 litre bucket with everything else in the flat turned off. It
takes 55 seconds, nearly twice WaterSafe's 30.

The first question is whether WaterSafe's guide applies at all. It is written for a home with a
suitable single service pipe, and three flats in one old house may well share a pipe from the
street. She doesn't know, and a bucket can't tell her. Nor can her tap show what the company
supplies, because WaterSafe says pressure is measured at the outside stop valve or boundary, and
she is two floors up and at the far end of the house's own pipes.[2]

So the bucket has done its job: it says something is worth asking about. WaterSafe points readers
to their water company for more information,[2] and that is her next step. The two questions she
takes to it are her own: is the supply to the house shared, and what is the pressure at the
boundary? If it turns out the flats share one
pipe and that pipe needs work, CCW's account (general information, not legal advice) makes it the
joint responsibility of all the owners connected to it.[1] The bucket test is **Yours**. Any work
on a shared supply pipe is **Call someone**, and a conversation with the neighbours first.

## How much a drip wastes

People leave drips alone because a drip looks like nothing. The numbers say otherwise, and they
come from different places measuring different things, so read each one with its label.

:::predict Guess before you read on. A tap that drips once a second, day and night, for a year: how much water is that? Pick one: about 100 litres, about 1,000 litres, about 10,000 litres, or about 100,000 litres.
About 10,000 litres. EPA's WaterSense programme says a tap dripping at one drip per second "can
waste more than 3,000 gallons per year".[3] Those are US gallons of about 3.8 litres each, so more
than 11,000 litres. Most people guess low, because one drop is tiny and a year has more than 31
million seconds in it.
:::

The figures, each with its source and what it counts:

- **One tap, United States:** "A leaky faucet that drips at the rate of one drip per second can
  waste more than 3,000 gallons per year" (EPA WaterSense, ["Fix a Leak
  Week"](https://www.epa.gov/watersense/fix-leak-week)).[3] "Faucet" is the American word for a
  tap.
- **One tap, England:** Southern Water, an English water company, says a slow drip can waste "up to
  10,000 litres per year".[5] It does not define "slow", and "up to" makes it a ceiling, not an
  average.
- **Whole households, United States:** "The average household's leaks can account for more than
  9,300 gallons of water wasted every year and nine percent of homes have leaks that waste 50
  gallons or more per day" (EPA, "Fix a Leak Week").[3] EPA's own "Statistics and Facts" page gives
  9,400 gallons for the same average, so call it more than 9,000 US gallons.[4] Worn toilet flappers
  and dripping faucets are among the common household leaks EPA names. A flapper, in EPA's
  description, is the rubber part that holds water in a US toilet tank and lets it into the bowl
  when you flush.[3]
- **Toilets, one English water company's customers:** United Utilities says "Nearly 80% of all
  leaks reported to us last year were leaky loos!" and that a faulty cistern can cost "as much as
  £400 a year" in wasted water.[6] That is a share of the leaks its own customers reported, not of
  all leaks. The page does not say which year "last year" was, and £400 is a ceiling.

One trap for anyone who reads both countries' advice: **a US gallon is not a UK gallon.** EPA's
gallons are US gallons of about 3.8 litres (this course's conversion). WaterSafe's "4.5 litre (one
gallon)" bucket[2] is an imperial gallon, by this course's reading. Convert through litres and the two countries' figures sit side by side: EPA's
one-drip-a-second tap wastes more than 11,000 litres a year, and Southern Water's slow drip up to
10,000. They are different measures from different organisations, and they land in the same
range.

Drips scale simply. A tap that drips once every two seconds rather than every second wastes about
half EPA's figure, a little over 1,500 US gallons or about 5,700 litres a year. That is this
course's arithmetic, and it assumes drips of the size EPA's figure implies. To turn water into
money, use the price per cubic metre (UK) or per thousand gallons (US) on your own bill, if you're
metered. Fixing the drip itself is the next lesson, lesson 3, Taps and toilets.

## Proving a leak before you hunt for it

A leak you can see is easy. The expensive ones are those you can't, and EPA gives two tests that
find them without opening anything.[3]

**The meter test.** Read your water meter "before and after a two-hour period when no water is
being used. If the meter changes at all, you probably have a leak."[3] Two hours with no water
means nobody flushes, runs a tap, or starts the dishwasher or the washing machine. The test needs a
meter, so it is no use in an unmetered home. [United
Utilities](https://www.unitedutilities.com/help-and-support/your-water-supply/your-pipes/leakage/inside-leaks/)
adds a UK wrinkle for its own customers: if your meter is outside the house and you have checked
inside and found nothing, the leak may be on your outside supply pipe.[6] In England and Wales that
is the pipe CCW puts on the homeowner's side of the boundary.[1]

**The dye test.** Put a few drops of food colouring in the toilet tank (the cistern, in UK terms)
and don't flush. "If any color shows up in the bowl after 10 minutes, you have a leak."[3] EPA
suggests flushing straight afterwards so the tank does not stain.[3] EPA wrote the test for
American toilets. Using it on a UK cistern is this course's own step, and the reasoning is only
this: the test asks whether water gets from the cistern into the bowl without a flush, and that
question is the same whatever is inside the cistern.

EPA also gives a rough check on the bill. For a family of four, if water use in a colder month
such as January or February goes above 12,000 US gallons, there could be serious leaks.[3]

Both tests are **Yours**. They are EPA's advice to householders, they change nothing, and they
suit any home.

## Frozen pipes

**Why a frozen pipe splits.** The [American Red
Cross](https://www.redcross.org/get-help/how-to-prepare-for-emergencies/types-of-emergencies/winter-storm/frozen-pipes.html)
gives the mechanism in a line: water "expands as it freezes", and "No matter the strength of a
container, expanding water can cause pipes to break."[10] The
[Met Office](https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/warnings-and-advice/seasonal-advice/your-home/frozen-or-burst-pipes)
adds the part people miss: pipes freezing and then thawing can cause them to burst, so the danger
is not over when the ice melts.[8]

**The pipes most at risk,** in the Red Cross's list for US homes, are outdoor hose bibs (outside
taps), pipes in unheated basements, crawl spaces, attics, garages and kitchen cabinets, and pipes
against outside walls with little or no insulation.[10]

### Keeping them from freezing

This is advice, and each country's comes from its own bodies.

**In the UK,** CCW's [winter advice](https://www.ccw.org.uk/advice-and-support/households/winter-advice/)
is to find your stop tap, lag exposed pipes (wrap them in insulation), fit covers on outside taps, put insulating jackets on
tanks, cisterns and boilers, fix dripping taps, and keep a registered plumber's number handy.[9]
The Met Office says: "Make sure you know where your stop tap is and check it’s working every six
months." It also says to leave the heating on low if you go away, and to insulate pipes in the loft
or roof space.[8]

**In the US,** the Red Cross says to drain and store outdoor hoses, close the inside valves that
supply outdoor hose bibs and open the outside bibs so they drain, add insulation, and open the
cabinet doors under sinks so warmer air reaches the pipes.[10] In very cold weather, "let the cold
water drip from the faucet served by exposed pipes". Keep the thermostat at the same setting day
and night, and if you're away, keep the heat "no lower than 55° F", about 13°C.[10]

### Thawing one: two countries, two orders

:::predict Before you look at the table: while you thaw a frozen pipe, should the water supply to the house be on or off? Commit to an answer and a reason.
It depends which country's advice you follow, and that is the point of the table below. The UK
sources read here turn the stop tap off first. The US source keeps the faucet open so running
water helps melt the ice, and doesn't mention turning the supply off. If you answered either way with a reason, you
have one country's answer. Read on for the other.
:::

Here the advice differs, and it is the clearest case in this lesson of the course's question:
**law or advice, whose, and where?** Both are advice, from the bodies this course read: the Met
Office, the UK's national weather service, and CCW on one side, and on the other the American Red
Cross, a charity. Follow your own country's.

| | In the UK (Met Office; CCW, for England and Wales) | In the US (American Red Cross) |
|---|---|---|
| The supply | Met Office: "turn off your inside stop tap immediately"[8]; CCW: "Turn off your water supply at the stop tap"[9] | "Keep the faucet open."[10] The page does not mention turning the supply off |
| The taps | Met Office: open all the taps to drain, saving some water in a bucket, and turn them off when the water stops; then turn on nearby cold taps with the stop tap still off, to relieve pressure on the frozen pipe[8]. CCW: leave the cold tap on so thawed water can escape[9] | Keep the faucet open, because water running through the pipe helps melt the ice[10] |
| The heat | Hot water bottles or towels soaked in hot water (Met Office)[8]; a hairdryer on warm, a hot water bottle or a heated cloth (CCW)[9] | An electric heating pad, a hair dryer, a space heater kept away from anything flammable, or towels soaked in hot water[10] |
| Never | "Never use a naked flame or blowtorch" (Met Office)[8]; "Never use a naked flame or blow torch." (CCW)[9] | "Do not use a blowtorch, kerosene or propane heater, charcoal stove, or other open flame device."[10] |
| Also | CCW: switch off the immersion heater, if you have one, and the central heating[9] | Keep applying heat until full pressure is back, and check the other faucets, because if one pipe freezes others may too[10] |
| Who to call | CCW: a registered plumber, before your pipes burst[9]; the Met Office points to WaterSafe[8] | A licensed plumber, if you can't find the frozen section, can't reach it, or can't thaw it[10] |

Where they agree: **no flame, ever**. The Met Office gives its reason: direct heat may damage the
pipe and could start a fire.[8] All three sources here give that rule, so it is the one to hold
onto. The two UK pages also say to thaw the pipe slowly;[8][9] the Red Cross says to keep applying
heat until full water pressure is back.[10]

Where they differ, each gives part of its reasoning. The Red Cross keeps the tap open because
running water helps melt the ice.[10] The Met Office opens the cold taps to relieve pressure on the
frozen pipe.[8] CCW leaves the cold tap on so thawed water can escape.[9] Neither UK page says in
so many words why the stop tap goes off first. CCW doesn't say why the immersion heater and heating
go off, and the Met Office's frozen-pipe steps don't mention them. This course won't invent the
reasons, and it calls neither country's order the wrong one.

Thawing a pipe you can reach is **Yours**, on the Met Office's and the Red Cross's advice; CCW's
steps also say to contact a registered plumber before the pipe bursts.[9] If it has already split,
go to lesson 1's burst-pipe order, and then **Call someone**: CCW says a burst pipe needs a
professional plumber.[9] If water is reaching a socket, a light or anything plugged in, or dripping
near where you'd plug in a hair dryer or heater, lesson 1's reading applies: don't touch wet
switches, sockets or fittings, go to the consumer unit or panel only if you can reach it without
standing in water or touching anything wet, and otherwise keep clear and call an electrician.

:::checkpoint Two houses on the same freezing night. In a flat in Fife, the bathroom cold tap gives a trickle. In a house in North Dakota, the kitchen faucet on an outside wall does the same. For each, what's the first thing to do, and whose advice is it?
Fife is in Scotland, so the Met Office's UK advice applies (CCW's page is for England and Wales,
and also puts the stop tap first): turn off the stop tap first, then open the taps and warm the pipe slowly with
hot water bottles or hot towels, and no flame.

North Dakota is in the US, so the American Red Cross's advice applies: keep the faucet open and
warm the pipe with a heating pad, a hair dryer, a space heater kept clear of anything flammable,
or hot towels, and no flame. The Red Cross's reason is that running water helps melt the ice.

If you gave each house the other country's order, go back to the table. Each order is the advice
its own country's sources give.
:::

## A leak nobody can see

This is the harder of the lesson's two worked cases, and it stops partway for you.

A family of four in an older single-storey house in Missouri, built over a crawl space, open their February
water bill and find it has jumped. They used over 14,000 US gallons in the month, above EPA's
12,000-gallon line for a family of four in a colder month.[3] Nobody is taking longer showers.

**Step 1: prove the leak.** One Sunday morning they read the meter, use no water for two hours,
and read it again. It has moved. On EPA's test they probably have a leak.[3]

**Step 2: rule out the toilets.** They put food colouring in both toilet tanks and wait ten
minutes without flushing. Both bowls stay clear, so neither toilet is leaking from tank to bowl.[3]

**Step 3: rule out the fittings you can see.** They check every tap, and the pipes under each sink,
for water on the outside of a fitting, as EPA suggests.[3] Everything is dry.

:::predict The meter moves, the toilets are clean, and the visible fittings are dry. Before reading on: using what you've learned about frozen pipes, where would you look next, and why?
Look at the pipes the Red Cross lists as freezing most often: the outdoor hose bibs, and pipes in
the crawl space and along outside walls.[10] A leak that the meter sees and nobody else does is on
a pipe nobody looks at, and a pipe that froze in January may have split without anyone noticing.

In this house the father goes to the hose bib on the back wall. A hose is still attached from the
autumn, and the inside valve that supplies the bib was never closed. He crawls under the house with
a torch and finds wet soil beneath the pipe that runs to the bib. The pipe split in a January
freeze, as the Red Cross warns pipes can, and has been leaking into the crawl space since the thaw.
:::

**Step 4: isolate it.** He closes the inside valve to the hose bib, the valve the Red Cross says to
close before cold weather.[10] That is **Yours**: it is the Red Cross's own advice to householders,
and it opens nothing.

**Step 5: check the isolation worked.** Two more quiet hours, and the meter stays still. The leak
is on that one branch.

**Step 6: who fixes it.** A split supply pipe is **Call someone**: a licensed plumber. This course
teaches no pipe repair, and the Red Cross sends people to a licensed plumber for a frozen section
they can't reach.[10] Whether the family or their utility is responsible for any given pipe outside
is a question for the utility, because US responsibility varies and this course did not research
it.

The order is what carries over: prove the leak, then rule places out from the cheapest check
upwards, and let the meter tell you when you've found it. Had the same meter moved in a home in
England with the meter outside and nothing wrong indoors, United Utilities' advice would send you
to the outside supply pipe, which CCW puts on the homeowner's side of the boundary.[6][1]

## What people get wrong

**"A slow drip wastes almost nothing."** A tap dripping once a second wastes more than 3,000 US
gallons a year on EPA's figure, and Southern Water puts a slow drip at up to 10,000 litres.[3][5]
Each drop is small. There are a great many of them.

**"A gallon is a gallon."** A US gallon is about 3.8 litres and an imperial gallon about 4.5,
WaterSafe's "4.5 litre (one gallon)"[2] (the 3.8 is this course's conversion).
Mix them and your arithmetic is out by a fifth.

**"The water company fixes leaks."** In England and Wales it deals with the ones on its side of
your boundary. From the boundary in, CCW puts the pipe with the homeowner.[1]

**"The meter moved overnight, so there's a leak."** Only if nothing used water in the window. EPA's
test is two hours with no water used at all, and a timed dishwasher or a flush in the night spoils
it.[3]

**"Thaw it fast with a blowtorch."** Every source read here says never: the Met Office, CCW and the
American Red Cross.[8][9][10]

**"The other country's thawing order is wrong."** The UK sources turn the supply off first; the US
source keeps the faucet open. Each is advice from its own country's sources, and neither says the
other is wrong. Follow your own.

## Practice

:::exercise Find out whether you have a leak
Take 20 minutes over these, spread across a morning. None of them opens anything or changes
anything in your home, so they suit a renter too.

1. If you have a water meter, find it and read it. Your bill or your water company's website says
   where your meter is and how to read it. Don't lift anything heavy or open a chamber you aren't
   sure is yours to open. Then use no water at all for two hours (no
   flushing, no taps, no dishwasher or washing machine) and read it again. Write down both readings
   and whether it moved. If you have no meter, note that you can't do this test.

2. Do the dye test on every toilet: a few drops of food colouring in the tank or cistern, no flush,
   and look at the bowl after 10 minutes. Flush afterwards so the tank doesn't stain. Write down
   which, if any, showed colour.

3. Walk round every tap, and the pipes under each sink, looking for water on the outside of a
   fitting. Note anything wet.

4. Make a list of the pipes in your home that nobody looks at: a loft or roof space, a crawl space,
   a garage, an outside wall, an outside tap. Don't open anything or climb anywhere to find them.
   Just list where you know or suspect they run.

If the meter moved but the toilets and taps are clean, you are where the family in Missouri was
after step 3, and your list from step 4 is where to look next.
:::

:::exercise Put a price on a drip
Take 10 minutes over this.

1. If any tap in your home drips, count the drips in one minute with a timer. Work out how many
   seconds there are between drips, and scale EPA's figure: one drip a second is more than 3,000
   US gallons a year, so one drip every three seconds is a third of that. Convert to litres at
   about 3.8 litres to the US gallon.

2. If you're metered, find the price per cubic metre (UK) or per thousand gallons (US) on your own
   bill, and turn your answer into money.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your drip arithmetic against this one. A tap drips 20 times in a minute. What's that a year, in US gallons and in litres?
Twenty drips a minute is one drip every three seconds, a third of EPA's one-a-second rate. A third
of EPA's more than 3,000 US gallons is a little over 1,000 US gallons, about 3,800 litres. If you got
about 9,000, you multiplied by three instead of dividing: the gap between drips went up, so the
number of drips went down.
:::

## For your plan

This lesson adds to your seasonal plan: a meter test and a dye test twice a year (EPA's tests),
and the stop tap checked every six months (the Met Office). It adds an autumn line before the first
frost: pipes lagged and outside taps covered (UK), or hoses drained and the inside valves to the
hose bibs closed (US). It adds a WaterSafe plumber's or a licensed plumber's number to your call
list. And it adds to your map every pipe that runs where nobody looks.
