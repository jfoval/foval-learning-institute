Burst pipes rarely pick a good time. When one goes, there is one valve that stops the water to the
whole house, one switch that cuts the power to every circuit, and one handle that shuts off the gas.
If you've found them already, a burst costs you a mop and a plumber. If you're looking for them with
a torch while the kitchen ceiling bulges, it can cost a ceiling, a floor and the wiring in between.
This lesson finds all three with you, in a British home and an American one, and gives you the order
to use them in. It also sets out how the rest of the course sorts every job it mentions, because
"can I do this myself?" has a different answer for a tap, a fuse box and a gas pipe, and a different
answer again in Wales and in Texas.

:::callout Before you pick up a tool
This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

That box opens every lesson in this course, word for word, because someone may arrive at the
lesson on radiators with a smell of gas in the hall and never read this one. Notice that its gas

## A house is four flows

Strip a house back to what moves through it and there are four flows, each with its own way in or
out and its own control.

**Water in, under pressure.** The water company's main runs under the street. A pipe runs from it
to the edge of your property and another from there into the house. The Consumer Council for Water
(CCW), the statutory consumer body for water in England and Wales, splits the ownership at the
boundary: the company owns the main and the pipe up to it, and "The homeowner is responsible for
maintaining any water pipes located on private property up to the boundary of the road where the
company’s water main is laid."[3] The supply arrives under pressure, and on its way into the house it
passes a valve that WaterSafe calls "the point where you can shut off the water supply to your home
or business."[1]

**Water out, by gravity.** Drains don't push; they fall. Every sink, bath and toilet empties
through a trap, the U-bend, which holds a plug of water that keeps sewer air out of the room.
England's statutory guidance on drainage says traps "should retain a minimum seal of 25mm of water
or equivalent" under working conditions, and that "discharge stacks should be ventilated" so the
seals are not sucked out by pressure in the pipes.[21] The American Society of Home Inspectors
(ASHI) puts the same job in plain words: "Vent piping allows air into the system so drains flow
easily."[22] There's no shut-off on this flow to find. Lesson 4,
"Water out: traps, vents and blocked drains", is about what goes wrong with it.

A London workshop that teaches beginners plumbing sums up those first two flows in one line:
"Plumbing = water in and water out."[23]

**Power in, through protective devices.** Electricity arrives at a consumer unit (the British
name; people still call it the fuse box) or a panel (the American one). Electrical Safety First
(ESF), a UK electrical safety charity, describes the British box: "Modern consumer units have a main
switch and fuses and/or circuit breakers."[6] The devices in it are not all doing the same job. The
US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) put the difference in 1989: a ground fault circuit
interrupter (GFCI) cuts the power fast when current leaks where it should not, "whereas circuit
breakers and fuses function primarily to prevent the home wiring system from causing a fire."[8]
ESF says the British equivalent of the GFCI, the residual current device or RCD, "protects you from
electric shocks if a product is faulty."[7] So breakers protect the wiring and RCDs and GFCIs
protect the person. Lesson 5, "Electricity: what trips, and why", takes that apart.

**Fuel in, combustion products out.** Gas (or oil, or wood) comes in, burns in a boiler, a
furnace, a cooker or a fire, and what is left after burning has to leave by a flue or chimney. The
gas has a shut-off at the meter. The way out is the half people forget, and lesson 8, "Carbon
monoxide, smoke and the alarms that warn you", is about keeping it working.

That gives you three controls to find: water, power and gas. The rest of this lesson is where
each one is, what it does and does not stop, and in what order to use them.

## Water: the stop tap and the main shut-off valve

The two countries give the same control different names and put it in different places, so here
they are separately.

### In the UK

[WaterSafe](https://www.watersafe.org.uk/advice/general_plumbing_advice/winter_advice/locate-internal-stop-tap/), the approved-plumber register run for the water industry, calls the control the
internal stop tap, "also sometimes called a stop valve or stopcock". "It looks like a spout-less
tap or lever and sits between two lengths of pipe acting as a connector." It is usually under the
kitchen sink, and WaterSafe also lists a kitchen cupboard, a downstairs bathroom or toilet, a garage
or utility room, a cellar and the space under the stairs.[1]

Using it takes three facts, all WaterSafe's: "Turning the valve clockwise will close it"; it "may
take a few minutes for the water to stop altogether"; and "Turn it on and off slowly, never force
it." If it will not turn, or will not shut the water off, WaterSafe's advice is to call a plumber.[1]

There's a second stop tap outside, and it isn't yours. CCW describes "The external stop tap usually
located in the public footpath" as the water company's.[3] It stops the water to your house too,
but it is under a cover in the pavement, it belongs to the company, and in an emergency the one
inside is the one you will reach first.

The Met Office adds a habit: "Make sure you know where your stop tap is and check it’s working every
six months."[2] That's the advice of a national weather service on frozen and burst pipes, not a law,
and the reason for it is WaterSafe's last sentence. A valve that has not been turned for years is
the one most likely to be stiff on the night you need it.

### In the US

[WSSC Water](https://www.wsscwater.com/customer-service/report-problem/find-and-operate-your-main-water-valve), a public water utility in Maryland, calls it the main water shut-off valve and says
where to look by house type. In a house with a basement it is "typically located near the front
foundation wall" and "typically within three to five feet of where the main water enters". In
crawl-space and slab-on-grade houses it may be near the water heater or under the kitchen sink,
"but anywhere is possible."[5] WSSC also says "Every home was required to have a main water shut-off
valve installed inside the home when it was built," and that sentence is about its own service
area. It is a utility describing its own customers' houses, not a national rule, and an old house
elsewhere may surprise you.

On operating it: "Round “wheel” handle valves will turn off by turning the handle clockwise. It may
take two or more full revolutions." Turn it back on slowly, part of the way first.[5] There is also
an underground valve outside at the property line, often in a meter pit, and WSSC says that one is
for a registered master plumber or the utility.[5] One more detail from WSSC matters in some
houses: where a home has fire sprinklers, you shut off the plumbing supply and leave the sprinkler
pipe live, unless the sprinkler pipe is what is leaking.[5]

This course read no US source giving a householder the six-monthly check. It read one Maryland
utility's page, closely, and it treats that page as a good description of how these valves work,
not as the rule where you live.

:::predict You have found the valve. Before you read on: turning a stop tap or a wheel-handle valve which way closes it, clockwise or anticlockwise? And how sure are you?

Clockwise closes. WaterSafe and WSSC, one British and one American source, say so in the same
words.[1][5] If you were not sure, you're in good company: while this course was being researched,
a search engine's own summary of the subject had it backwards. That's the reason to read the
source, and the reason to try the valve on a quiet afternoon rather than work it out in a flood.
:::

### Two houses, first look

Here's the first case, the simple one. A couple have just moved into a 1930s semi in England. The
stop tap turns out to be where WaterSafe says it usually is, under the kitchen sink, behind a
bottle of bleach and a bucket.

They turn it clockwise, slowly, with the kitchen cold tap open. The flow tails off and stops after
a couple of minutes, which is WaterSafe's "may take a few minutes". Then they turn it back,
anticlockwise, and half a turn in it goes stiff. Water is flowing, but not at full strength. They
don't reach for pliers. WaterSafe says never force it, so they leave it where it is, note on
their shut-off map that it is stiff and that full flow is not back, and book a plumber to look at it.
They have learned something they'd far rather know on a Sunday afternoon than find out in January.

Next they open the door of the consumer unit. There is a main switch, a row of circuit breakers and
a button marked "T". ESF: "To check whether your consumer unit has an RCD, look for the ‘Test’ or
‘T’ button."[6] They leave the button for lesson 5. Two of the breakers have no label, so they tell
each other what is about to go off, switch the first unlabelled one off, walk round the house to see
what stopped, write it on the chart, and switch it back on; then the second. That's labelling from
the front of the box, which is what the University of Georgia's home maintenance checklist means
when it says "label switches if needed".[11] If a breaker would not go back on, or tripped again,
they'd leave it off and call an electrician, as the box at the top says.

## Electricity: the consumer unit and the panel

The main switch on a British consumer unit, and the main breaker in an American panel, cut the
power to the house's circuits. That's all a householder needs them to do in an emergency, and it is
where a householder's work on the box stops.

The reason it stops there is in one sentence from CPSC's [leaflet on AFCIs](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/5133.pdf). Explaining why a householder should not
install a type of breaker called an AFCI, it says: "Be sure to have a qualified electrician install
AFCIs; do not attempt this work yourself. The installation involves working within electrical panel
boxes that are usually electrically live, even with the main circuit breakers turned off."[9]
CPSC gives that fact and not the full reason behind it, and this course will not add one. The fact is
enough.

:::predict A neighbour says: "It's fine to take the cover off the fuse box once you've flipped the main switch, the whole thing's dead then." From what you have just read, what is wrong with that?

The main switch cuts the circuits, not everything inside the box. CPSC says panel boxes are
"usually electrically live, even with the main circuit breakers turned off".[9] So the cover stays
on, whatever the main switch says, and anything behind it is a job for an electrician.
:::

Two other facts come up wherever electricity does in this course. First, a pocket "volt stick"
that stays silent proves nothing: the Health and Safety Executive's guidance for people who work on
electrical systems says the use of "non-contact devices such as 'volt sticks'" is not recommended
for proving a circuit dead.[10] Second, the course teaches no wiring at all. It explains, in lesson
16, "Who may do what: reserved work, and the argument about licensing", which electrical work the
law lets a householder do in each UK nation and in five US places, and it teaches the procedure for
none of it. That's this course's decision about what a page can check, not a claim that the work is
unlawful wherever you are.

So the front of the box is yours: the main switch, the breakers' switches, the test button, and the
labels. Behind the cover is somebody else's.

## Gas: the emergency control valve

### In Great Britain and Northern Ireland

Cadent, the largest gas network in Great Britain, describes the valve on its own meters in its
[guide to locating the meter](https://cadentgas.com/locating-your-meter-and-turning-off-the-gas). The meter
may be in a garage, at the back of the house, under the stairs, in the kitchen, or outside at the
front or side; in buildings with several homes it may be in a communal area, and a meter box needs
a key.[12] Then: "Open your gas meter box and locate the red tap handle near to the pipe. This is
the emergency control valve (ECV). This handle will be aligned with the pipe; to switch off the
supply you should turn it either 90 degrees to the left or 90 degrees to the right (a quarter turn
clockwise or anti-clockwise)."[12] That's Cadent's description of the meters on its network, and
it is the one this course read; other networks' guides were not.

Cadent's page on smelling gas says when to use it: open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the
meter, and "If the gas leak is in a cellar or basement, do not enter and instead evacuate the
building." The number is 0800 111 999, day or night.[12][14] In Northern Ireland, the gas supply
company's advice is the same shape, with its own number: turn the gas off at the meter "unless the
meter is located in a cellar or basement", and phone 0800 002 001.[13]

Find the meter and the handle now, and look at them. Don't turn the handle to see if it moves. Gas
isn't water: you don't practise on it, and Cadent's own words are that once it is off you "Don’t
turn it back on until advised it’s safe to do so."[12]

### In the US

The US utilities this course read give a different first step. Con Edison, in New York: "Leave
immediately and take others with you." SoCalGas and Atmos Energy say the same: leave, and call from
a safe distance.[15] So in an American house the job is to know where the meter is, so you can tell
the utility, and then to leave it alone. The course did not read any US utility's instructions for
turning off a household meter, and gives none.

### And the law, in one sentence for now

In Great Britain the law requires anyone who does gas work to be competent (regulation 3(1) of the
Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998), and requires employers and the self-employed to
be on the Gas Safe Register (regulation 3(3)); HSE's guidance says registration "is required only by
businesses carrying out gas-fitting work", and its code of practice sets competence at an
industry-recognised course followed by an assessment.[16][17] Northern Ireland has its own 2004
Regulations, whose regulation 3 has the same shape. This is general information, not legal advice.
The rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and between US states and
cities, and they change.

So "it's illegal to do your own gas work" overstates it, and "it's legal" misleads. The advice is
not in doubt: "HSE strongly advises that all gas appliances, flues and pipework should be
installed, regularly maintained and serviced at least annually by a Gas Safe registered
engineer."[18] The Register's own line is "Don’t DIY with gas appliances, always use a Gas Safe
registered engineer".[19] What a householder does is what the appliance's user instructions give
the user to do. Lesson 7 has the rest.

## When a pipe bursts

:::predict A pipe has burst in the loft of a British house and water is coming through the landing ceiling. Put these in the order you'd do them: soak up the water; turn off the stop tap; call a plumber; open the taps; switch off the boiler.

The Met Office's order is: stop tap off, boiler off, open the taps to drain the pipes, soak up what
you can, and call a WaterSafe plumber.[2] CCW gives the same order and adds two things: switch off
the immersion heater as well as the heating, and photograph the damage for your insurer.[4] If you
put soaking up first, that's the instinct everyone has, and it is the wrong way round: every minute
the stop tap is open, more water arrives than any towel takes away.
:::

What insurance covers, and how to think about whether you're insured for it, is Personal Finance
Fundamentals lesson 8, "Handing off what you cannot absorb". This course only passes on CCW's
practical point: photograph it before you clear it up.[4]

### When the water reaches the electrics

This is the part that can hurt someone, and the Met Office's own wording needs care. It says: "If
water has leaked near your electrics, do not touch them and switch them off at the fuse box."[2]
Read quickly, that tells you to go to the fuse box whatever state it is in. ESF's current [advice
after flooding](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/safety-advice/home-and-people/house-maintenance/electrical-safety-after-a-flood/) is more careful. "Switch off the electricity supply at the consumer unit/ fusebox,
only if it is safe to do so." And: "Seek professional advice from a registered electrician if there
is any sign of water inside the consumer unit/ fusebox or any signs of arcing or overheating." It
also says: "Keep away from any exposed wiring as it may still be live."[20]

Neither source says what makes it safe. So here's this course's plain reading, and it is the
course's, not a quotation. Don't touch wet switches, sockets or fittings. Go to the consumer unit
or panel only if you can reach it without standing in water or touching anything wet, and if you
can't, keep clear and call an electrician. You met the same idea in First Aid and CPR lesson 2,
where the danger step said to make sure the power is off before touching someone in contact with a
supply. The principle is the one behind this whole section: you're no use to anyone if you become
the second problem.

### The second house

Now the wrinkle. It's winter in a two-storey house in the American Midwest. A pipe to the upstairs
bathroom has split, and water is coming through the fitting of the hall ceiling light. The panel is
on the basement wall, and when the owner opens the basement door there is already about an inch of
water across the floor. The main shut-off valve is where WSSC says to look, near the front
foundation wall, on the far side of the basement.

The light first. The owner does not touch the hall light switch, on or off, and keeps away from
the fitting. The switch is wet and so is the light, and that's the course's rule above.

:::checkpoint Before you read on: does the owner go down to the valve, or to the panel? Decide, and give the reason for each one.

Neither, on this course's reading. Both the valve and the panel are across a basement floor under
an inch of water, with a wet circuit upstairs feeding somewhere into that panel. ESF says to switch
off at the panel "only if it is safe to do so", and nothing here says it is. Walking through water
to reach either one is exactly what the course's plain reading rules out. If the basement were dry
and the panel reachable without touching anything wet, the owner would close the valve clockwise
(two or more turns, WSSC says) and switch off at the main breaker. It isn't, so the owner doesn't.
:::

So what does the owner do? They get everyone away from the wet hall and out of the basement. They
call the water utility, because WSSC says the outside valve at the property line is for the utility
or a registered master plumber, and a utility can shut the water off without anyone going into the
basement.[5] They call an electrician about the wet circuit and the panel, since ESF sends any sign
of water inside a consumer unit to one, and nobody can see inside this one from the stairs.[20] The water keeps coming until the utility arrives, and
that's the cost of a valve you can't reach. It is also the strongest argument in this lesson for
knowing, before a winter, whether you could reach yours with the basement flooded.

Notice what the owner did not need to know: nothing about wiring, and nothing about pipe repair.
Every decision in that example was about which control to touch and which to leave.

## Law or advice, whose, and where?

Household advice goes wrong in one way more often than any other: a rule from one place, or one
kind of body, or one kind of house, gets repeated as if it bound everyone everywhere. You have already
met three in this lesson. WSSC's "Every home was required" is about homes in one utility's area. The
six-monthly stop-tap check is the Met Office's advice, not a law. And "gas work is illegal" is half
of a two-part rule that binds businesses one way and everyone another.

So every rule in this course carries, in the sentence, three things. **Who says it**: a statute or
regulation, a regulator's code or guidance, an official body's advice, a maker's instructions for
its own product, a trade body, a charity, a university extension service. **Whether it is law or
advice.** And **where it applies**: England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland each by name, the
US federally or by named state or city.

Every job the course names carries one of three labels, too:

- **Yours.** The sources put it in a householder's hands. Turning off the stop tap is the first
  example.
- **Yours, on conditions.** Allowed if a named condition is met, and the course names it: "follow
  the appliance's user instructions", "only if you can reach it without standing in water", "not
  if the meter is in a cellar".
- **Call someone.** The law where you are reserves it, or every official source the course read
  advises against a householder doing it.

Where the law allows a householder more than the advice does, and gas in Great Britain is the
first case you have met, the course gives both, law first, and the label follows the advice. That's
why gas work is "Call someone" even though the registration duty does not bind a householder.

:::checkpoint A friend in England reads online that "the council says every house must have its circuits labelled". Before you take it on board, what three questions does this course ask of that sentence, and what do you know already about this particular rule?

Who says it, is it law or advice, and where does it apply? For circuit labels, the source this
lesson read is the University of Georgia's extension checklist, a US university's advice to
homeowners, "label switches if needed".[11] That's a sensible habit, but it is not a council's rule
and it is not English law. Nothing this course read makes labelling a legal duty in England. So the
friend's sentence is, at best, a piece of advice with its source and place forgotten.
:::

## What this course leaves out

A course that tells you what it covers should tell you what it does not, so you know where to look
elsewhere.

**No gas work and no wiring**, beyond what the box at the top says. The course explains the law on
both and teaches the procedure for neither. It stops at the front of the consumer unit or panel,
and at what an appliance's user instructions give the user. It also does not teach opening a panel,
testing a water heater's pressure relief valve, or any method for working on asbestos.

**Structural work**, extensions, loft conversions, roofing and chimney work, planning permission
and party walls. They need permission and professionals, and they're named, not taught.

**Fitting a lock.** Personal Safety lesson 5, "Home, the door and the doorstep", said this course
would teach it. It does not, because the research behind this course found no source on fitting or
maintaining a lock that it could teach from, and a lesson written from general knowledge is the
thing this institute exists not to publish. For which locks and lights make a difference to
burglary, that lesson is still the place to go.

**Not researched, so not taught:** wallpapering, tiling, flooring, sticking doors, silicone
sealant, wasp nests, appliance repair, septic system design, pools, solar panels and the garden.
**Owned by other courses:** woodworking and making things (Tools and Making Things), energy policy
(Energy: How the World Is Powered), floods, storms and power cuts beyond the house side (Emergency
Preparedness), insurance and scams (Personal Finance Fundamentals lessons 8 and 10), and first aid
(First Aid and CPR, which this course builds on and never reteaches).

**Landlord and tenant law** is not taught. If you rent, the course points you where it matters,
starting now: every exercise that changes anything says "if it is your home to repair, or your
landlord agrees", and that's the course's own line, not a statement of tenancy law.

## What people get wrong

**"Clockwise opens it."** It closes it, for stop taps in WaterSafe's account and wheel-handle
valves in WSSC's.[1][5] A search engine's summary had it backwards while this course was being
written, which is how the wrong version spreads.

**"The stop tap in the pavement is the one to use."** It is the water company's (CCW), and the one
inside your house is yours.[3] In the US, the valve at the property line is for the utility or a
master plumber (WSSC).[5]

**"A stiff stop tap just needs more force."** WaterSafe: "Turn it on and off slowly, never force
it," and call a plumber if it will not turn.[1]

**"Once the main switch is off, the fuse box is safe to open."** CPSC says panels are usually live
even with the main breakers off.[9]

**"It's illegal to do any gas work yourself."** In Great Britain the registration duty binds
businesses and the competence duty binds everyone. The advice to use a Gas Safe registered engineer
is the same either way.[16][17][18] This is general information, not legal advice; lesson 7 has the
detail.

:::exercise Your shut-off map, and twelve jobs

Take 45 minutes over this, at home, in daylight, with a phone camera and a pen. If you rent, do the
looking and the writing, and turn the stop tap only if it is your home to repair or your landlord
agrees.

**1. Water.** Find the stop tap (UK) or main shut-off valve (US). Photograph it and write down
where it is in words someone else could follow in the dark. Then, with the kitchen cold tap open,
turn it clockwise slowly until it stops, and time how long the tap takes to run dry. Then turn it
back on, anticlockwise and slowly. Have a plumber's number to hand before you start, because a valve
that sticks on the way back can leave you short of water until someone sees to it (that's this
course's advice, not WaterSafe's). **If it is stiff, stop. Don't force it, don't use a tool on it, and call a
plumber**, as WaterSafe says. Write down whether it turned freely, and how long it took.

**2. Power.** Find the consumer unit or panel. Photograph the front with the door open. Note the
main switch or main breaker and any button marked "T" or "Test", but leave the test button for
lesson 5. Copy down the labels that are there and mark which circuits have none. Don't take off any
cover, and don't use a tester or volt stick on anything.

**3. Gas.** If you have gas, find the meter and, in the UK, the handle beside it. Photograph it.
**Don't turn it**, not even a little to check it moves: that's for an emergency, and the US utilities
this course read say to leave, not to turn anything. Note whether the meter is in a cellar or
basement, because in the UK that changes what you do if you ever smell gas.

**4. Your card.** On one side of a card, write the burst-pipe order for your country's sources as
this lesson gave it, and for the UK, the gas number for your part of it (0800 111 999 in Great
Britain, 0800 002 001 in Northern Ireland). In the US, write 911 and your gas utility's emergency
number from its own website. Put the card by the stop tap or on the fridge.

**5. Sort twelve jobs.** Give each one a label (Yours; Yours, on conditions, naming the condition;
or Call someone) and say whose rule sits behind it, whether it is law or advice, and where it applies.
Write your answers before you open the checkpoint below.

1. Turning off the inside stop tap when a pipe bursts (UK)
2. Turning the stop tap under the cover in the public footpath (England)
3. Closing the underground valve at the property line (a house in WSSC's area)
4. Taking the cover off a panel to look at a scorched breaker (US)
5. Labelling the circuits on the consumer unit door
6. Switching off at the consumer unit with water running down the wall beside it
7. Resetting a breaker that trips again straight away
8. Turning off the gas at the meter handle when you smell gas, the meter being in the hall (Great
   Britain)
9. Replacing a cooker's control knob that the maker designed the user to replace (Great Britain)
10. Moving a gas hob to a new position on the worktop (Great Britain)
11. Having the gas boiler serviced (Great Britain)
12. Going back into a house after smelling gas to open the windows (US)
:::

:::checkpoint Check your twelve against these.

1. **Yours.** WaterSafe's and the Met Office's advice, UK.
2. **Call someone**, in the sense that it is the water company's: CCW says it is theirs (England and Wales); that you should leave it to them is this course's reading.
3. **Call someone.** WSSC's advice about its own area.
4. **Call someone.** CPSC's advice, US federal.
5. **Yours.** UGA's advice ("label switches if needed"), a US extension service; a sensible habit anywhere, and law nowhere this course read.
6. **Yours, on conditions**, and here the condition fails: ESF's advice (UK) is "only if it is safe to do so", and the course's reading is not while standing in or touching water, so keep clear and call an electrician.
7. **Call someone.** This course's instruction in the box at the top, with ESF's advice that an RCD that keeps tripping means a fault for a registered electrician.
8. **Yours, on conditions**: not if the meter is in a cellar or basement, and only if you can do it safely. Cadent's and HSE's advice, Great Britain.
9. **Yours, on conditions**: that it is a task the user instructions give the user. The Gas Safe Register's statement, Great Britain.
10. **Call someone.** The law in Great Britain requires competence of anyone and registration of businesses, and HSE and the Register advise a Gas Safe registered engineer; the label follows the advice. This is general information, not legal advice.
11. **Call someone.** HSE's advice, "at least annually", Great Britain.
12. **Call someone**, and don't go back in: Con Edison, SoCalGas and Atmos all say leave and call from a safe place, US. Opening windows is British advice, for someone who has not left yet.

If you gave 8 as plain **Yours**, look again at the conditions. If you gave 10 as **Yours** because
the registration rule binds businesses, that's the trap this lesson is built to catch: the law
is not the whole answer, and the label follows the advice.
:::

## For your plan

The course project is a maintenance plan for your own home, and it starts here. This lesson adds
three things: the **shut-off map** (where the stop tap or main valve, the consumer unit or panel and
the gas meter are, with photographs, and whether the stop tap turned freely), the **emergency card**
(the burst-pipe order and the gas number or utility for your country), and the first item on the
calendar: **try the stop tap every six months**, the Met Office's interval.[2]
