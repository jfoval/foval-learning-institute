Burst pipes rarely pick a good time. When one goes, there is one valve that stops the water to the
whole house, one switch that cuts the power to every circuit, and one handle that shuts off the gas.
If you've found them already, a burst costs you a mop and a plumber. If you're looking for them with
a torch while the kitchen ceiling bulges, it can cost a ceiling, a floor and the wiring in between.
This lesson finds all three with you, in a British home and an American one, and gives you the order
to use them in. It also sets out how the course sorts every job, because "can I do this myself?"
has a different answer for a tap, a fuse box and a gas pipe, and again in Wales and in Texas.

:::callout Before you pick up a tool
This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

That box opens every lesson in this course, word for word. Its gas advice splits by country on
purpose: British gas networks and American gas utilities give different first steps, and lesson 9,

## A house is four flows

**Water in, under pressure.** The water company's main runs under the street, and a pipe runs
from it, across your land, into the house. The [Consumer Council for
Water](https://www.ccw.org.uk/faq/who-is-responsible-for-my-water-pipes/) (CCW), the statutory
consumer body for water in England and Wales, splits the ownership at the boundary: the homeowner
maintains the pipes "on private property up to the boundary of the road where the company’s water
main is laid."[3] On its way in, the supply passes a valve that WaterSafe calls "the point where you
can shut off the water supply to your home or business."[1]

**Water out, by gravity.** Drains don't push; they fall. Every sink, bath and toilet empties
through a trap, the U-bend, which holds a plug of water that keeps sewer air out of the room.
England's statutory guidance on drainage says traps "should retain a minimum seal of 25mm of water
or equivalent" (about an inch) under working and test conditions, and that "discharge stacks should
be ventilated", so the seals are not lost to pressure in the pipes.[21] The American Society of Home
Inspectors puts it plainly: "Vent piping allows air into the system so
drains flow easily."[22] This flow has no shut-off to find. Lesson 4, "Water out: traps,
vents and blocked drains", is about what goes wrong with it.

**Power in, through protective devices.** Electricity arrives at a consumer unit (the British
name; people still call it the fuse box) or a panel (the American one), and the devices in it do
two different jobs. The US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) put the difference in 1989: a
ground fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) cuts the power fast when current leaks where it should not,
"whereas circuit breakers and fuses function primarily to prevent the home wiring system from
causing a fire."[8] Electrical Safety First (ESF), a UK electrical safety charity, says the British
equivalent of the GFCI, the residual current device or RCD, "protects you from electric shocks if a
product is faulty."[7] So breakers and fuses are there mainly to protect the wiring, and RCDs and
GFCIs to protect the person. Lesson 5, "Electricity: what trips, and why", takes that apart.

**Fuel in, combustion products out.** Gas (or oil, or wood) comes in, burns in a boiler, a
furnace, a cooker or a fire, and what is left after burning has to leave by a flue (the pipe or
duct that carries it outside) or a chimney. The gas has a shut-off at the meter. The way out has no
switch to find, and lesson 11, "Carbon monoxide and the alarms that warn you", is about
keeping it working.

That gives you three controls to find: water, power and gas.

## Water: the stop tap and the main shut-off valve

### In the UK

[WaterSafe](https://www.watersafe.org.uk/advice/general_plumbing_advice/winter_advice/locate-internal-stop-tap/), the approved-plumber register run for the water industry, calls the control the
internal stop tap, "also sometimes called a stop valve or stopcock". "It looks like a spout-less
tap or lever and sits between two lengths of pipe acting as a connector." It is usually under the
kitchen sink, and WaterSafe lists other places, from a garage to the space under the stairs.[1]

Using it takes three facts, all WaterSafe's: "Turning the valve clockwise will close it"; it "may
take a few minutes for the water to stop altogether"; and "Turn it on and off slowly, never force
it." It gives the reason for the last one: "If you over tighten or use excessive force you may
damage the stop tap." If it won't turn or won't shut off, WaterSafe's advice is to call a
plumber.[1]

A second stop tap sits outside, and it is not yours. CCW describes "The external stop tap usually
located in the public footpath" as the water company's.[3] It stops your water too, but it sits under a
cover in the pavement, and in an emergency the one inside is the one you reach first.

The [Met Office](https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/warnings-and-advice/seasonal-advice/your-home/frozen-or-burst-pipes)
adds a habit: "Make sure you know where your stop tap is and check it’s working every six
months."[2] This is a national weather service's advice, not a law.
It gives no reason. This course's reading is that a valve you've tried recently is one you know
will turn.

### In the US

[WSSC Water](https://www.wsscwater.com/customer-service/report-problem/find-and-operate-your-main-water-valve), a public water utility in Maryland, calls it the main water shut-off valve and says
where to look by house type. In a house with a basement it is "typically located near the front
foundation wall" and "typically within three to five feet of where the main water enters" (about 1
to 1.5 m). In slab-on-grade houses (built straight onto a concrete slab) and crawl-space houses
(with a low open space under the floor) that have no basement, it is typically near the water heater
or under the kitchen sink, "but anywhere is possible."[5] WSSC also says "Every home was required to
have a main water shut-off valve installed inside the home when it was built." It describes its own
customers' houses, not a national rule, and an old house elsewhere may surprise you.

On operating it: "Round “wheel” handle valves will turn off by turning the handle clockwise. It may
take two or more full revolutions." A lever handle is different: WSSC says it takes a quarter turn,
"until the handle is not parallel with the pipe. It should stop at a ¼ turn." Turn it back on
slowly, part of the way first.[5] There is also an underground valve outside at the property line
(at some homes the meter is out there too, in a pit, WSSC says). WSSC says that one is for a
registered master plumber (a licensed plumber) or the utility, and that it charges a fee to operate
it.[5] One more detail matters in some houses: where a home has fire sprinklers, WSSC says you shut
off the plumbing supply and leave the sprinkler pipe live, unless the sprinkler pipe is what is
leaking.[5]

This course read no US source giving a householder the six-monthly check.

:::predict You have found the valve, and it has a round handle. Before you read on: turning a stop tap or a wheel-handle valve which way closes it, clockwise or anticlockwise? And how sure are you?

Clockwise closes a tap or wheel handle. WaterSafe (British) and WSSC (American)
say the same thing.[1][5] If you were unsure, you're in good company: while this course was being
researched, a search engine's own summary had it backwards. Try the valve on a quiet afternoon
rather than work it out in a flood.
:::

### Two houses, first look

First, the simple case. A couple have just moved into a 1930s semi-detached house
in England. The stop tap turns out to be where WaterSafe says it usually is, under the kitchen sink,
behind a bottle of bleach and a bucket.

They turn it clockwise, slowly, with the kitchen cold tap open. The flow stops after a couple of
minutes, which is WaterSafe's "may take a few minutes". Turning it back, anticlockwise, it goes
stiff half a turn in, with the water flowing but not at full strength. WaterSafe says never force
it, so they don't reach for pliers. They note on their shut-off map that it is stiff, and book a
plumber.

Next they open the door of the consumer unit: a main switch, a row of circuit breakers and a
button marked "T". ESF: "To check whether your consumer unit has an RCD, look for the ‘Test’ or ‘T’
button."[6] They leave the button for lesson 5. Two breakers have no label, so they switch each off
in turn, walk round to see what stopped, write it down, and switch it back on. This is labelling from
the front of the box, this course's reading of the University of Georgia's home maintenance
checklist, which says "label switches if needed".[11] A breaker that wouldn't go back on, or tripped
again, they'd leave off and call an electrician about, as the box at the top says.

## Electricity: the consumer unit and the panel

The main switch on a British consumer unit, and the main breaker in an American panel, cut the
power to the house's circuits. A householder's work on the box stops there.

CPSC gives the reason in its [leaflet on AFCIs](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/5133.pdf), a
type of breaker it says a householder shouldn't install: "Be sure to have a qualified electrician install
AFCIs; do not attempt this work yourself. The installation involves working within electrical panel
boxes that are usually electrically live, even with the main circuit breakers turned off."[9]
CPSC doesn't say what keeps the box live, and this course won't guess.

:::predict A neighbour says: "It's fine to take the cover off the fuse box once you've flipped the main switch, the whole thing's dead then." From what you have just read, what is wrong with that?

The main switch cuts the circuits, not everything inside the box. CPSC says panel boxes are
"usually electrically live, even with the main circuit breakers turned off".[9] So the cover stays
on, whatever the main switch says, and anything behind it is a job for an electrician.
:::

Two more facts go wherever electricity does in this course. First, a pocket "volt stick"
that stays silent proves nothing: Britain's Health and Safety Executive (HSE), in its guidance for
people who work on electrical systems, says the use of "non-contact devices such as 'volt sticks'"
is not recommended for proving a circuit dead.[10] Second, the course teaches no wiring at all.
Lesson 22, "Who may do what: reserved work, and the argument about licensing", explains which electrical work the law lets a householder do in each UK nation and in five
US places, and teaches the procedure for none of it. This is the course's decision about what a page
can check, not a claim that the work is unlawful wherever you are.

So the front of the box is yours: the switches, the test button and the labels. Behind the cover
is somebody else's.

## Gas: the emergency control valve

### In Great Britain and Northern Ireland

Cadent, the largest gas distribution network in Great Britain, describes the valve on its own
meters in its [guide to locating the meter](https://cadentgas.com/locating-your-meter-and-turning-off-the-gas). The meter
may be inside (a garage, under the stairs, the kitchen) or outside, in a box that needs a key.[12] Then: "Open your gas meter box and locate the red tap handle near to the pipe. This is
the emergency control valve (ECV). This handle will be aligned with the pipe; to switch off the
supply you should turn it either 90 degrees to the left or 90 degrees to the right (a quarter turn
clockwise or anti-clockwise)."[12] This is Cadent's description of the meters on its own network; this course didn't read other
networks' guides.

Cadent's page on smelling gas says when to use it: open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the
meter, and "If the gas leak is in a cellar or basement, do not enter and instead evacuate the
building." The number is 0800 111 999, day or night.[12][14] In Northern Ireland, one gas
supplier's advice (SSE Airtricity's) is the same shape, with its own number: turn the gas off at
the meter "unless the meter is located in a cellar or basement", and phone 0800 002 001.[13]

Find the meter and the handle now, and look, but don't turn it to see if it moves. Gas isn't
water: Cadent says that once it is off, "Don’t turn it back on until advised it’s safe to do so."[12]

### In the US

The US utilities this course read give a different first step. Con Edison, in New York: "Leave
immediately and take others with you." SoCalGas and Atmos Energy say the same: leave, and call from
a safe distance.[15] So in an American house, the course's advice is to know where the meter is and
then leave it alone; this course read no US instructions for turning one off.

## Gas and the law, in brief

This is general information, not legal advice.

In Great Britain, [regulation 3](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1998/2451/regulation/3) of the
Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998 requires anyone who does gas work to be
competent, and HSE's guidance says that includes people doing their own (regulation 3(1)). It
requires employers and the self-employed to be on the Gas Safe Register, the HSE-approved register
of gas businesses (regulation 3(3)). HSE's code of practice sets competence at an
industry-recognised course followed by an assessment.[16][17] Northern Ireland's own 2004
Regulations have the same shape. So "it's illegal to do your own gas work" overstates the law, and
"it's legal" leaves out the competence duty.

The advice is not in doubt. [HSE](https://www.hse.gov.uk/gas/domestic/faqownerocc.htm): "HSE
strongly advises that all gas appliances, flues and pipework should be installed, regularly
maintained and serviced at least annually by a Gas Safe registered engineer."[18] [Gas
Safe](https://web.archive.org/web/20251023004454/https://www.gassaferegister.co.uk/gas-safety/home-gas-safety/home-improvements/)
says "Don’t DIY with gas appliances, always use a Gas Safe registered engineer", and that a
householder can do the tasks the maker's user instructions give the user.[19] Lesson 10 has the
rest.

## When a pipe bursts

### In the UK

:::predict A pipe has burst in the loft (the attic) of a British house and water is coming through the landing ceiling. Put these in the order you'd do them: soak up the water; turn off the stop tap; call a plumber; open the taps; switch off the boiler.

The Met Office's order is: stop tap off, boiler off, open the taps to drain the pipes, soak up what
you can, and call a WaterSafe plumber.[2] CCW gives the same order and adds two things: switch off
the immersion heater (the electric heater in a hot water cylinder) as well as the heating, and
photograph the damage for your insurer.[4] If you put soaking up first, the two sources disagree
with you: both put the stop tap first. Neither says why. This course's reading is that a towel
can't keep up with a pipe still being fed.
:::

What insurance covers is Personal Finance Fundamentals lesson 8, "Handing off what you cannot
absorb".

### In the US

This course read no US agency's burst-pipe order. The one American utility page it read, WSSC's in
Maryland, says that once the main valve is closed you "Open a tub or sink faucet (hot and cold) on
the highest level of the home to relieve pressure, and watch that spout to ensure that water has
stopped flowing."[5] This is one utility's advice to its own customers, not a national rule. After
that, call a plumber; that step is this course's line, not WSSC's.

### When the water reaches the electrics

This is the part that can hurt someone, and the Met Office's wording needs care. It says: "If
water has leaked near your electrics, do not touch them and switch them off at the fuse box."[2]
Read quickly, that tells you to go to the fuse box whatever state it is in. ESF's current [advice
after flooding](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/safety-advice/home-and-people/house-maintenance/electrical-safety-after-a-flood/) is more careful. "Switch off the electricity supply at the consumer unit/ fusebox,
only if it is safe to do so." And: "Seek professional advice from a registered electrician if there
is any sign of water inside the consumer unit/ fusebox or any signs of arcing or overheating." It
also says: "Keep away from any exposed wiring as it may still be live."[20]

Neither source says what makes it safe. So here's this course's own plain reading, not a quotation. Don't touch wet switches, sockets or fittings. Go to the consumer unit or
panel only if the box and the wall around it are dry, with no water on it, in it or running towards
it, and you can reach it without standing in water or touching anything wet. If you can't, keep
clear and call an electrician. Both sources are British, and this course read no American source on
water near a panel, so for a US panel this reading is the course's own. This is the idea behind First
Aid and CPR lesson 2's danger step: make sure the power is off before you touch someone in contact
with a supply.

### The second house

Now a harder case: winter, in a two-storey house in the American Midwest. A pipe to the upstairs
bathroom has split, and water is coming through the fitting of the hall ceiling light. The panel is
on the basement wall, and when the owner opens the basement door there is already about an inch
(2.5 cm) of water across the floor. The main shut-off valve is where WSSC says to look, near the
front foundation wall, on the far side of the basement.

The light first: the switch and the fitting are wet, so the owner touches neither.

:::checkpoint Before you read on: does the owner go down to the valve, or to the panel? Decide, and give the reason for each one.

Neither, on this course's reading. Both are across a basement floor under an inch of water, with a
wet circuit upstairs feeding into that panel. This course read no American source on water near a
panel; the British charity Electrical Safety First says to switch off at a consumer unit "only if it
is safe to do so", and the course applies the same caution here as its own reading. Walking through
water to reach either one is what that reading rules out. If the basement
were dry, and the panel and the wall round it dry too, the valve would be the owner's to close
(clockwise, two or more turns, WSSC says), and on this course's reading the main breaker would be
the owner's to switch off. It isn't, so the owner doesn't.
:::

The owner gets everyone out of the wet hall and the basement, and calls their water utility,
whose number is on its website or the bill, or a plumber. WSSC in Maryland, the one water utility
this course read, says the underground valve at the property line is for the utility or a registered master plumber, and that WSSC charges to operate it.[5] A
utility in the Midwest may work differently, so ask it. Either way, the water can be stopped
outside without anyone going into the basement. The owner also calls an electrician about the wet circuit and
the panel: on this course's reading, water that may have reached a panel is an electrician's to
look at (Electrical Safety First says the same of a British consumer unit).[20] Until someone closes
that outside valve, the water keeps coming. This is the cost of a valve out of reach, and the
reason to find out, before a winter, whether you could reach yours with the basement flooded.

## Law or advice, whose, and where?

This is general information, not legal advice.

Household advice most often goes wrong one way: a rule from one place, or one kind of body, gets
repeated as if it bound everyone everywhere. You have met three already. WSSC's "Every home was required" is about homes in one utility's area. The
six-monthly stop-tap check is the Met Office's advice, not a law. And "gas work is illegal" is half
of a two-part rule that binds businesses one way and everyone another.

So every rule in this course carries three things. **Who says it**: a law, a
regulator, a charity, a maker about its own product, or a university's public advice service (in
the US, an "extension service"). **Whether it is law or advice.** And **where it applies**: each UK
nation by name, and the US federally or by named state or city.

Every job the course names carries one of three labels, too:

- **Yours.** The sources put it in a householder's hands, like turning off the stop tap.
- **Yours, on conditions.** Allowed if a named condition is met: "only if the box and the wall
  around it are dry", "not if the meter is in a cellar".
- **Call someone.** The law where you are reserves it, or every official source the course read
  advises against a householder doing it.

Where the law allows more than the advice, as with gas in Great Britain, the course gives both,
law first, and the label follows the advice. So gas work is "Call someone" though the
registration duty does not bind a householder.

:::checkpoint A friend in England reads online that "the council says every house must have its circuits labelled". Before you take it on board, what three questions does this course ask of that sentence, and what do you know already about this particular rule?

Who says it, is it law or advice, and where does it apply? The source this lesson read on circuit
labels is the University of Georgia's checklist, "label switches if needed".[11] That is a sensible
habit, but it is advice, from a US university, and nothing this course read makes it a council's
rule or English law. The friend's sentence is advice with its source and place forgotten.
:::

## What this course leaves out

No gas work and no wiring: the course explains the law on both, and stops at the front of the box
and at the user instructions. Structural work, extensions, loft conversions, roofing and planning
permission can need permission as well as professionals; these are named, not taught.

**Fitting a lock.** This course doesn't teach it: its research found no source on fitting or
maintaining a lock to teach from, and this institute doesn't publish lessons written from general
knowledge. For which locks and lights make a difference to burglary,
Personal Safety lesson 5, "Home, the door and the doorstep", is the place to go.

Some jobs weren't researched and so aren't taught (tiling, flooring, appliance repair), and some
belong to other courses: insurance and scams to Personal Finance Fundamentals, first aid to First
Aid and CPR. Landlord and tenant law isn't taught either. If you rent, every exercise that changes
anything says "if it's your home to repair, or your landlord agrees", and that's the
course's own line, not a statement of tenancy law.

## What people get wrong

**"Clockwise opens it."** It closes a stop tap or a wheel handle (WaterSafe, WSSC).[1][5]

**"The stop tap in the pavement is the one to use."** It's the water company's (CCW); in the US, the
valve at the property line is for the utility or a master plumber (WSSC).[3][5]

**"Once the main switch is off, the fuse box is safe to open."** CPSC says panels are usually live
even with the main breakers off.[9]

**"It's illegal to do any gas work yourself."** It overstates the law, which binds businesses one
way and everyone another (see "Gas and the law, in brief" above).

:::exercise Your shut-off map, and eight jobs

Take 30 minutes for the map, at home, in daylight, with a phone camera and a pen, and 15 more to
sort the eight jobs. If you rent, do the looking and the writing, and turn the stop tap only if it's
your home to repair, or your landlord agrees.

**1. Water.** Find the stop tap (UK) or main shut-off valve (US). Photograph it and write down
where it is in words someone else could follow in the dark. If your home has fire sprinklers, don't
turn anything unless you know which valve is the plumbing one and which feeds the sprinklers,
because WSSC says the sprinkler pipe stays live; ask a plumber to show you. Otherwise, with the
kitchen cold tap open, turn it clockwise slowly until it stops (if it's a lever rather than a tap or
wheel handle, WSSC says a quarter turn, until the handle lies across the pipe), and time how long
the tap takes to run dry. Then turn it back on, slowly. Have a plumber's number to hand first, because a valve that sticks
on the way back can leave you short of water (this course's advice, not WaterSafe's). **If it is
stiff, stop. Don't force it, don't use a tool on it, and call a plumber**, as WaterSafe says. Write
down whether it turned freely, and how long it took.

**2. Power.** Find the consumer unit or panel. Photograph the front with the door open, noting the
main switch and any "T" or "Test" button (leave that for lesson 5). Copy down the labels that are there and mark which circuits have none. Don't take off any
cover, and don't use a tester or volt stick on anything.

**3. Gas.** If you have gas, find the meter and, in the UK, the handle beside it. Photograph it.
**Don't turn it**, not even a little: the handle is for an emergency, and the US utilities this course
read say to leave, not to turn anything. Note whether the meter is in a cellar or basement, because
in the UK that changes what you do if you smell gas.

**4. Your card.** On one side of a card, write the burst-pipe order for your country as this lesson
gave it (in the US, main valve off, then a plumber), and for the UK, the gas number for your part of
it (0800 111 999 in Great Britain, 0800 002 001 in Northern Ireland). In the US, write 911 and your
gas utility's emergency number from its own website. Put the card by the stop tap or on the fridge.

**5. Sort eight jobs.** Give each one a label (Yours; Yours, on conditions, naming the condition;
or Call someone) and say whose rule sits behind it, whether it is law or advice, and where it
applies. Where the answer is a law, this is general information, not legal advice. Write your answers before you open the checkpoint below.

1. Turning off the inside stop tap when a pipe bursts (UK)
2. Turning the stop tap under the cover in the public footpath (England)
3. Taking the cover off a panel to look at a scorched breaker (US)
4. Switching off at the consumer unit with water running down the wall beside it
5. Turning off the gas at the meter handle when you smell gas, the meter being in the hall (Great
   Britain)
6. Replacing a cooker's control knob that the maker designed the user to replace (Great Britain)
7. Moving a gas hob to a new position on the worktop (Great Britain)
8. Going back into a house after smelling gas to open the windows (US)
:::

:::checkpoint Check your eight against these.

1. **Yours.** WaterSafe's and the Met Office's advice, UK.
2. **Call someone**, on this course's reading: CCW says it's the water company's (England and Wales), and no source read gives it to a householder to operate.
3. **Call someone.** CPSC's fact (a US federal agency's advice about AFCIs) that panels are usually live with the main breakers off; that this rules out opening one is this course's reading.
4. **Call someone**, here. Switching off at the consumer unit is Yours, on conditions (ESF's advice, UK: "only if it is safe to do so"); on this course's reading, water running down the wall beside it fails the condition.
5. **Yours, on conditions**: not if the meter is in a cellar or basement, and only if you can do it safely. Cadent's advice, with National Gas's and HSE's to the same effect, Great Britain; the cellar condition is Cadent's and National Gas's.
6. **Yours, on conditions**: that it is a task the user instructions give the user. The Gas Safe Register's statement, Great Britain.
7. **Call someone.** The law in Great Britain requires competence of anyone and registration of businesses, and HSE and the Register advise a Gas Safe registered engineer; the label follows the advice.
8. **Call someone**: the utility or 911, from outside, and don't go back in. Con Edison, SoCalGas and Atmos all say leave and call from a safe place, US. Opening windows is British advice.

If you gave 5 as plain **Yours**, look again at the conditions. If you gave 7 as **Yours** because
the registration rule binds businesses, this is the trap this lesson is built to catch: the law
is not the whole answer, and the label follows the advice.
:::
