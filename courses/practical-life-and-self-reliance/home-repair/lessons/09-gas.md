When you smell gas at home, the obvious thing to do is the dangerous one. You reach for the light
switch to see better, or, in the US, where one utility says doorbells and phones can spark, you
ring the neighbour's bell or pick up your phone in the kitchen. The gas companies' advice on what
to do instead is short, specific and, between the UK and the US, different at the very first step.
Here are each country's steps in full, as the gas companies give them, and what to do when you
can't smell anything. Then two leaks, one in a British flat and one in an American house. You'll
finish with a card you could follow in the dark.

:::callout Before you pick up a tool
This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

The box gives the short version of the gas steps, and it splits them by country because the UK's
gas networks and American utilities tell you different things. This lesson gives each in full.
Follow your own country's, never the other one's.

Lesson 1, "How a house works, and where it switches off", found the emergency control valve beside
the meter and asked you to look at it without turning it. This lesson is about the night you might
need it, and the nights you should leave it alone. Who may work on gas, and how to check the engineer
who comes, is lesson 10, "Gas: the law and the engineer".

## If you smell gas: the UK steps

:::predict It's dark, you've just come in, and the hall smells of gas. The light switch is by your hand and the light is off. Before reading on: switch it on to see, switch it off if it was on, or leave it alone? Would your answer change if the light were already on?

Leave it alone either way. Cadent's list of things not to do starts "Turn any power or light
switches on or off", and National Gas says "Do not turn electrical switches on or off."[1][2]
Most people get the first half and miss the second, because switching off feels like making things
safe. Neither British page says why switches matter, though Cadent's list also rules out "any
appliances that could cause a spark". Con Edison, a US utility, gives the reason: switches, like
doorbells and phones, "can produce sparks that might cause the gas to explode."[5]
:::

### In Great Britain

Great Britain has several gas networks, the companies whose pipes bring gas to homes, and one
emergency number for all of them. This course read two sets of steps, not every network's.

[National Gas](https://www.nationalgas.com/emergency-contacts), which runs the national
transmission system, says "these emergency guidelines apply to all gas users", and gives them in
five lines:[2]

- "Do not smoke or light matches."
- "Do not turn electrical switches on or off."
- "Open doors and windows."
- "Turn off the meter at the control handle unless the meter is in the cellar."
- "Call the National Gas Emergency number (0800 111 999)."

The number belongs to the National Gas Emergency Service, it is free, and it is answered day and
night.[1] Because National Gas's guidelines cover all gas users, it does not depend on which company
sends your bill.

[Cadent](https://cadentgas.com/smell-gas) runs the largest network, covering the North West, the
West and East Midlands, the East of England and North London, and gives fuller steps for its own
customers.[1] Before you call:

- "Open doors and windows to ventilate the property."
- "Turn off the gas at the mains tap. This can usually be located near the gas meter and has a
  handle that can be turned 90 degrees. If the gas leak is in a cellar or basement, do not enter
  and instead evacuate the building."

And what not to do: turn any power or light switches on or off, light any sort of flame within the
property, or "Use any appliances that could cause a spark." Then: "Once you're safe, call the
National Gas Emergency Service number on 0800 111 999". Afterwards: "Always wait until the gas
engineer has given you the all-clear before returning inside your property." The engineer will
need to get in to make it safe.[1]

Great Britain's Health and Safety Executive (HSE) gives a shorter list on
[its page for home owners](https://www.hse.gov.uk/gas/domestic/faqownerocc.htm): call the emergency
number, open all the doors and windows, and "Shut off the gas supply at the meter control valve (if
you know where it is)". HSE's list has no cellar condition and says nothing about switches.[4] Its
page still calls the number National Grid's, an older name; the number is the same.

Cadent's "mains tap", National Gas's "control handle" and HSE's "meter control valve" are one
handle, the one lesson 1 called the emergency control valve. Once it's off, it stays off: Cadent
says "Don't turn it back on until advised it's safe to do so."[1]

Read the two cellar conditions closely, because they are not the same. Cadent's is about where the
leak is: if it is in a cellar or basement, do not go in, and get out of the building. National
Gas's is about where the meter is, "unless the meter is in the cellar", and it says nothing about
leaving. They come apart when the meter is in a cellar and the smell is upstairs. This course's
reading is to take both: if either the smell or the meter is in a cellar or basement, nobody goes
down to the valve. One more condition is this course's own, from the box at the top: "if you can do
it safely", which here means reaching the handle without going further into the smell.

Then, do you stay in or get out? Neither British page says in so many words to leave the building,
except for a leak in a cellar or basement. But Cadent says to call "once you're safe" and to wait
for the all-clear "before returning inside your property", which assumes you are outside. So this
course's reading, for anywhere in Great Britain, is: switches alone, doors and windows open, the
meter off if neither condition stops you, then everyone out, the call made from outside, and nobody
back in until the engineer says so. Cadent writes for its own network, and this course did not read
the other networks' pages; nothing on National Gas's or HSE's page says otherwise.

Two more cases. If the smell is outside, Cadent says to call the same number as soon as you can,
not to light any naked flame nearby, and to wait for the emergency engineer.[1] And if you or your
builder hit a gas pipe while digging or drilling, National Gas says to call 0800 111 999 "day or
night", even if no gas seems to be escaping.[2]

### In Northern Ireland

Northern Ireland has its own number, 0800 002 001, for the Northern Ireland Gas Emergency
Service. This course read two pages that give the same
steps: [Phoenix Energy](https://phoenixenergyni.com/safety-environment/in-an-emergency), a gas
network company there, and [SSE Airtricity](https://www.sseairtricitygasni.com/in-an-emergency/),
one of the suppliers.[3][8] Both put the call first. SSE opens: "If you smell gas or you are worried
about gas safety, phone the 24-hour Northern Ireland Gas Emergency Service on 0800 002 001." Phoenix's
first step is "Call the gas emergency team 0800 002 001". Then, in SSE's order:[3]

- "Turn off the gas supply at the meter, unless the meter is located in a cellar or basement", in
  which case, it says, do not go in.
- If the smell is in the cellar or basement, get out of the building.
- "Extinguish all naked flames and do not smoke or strike any matches."
- "Open doors and windows for ventilation."
- "Check gas appliances to see if the gas has been left on unlit or that a pilot is out." (A pilot
  light is a small flame some appliances keep burning.)
- Do not turn any electrical switches on or off, "including door entry systems".

Both pages end the same way: "Never leave it to anyone else to call", and make sure you "allow
immediate access" to the engineer.[3][8] Neither says to leave the building unless the smell is in
a cellar or basement.

## If you smell gas: the US steps

American utilities start somewhere else. [Con
Edison](https://www.coned.com/en/safety/energy-safety/gas-safety), in New York: "Leave immediately
and take others with you." If the leak is outside, move to a safe spot far away. Then call 911 or
the utility. Three more of its lines are worth learning word for word:[5]

- "Even if the odor isn’t very strong, you should still leave the area immediately, taking others,
  and then call."
- "Don’t assume someone else has already called."
- "Don’t light a match, smoke, flip a switch, ring a doorbell, or touch appliances or electronics,
  including your phone. Doing so can produce sparks that might cause the gas to explode."

[SoCalGas](https://www.socalgas.com/safety/safety-and-prevention/natural-gas-leak) says "IMMEDIATELY
EVACUATE THE AREA and call us from a safe location", and to call 911 from a safe place too if life
or property is at risk. Its list of things not to do differs from Con Edison's. It has no flame of
any kind and "DO NOT turn electrical appliances or lights on or off", and it adds motorized
equipment, vehicles and any device that could cause a spark, and "DO NOT attempt to control the leak
or repair a damaged pipe or meter." It does not mention doorbells or phones.[6]

[Atmos Energy](https://www.atmosenergy.com/safety/recognizing-leak/): "leave the area immediately!
From a safe distance call 911" and the utility's own emergency line, and "Do not assume someone else
will report the leak."[7] Atmos's page says nothing about switches or flames.

None of the three leak pages tells you to open windows or to turn anything at the meter, and
SoCalGas's tells you not to try to control the leak at all. So on those three pages, when you smell
gas at home you leave and call, and you do not go to the meter. But US utilities do not all say the same thing
everywhere. SoCalGas's separate [earthquake and emergency
page](https://www.socalgas.com/safety/emergency-information/emergency-preparedness) tells its
customers to turn off the meter if they smell gas, "and ONLY if it is safe to do so", and to leave
it off once it's off.[9] Your own utility's page decides, and this course read three. Find it now,
follow it, and copy its emergency number from its website or your bill before you need it.

Put side by side, and this is this course's summary: every page read sends you to an emergency line,
and every page that gives a list of things not to do (Cadent, National Gas, SSE Airtricity, Phoenix,
Con Edison, SoCalGas) rules out a flame and touching a switch. Atmos's page and HSE's short list
mention neither. What they do not share is the first move: in the UK you ventilate and turn the gas
off at the meter; in the US you leave first and touch nothing. None of the sources read says why the
advice differs between the countries, and this course won't guess. Learn your own country's, and
don't borrow the other's.

## When you can't smell it

Natural gas has no smell of its own. Con Edison explains that a chemical called mercaptan is added
so that you can detect a leak, and that many people compare its smell to rotten eggs.[5] The smell
is the alarm, and the alarm can fail.

It can fail in you. SoCalGas says you may not smell leaking gas if your sense of smell is
diminished, if you've been around it long enough to stop noticing (odour fatigue), if a cold, sinus
trouble or allergies have blocked it, or if other smells mask it.[6] Cadent points people who have
lost their sense of smell to SmellTaste, a charity for people with smell and taste disorders.[1]

And it can fail in the gas. SoCalGas says the odorant can be lost, "making it undetectable by
smell", and that this "odor fade" is more likely in four situations: in new steel pipe that was
recently made or hasn't carried odorised gas before; in systems at higher pressure, and when the flow is
limited or intermittent; where rust, moisture or other substances are in the pipe; and depending on
how the customer's own piping is designed and built, such as its length and diameter. Its
conclusion: "it's important to not rely only on your sense of smell to be alerted to a natural gas
leak."[6] Con Edison's list is similar. It includes plastic as well as metal pipe, and says that
when a leak is underground, the soil around it can cause odour fade.[5] Atmos puts it in
proportion: for most people smell is "a highly reliable indicator of a natural gas release", and it
calls the gas's own smell fading something that happens in "certain rare situations".[7] This is
the US utilities' account of their own pipes. The UK pages this course read do not discuss odour
fade.

So the other senses matter. SoCalGas lists hissing or whistling, dirt or water blowing into the air,
and a dry patch of grass. Con Edison adds a white cloud, bubbles in water, blowing dust, dying
plants, and a roar.[5][6] If you suspect a leak but can't smell it, Con Edison says you should "still
find a phone away from the area and call".[5]

Two US utilities go a step further for their own customers. Con Edison says "A slow gas leak may
not produce enough scent to be detectable" and that "A gas leak detector can give you peace of
mind", and Atmos says "residential methane detectors are commercially available and can provide an
additional ability to detect the presence of gas".[5][7] That is Con Edison's and Atmos's advice,
for US homes, and this course names no product. This course read no British page on gas detectors,
for them or against them.

One more thing a smell can't tell you about: a carbon monoxide alarm is not a gas alarm. Con Edison
says most carbon monoxide detectors "do not detect combustible gasses", and to check the label.[5]
Carbon monoxide, which has no smell at all, is lesson 11, "Carbon monoxide and the alarms that warn you".

:::checkpoint A man in Great Britain has had no sense of smell since an illness last winter. His partner is away for a week. On this lesson's sources, what does his missing sense change about gas at home, and what does it leave the same?

It takes away his first warning. SoCalGas lists a diminished sense of smell as a reason you may not
notice leaking gas, and Cadent points people who have lost their sense of smell to the charity
SmellTaste for advice.[1][6] With his partner away, nobody in the house has that warning this week,
and the British pages read give him no other sign to watch for; the signs the US pages list,
indoors, mostly mean a hiss. It leaves the rest the same: if he does suspect a leak, Great Britain's
steps and number are unchanged. A carbon monoxide alarm does not fill the gap, because Con Edison
says most carbon monoxide detectors do not detect gas.[5] Con Edison and Atmos recommend a gas
detector to their US customers, but this course read no British page on gas detectors either way, so
what else would help him is a question for SmellTaste or his gas network.
:::

## Two leaks, worked through

### A shared flat at eleven at night

The simple case first, in Great Britain. Isla lives in a shared flat in a converted Victorian house
in the Black Country, in the West Midlands, on Cadent's network. At eleven at night she comes back
to a strong smell of gas in the hall. The meter is in a cupboard by the front door. Two flatmates
are in their rooms.

She works through Great Britain's steps.

1. **The switch.** The hall light is off. She leaves it off and doesn't touch the switch by the
   door (Cadent, National Gas).[1][2]
2. **Air.** She opens the front door and the hall window (Cadent, National Gas).[1][2]
3. **The meter.** The meter is in a cupboard and the smell is in the hall, so neither is in a
   cellar or basement and neither network's condition stops her. She can reach the handle without going further into the
   smell, which is this course's condition, so she turns it a quarter turn, the way Cadent's meter
   page describes.[1][2]
4. **The flatmates.** She knocks on their doors and tells them to come out with her, so that all
   three are out of the flat before she calls. Nobody uses a switch or an appliance on the way.[1]
5. **The call.** Outside, away from the front door, she rings 0800 111 999. Cadent: "Once you're
   safe, call".[1] Going outside to be safe is this course's reading of that line.
6. **The wait.** Nobody goes back in until the engineer has given the all-clear, and she lets the
   engineer in when they arrive (Cadent).[1]

Who calls? Cadent and National Gas tell the person who smells gas to call.[1][2] What they do not
add is SSE Airtricity's "Never leave it to anyone else to call the Emergency number", or Con Edison's
and Atmos's warning not to assume someone else has called.[3][5][7] So Isla makes the call herself.
Not leaving it to a flatmate is this course's reading, drawn from those three, not from a British
page. The gas then stays off until the emergency engineer says otherwise.

:::predict Isla's flatmate says the meter in their last flat was in the basement, but the smell that night was in the kitchen, two floors up. Before reading on: does step 3 survive?

No. The smell isn't in the basement, so Cadent's condition alone wouldn't stop you. But National
Gas's condition is about the meter, and SSE Airtricity and Phoenix say the same for Northern
Ireland.[2][3][8] So on this course's reading, which takes both, nobody goes down to the valve.
Everything else stands: switches alone, doors and windows open, everyone out, and the call made once
you're safe.
:::

The job labels from lesson 1 fit this without strain. Turning the valve is **Yours, on
conditions**: in Great Britain or Northern Ireland, if neither the smell nor the meter is in a
cellar or basement, and, this course adds, if you can reach it without going further into the smell.
The call is **Yours**. Everything to do with the leak after that belongs to the emergency engineer.

### A split-level house in Connecticut, with a gap for you

Now the wrinkle, in the US. The Okafors live in a split-level house in Connecticut. On a Sunday
morning their teenage son smells gas on the lower level, where the meter and the furnace are. A
cousin visiting from England says: "Go down and turn it off at the meter, then open the windows.
That's what we do at home."

The cousin's steps are Great Britain's, and this house is in Connecticut. This is how the US sources
read it.

1. **Nobody goes down.** None of the US leak pages read tells a householder to turn the meter, and
   SoCalGas says "DO NOT attempt to control the leak".[6] The windows stay as they are: none of the
   three utilities says to open them.
2. **Everyone leaves, now.** Con Edison's first instruction is to leave and take everyone, even if
   the smell isn't strong.[5] The son doesn't stop to switch the lower-level light off, and nobody
   picks up the kitchen phone or rings the next-door bell on the way out (Con Edison's list).[5]

:::checkpoint Your step. They are all on the front lawn. Who makes the call, from where, and to whom? And should the son go back in for his phone, which is charging in his room?

From a safe place away from the house (SoCalGas, Atmos), to 911 or the gas utility's emergency line
(Con Edison, Atmos). Whoever has a phone makes the call now rather than assuming someone else has
(Con Edison, Atmos).[5][6][7] Nobody goes back in for anything: Con Edison says to leave and to touch
no electronics, the phone included, and going back in undoes the leaving. If nobody has a phone,
they use a neighbour's, away from the house; Con Edison's line is to "find a phone away from the
area and call".[5]
:::

The cousin's advice is Great Britain's, from British gas networks. In Connecticut, the advice to
follow is the Okafors' own gas utility's. This course did not read it, but the three US utilities it
did read all start by leaving, which is why the callout splits by country.

The labels, in the US: the meter valve is **Call someone** on every leak page read, the utility's to
deal with and not yours, unless your own utility's page tells you otherwise. The call is **Yours**,
from a safe place.

## What people get wrong

**"Switching the lights off makes it safer."** It feels like making things safe. Cadent and National
Gas say not to turn switches on or off, and Con Edison gives the reason, sparks.[1][2][5]

**"No smell, no leak."** The smell is the warning you've been told to expect, so its absence feels
like an all-clear. A lost sense of smell and odour fade can both hide a leak, SoCalGas and Con
Edison say, though Atmos calls fade in the gas rare. Listen and look as well.[5][6][7]

**"If the meter's in the cellar, just be quick."** It feels like the fastest way to stop the gas.
Cadent and SSE Airtricity say don't go into a cellar or basement with a leak in it, and get out of
the building; National Gas says not to turn off at the meter if the meter is in the cellar. None of
them gives a reason.[1][2][3]

**"Ringing the neighbours' bells is the responsible thing."** In the US, Con Edison lists ringing a
doorbell among the things not to do; take people with you instead.[5]

**"Someone will have called by now."** When several people can smell it, each can assume another
has rung. SSE Airtricity, Phoenix, Con Edison and Atmos all say not to assume it or leave it to
anyone else.[3][5][7][8]

**"The UK steps and the US steps are the same thing in different words."** They differ at the first
move. Follow your own country's.

## Practice

:::exercise Your gas card, your meter, and four leaks

Take 25 minutes over these. Don't turn the meter valve for this exercise, not even a little, and
don't move, open or unscrew any gas appliance. If you rent, all of it is looking and writing.

If your home has no gas, do parts 1 and 3 anyway: you'll meet gas in other people's homes and in the
street, and the card for your country is the same. Skip part 2.

1. **Your gas-leak card.** Write, for your own country only, the steps as this lesson gave them, in
   order, with the number. Great Britain: 0800 111 999. Northern Ireland: 0800 002 001. US: 911 and
   your gas utility's emergency number and its own leak steps, copied from the utility's website or
   your bill. Put it where you'd see it in the dark, next to lesson 1's card.
2. **Your meter.** Find the meter again. In Great Britain or Northern Ireland, find the handle and
   note which way it lies now, without touching it. Note whether the meter is in a cellar or
   basement, because that removes the valve step. In the US, note where the meter is, and what your
   own utility's page says about it.
3. **Four leaks.** For each, write the first three things to do and one thing not to do, and name
   whose advice each comes from.
   1. Great Britain: a smell of gas in the kitchen of a bungalow; the meter is in an outside box
      with its key hanging beside the back door.
   2. Northern Ireland: a smell in a first-floor flat in a converted house; the meter is in the
      hall cupboard, and there's an entry-phone by the flat's door for letting visitors in.
   3. US: a faint smell in the garage of a house in a small town; the car is parked inside with
      the garage door shut.
   4. Great Britain: your builder has just put a spade through a pipe in the garden, and nothing
      can be smelled yet.

Write your answers before you open the checkpoint.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your four leaks.

1. **Great Britain, bungalow.** No switches or flames; doors and windows open; the meter box is
   outside, not in a cellar, so turn off at the handle if you can reach it without going into the
   smell (this course's condition; Cadent notes that a meter box needs a key); then everyone out,
   the call to 0800 111 999 once you're safe, and nobody back in before the engineer's all-clear
   (Cadent, National Gas).[1][2]
2. **Northern Ireland, flat.** Call 0800 002 001 yourself; the meter in the hall cupboard isn't in
   a cellar, so turn it off; put out flames; open doors and windows; leave the entry-phone alone,
   which SSE Airtricity and Phoenix name among the switches not to use ("including door entry
   systems"); and let the engineer in when they come.[3][8]
3. **US, garage.** Leave, taking others; don't start the car or run the garage door's motor,
   because SoCalGas says not to operate motorized equipment or vehicles; call 911 or the utility
   from a safe place, as your own utility's page says (SoCalGas, Con Edison).[5][6]
4. **Great Britain, a struck pipe.** Call 0800 111 999 at once, even with nothing to smell yet
   (National Gas), and keep flames away.[2]

If you gave any US answer an open window, look again at the table: that is the mix-up the callout
exists to prevent.
:::

## For your plan

This lesson adds to the **emergency card**: the full leak steps for your country, with the number,
beside lesson 1's burst-pipe order. It adds to the **map**: the meter, which way the valve handle
lies, and whether it's in a cellar or basement. And it adds to the **stop list**: in a leak, every
switch and flame; in Great Britain, going back in before the engineer's all-clear; and in the US,
bells and phones inside, and the meter unless your own utility tells you otherwise.
