In England in the year to March 2026, fire and rescue services found no smoke alarm at all in 23%
of the dwelling fires they attended, and 24% of the deaths in those fires happened where there was
none.[6] The less familiar figure is the next one: in another 19% of those fires an alarm was there
and didn't go off. This lesson is about where smoke and heat alarms go, what the law in each place
requires, the routine that keeps them working, and what the fire figures say about why they fail.
It also covers the tumble dryer, which England's fire table names more often than any other
appliance once cooking appliances and electrical distribution, the wiring among it, are set aside.

If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.

The callout's "no wiring" matters here: a mains-wired alarm is wired into the house, so fitting or
moving one is an electrician's job, and this lesson says so wherever it comes up. What to do when
one goes off in the night is in the Home Office guide this lesson cites, and this course doesn't
teach it. One section below states law. This

Northern Ireland, and between US states and cities, and they change.

## Where smoke alarms go

This is general information, not legal advice.

The law first, where this course read it. In England, the [Smoke and Carbon Monoxide Alarm
(England) Regulations 2015](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/1693/regulation/4) require a
landlord of a tenancy they cover to make sure "a smoke alarm is equipped on each storey of the
premises on which there is a room used wholly or partly as living accommodation".[1] The regulation
treats a bathroom or lavatory as living accommodation, and counts a hall or landing as a room.[1]
The landlord must also check that each alarm is "in proper working order on the day the tenancy
begins if it is a new tenancy".[1] If you rent in England, that first-day check is the landlord's.
The Regulations bind landlords; this course read no rule that requires an owner-occupier in England
to fit an alarm in an existing home.

In Scotland, every home, owned or rented, has since 1 February 2022 had to meet a tolerable
standard that includes "satisfactory equipment installed for detecting, and for giving warning of,
fire or suspected fire".[10] The [Scottish Government's
factsheet](https://www.gov.scot/publications/fire-and-smoke-alarms-in-scottish-homes/) says what
that means: "Every home in Scotland must have interlinked fire alarms", which is a smoke alarm in
the living room or the room you use most, one in every hallway and landing, and a heat alarm in the
kitchen, all on the ceiling and interlinked. Interlinked, in the factsheet's words, "means if one
goes off, they all go off". And "It is the property owner’s responsibility for meeting the
standard."[2] A heat alarm is the kind Scotland's standard asks for in a kitchen, and England's
advice suggests there, in place of a smoke alarm; the sources this course read don't describe
how one works, so the maker's instructions are the place to look.

This course didn't read the smoke alarm law for Wales or Northern Ireland, or for any US state. In
the US, the [US Fire Administration
(USFA)](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/prevention/home-fires/prepare-for-fire/smoke-alarms/) says "Your city, county or state may require a specific type of alarm. Please check
with your local fire marshal for information on what type of alarm you need."[3]

Then the advice, each country's as its authorities give it.

**In England.** The UK government's [Fire Kills campaign](https://firekills.campaign.gov.uk/detect/):
"Make sure you have at least one on every level of your home and test them at least monthly." It
goes on: "The ideal position is on the ceiling, in the middle of a room, and on the hallway and
landing, so you can hear an alarm throughout your home and so they protect your escape routes." And:
"For extra safety, fit smoke alarms in the bedrooms too". For the kitchen, "Do not put smoke alarms
in or near kitchens or bathrooms where smoke or steam can set them off by accident", and "Heat alarms
can be placed in a kitchen so you may consider installing one there."[5] The Home Office's [2022
guide](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/make-your-home-safe-from-fire/fire-safety-in-the-home-accessible-version),
which applies to England: "Vacuum your smoke alarms every six months to remove dust."[4] On a false
alarm, Fire Kills says: "Never disconnect or take the batteries out of your alarm if it goes off by
mistake unless you are replacing them."[5] A ten-year sealed battery alarm is replaced whole at ten
years.[5]

**In the US.** USFA: "Installing
smoke alarms in every bedroom, outside each separate sleeping area, and on every level of a home",
and "Put smoke alarms on the ceiling or high on the wall." Also "Smoke alarms should be
interconnected." And "Test smoke alarms every month and replace 9-volt smoke alarm batteries at
least once every year." Alarms "need to be replaced 10 years from the manufacture date."[3] The
National Fire Protection Association (NFPA), a US fire safety body, adds in its [tip
sheet](https://www.nfpa.org/-/media/project/storefront/catalog/files/safety-tip-sheets/smokealarmssafetytips.pdf)
that they should be "at least 10 feet (3 meters) from the stove".[8] On a false alarm while cooking,
USFA says "Never take the battery out of your smoke alarm while cooking!" and gives three steps:
"Open a window or door and press the “hush” button.", "Wave a towel at the alarm to clear the air.",
and "Move the entire alarm several feet away from the kitchen or bathroom."[3] That third step
reads, on this course's reading, as advice for a battery alarm: moving a mains-wired one would mean
wiring, and USFA itself says "Only qualified electricians should install hardwired smoke
alarms."[3]

**Scotland's view of replaceable batteries.** "Replaceable batteries cannot be used because the
sensors in the alarm degrade over time and so will not be able to detect heat or smoke", and "There
have been several tragedies over the years where alarms failed because their batteries expired, or
people have removed them."[2] So in Scotland the choice is sealed long-life battery alarms, which
the factsheet says you can fit yourself, or mains-wired ones, which "must be fitted by a qualified
electrician".[2]

The job labels follow. Testing, vacuuming and replacing a battery are **Yours**. Fitting or moving
a sealed battery alarm is **Yours, on conditions**: that it's your home to repair (if you rent, tell
the landlord instead), the maker's instructions, a ceiling you can reach safely (ladders are lesson
15, "Ladders and working at height", and lesson 16, "Stepladders, and what the ladder injury figures
count", works through reaching a landing ceiling), and nothing drilled until lesson
14, "Before you drill: cables, pipes and what's in the wall", has covered what might be behind the surface,
and lesson 19, "Asbestos, lead paint and radon: what not to disturb", what might be in an old
textured ceiling. A mains-wired alarm is **Call someone**: the Home Office says mains alarms "need
to be installed by a qualified electrician".[4] And if fitting one yourself is hard, the same
guide says to "contact your local fire and rescue service for help."[4]

MHCLG's footnote says what "did not reach" can mean: alarms "poorly sited (for example not on the
floor of origin) so the smoke did not reach the detector."[6] Fire Kills puts it more strongly, with
no date or source: "In more than a third of fires in the home, smoke did not reach a smoke alarm in
time to raise the alert and warn the household of the danger."[5] That isn't the same measure as
MHCLG's. On MHCLG's figures, smoke that did not reach the detector was about 9% of all dwelling fires
in 2025/26 (49% of the 19% where an alarm failed to operate, by this course's arithmetic), and
neither source explains the difference.

The table has a second column, for fires in which someone was hurt or killed, and it reads
differently. There, missing and defective batteries came to about 16% of the reasons (7.2% and
8.7%, by this course's addition), smoke that did not reach the detector 8.7%, and fire in an area
with no detector 10%. MHCLG says the commonest category in those fires, as it has been every year
since the year to March 2011, was "other".[6] So across all fires, siting is the biggest reason
alarms stay silent, but in the fires that hurt people the battery reasons count for about as much.
Fire Kills' advice answers the first: "Having more smoke alarms in more places in your home
increases your protection".[5] The battery advice above answers the second.

The American picture, from an older and different count, looks the other way round. [NFPA's fact
sheet](https://content.nfpa.org/-/media/Project/Storefront/Catalog/Files/Research/NFPA-Research/Fact-sheets/SmokeAlarmsFactSheet.pdf)
on US home fires in 2009 to 2013 found that where alarms were present but did not operate, "Almost
half (46%) of the smoke alarms had missing or disconnected batteries. Nuisance alarms were the
leading reason for disconnected smoke alarms", and "Dead batteries caused one-quarter (24%) of the
smoke alarm failures."[7] Different country, different years, and the categories differ in a way
that matters. NFPA counts a fire too small to set off an alarm (12% of US home fires) as its own
category, not as a failure, and its list of reasons has no category for smoke that did not reach the
alarm (its nearest, "Improper installation or placement", is 3%), while MHCLG counts smoke that did not reach the detector as a failure to operate.[7][6]
Neither source compares the two counts, and this course can't say how much of the gap that
difference accounts for. NFPA's sheet has a related pattern of its own: alarms present but not
operating were 8% of the US home fires it counted but 21% of the deaths.[7] What
the two sources together show is that both kinds of failure are real, and that "keep the battery
in" and "put the alarm where the smoke goes" are separate pieces of advice, not one.

How much difference does a working alarm make? The UK government gives two figures, in slightly
different words. Its 2022 guide says "You’re around 8 times more likely to die in a fire if you do
not have a working smoke alarm in your home."[4] Its Fire Kills page says "around 11 times", if you
do not have "any working smoke alarms".[5] Nothing this course read says one figure replaced the
other. Neither gives the years behind its
figure, and MHCLG now works the comparison out on a five-year average.[6] NFPA's
US figure is that "Three of every five home fire deaths resulted from fires in homes with no smoke
alarms (38%) or no working smoke alarms (21%)", for 2009 to 2013.[7]

## The tumble dryer

In England's table the tumble dryer is counted among electrical appliances, and it belongs in the
fire routine. MHCLG's table for England in 2025/26 names tumble dryers as the source of 579
accidental dwelling fires. That's the largest single appliance in its category of other electrical
appliances, and more than any appliance outside its cooking appliances and its wiring and
electrical distribution rows (lesson 6, "Plugs, leads and electrical fires", read the electrical
side of the same table).[6]

This course read no UK official page on dryer care; the routine below is the [US Fire
Administration's flyer](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/downloads/pdf/publications/clothes_dryer_fire_safety_flyer.pdf),
which covers both electric and gas dryers. "Clean the lint filter before and after each cycle.",
don't run the dryer without its filter, and "Make sure that the outdoor vent covering opens when
the dryer is operating."[9] Those are **Yours**. It also says to clean the back of the dryer and
check the vent behind it isn't crushed.[9] That's **Yours, on conditions**: that you can get behind
the dryer without moving a gas one, since, on this course's reading, moving a dryer on a gas
connection is part of the flyer's "Have your dryer installed and serviced by a professional."[9]
That professional work is the rest, and **Call someone**. This course found no official US figure
for dryer fires that it could read.

## What people get wrong

**"Take the battery out if it keeps going off."** Fire Kills and USFA both say never; USFA's
answer to a false alarm while cooking is the hush button, a towel and, for a battery alarm, moving
it further from the kitchen (in a rented home, by asking the landlord).[5][3]

**"Replaceable-battery alarms are fine in Scotland."** Not under Scotland's standard: see "Where
smoke alarms go". The factsheet says they "cannot be used".[2]

**"Smoke alarms last forever."** USFA: replace them "10 years from the manufacture date"; England's
advice (Fire Kills) replaces a ten-year sealed alarm whole at ten years.[3][5]

**"Smoke alarms fail because of flat batteries."** Not across all fires in England's 2025/26
figures, where siting and coverage were 66% of the reasons and batteries about 5%, by this course's
addition. In the fires where someone was hurt, though, batteries were about 16% and siting about
19%. NFPA's older US figures found batteries behind most failures; the two counts are of different
fires, sorted differently.[6][7]

**"A smoke alarm in the kitchen gives the earliest warning."** England's official advice (Fire
Kills) puts smoke alarms nowhere in or near a kitchen and suggests a heat alarm there, and
Scotland's standard puts a heat alarm there instead; NFPA says at
least 10 feet (3 meters) from the stove.[5][2][8]

## Practice

:::exercise The smoke alarm walk-round
Take 20 minutes over this. It is looking, pressing test buttons and writing. This is general
information, not legal advice. If you rent, do the looking and the writing, and tell your landlord
about anything missing or faulty rather than changing anything yourself.

1. **Draw each floor** roughly, and mark every smoke and heat alarm on it, what powers it (sealed
   battery, replaceable battery, or wired to the mains), and any date you can read from the floor.
   Don't take down a mains-wired alarm to look.
2. **Mark the gaps** against your own place's rule and advice: in England, a storey with living
   accommodation and no alarm, and the advice's every level; in Scotland, the living room, every
   hallway and landing, and a heat alarm in the kitchen, interlinked; in the US, USFA's every
   bedroom, outside each sleeping area and every level. Mark any smoke alarm in or near a kitchen or
   bathroom.
3. **Test each one by its button**, as its maker's instructions say, and only if you can reach the
   button from the floor. No ladder or chair for this exercise: lesson 15 comes first. Warn the
   household first. Write down any that don't sound; Fire Kills says a smoke alarm that doesn't
   sound needs a new battery, or possibly a new alarm, and a mains-wired one is an electrician's
   job.
4. **The dryer**, if you have one: is the lint filter in and clean, and can you see from outside
   that the vent flap opens while it runs? Don't pull the dryer out to look behind it for this
   exercise.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your walk-round. For each of these findings, what does it point to, and whose job is it?

**A floor with no alarm:** a gap against the advice everywhere, and in England a gap against the
law too if it's a let home and the floor has living accommodation. Fitting a sealed battery alarm is
**Yours, on conditions**; a mains-wired one is **Call someone**; if you rent, tell the landlord.[1][5]

**A smoke alarm on the kitchen ceiling:** wrongly placed on the advice for England, and in Scotland it
should be a heat alarm. Moving a battery one is **Yours, on conditions**, the same as fitting one;
moving a mains-wired one is **Call someone**; if you rent, tell the landlord.[5][2]

**An alarm that didn't sound:** a battery, or a new alarm (Fire Kills); a sealed alarm is
replaced whole. **Yours** for a new battery; **Yours, on conditions** for replacing a sealed
alarm, the same as fitting one; **Call someone** for a mains one.[5]

**A lint filter caked with fluff:** clean it before and after each cycle from now on (USFA).
**Yours.**[9]
:::

## For your plan

This lesson adds to the **calendar**: every smoke and heat alarm tested monthly, vacuumed every six
months (the Home Office guide, for England), 9-volt batteries changed yearly (USFA), each alarm's replace-by year, and
the dryer's lint filter cleaned before and after each cycle. It adds to the **map**: every alarm, what
powers it, and where the gaps are. It adds to the **stop list**: an alarm with its battery out, and
any mains-wired alarm that needs fitting, moving or replacing.
