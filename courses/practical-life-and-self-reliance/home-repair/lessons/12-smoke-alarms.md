In England in the year to March 2026, fire and rescue services found no smoke alarm at all in 23%
of the dwelling fires they attended, and 24% of the deaths in those fires happened where there was
none.[6] That's the reason most people give for fitting one. The less familiar figure is the next
one: in another 19% of those fires an alarm was there and didn't go off, and the commonest reason
wasn't a flat battery. This lesson is about where smoke and heat alarms go, what the law in each
place requires, the routine that keeps them working, and what the fire figures say about why they
fail. It also covers the one appliance in the house most often behind a fire that isn't a cooker:
the tumble dryer.

This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

## Where smoke alarms go

This is general information, not legal advice.

The law first, where this course read it. In England, the Smoke and Carbon Monoxide Alarm
(England) Regulations 2015 require a landlord of a tenancy they cover to make sure "a smoke alarm is equipped on each storey of the premises on which there is a
room used wholly or partly as living accommodation".[1] In Scotland, "Every home in Scotland must
have interlinked fire alarms": a smoke alarm in the living room or the room you use most, one in
every hallway and landing, and a heat alarm in the kitchen, all on the ceiling and interlinked.[2]
Interlinked, in the factsheet's words, "means if one goes off, they all go off".

Then the advice. It differs between the countries mostly in how many alarms, and each is given as
its authorities give it.

**In the UK.** The government's Fire Kills campaign: "Make sure you have at least one on every level
of your home and test them at least monthly", and "Do not put smoke alarms in or near kitchens or
bathrooms where smoke or steam can set them off by accident."[5] The Home Office's 2022 guide:
"Vacuum your smoke alarms every six months to remove dust."[4] On a false alarm, Fire Kills says:
"Never disconnect or take the batteries out of your alarm if it goes off by mistake unless you are
replacing them."[5] A ten-year sealed battery alarm is replaced whole at ten years.[5]

**In the US.** The US Fire Administration (USFA): "Installing smoke alarms in every bedroom,
outside each separate sleeping area, and on every level of a home", and "Test smoke alarms every
month and replace 9-volt smoke alarm batteries at least once every year." Alarms "need to be
replaced 10 years from the manufacture date."[3] The National Fire Protection Association (NFPA),
a US fire safety body, adds that they should be "at least 10 feet (3 meters) from the stove".[8] On
a false alarm while cooking, USFA says "Never take the battery out of your smoke alarm while
cooking!" and gives three steps: "Open a window or door and press the “hush” button.", "Wave a
towel at the alarm to clear the air.", and "Move the entire alarm several feet away from the kitchen
or bathroom."[3] That third step reads, on this course's reading, as advice for a battery alarm: moving a
mains-wired one would mean wiring, which this course leaves to an electrician.

**Scotland's view of replaceable batteries.** "Replaceable batteries cannot be used because the
sensors in the alarm degrade over time and so will not be able to detect heat or smoke", and "There
have been several tragedies over the years where alarms failed because their batteries expired, or
people have removed them."[2] So in Scotland the choice is sealed long-life battery alarms, which
the factsheet says you can fit yourself, or mains-wired ones, which "must be fitted by a qualified
electrician".[2]

The job labels follow. Testing, vacuuming and replacing a battery are **Yours**. Fitting a sealed
battery alarm is **Yours, on conditions**: the maker's instructions, a ceiling you can reach safely
(ladders are lesson 15, "Ladders and working at height"), and nothing drilled until lesson
14, "Before you drill: cables, pipes and what's in the wall", has covered what might be behind the surface,
and lesson 19, "Asbestos, lead paint and radon: what not to disturb", what might be in an old
textured ceiling. A mains-wired alarm is **Call someone**: the Home Office says mains alarms "need
to be installed by a qualified electrician".[4] And if fitting one yourself is hard, the same
guide says to "contact your local fire and rescue service for help."[4]

:::predict England's fire and rescue services record why a smoke alarm that was fitted didn't go off. Before reading on: in the year to March 2026, what do you think was the commonest reason?

Not batteries. Smoke that didn't reach the detector was 49% of the reasons, and fire in an area
with no detector another 17%: 66% together, both about where the alarms were rather than whether
they worked.[6] Missing and defective batteries came to about 5%.
:::

MHCLG's footnote says what "did not reach" usually means: alarms "poorly sited (for example not on
the floor of origin) so the smoke did not reach the detector."[6] Fire Kills makes the same point
without a date: "In more than a third of fires in the home, smoke did not reach a smoke alarm in
time to raise the alert and warn the household of the danger."[5] The lesson for a householder is
the one in the chart: the alarm you have is only as good as where it is, and the most useful
second alarm is the one on the floor or in the room that has none.

The American picture, from an older and different count, looks the other way round. NFPA's fact
sheet on US home fires in 2009 to 2013 found that where alarms were present but didn't operate,
"Almost half (46%) of the smoke alarms had missing or disconnected batteries. Nuisance alarms were
the leading reason for disconnected smoke alarms", and "Dead batteries caused one-quarter (24%) of
the smoke alarm failures."[7] Different country, different years, different categories: this
course read nothing that explains the gap, and it doesn't offer a reason. What it does show is that
both kinds of failure are real, and that "keep the battery in" and "put the alarm where the smoke
goes" are separate pieces of advice, not one.

How much difference does a working alarm make? The UK government's own figure has moved. Its 2022
guide says "You’re around 8 times more likely to die in a fire if you do not have a working smoke
alarm in your home."[4] Its Fire Kills page today says "around 11 times".[5] Neither gives the
years behind the figure, and MHCLG now works the comparison out on a five-year average.[6] NFPA's
US figure is that "Three of every five home fire deaths resulted from fires in homes with no smoke
alarms (38%) or no working smoke alarms (21%)", for 2009 to 2013.[7]

## The tumble dryer

One appliance that isn't fuel-burning belongs in the fire routine. MHCLG's table for England in
2025/26 names tumble dryers as the source of 579 accidental dwelling fires, the largest single
appliance in its category of other electrical appliances (lesson 6, "Plugs, leads and electrical
fires", read the electrical side of the same table).[6] The US Fire Administration's flyer gives the routine: "Clean the lint
filter before and after each cycle.", don't run the dryer without its filter, clean behind it,
check the vent behind it isn't crushed, and "Make sure that the outdoor vent covering opens when
the dryer is operating."[9] Those are **Yours**. The flyer's "Have your dryer installed and
serviced by a professional."[9] is the rest, and **Call someone**. This course found no official
US figure for dryer fires that it could read.

## What people get wrong

**"Take the battery out if it keeps going off."** Fire Kills and USFA both say never; USFA's
answer to a false alarm while cooking is the hush button, a towel and, for a battery alarm, moving
it further from the kitchen.[5][3]

**"Replaceable-battery alarms are fine in Scotland."** The Scottish Government says they "cannot be
used".[2]

**"Smoke alarms last forever."** USFA: replace them "10 years from the manufacture date"; the UK
advice replaces a ten-year sealed alarm whole at ten years.[3][5]

**"Smoke alarms fail because of flat batteries."** Not in England's 2025/26 figures, where siting
and coverage were 66% of the reasons and batteries about 5%. NFPA's older US figures found
batteries behind most failures; the two counts are of different fires.[6][7]

**"A smoke alarm in the kitchen gives the earliest warning."** The UK advice puts smoke alarms
nowhere in or near a kitchen, and Scotland's standard puts a heat alarm there instead; NFPA says at
least 10 feet (3 meters) from the stove.[5][2][8]

## Practice

:::exercise The smoke alarm walk-round
Take 20 minutes over this. It is looking, pressing test buttons and writing. This is general
information, not legal advice. If you rent, do the looking and the writing, and tell your landlord
about anything missing or faulty rather than changing anything yourself.

1. **Draw each floor** roughly, and mark every smoke and heat alarm on it, what powers it (sealed
   battery, replaceable battery, or wired to the mains), and any date you can read from the floor.
   Don't take down a mains-wired alarm to look.
2. **Mark the gaps** against your own place's rule and advice: in England, a storey with living
   accommodation and no alarm, and the advice's every level; in Scotland, the living room, every
   hallway and landing, and a heat alarm in the kitchen, interlinked; in the US, USFA's every
   bedroom, outside each sleeping area and every level. Mark any smoke alarm in or near a kitchen or
   bathroom.
3. **Test each one by its button**, as its maker's instructions say, and only if you can reach the
   button from the floor. No ladder or chair for this exercise: lesson 15 comes first. Warn the
   household first. Write down any that don't sound; Fire Kills says a smoke alarm that doesn't
   sound needs a new battery, or possibly a new alarm, and a mains-wired one is an electrician's
   job.
4. **The dryer**, if you have one: is the lint filter in and clean, and can you see from outside
   that the vent flap opens while it runs? Don't pull the dryer out to look behind it for this
   exercise.
:::

:::checkpoint Check your walk-round. For each of these findings, what does it point to, and whose job is it?

**A floor with no alarm:** a gap against the advice everywhere, and in England a gap against the
law too if it's a let home and the floor has living accommodation. Fitting a sealed battery alarm is
**Yours, on conditions**; a mains-wired one is **Call someone**; if you rent, tell the landlord.[1][5]

**A smoke alarm on the kitchen ceiling:** wrongly placed on the UK advice, and in Scotland it should
be a heat alarm. Moving a battery one is yours; moving a mains-wired one is an electrician's.[5][2]

**An alarm that didn't sound:** a battery, or a new alarm (Fire Kills). **Yours** for a battery
alarm; **Call someone** for a mains one.[5]

**A lint filter caked with fluff:** clean it before and after each cycle from now on (USFA).
**Yours.**[9]
:::

## For your plan

This lesson adds to the **calendar**: every smoke and heat alarm tested monthly, vacuumed every six
months (the UK advice), 9-volt batteries changed yearly (USFA), each alarm's replace-by year, and
the dryer's lint filter cleaned before and after each cycle. It adds to the **map**: every alarm, what
powers it, and where the gaps are. It adds to the **stop list**: an alarm with its battery out, and
any mains-wired alarm that needs fitting, moving or replacing.
