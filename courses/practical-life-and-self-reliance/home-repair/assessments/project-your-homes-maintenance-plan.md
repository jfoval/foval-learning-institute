Most homes fail slowly, and most of what stops them is decided on an ordinary afternoon: where the
valve is, what gets checked in autumn, which jobs you'll leave alone, and whose number is on the
fridge. Every lesson in this course ended with a few lines headed "For your plan". This project
gathers them into one private document for your own home, in six parts: **a shut-off map**, **three
emergency cards**, **a seasonal calendar**, **a stop list**, **a call list**, and **one repair
written up**.

It takes about six and a half hours of your own time, spread over two or three weeks: an hour and a
half for the map, because it means walking the whole house with a camera and a notebook; half an
hour for the cards; an hour and a half for the calendar, since each item means going back to its
lesson for the interval and the source; an hour for the stop list; three quarters of an hour for the
call list, most of it finding registers by your own route; an hour for the repair write-up; and a
quarter of an hour to mark it all against the rubric. If you did the lesson exercises as you went,
much of it is already written, and it will take less. The repair itself, if you do one, is extra.

:::callout Before you pick up a tool
This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
:::

Parts 4 and 5 ask what the law says where you live. This is general information, not legal advice.
The rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and between US states and
cities, and they change.

## Four rules for every part

**It's private.** Nothing here is submitted anywhere, and nothing in it should carry your name or
address if anyone you haven't chosen could see it. Don't post a photograph of your meter or your
consumer unit or panel online. Keep the plan on paper, or on a device only your household uses.

**This page teaches no method.** Every instruction in it is a lesson's, and where a part touches
water, power, gas, heat, a ladder, asbestos or old paint, it names the lesson and you follow that
lesson as it stands. Nothing here adds a step, and nothing here lifts a condition a lesson set. If a
line below seems to ask for more than its lesson allowed, the lesson wins.

**Nothing is tested in the world.** Don't call a gas emergency number, 911, 105 or any utility's
line to see if it works, and don't contact a trader, book anything or give out your address for this
project. Don't turn the gas meter's handle, press anything a lesson told you to leave, or climb
anything for it: the whole project is done from the floor or the ground.

**Every item carries its label and its source.** The labels are lesson 1's: **Yours**; **Yours, on
conditions**, naming the condition; or **Call someone**. The question is lesson 1's too: law or
advice, whose, and where? Where a lesson said a line was this course's own reading, and not a
source's, your plan says so as well.

## If you rent

You can do the whole project, and earn full marks on every part, without turning, pressing, opening
or changing anything in your home. Wherever a part asks you to try, test or fix something, you look,
write, and **ask your landlord** instead: in writing, so you have the answer, with the date you
asked. "Asked on 3 March, no answer yet" is a complete line. If your landlord agrees, you may do the
things the lessons allowed with that agreement, but nothing in this project requires it. That's
this course's own line, from lesson 1, and not a statement of tenancy law, which this course doesn't
teach. Put the landlord's name and contact details at the top of your call list, as lesson 23
said, since most of these calls are the landlord's to make, and on each card, as Part 2 says.

## If your home has no gas

You still write the gas card in Part 2, for your own country, as lesson 9's exercise said: you'll
meet gas in other people's homes and in the street, and the card is the same. On the map, write "no
gas supply" and move on. Carbon monoxide doesn't come only from gas: lesson 11, "Carbon monoxide and
the alarms that warn you", gives HSE's line that it comes from the incomplete burning of
carbon-based fuels, oil, wood and coal included. So if you have an oil boiler, a wood burner or an
open fire, it goes on the map, its service and its chimney go on the calendar, and lesson 11's rules
and advice for your place say whether it needs a carbon monoxide alarm. If nothing in your home burns anything, write that on the map, and the carbon monoxide items
in the calendar become "none in this home", with that reason.

## Which country

Do the whole project for where you live, and write it at the top: your nation of the UK (England,
Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland), or your US state and city. The UK and the US get the same six
parts, side by side, and each part below says where they differ. Two rules from the course hold
throughout. **Never put the other country's steps on your card.** And where the course read nothing
for your place (a nation's rule it didn't read, a US state or city outside the five places lesson 22 read), write "not
read by this course", and the question you'd put to the body lesson 22's exercise had you find.

## What you keep

Six things, in one folder or one notebook:

1. The shut-off map, with photographs.
2. Three emergency cards, each on one side of a card, where you'd see them in the dark.
3. A seasonal calendar: monthly, spring and autumn, yearly, and dates of their own.
4. The stop list, each job with the rule behind it.
5. The call list, each trade with its register and the check you'd make at the door.
6. One repair written up, or a written plan for the likeliest one.

## Part 1: the shut-off map

From lesson 1, "How a house works, and where it switches off", with a layer from most lessons
since. Walk the house in daylight with a phone camera and a notebook. The map can be a sketch of
each floor, a set of labelled photographs, or a list of places written so someone else could follow
it in the dark; what matters is that someone who has never been in your home could use it.

**The three controls.** These are the core of the map.

1. **Water.** The stop tap (UK) or main shut-off valve (US): where it is, in words; a photograph;
   which way closes it and what kind of handle it has; whether it turned freely; and how long the
   kitchen cold tap took to run dry. Those last two come from lesson 1's exercise, done by lesson
   1's rules, which include stopping and calling a plumber if it's stiff, and not turning anything
   in a home with fire sprinklers until you know which valve is which. In the US, add where the
   outside valve at the property line is, if you know, and whether you could reach the inside valve
   with the basement flooded, lesson 1's question.
2. **Power.** The consumer unit (UK) or panel (US), drawn from the front with its door open, as in
   lesson 5's exercise: the main switch or breaker, each RCD or GFCI and what it covers, and each
   breaker or fuse with its label. In the US, add every GFCI receptacle you found. Mark any circuit
   that has no label. Nothing comes off and nothing is tested with a tester or volt stick.
3. **Gas.** The meter; in the UK, its handle and which way it lies now, untouched; and whether the
   meter is in a cellar or basement, which lesson 9, "Gas: the smell and the valve", says changes
   what you do in a leak. In the US, where the meter is and what your own utility's page says about
   it.

**The rest of the house**, as your home has it. Each line names the lesson that put it there.

- Isolation valves under taps and toilets, and which taps are washer taps and which ceramic (lesson
  3, "Taps and toilets").
- Each trap and how it opens (lesson 4, "Water out: traps, vents and blocked drains"), and every pipe
  that runs where nobody looks (lesson 2, "Water in: supply, leaks and frozen pipes").
- Which heating system you have, where its pressure gauge and the filling controls the maker shows
  you are, or where the filter is, and the pressure figure from your boiler's manual (lesson 7,
  "Heating: boilers, radiators and furnaces").
- The water heater or cylinder, its type, and where the relief valve's pipe or the tundish is
  (lesson 8, "Hot water: scalds, legionella and the tank").
- Every fuel-burning appliance and its room, and every carbon monoxide alarm (lesson 11); every
  smoke and heat alarm and what powers it (lesson 12, "Smoke alarms: where they go, and why they stay
  silent").
- Cables and pipes you've found in walls you've scanned (lesson 14, "Before you drill: cables, pipes
  and what's in the wall").
- Where a ladder can stand at each side of the house, where any overhead lines run, and which walls
  have a strong resting point rather than a plastic gutter or glass (lesson 15, "Ladders and working
  at height"); which rooms and landings have a level, clear spot for a stepladder, and which high
  jobs don't (lesson 16, "Stepladders, and what the ladder injury figures count").
- Where each downpipe discharges, the ground level at each wall, and any gutter, pipe or roof that
  might be asbestos cement (lesson 17, "Keeping water out: roof, gutters, ground and walls").
- Every extractor fan, trickle vent, wall vent and air brick, and whether each works (lesson 18,
  "Damp inside: condensation, mould, ventilation and rising damp").

**If you rent:** photograph and describe what you can see, and don't turn the stop tap. Ask the
landlord where it is if you can't find it, whether it turns freely, and when it was last tried, and
write down the answer. If the consumer unit's circuits aren't labelled, ask the landlord for the
list rather than switching breakers off to find out.

**If you can't find a control:** write that down, with where you looked. The course read no source
on a stop tap or valve nobody can find, so asking a plumber (UK) or your water utility, whose number
is on its website or your bill (US), is this project's suggestion, not a lesson's. A control nobody
can find is the first thing the plan has taught you.

## Part 2: the emergency cards

From lessons 1, 5 and 9, with a line from lesson 23. Three cards, each on one side, written for your
own country only and kept where you'd see them in the dark: by the stop tap, by the consumer unit or
panel, or on the fridge. Copy each from its lesson, in the lesson's order, with whose advice each
line is and the numbers you'd need. Don't shorten a condition to save space: "if you can do it
safely" and "not if the meter is in a cellar or basement" are the parts that keep people safe.

**Card A: a burst pipe.** Lesson 1, under "When a pipe bursts". In the UK, the Met Office's order,
with the two things CCW adds. In the US, the main valve off, then a plumber, as lesson 1's exercise
put it, with WSSC's step labelled as one Maryland utility's advice, and your own water utility's
number for the valve outside. In either country, add lesson 1's condition for the electrics, marked
as this course's own reading: the consumer unit or panel only if the box and the wall around it are
dry and you can reach it without standing in water or touching anything wet, and otherwise keep
clear and call an electrician. Add a plumber's number: a WaterSafe plumber in the UK, or a licensed
plumber in the US (lesson 2).

**Card B: a smell of gas.** Lesson 9, "Gas: the smell and the valve", and its exercise's part 1:
the steps for your own country, in order, with the number. Great Britain and Northern Ireland have
different numbers and a different first move, and the US differs from both; lesson 9's table, "The
two side by side", shows where. In the US, copy your own utility's leak steps and emergency number
from its website or your bill, because lesson 9 says your utility's page decides. Card B goes on
the wall whether or not you have gas.

**Card C: a device that keeps tripping.** Lesson 5, "Electricity: what trips, and why". In the UK,
first whether it's a power cut, by the checks the network operators give, with 105 for a power cut.
Then the reset from the front of the box with the load off, as your country's source in lesson
5 gives it. Then the stop: a device that trips again with the load off, or keeps tripping, is left
off and an electrician is called; the one-reset line is this course's reading, and your card says
so. Lesson 1's dry-box condition goes on this card too.

**Under each card**, lesson 23's line for your country: in the UK, "urgent repairs I call for can't
be cancelled; anything extra sold on the visit can"; in the US, "no cash, no wire, no signing over
the insurance check".

**If you rent:** the landlord's number goes at the top of each card, beside the emergency numbers,
not instead of them.

## Part 3: the seasonal calendar

Four sections: **monthly**; **spring and autumn** (or twice a year); **yearly**; and **dates of their
own**, such as replace-by years and a date a year from now. Each item gets its interval, its label
and its source, with where that source applies, all copied from the lesson named. Where a lesson said
no source gave an interval, write your own and mark it "my interval". Adapt it to your home and
climate: an item that doesn't apply stays on the list with "not in this home" and the reason (no
chimney, no septic tank, no frost), so a reader can see you considered it.

Many items split by country. Where a lesson gave the UK and the US different intervals or different
sources, use your own country's and don't average them. The University of Georgia's *Home
Maintenance Checklist*, which lesson 1 named as the checklist this project borrows from, is American: use its
lines where a lesson used them, with its name.

Two cautions travel with the calendar. **A date on the calendar is not permission for the job.** For
septic pumping, a service, a chimney sweep or a gutter you can't reach by lesson 15's rules, the date
is yours and the work is someone else's, as lesson 4 put it: label it **Call someone**, and the line
on your calendar is "book it". And **an item that needs a ladder, a panel or a manual is done by
that lesson's rules or not at all**: the relief valve by your heater's own manual (lesson 8), the
test buttons from the floor (lessons 11 and 12), the filter by your unit's size and your furnace's
manual (lesson 7).

**If you rent:** the calendar is the same list, and each item that touches anything reads "ask the
landlord", or "the landlord's", with the date you'll ask. The items that are looking, such as
watching the downpipes in rain, the walk-rounds and looking behind furniture, stay yours.

## Part 4: the stop list

From lessons 5, 6, 8, 10, 19 and 22, and the table at the top of lesson 23, "Hiring a tradesperson,
and when to stop", which gathers the Call someone jobs from lessons 1 to 21. Add the stop lines from
every other lesson's "For your plan" that could arise in your home. For each job, write four things:

1. **The job**, in your home's terms ("the textured ceiling in the hall", not "asbestos").
2. **The reason**, as its lesson gave it.
3. **What kind of rule it is**: law, advice, or this course's own line. Lesson 23 found all three
   kinds in its table, and asked you to label them honestly: "it's illegal" is a different claim from
   "every body I read advises against it", and a stop list that says the first where the lesson said
   the second will be believed less, not more.
4. **Whose, and where**: the body, and your nation or state.

At least these belong on it:

- Every row of lesson 23's table that could arise in your home, from gas work beyond the user
  instructions to a cistern with no maker's leaflet.
- For your nation or state, the jobs lesson 22 found reserved to a registered or licensed trade, and
  the notifiable or permitted jobs you'd otherwise have done without telling anyone. If lesson 22
  didn't read your place, write the question you'd ask your building control body or building
  department instead.
- From lesson 19, "Asbestos, lead paint and radon: what not to disturb", your home's own items: each
  material that might be asbestos, marked "might be", and old paintwork by your country's dates, with
  the job that would disturb each.
- From lesson 11, a carbon monoxide alarm going off, which goes straight to First Aid and CPR lesson
  14's response.

**If you rent:** the list is the same, and lesson 1's line adds one more: anything that changes the
home, unless it's your home to repair or your landlord agrees.

## Part 5: the call list

From lesson 23's exercise, "Your call list", with the lines lessons 2, 5, 10, 19, 21 and 22 added.
Reading and writing only: don't contact anyone, and **find every register yourself**, never by a
link or number a trader gave you, which is the own-route rule lesson 23 took from Digital Literacy.

For each trade you might need, write the register, scheme or licensing body that covers it where you
live, its web address found by your own route, and the one check you'd make at the door. At least:

- **A gas engineer, or your fuel's equivalent.** In Great Britain, the Gas Safe Register and its
  check, as lesson 10, "Gas: the law and the engineer", showed it: the card, the categories on its
  back, and the Register's own service; add the Register's helpline and HSE's Gas Safety Advice Line
  from lesson 10. In the US, a qualified professional, as lesson 10 put it, and whatever licence
  your state or city requires, which lesson 22 read for some places. A home with no gas writes its
  own fuel's trade, or "none".
- **An electrician**, through GOV.UK's list of competent person schemes in England, or your state's
  or county's licence lookup in the US (lesson 23), and, for anything lesson 22 says to ask about,
  your building control body or building department and, in the US, your state's licensing board.
- **A plumber**: a WaterSafe approved plumber in the UK, a licensed plumber in the US (lesson 2).
- **Anyone your home's age sends you to** (lesson 19): in the UK, your council's environmental health
  officer for asbestos questions, and in Great Britain, for removing sprayed coatings, lagging or
  asbestos insulating board, a contractor licensed by HSE (lessons 19 and 23); in the US, your state's asbestos and radon programmes, EPA's National Lead Information Center, and,
  for paint in a home built before 1978, a lead-safe certified contractor found through EPA's search.
- **A chimney sweep**, if you have a chimney in use: in the UK, a sweep from HETAS's scheme (lesson
  23); in either country, the check lesson 11 gives, HETAS's line, to ask to see identification.
- **A pest controller**, checked on the trade association's own site in the UK or your state's in
  the US (lesson 21).
- **Your networks and utilities**: 105 for a power cut in the UK (lesson 5), and in the US your water
  utility and gas utility, with their numbers from their own websites or your bills (lessons 1 and 9).

Then, from lesson 23: your country's cancellation period for a contract signed at home; the deposit
rule where you live, marked as law or advice (in England it's Citizens Advice's advice; in
California it's law; elsewhere in the US, ask your state consumer agency whether your state limits
deposits); and three questions you'd ask every trader before you accept a quote.

**If you rent:** the landlord's name goes at the top. You still write the rest, because it's how
you'll know whether the person who arrives is who they say.

## Part 6: one repair, written up

Choose one job that a lesson from 2 to 8, or lesson 20, labelled **Yours** or **Yours, on
conditions**, where you meet the conditions. The candidates, each by its lesson's method and nothing
else:

- **Lesson 2:** thawing a frozen pipe you can reach, by your own country's order in that lesson.
- **Lesson 3:** a washer tap, by Southern Water's method; a ceramic-disc tap or a US faucet, with the
  maker's instructions for that tap; a showerhead leaking at the pipe stem, by EPA's advice; a US
  toilet flapper, the one made for your toilet; a UK cistern, only with its maker's instructions.
- **Lesson 4:** a blocked sink with only water and waste in it, by WaterSafe's steps 1 to 7.
- **Lesson 5:** a tripped device reset from the front of the box with the load off.
- **Lesson 6:** a plug fuse replaced with one of the same rating, on ESF's and the maker's
  conditions.
- **Lesson 7:** bleeding a radiator, by its makers' steps; topping up a sealed system, only by your
  boiler's own user instructions and every condition lesson 7 set; a furnace filter, by your unit's
  size and your furnace's manual.
- **Lesson 8:** on a US storage water heater, the T&P valve check, only by your own heater's manual.
  In a UK home, lesson 8 gives no householder's check; a dripping tundish is **Call someone**.
- **Lesson 20:** filling and repainting a small patch of plain plastered wall you can reach from
  the floor, after lesson 20's first step, reading the wall, and lesson 19's check of the paint's age.

Write it under five headings:

1. **Diagnosis.** What was wrong, how you know, and which lesson's diagnosis you used: which kind of
   tap, which half of SCE's list, cold at the top or the bottom, what the wall told you.
2. **Isolation.** What you turned off or took out of the job first, as the lesson said: the water at
   the stop tap or an isolation valve, the plug out of the socket, the heating off and the radiators
   cool, the load off the circuit.
3. **The fix.** The steps, each attributed to the lesson's source or the maker's instructions you
   used. If your fix makes dust, lesson 13's exercise step 2 is its plan; if it needs a drill, lesson
   14's order is.
4. **The check.** How you knew it worked: the tap run, the drip stopped, the radiator
   warm at the top, the pressure back to your boiler's figure.
5. **The stop point.** What would have made you stop and call someone, in the lesson's words: a stop
   tap that stiffens, a sink that still backs up after the trap, a device that trips again with the
   load off, a pressure that needs topping up more than twice a year, a crack you can't account for.

**If nothing needs fixing, or you rent**, write the same five headings as a plan for the repair
your home is likeliest to need, and label it "plan". If you rent, step 0 is asking your landlord. A
plan earns the same marks as a repair done.

Not eligible, whatever your skill: anything a lesson labelled **Call someone**, anything on your
stop list, and anything the course read no method for, such as a UK cistern without its leaflet,
sealant round a bath or a sticking door (lessons 3 and 20).

## If something doesn't fit your home

- **You live in a flat.** Gutters, roofs, the outside walls and often the main valve belong to the
  building. On the map and the calendar, write who manages each (the landlord, the managing agent,
  the freeholder or the building's owner) and how you'd report a fault to them.
- **You have fire sprinklers.** Lesson 1's rule: don't turn a water valve until you know which feeds
  the plumbing and which the sprinklers, and ask a plumber to show you.
- **Someone at home depends on powered medical equipment.** Don't press any RCD or GFCI test button;
  lesson 5 sends the test to your electrician or landlord.
- **You live outside the places the course read.** Use your own government's and services' pages,
  found by your own route, and write "not read by this course" wherever that's true.

## Rubric

Mark each part yourself, or with someone you trust, at one of three levels.

- **The shut-off map.** Full marks: all three controls found, photographed and described so a
  stranger could find them in the dark, or noted as absent ("no gas supply"), or, for one nobody can
  find, where you looked and whom you've asked; the stop tap's stiffness and timing recorded, or, if you
  rent, what you asked the landlord and when; the box drawn with every RCD or GFCI and label; and the rest of the house as
  your home has it. Partly there: the three controls found or accounted for, with the timing, the drawing or most of
  the rest missing. Not yet: a control missing with no note of where you looked, or a step on the
  map that a lesson ruled out.
- **The emergency cards.** Full marks: three cards, each for your own country, in its lesson's order
  and with its conditions, every line attributed, the numbers right, lesson 23's line under each, and
  each card where it would be seen. Partly there: the right steps with an attribution or a condition
  missing, or a card not yet up. Not yet: a wrong number, a card missing, or steps out of the lesson's
  order in a way that matters.
- **The calendar.** Full marks: every item in the table, each with its interval, label and source,
  your own intervals marked as yours, and anything not in your home explained. Partly there: every
  item present, with labels or sources missing in places. Not yet: items missing, or a Call someone
  job written as yours to do.
- **The stop list.** Full marks: every job that could arise in your home, each with its reason, law,
  advice or this course's own line said correctly, and whose and where, consistent with your nation
  or state. Partly there: the right jobs, with the kind of rule missing or wrong in places. Not yet:
  a job that could arise in your home and that lesson 23's table or lesson 22 puts on it missing, or
  advice called law.
- **The call list.** Full marks: every trade your home needs, each with its register found by your
  own route and one check at the door; the cancellation period, the deposit rule marked law or
  advice, and three questions. Partly there: the trades and registers, with checks or the contract
  rules missing. Not yet: no registers, or a register reached through a trader's own link or number.
- **The repair.** Full marks: an eligible job, all five headings, every step attributed to its lesson
  or the maker, and a stop point in the lesson's words; or the same as a plan. Partly there: the
  right order, with an attribution or the stop point missing. Not yet: a job that isn't eligible, a
  step no lesson gave, or no isolation where the lesson required one.

A part, or an item in one, that you skipped for safety, or because you rent, counts as complete if
you've written down the reason.

The project passes when no part is at "Not yet" and nothing on the list below appears.

**Any one of these fails the project outright, whatever else is right**, because each is a step a
lesson said can hurt someone:

- any gas task beyond what the appliance's user instructions give the user, written as something
  you'll do (lessons 1 and 10);
- the other country's gas-leak steps on your card (lesson 9);
- taking the cover off a consumer unit or panel, or proving a wire dead with a volt stick, written
  as a step (lessons 1 and 5; the door in front of the switches is not the cover);
- testing a water heater's relief valve without your own heater's manual (lesson 8);
- drilling, cutting, sanding, scraping, breaking or sampling anything that might be asbestos, or
  dry-sanding, burning or torching old paint, as a step (lessons 13, 14 and 19);
- a generator anywhere but outside, at least 20 feet (about 6 metres) from the house with the
  exhaust facing away, charcoal burned indoors, or a gas oven used for heat
  (lesson 11);
- a ladder resting on a plastic gutter or glass, or any job within 6 m of an overhead line (lesson
  15);
- a fogger for cockroaches, or sweeping or vacuuming rodent droppings (lesson 21);
- paying a contractor in cash, or, in the US, paying by wire transfer or gift card, or signing your
  insurance check over to a contractor (lesson 23).

## What this project is not

It isn't a survey of your home, a trade qualification or a legal opinion, and it doesn't make any
job on it safe to do. It's the plan you'd want to have made before the night the ceiling bulges:
where the valve is, what you'll leave alone, and who you'll call, written down on a quiet
afternoon, which is where this course began.

lesson 1; the supply, the leak tests and frozen pipes in lesson 2; taps and toilets in lesson 3;
traps and drains in lesson 4; the devices, the reset and the trip rule in lesson 5; plugs, leads and
England's landlord reports in lesson 6; heating, pressure and filters in lesson 7; hot water and the
relief valve in lesson 8; each country's gas-leak steps in lesson 9; the gas law and the engineer in
lesson 10; carbon monoxide, generators and chimneys in lesson 11; smoke alarms in lesson 12; tools
and dust in lesson 13; drilling in lesson 14; ladders and stepladders in lessons 15 and 16; the
outside of the house in lesson 17; damp inside in lesson 18; asbestos, lead paint and radon in lesson
19; walls and paint in lesson 20; pests in lesson 21; reserved work in lesson 22; and checking,
paying and cancelling in lesson 23. The project adds no new source.
