# Outline: Home Repair and Maintenance

*Stage 2, 2026-09-25. Built from `research/SOURCES.md` (the synthesis read in full; Parts A to D read
for every lesson below), after `npm run taught`, which shows nineteen earlier courses and 190
lessons, all written. Sequenced for how the ideas build and for where the danger is. **Not a
standards 3.4 domain as a whole** (`sensitive_domain: false` stays), but it states law in two
countries and four UK nations and gives instructions that can hurt someone if wrong. So every legal
statement, phone number and safety step is fact-checked against the primary, not against SOURCES,
and **lesson 22, the licensing question, gets the neutrality audit** because occupational licensing
is an economics question (standards 3.4 lists economics; SOURCES opening).*

## How many lessons, and why

**Sixteen.** SOURCES suggested sixteen and warned against more (open concern 1). This outline keeps
the number and changes what is in the lessons:

- **Repairs are taught where their mechanism is taught, not in two "common repairs" lessons at the
  end.** SOURCES essential 7 says most common repairs are diagnosis first, and diagnosis needs the
  mechanism: a radiator cold at the top is air and cold at the bottom is sludge, which is only
  obvious once you know how a sealed system works. So the dripping tap and the running toilet sit in
  lesson 3, after the supply in lesson 2, the blocked trap in lesson 4 with the drains, resetting a trip in lesson
  5 with the protective devices, and bleeding a radiator in lesson 7 with the heating. The two
  suggested repairs lessons are gone, and their slots go to damp (split in two) and to walls and
  paint.
- **The building envelope is two lessons, not one.** The suggested lesson 7 carried the roof,
  gutters, grading, four kinds of damp, condensation, mould, ventilation and the rising damp dispute.
  That is past two hours. **Lesson 17** keeps water out (roof, gutters, ground, walls, penetrating
  damp); **lesson 18** deals with damp inside (condensation, mould, ventilation, rising damp).
- **Carbon monoxide gets its own lesson, with smoke alarms, after gas.** Gas (lessons 9 and 10) carries the
  leak drill and the law, which is enough for a sitting. CO comes from every fuel, not only gas, and
  the course's point about it (an alarm is not the protection; servicing is) pairs naturally with the
  smoke-alarm routine, so lessons 11 and 12 are "the alarms and what they're not".
- **Tools and ladders move before the envelope**, because clearing a gutter is a ladder job, and
  before hazards and decorating, because the before-you-drill routine is where asbestos first has to
  stop someone.
- **Decorating is lesson 20, after the hazards.** Lead paint is taught once, in lesson 19, and lesson
  20 applies it (overcoat or strip) rather than reteaching it.
- **The seasonal checklist is the project, not a lesson** (SOURCES open concern 1). Every lesson
  contributes its routine items and the project assembles them.

## What is deliberately left out, and said so in lesson 1

- **Gas work of any kind**, beyond the tasks the appliance's user instructions give the user (decision
  6). No boiler repair, no hob connection, no pipework.
- **Wiring procedures of any kind.** The course explains which electrical work the law lets a
  householder do, and where, and teaches none of it (decision 7; SOURCES open concern 2). It stops at
  the front of the consumer unit or panel: switching, resetting, testing RCDs and GFCIs, plug fuses
  and plug-in RCDs.
- **Opening a consumer unit or panel**, testing a temperature and pressure relief valve, adjusting an
  electric water heater's thermostat behind its panels, repressurising past the maker's figure, and
  any method for asbestos or for removing textured coating (safety-critical guidance 2, 5, 9).
- **Structural work, extensions, loft conversions, roofing and chimney work**, planning permission
  and the Party Wall Act. Named once as needing permission and a professional; not taught (gate
  G-L11).
- **Fitting a lock.** Personal Safety's outline said this course owns it, but Stage 1 read no source
  on fitting or maintaining a lock (the only trace is a City & Guilds unit title). Not taught unless
  gate G-N3 closes; lesson 1 says the course did not research it and points to Personal Safety lesson
  5 for which locks and lights matter. **For the orchestrator:** a `docs/QUEUE.md` research-gap line.
- **Wallpapering, tiling, flooring, sticking doors and silicone sealant.** Not researched, or read
  only as a line in a checklist (gate G-R1). Wallpaper is named once, as a stepladder task HSE says
  needs a handhold justified.
- **Woodworking, fixings in depth and making things.** Tools and Making Things (Term 23) owns them.
- **Energy policy, grants and savings in pounds.** Energy: How the World Is Powered (Term 9) owns
  policy; the Energy Saving Trust's savings figures are tied to a price period and are left out

- **Landlord and tenant law**, including Awaab's Law and the HHSRS (SOURCES open concern 6; gate
  G-W9). One pointer for renters where damp, gas checks and alarms come up; the landlord duties the

- **Insurance and scams in general.** Personal Finance Fundamentals lessons 8 and 10 own them

- **First aid.** First Aid and CPR lessons 2, 10 and 14 own shock, electrical burns and CO poisoning.
- **Floods, storms and power cuts beyond the house side.** Emergency Preparedness (Term 24).
- **Wasp nests** (gate G-R4), **appliance repair**, **septic system design**, **pools**, **solar

## Eighteen decisions, settled here so no lesson re-opens them

**1. Honest objectives, and exercises that cannot hurt anyone.** Objectives use verbs a reader can
be tested on from a page: **explain, identify, diagnose, compare, apply, decide, state, plan,
sort**. No objective says "rewire", "install", "repair a boiler" or "remove". `course.yaml` outcomes
are reworded to match (done at this stage). Exercises are look-and-list walk-rounds, measurements,
written plans, calculations, sorts and the few safe hands-on tasks the sources give a householder:
turning the stop tap slowly and back (WaterSafe), the meter test and dye test (EPA WaterSense),
pressing the RCD or GFCI test button and resetting it (ESF; CPSC), reading a boiler pressure gauge,
bleeding a radiator if one needs it (Worcester Bosch, Vaillant), testing a smoke or CO alarm by its
button, setting a ladder at 1 in 4 on firm level ground without climbing it. **No exercise asks a
reader to:**

- operate the gas emergency control valve or meter valve to practise, or do any gas work;
- open a consumer unit, panel, socket or fitting, or use a volt stick or tester on anything;
- test a temperature and pressure relief valve, or adjust a water heater thermostat;
- drill, cut, sand or scrape anything that might be asbestos or old paint, or take a sample;
- climb a ladder for the exercise, go on a roof, or clear a gutter from height;
- touch rodent droppings, set poison, or use a fogger;
- change anything in a home the reader rents without the landlord's agreement (the course's own
  inference: no tenancy law was read; say it as the course's line).

The drafter writes these limits into each exercise, not into a footnote. An exercise that turns
something (a stop tap, an RCD) says in the exercise what to do if it doesn't behave: a stiff stop
tap is not forced ("Turn it on and off slowly, never force it", WaterSafe; call a plumber); an RCD
that won't reset is left off and an electrician called.

*Amended 2026-09-25, at lesson 7's Stage 4 (finding R2, option (a)):* the safe hands-on tasks also
include **topping up a sealed heating system's pressure, only by the boiler's own user instructions,
and only where those instructions give the user a method** (Worcester Bosch's *System Filling Guide*
and Ideal's page are the two read; Ideal's is for combis whose filling loop or controls are clearly
identifiable under or built into the boiler). Bleeding, already on the list, loses pressure on both
makers' account, so an exercise that allows bleeding needs the top-up to leave a reader with a safe
gauge. The exercise writes in the conditions: a filling method for the user in the boiler's own
manual, filling controls the reader can clearly identify, the boiler off and cold, and a home that is
the reader's to repair or a landlord who agrees (a renter asks the landlord). A filling loop anywhere
else, or controls the reader can't clearly identify, is for the installer or a heating engineer.
Nothing here opens the boiler beyond a panel the maker's user guide tells the user to remove.

**2. Jurisdictions named exactly.** SOURCES' largest single source of error is "England is not the
UK, and Wales is not England" (access problem 8; Part C opening). Rules:

- **Building regulations and electrical work:** "England", "Wales", "Scotland", "Northern Ireland",
  each by name. "England and Wales" only where both versions say the same thing (reg 3, s35, s36,
  Schedule 3), never for notifiable electrical work.
- **Gas law:** "Great Britain" for the 1998 Regulations (reg 3 extends to England, Wales and
  Scotland); "Northern Ireland" for the 2004 Regulations. Gas Safe Register covers "Great Britain,
  Northern Ireland, the Isle of Man and Guernsey" (HSE); the course's "the UK" for the Register is
  within that. Emergency numbers: Great Britain and Northern Ireland only (gate G-G4).
- **Workplace ladder law:** "Great Britain" (WAHR 2005 extent E+W+S).
- **Consumer law:** the Consumer Rights Act 2015 extends to the UK; the Consumer Contracts
  Regulations are read as UK regulations; Citizens Advice's page is England's.
- **"The UK"** only where the source covers the UK (UKHSA's lead ban date; the Register's cover).
- **US:** federal rules as federal (OSHA, EPA's RRP rule, the FTC's Cooling-Off Rule); anything
  else by named state or city. **A lesson never says "in the US you can do your own wiring"**
  (SOURCES open concern 3; Part C (e) 8). Five places were read: California, Texas, Florida,
  Washington, New York City. A lesson may use two or three as worked examples and always sends the
  reader to their own local building department or state licensing board.

**3. The UK and the US side by side, and neither called wrong.** Where the official advice differs,
each country's advice is taught as its authorities give it, and the course's voice calls neither
wrong, better or behind. The cases: the gas-leak steps (ventilate and valve off, against leave and
touch nothing); thawing a frozen pipe (stop tap off first, against keep the tap open); CO alarm siting
(the appliance room, against every level and outside sleeping areas); radon action levels (200
Bq/m³, against 4 pCi/L); ladder numbers (1 m and 75°, against 3 feet and a quarter of the working
length); smoke alarm routines; cancellation periods; deposits (guidance in England, law in
California). Rules:

- **Differences go in a table or an "In the UK / In the US" set, never interleaved through the
  prose.** Where they agree, one sentence says so, because agreement is information too (the shared
  gas core: no flames, no switches).
- **A UK learner is never given a US step and a US learner is never given a UK step** in anything
  that could be acted on in an emergency. The gas lesson says plainly: follow your own country's.
- **Where a reason for a difference is not in a source, the lesson says so** and does not supply
  one (decision 13).
- **Names and units both**, first use in each lesson: stop tap (stopcock) and main shut-off valve;
  consumer unit and panel; RCD and GFCI; emulsion and latex; °C and °F; bar and psi; metres and
  feet; Bq/m³ and pCi/L. **Spelling:** "mould" in the course's prose (it's a UK-spelling institute),
  "mold" inside US quotations and titles.
- Numbers are never merged across countries: a US emergency-department ladder figure is never set
  beside a UK figure the course doesn't have (gate G-H1).

**4. A safety callout on every lesson, word for word, directly after the opening paragraph(s), and
the build may later enforce it.** The pattern is First Aid's (`FIRST_AID_SAFETY`) and Personal
Safety's (`PERSONAL_SAFETY`) in `scripts/build.mjs`. The text:

> :::callout Before you pick up a tool
> This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas in Great Britain, open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter if you can do it safely (not if the meter is in a cellar or basement), and call 0800 111 999; in Northern Ireland the number is 0800 002 001. In the US, leave straight away and call 911 or your gas utility once you're outside. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. Isolate the water or power before you open anything up, and if a breaker, RCD or GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician. Which jobs the law leaves to a registered or licensed trade differs between England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, and it changes.
> :::

Sources for each clause: "education, not ... legal advice" (standards 2.5; Part C (e) 2); "no gas
work and no wiring" (decisions 6 and 7; SOURCES safety-critical 1 and 2); the Great Britain steps
(Cadent: "Open doors and windows to ventilate the property", "Turn off the gas at the mains tap",

002 001"; its "unless the meter is located in a cellar or basement"); the US steps (Con Edison, "Leave

tripping" (ESF FAQ: "If the RCD in your fusebox keeps tripping ... You should get a registered
electrician to investigate"; GOV.UK's danger sign, breakers that "trip for no obvious reasons");
"leave it off" (the course's reading of ESF, and SOURCES synthesis safety-critical 2: a device that
trips again "is not forced, taped or held on"; not quoted from a source, so it stays as the course's

number in it is re-read on the network's own page on the day lesson 1 is drafted** (gate G-N1).

**For the orchestrator:** the check goes beside `FIRST_AID_SAFETY` as `HOME_REPAIR_SAFETY`, keyed on

**The callout is not the lesson.** Lesson 9 teaches the full leak steps for each country, including

**5. The course's question: law or advice, whose, and where?** Memory added the sample, Mental
Fitness what the comparison group got, Nutrition "instead of what?", Cooking "what is this rule
protecting?", First Aid "which guideline, which year, and how strongly?", Personal Safety "measured
on whom, and measured as what?". **Household advice fails in one way more than any other: a rule
from one place, one kind of body or one kind of building is repeated as if it bound everyone
everywhere.** "Gas work is illegal" (it binds businesses, and competence binds everyone). "Kitchen
electrics are notifiable" (in Wales, not England). "1 to 1.5 bar" (two makers' figure for sealed
systems). "Test the T&P valve every year" (a US extension checklist; no maker's manual read). The
ladder rules (written for workplaces). "Never sweep droppings" (US guidance against a US virus,
good practice anywhere by the course's reading). Every rule a lesson gives carries, in the sentence:
**who says it** (a statute, a regulator's code or guidance, an official body's advice, a maker's
instruction for its own product, a trade body, an extension service), **whether it is law or
advice**, and **where it applies**. Introduced in lesson 1, used in every lesson, and made the whole
subject of lesson 22.

**The job labels, taught in lesson 1 and used in every lesson.** Every job the course names carries
one of three labels, in the prose, not as a badge:

- **Yours**: the sources put it in a householder's hands (turn off the stop tap, bleed a radiator,
  change a washer, test and reset an RCD, replace a plug fuse with the same rating, test an alarm,
  clean a small patch of mould).
- **Yours, on conditions**: allowed if a stated condition is met, and the condition is named: the
  appliance's user instructions (gas); notifying building control before starting (notifiable
  electrical work in England and Wales); a property-owner permit and inspection (Washington);
  a small area only (mould, EPA under about 10 sq ft); the maker's pressure figure (repressurising).
- **Call someone**: the law in the reader's jurisdiction reserves it, or every official source read
  advises against a householder doing it (gas work; work inside a panel; a breaker that keeps
  tripping; asbestos repair or removal; boiler repairs; the outside underground water valve).

Where the law allows a householder more than the advice does (gas in Great Britain, asbestos, lead,
DIY electrics in England), **the lesson gives both, in that order, in adjacent sentences, and the
label follows the advice** (decision 8). A value question (whether householders should be allowed to
do their own electrical work) is not a label: it is described, each side in its own terms, and not
resolved (standards 3.1, kind 3).

**6. The gas law, exactly as SOURCES settles it.** This is the claim in the course most likely to be
stated wrongly (SOURCES, "The gas-work law, stated exactly"). Every lesson that touches gas says it
this way:

- **In Great Britain, registration binds those who work as a business; competence binds everyone.**
  Regulation 3(3) of the Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998 requires an employer or
  self-employed person doing gas work to be in the HSE-approved class (the Gas Safe Register). It
  does not reach a householder working on their own home. Regulation 3(1) requires anyone doing
  gas work, householder included, to be competent. HSE's guidance says "work" includes do-it-yourself
  work and favours for friends (L56 para 52, 83); its Approved Code of Practice sets competence at an
  industry-recognised training course followed by assessment (para 81), and the Code has special
  legal status in court.
- **Therefore** "it's illegal to do any gas work yourself" overstates the law, and "you may legally do
  your own gas work" misleads. Lessons say neither.
- **Every householder is told to use a Gas Safe registered engineer** for any gas work, plainly, as
  the advice of HSE ("HSE strongly advises ...") and the Register ("Don't DIY with gas appliances,
  always use a Gas Safe registered engineer"). The job label is **Call someone**.
- **What a householder may do** is what the appliance's user instructions give the user to do, and
  replacing or adjusting a component designed to be operated or replaced by the consumer, such as a
  cooker control knob (Gas Safe, archived). Moving a bayonet-fitted cooker to clean behind it is not
  "work" (HSE).
- **Gas Safe's "both parties would be breaking the law" line** (a non-registered person's work
  checked by a registered engineer) is **attributed to Gas Safe in the sentence, or left out**. No
  provision was found that makes a competent householder who has the work checked a law-breaker (gate
  G-G1; contested question 16). It is never stated in the course's voice.
- **"It is illegal for anyone to use a gas appliance if they suspect it is unsafe"** (HSE) is stated
  as HSE states it.
- **Northern Ireland** has its own 2004 Regulations whose regulation 3 mirrors Great Britain's, with
  approval given by "the Executive"; HSE says the Register covers Northern Ireland (gate G-G2).
- **In the US** there is no general rule the course read. A utility says a "qualified professional"
  should do all work on gas lines and appliances (Columbia Gas of Ohio); New York City requires a
  Licensed Master Plumber for gas piping; Texas's plumbing licence law exempts a homestead owner,
  and its "plumbing" includes gas piping. The lesson states the Texas exemption as a fact about the
  licensing statute, beside the utility's advice and the point that permits are local.

**7. Electricity: the householder stops at the front of the consumer unit, and wiring is explained,
not taught.** SOURCES open concern 2 settles the scope and this outline adopts it:

- **Taught as tasks:** switching off at the main switch; resetting a tripped breaker, RCD or GFCI by
  the method gate G-E5's source gives; testing an RCD or GFCI with its button; replacing a plug fuse
  with the same rating; using a plug-in RCD; checking leads and sockets for warning signs.
- **Explained as law, not taught as procedure:** which electrical work a householder may lawfully do,
  in England (non-notifiable work needs no notice but must meet P1, and AD P para 3.13 says it
  "should be designed and installed, and inspected, tested and certificated in accordance with BS
  7671"; notifiable work by notifying building control before starting), Wales (more of it
  notifiable), Scotland (most work in a house needs no warrant; standard 4.5 always applies) and
  Northern Ireland (no statutory building regulation for domestic electrical work, ESF), and in the
  five US places read. **No lesson describes how to replace a socket, a switch, a light fitting or a
  consumer unit, or how to prove a circuit dead.** Lesson 22 says why in one plain paragraph: proving
  dead needs a two-pole tester used by HSG85's prove-test-prove sequence and training, and a page
  cannot check a learner's work (SOURCES open concern 2). That is the course's scope decision,
  labelled as the course's, not a claim that the work is illegal where it is legal.
- **Two facts every electrical passage keeps:** a panel is usually live even with the main breaker
  off (CPSC 5133), and a non-contact "volt stick" is not recommended for proving dead (HSG85 para
  54). For a householder doing non-electrical work near cables (drilling a wall), ESF's own lower
  test ("plug an appliance in or try switching on lights") is what ESF says, and is reported as ESF's.
- **The RCD and GFCI mechanism** is CPSC's account of the GFCI (it compares current out and back and
  cuts power at a difference "as little as 0.006 amperes"). That a UK RCD works on the same principle
  is not in a read source (ESF's explanatory body did not load): the lesson says the two do the same
  job (ESF, CPSC) and gives the mechanism as CPSC gives it for the GFCI, unless gate G-E1 closes with
  a UK source. The 30 mA rating and 6 mA are different standards; neither is set beside the other as
  equivalent.

**8. Where the law allows more than the advice, both are stated together.** The same shape recurs:
gas in Great Britain; asbestos (owner-occupiers doing their own work are outside the Control of
Asbestos Regulations 2012; US federal law requires no training for single-family homes; every
authority read says don't repair or remove without training); lead paint (neither country's rules
bind a householder working on their own home; both say work wet and never burn); ladders (the Work
at Height Regulations and OSHA bind work, not DIY; they are still the best-specified practice); DIY
electrics in England (legal with notice; ESF advises a registered electrician). **Rule:** the legal
position and the advice sit in adjacent sentences, the law first and labelled as general
information, then the advice with its source, and the job label follows the advice. A lesson never
states only one half. Lesson 19 names the pattern outright, having seen it in lessons 10 and 15.

**9. The law is general information, labelled at the point of use, every time.** Every passage that
states a legal rule carries, at that point: "This is general information, not legal advice. The rules
differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and between US states and cities, and
they change." (Part C (e) 2.) Rules:

- **No lesson tells a reader what they may lawfully do in their own situation.** Legal worked
  examples and exercises ask which rule applies in a named place and what to ask the local body,
  never "would this be legal for you?".
- **Statutes and regulations are quoted from the live page at drafting**, not from SOURCES, and the
  drafter reads the whole section once (First Aid's course-wide lesson: SOURCES records the parts
  that suited the research). Dated rules (California's $1,000 threshold, the £42 threshold, the FTC's
  $25 and $130, s36's 10 years and its Welsh commencement) are re-read too (access problem 9).
- **Figures from a government-published secondary source are checked against the primary before
  use**: the Pye Tait report's summary of England is wrong against reg 12(6A) (C20); ESF's "must be
  able to prove" compliance sentence goes beyond the primaries (C32) and is never repeated; GOV.UK's
  "Building regulations approval" guide is right for England only on electrics (C7).
- **Certificates:** reg 20(3) says a scheme member gives the certificate within 30 days; GOV.UK says 8
  weeks. Quote the regulation if a number is needed, and say GOV.UK gives a different figure.
- **Where the law is not contested, it is stated plainly**, once, with its label. The gas law, the
  notifiable lists, the RRP rule's scope and the cancellation periods are law, not opinion.

**10. No products, no brands recommended, no prices from memory.** No brand, model, tool, alarm,
detector, filler, paint or pest product is recommended or named for purchase, and no retailer is
linked. Manufacturer pages are used as sources for their own products and labelled so, in the
sentence: Worcester Bosch and Vaillant for their boilers' pressure and bleeding, Bristan for its own
tap, Bosch for its own detectors, Dulux for its own paints (a page with "product promotion", Part B
§4). A figure such as "1 to 1.5 bar" is always "Worcester Bosch and Vaillant give", never the course's
number, and always with "check your boiler's manual". The only prices given are those a source gives
with a date (EPA's "$10-$50" hygrometer; EST's figures are left out, gate G-W10). No affiliate links,
ever (standards 4.5). **Scheme and register names are not endorsements:** a lesson says what a
scheme is and how to check membership on the scheme's own site, and TrustMark is "describes itself
as the Government Endorsed Quality Scheme", with Citizens Advice's "the Government's trader scheme"
attributed (gate G-L12). Alarms are described by the standard the law names (BS EN 50291-1 for CO in
England and Scotland), never by make; the CPSC warnings about particular unlisted alarms are reported
without naming the products.

**11. Every figure carries its source, year, geography and what it counts.** The convention is in the
sentence: "In England, fire and rescue services attended 23,795 accidental dwelling fires in the year
to March 2026 ...". Rules:

- **Scope is part of the number.** CDC's "more than 400" non-fire CO deaths and CPSC's 274 are
  different scopes (all causes against consumer products under CPSC's jurisdiction), not a conflict;
  say so where both appear. The NFPA electrical fire figures are "involving electrical distribution
  and lighting equipment", not DIY. ESF's electricians' survey measures what electricians reported,
  not how often DIY causes shocks.
- **Old data carry their years at the point of use:** NFPA 2015 to 2019 and 2009 to 2013; CPSC's
  older-adult ladder data 1997 to 2002; D'Souza 1990 to 2005; CPSC's GFCI dates (the list stops at
  2005 and is history, not current code).
- **The two EPA WaterSense pages disagree** (9,300 against 9,400 gallons; "nearly 1 trillion" against
  "approximately nearly 900 billion"): use rounded wording or cite one page by name.
- **"Electrical" fires are defined before a share is given**: in England in 2025/26, electrical
  distribution plus non-cooking electrical appliances started about a quarter of accidental dwelling
  fires (the course's arithmetic from FIRE0602, labelled as such); ESF's "almost half" is snippet only
  and never printed.
- **Re-check trigger.** The Home Office publishes its detailed fire analysis each August; CPSC's CO
  report is annual; HSE's asbestos statistics each July. A figure is dated to its release, never
  "current".

**13. No mechanism and no method from general knowledge.** Each lesson explains only the mechanism
and gives only the method its sources give, and says so where they give none. Specifically:

- **The UK cistern** (siphon, flush valve, fill valve): no source read (gate G-W2). The lesson teaches
  the dye test, the overflow sign and United Utilities' figures, and sends the repair to the maker's
  instructions or a WaterSafe plumber.
- **Clearing a blocked sink or trap, plungers, drain chemicals** (gate G-W3): no method until read.
  AD H para 1.6 (traps "should be removable or be fitted with a cleaning eye") is the one sourced
  fact.
- **The temperature and pressure relief valve's mechanism, test and interval** (gate G-W1): no
  method. UGA lists it as a spring and autumn check; the course frames it as "check your manual or

- **Resetting a tripped circuit** (gate G-E5): the method only from the source that closes the gate.
- **Why official gas advice differs between the countries**: no source gives the reason; say so.
- **Why CO alarms are sited differently in the UK and US**: no source gives the reason; say so.
- **Ladder geometry:** the lesson may show that 1 out for every 4 up is about 75° (arithmetic a
  reader can check), but gives no force analysis of why a ladder slips or tips unless a source is
  read.
- **Condensation, capillary rise and salts** are explained only in the words the sources give (GOV.UK's

- **Sticking doors, silicone sealant, wallpapering**: no method (gate G-R1).
- **Where cables run in a wall**: "often straight up, down or across from sockets and switches, and a

**18. Quiz and test items.** Each item on a law, an official step or an emergency names the

## Lesson sequence

Every lesson opens with its reason to care, then the safety callout (decision 4), and ends with
Connections, Go deeper, Sources and the quiz. Two or three `:::predict` and at least two
`:::checkpoint` per lesson, one worked example with a gap, at least one exercise that opens "Take NN
minutes". Every lesson's worked examples include a UK home and a US home somewhere, and no place name
is reused across lessons (the brief's used-places script). Each lesson ends its body with a short
"For your plan" paragraph naming the items it adds to the project's calendar, map or call list, so the
project assembles itself.

### 1. How a house works, and where it switches off (~85 min)

- **Objectives:** explain the house as a set of flows (pressurised water in, drainage out by gravity
  through sealed traps, power in through protective devices, fuel in and combustion products out) and
  what each shut-off controls; identify the main water, electricity and gas shut-offs in a UK or US
  home and describe how to operate the water shut-off; apply the burst-pipe order to a described
  emergency, including water near electrics; sort jobs with the course's three labels, saying for each
  whether the rule behind it is law or advice, whose, and where.
- **Core ideas:** the reason to care: water, power and gas each have one control that stops the whole
  house, and the time to find it is before 2 a.m. **The flows** (SOURCES essential 2; AD H H1 guidance;
  ASHI; Goodlife's "Plumbing = water in and water out", cited as the centre's). **Water shut-offs.**
  UK: the internal stop tap, "also sometimes called a stop valve or stopcock", looks like "a spout-less
  tap or lever", usually under the kitchen sink but also in a cupboard, cloakroom, garage, cellar or
  under the stairs; "Turning the valve clockwise will close it"; it "may take a few minutes for the
  water to stop altogether"; "Turn it on and off slowly, never force it"; if it won't turn, call a
  plumber (WaterSafe). The external stop tap "usually located in the public footpath" is the water
  company's, and the pipe from the boundary in is the homeowner's (CCW). Check it "every six months"
  (Met Office). US: the main shut-off valve, "typically located near the front foundation wall" in a
  basement and "within three to five feet of where the main water enters", near the water heater or
  under the kitchen sink in slab and crawl-space homes, "but anywhere is possible"; a wheel handle closes
  clockwise and "may take two or more full revolutions"; the outside valve at the property line is for
  the utility or a master plumber; in a home with sprinklers, shut the plumbing and leave the sprinkler
  pipe live (WSSC, a Maryland utility, about its own area). **Electricity.** "Modern consumer units have
  a main switch and fuses and/or circuit breakers" (ESF); the US panel's main breaker; label the
  circuits (UGA); the panel is "usually electrically live, even with the main circuit breakers turned
  off" (CPSC 5133), which is why a householder's work stops at the front. **Gas.** The emergency control
  valve near the meter, "a handle that can be turned 90 degrees" (Cadent; gate G-W8); not in a cellar;
  in the US, the utility's advice is to leave (preview of lesson 9; the reader locates the meter and
  does not turn anything). **The burst-pipe order** (Met Office; CCW): stop tap off, heating and boiler
  off (and the immersion heater, CCW), open the taps to drain, soak up, call a WaterSafe plumber,
  photograph the damage for the insurer (CCW; pointer to Personal Finance lesson 8); water near
  electrics by gate G-W5. **The course:** the three job labels and the question (decision 5); the gas
  law in one sentence with a pointer to lesson 10; the law as general information (decision 9); nations
  and states named (decision 2); UK and US side by side (decision 3); what is left out, said once,
  including fitting locks, wallpapering and wasp nests as not researched.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why one valve stops the whole house (the supply enters by one pipe from the
  company's main, CCW, WSSC); why the consumer unit's main switch cuts the circuits but not everything
  in the box (CPSC's fact; the lesson gives no more of the reason than CPSC does); why a stop tap is
  turned slowly (WaterSafe's instruction; no mechanism is given, so none is supplied).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a couple who have just moved into a 1930s semi in England find the
  stop tap under the kitchen sink behind the bleach, turn it clockwise, count how long the kitchen tap
  takes to stop, turn it back slowly, find it stiff on the way back and leave it rather than force it,
  then map the consumer unit and label two circuits by switching them off one at a time. (b) wrinkle:
  water is coming through a ceiling light in a US house with the panel in a basement that already has
  an inch of water on the floor. The main valve (WSSC's basement guidance), the light circuit, the panel
  in standing water, gate G-W5's reading, and who to call; the worked example stops at what the
  householder does and doesn't touch.
- **Misconceptions to address:** clockwise opens a valve (WSSC's note: a search summary had it
  backwards; WaterSafe and WSSC agree clockwise closes); the stop tap in the pavement is the one to use
  (it's the company's); switching off the main makes the panel safe to open (CPSC); "it's illegal to do
  any gas work yourself" (preview; lesson 10); a stiff stop tap needs more force.
- **Practice:** **your shut-off map** (the first project deliverable): find the stop tap or main valve,
  photograph it, try it slowly and back with the limits of decision 1 written in, time it, note if it
  is stiff; find the consumer unit or panel and label what you can; find the gas meter and its valve
  without touching it; write your burst-pipe card. Then sort twelve drafter-written jobs into the three
  labels, each with "law or advice, whose, where", answer behind a checkpoint.
- **Images:** licensed photographs of a stop tap, a UK consumer unit, a US panel and a UK gas meter
  with its valve (gate G-N4). Optional SVG schematic of the four flows, labelled schematic.
- **Sources:** Part A §1 (WaterSafe, Met Office, CCW, WSSC), §3 (ESF), §4 (Cadent, SSE NI, US
  utilities), §7 (Goodlife); Part B §3 (CCW winter advice), §6 (UGA); Part C (e); synthesis essentials
  1, 2, 6, safety-critical 1, 2, 8; the gas section.
- **Connects:** First Aid lesson 2 (danger, "power off before you touch", the call); Personal Safety
  lesson 5 (the home, and why locks are not here). Leads to lesson 2 (what's on the other side of the
  stop tap).
- **Spaced review of:** First Aid lesson 2's danger step, used in the burst-pipe example.
- **Neutrality:** none contested. The renters line (decision 15).

### 2. Water in: supply, leaks and frozen pipes

*Note, 2026-09-25: the lesson outlined here as "Water in: supply, leaks, taps and toilets" (~95 min)
passed its seam and split at "Taps and toilets", as the seam list above planned. This section keeps
the supply, leaks and frozen pipes; lesson 3 below takes taps and toilets. Every later lesson moved up
by one, so the course was then seventeen lessons. (Two further splits on the same day, at lessons 5
and 7 below, made it nineteen, and four more on 2026-09-26, at lessons 9, 11, 13 and 15 below, made
it twenty-three; the numbers throughout this file are the twenty-three-lesson ones.)*

- **Objectives:** explain who owns which part of the water supply in England and Wales, and what the
  bucket test and the pressure standard show; apply the meter test and the dye test to find a leak,
  and estimate what a drip costs a year; compare the UK and US advice on thawing a frozen pipe and
  apply the right order for your country.
- **Core ideas:** **Ownership** (CCW: main and communication pipe the company's; the supply pipe from
  the boundary in the homeowner's; shared supply pipes jointly; internal leaks the homeowner's). US
  ownership varies by utility and was not researched: say so. **Pressure** (WaterSafe: measured at the
  outside stop valve or boundary; the first tap "should be able to fill a 4.5 litre (one gallon)
  bucket in 30 seconds"; companies aim at 10 metres head, about 1 bar, and are "committed to" a 7
  metre minimum, gate G-W4). No US national figure was read. **Leaks** (EPA WaterSense: the average
  household's leaks "more than 9,300 gallons" a year and "nine percent of homes" waste 50 gallons or
  more a day, with the two pages' disagreement handled by decision 11; one drip a second "more than
  3,000 gallons per year"; Southern Water "up to 10,000 litres per year" for a slow drip; United
  Utilities: "Nearly 80% of all leaks reported to us last year were leaky loos!", as much as £400 a
  year). The **meter test** ("before and after a two-hour period when no water is being used"). The
  **dye test** ("If any color shows up in the bowl after 10 minutes, you have a leak"). **Frozen
  pipes:** the mechanism ("expands as it freezes", Red Cross); prevention in each country (Red Cross:
  drain hose bibs, insulate, open cabinet doors, drip a cold tap, same thermostat day and night, "no
  lower than 55° F" away; CCW: lag pipes, cover outside taps, insulate tanks); thawing, taught as each
  country's authorities give it (CCW and the Met Office: stop tap off, immersion and heating off, cold
  tap open, warm slowly; Red Cross: "Keep the faucet open", heat with a pad, hair dryer or towels),
  with CCW's reason for isolating first (a split pipe floods when it thaws) as CCW's; no flame, both
  (contested question 11; decision 3).
- **Mechanism to explain:** what "metres head" means for a tap (WaterSafe's own conversion, 10 m about
  1 bar); why freezing splits a pipe (Red Cross).
- **Worked examples:** (b) wrinkle: a US family's bill has jumped; the meter moves over two quiet
  hours; the dye test shows nothing in either toilet; the leak is the outdoor hose bib whose inside
  valve was never closed after a freeze. Worked through with a gap: the reader decides the next check
  before it is shown.
- **Misconceptions to address:** a slow drip wastes almost nothing (the numbers); thaw with a
  blowtorch (both countries say never); one country's thaw order is the wrong one (decision 3).
- **Practice:** the meter test and the dye test at home, with the limits of decision 1; count a drip
  rate and compute a year's waste in litres and gallons; a frozen-pipe decision for a described house
  in each country behind a checkpoint.
- **Images:** optional SVG: the drip arithmetic as a small bar chart (drips a second against gallons a
  year, from EPA's one number and labelled as the course's arithmetic).
- **Sources:** Part A §1 (CCW, WaterSafe pressure, EPA Fix a Leak Week, Red Cross); Part B §3 (EPA
  WaterSense pages, United Utilities, Red Cross, CCW winter advice); gate G-W4.
- **Connects:** lesson 1's stop tap; leads to lesson 3 (the tap that drips), lesson 4 (where the water
  goes) and lessons 7 and 8 (the hot side). Personal Finance lesson 1 (where your money goes), for the water
  bill.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's "isolate before you open anything up" and its stop tap.
- **Neutrality:** the thaw order (decision 3), each country's advice as given.

### 4. Water out: traps, vents and blocked drains (~75 min)

- **Objectives:** explain how a trap's water seal and a ventilated stack keep sewer air out, and
  diagnose a smell or a gurgle from that; identify what may go down a sink and a toilet and why fats and
  wipes block drains; decide whose job a blockage is (inside the property or the public sewer) and apply
  the safe order for a blocked sink (gate G-W3); describe the dispute over "flushable" wipes and state
  what every side agrees a householder should flush.
- **Core ideas:** the reason to care: the commonest cause of sewage coming back into a house is what
  went down the sink. **Traps and seals** (AD H para 1.3: every point of discharge trapped, a seal of
  at least 25 mm retained; Table 1: 75 mm for a sink or basin, 50 mm for a bath, shower or WC; para 1.6:
  "removable or be fitted with a cleaning eye"; England's document, Wales publishes its own). **Vents**
  (AD H para 1.29: stacks ventilated "To prevent water seals in the traps from being lost"; air
  admittance valves in some layouts; ASHI's gallon jug, "glub, glub", and "The vent pipe also allows
  sewer gas out"). **Old US house traps** (City of Superior: a whole-house trap "no longer necessary"
  and a debris catcher). **Fats, oils and grease** (Thames Water: "one of the leading causes of
  blockage-related internal flooding"; up to 75,000 blockages a year, 28% FOG; its commissioned survey's
  61% milk, 31% meat juices and 25% thinking wipes flushable, cited as company-commissioned; Portland:
  FOG "sticks to pipes" and "gets thick and sticky when it cools"). **Wipes and the 3Ps** (Thames: wipes
  block pipes "even the ones labelled 'flushable'"; Fine to Flush ended in March 2024, WRc's account of
  why and its own dissent that plastic wipes, not certified flushable ones, are "the primary cause"; The
  Water Report's reason, consumer confusion; England's plastic-wipes Regulations and their commencement,
  gate G-W9). **Whose blockage** (Thames: inside the property the owner's; the public sewer the
  company's, attending within 48 hours, 72 when busy). **Septic tanks and sump pumps** (UGA: pumped
  "every 3 - 7 years", US; Superior: disconnect flexible sump hoses in winter). **A blocked sink**, by
  gate G-W3's source, or its fallback.
- **Mechanism to explain:** the water seal as a barrier; siphonage and pressure pulling the seal out,
  and the vent that stops it (AD H, ASHI); why fat that pours hot blocks cold (Portland).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a basin in a spare bathroom in a flat smells of drains after weeks
  unused; the seal has gone (the lesson reasons from AD H's seal, and the course says plainly that the
  source gives the seal, not a cause for this case); run the tap; if the smell persists, it's a job to
  have looked at. (b) wrinkle: a kitchen sink in a US house gurgles every time the washing machine
  empties and drains slowly; is it the vent or a blockage? Worked through with a gap, ending at the
  point where the sources stop and a plumber starts.
- **Misconceptions to address:** "flushable" wipes can be flushed; hot water and washing-up liquid carry
  fat away (Portland: it sets as it cools); a gurgle always means a blockage (vent); the water company
  clears everything; a whole-house trap is needed in a modern US house.
- **Practice:** a week's sink and toilet audit (what went down, count it); find each trap and whether it

  every side agrees).
- **Images:** SVG of AD H Table 1 (trap sizes and seal depths), labelled "England, Approved Document H

- **Sources:** Part A §2 (AD H, ASHI, Superior, Thames Water, Portland, WRc, The Water Report, SI

### 5. Electricity: what trips, and why (~90 min; seam fired, see the note)

*Note, 2026-09-25: the lesson outlined here as "Electricity: what trips, and why" (~90 min) passed
its seam and split at "Plugs, leads and warning signs", as the seam list above planned: the first
half alone measured 120 before trimming, and the whole about 145. This section keeps the devices,
testing and resetting, the stop rule and what stays shut; lesson 6 below takes plug fuses, leads,
the warning signs, inspection and the fire figures. Every later lesson moved up by one at this
split, and by one more at the heating and hot water split (see lesson 7's note), so the course was
then nineteen lessons (twenty-three after the 2026-09-26 splits, see lesson 9's note), and the
numbers throughout this file are the current ones. Gate G-E5 closed at
drafting (National Grid Electricity Distribution, SSEN and Southern California Edison), but no
source gave a method for isolating a faulty appliance by plugging things back one at a time, so
the lesson says it didn't find one and teaches none.*

- **Objectives:** explain what a fuse or circuit breaker protects (the wiring, from fire), what an
  RCD or GFCI protects (people, from shock), and what an AFCI adds, using the source that says each;
  apply the test routine for an RCD or GFCI and the reset steps a UK network operator and a US
  utility give, including what to switch off first and what to do if a device won't stay on;
  decide, for a described trip, whether it is yours to reset once, a plug-in appliance to take out
  of use, or a job for an electrician, and state whose rule sits behind the decision; explain why a
  switched-off main breaker and a silent volt stick don't make anything behind the cover safe to
  touch, and where the law and this course leave wiring.
- **Core ideas:** the reason to care: a trip at 9 p.m. is either a nuisance or a fault reporting itself,
  and the difference is the whole lesson. **Two jobs, two devices**: CPSC 1989, a GFCI "shuts down the
  power in milliseconds", "whereas circuit breakers and fuses function primarily to prevent the home
  wiring system from causing a fire"; ESF, an RCD "protects you from electric shocks if a product is
  faulty"; CPSC 5133, "AFCIs are intended to address fire hazards; GFCIs address shock hazards", and
  "Typical household fuses and circuit breakers do not respond to early arcing". **The UK consumer
  unit** (ESF: main switch, fuses or breakers, the "Test" or "T" button; older units without RCDs "not
  necessarily" unsafe; plug-in RCDs where there isn't one). **The US panel and the GFCI** (CPSC 099:
  "as little as 0.006 amperes"; test "At least once a month"; receptacle GFCIs may be fitted by
  consumers "with adequate knowledge and skills", breaker GFCIs by an electrician, stated as CPSC's
  and labelled with decision 7's scope line; the dated NEC list as history, gate G-E4; ESFi's monthly
  test). **Testing and resetting**: the test buttons (ESF: pressing it "will switch off the power to
  the areas of the home it protects"); the reset by gate G-E5's sources; "keeps tripping" means a fault
  "either with a particular appliance or the wiring itself" for a registered electrician (ESF). **Why
  it matters, in numbers**: CPSC's 100 electrocutions a year 2018 to 2020; the GFCI share as an
  estimate (contested question 14). **What stays shut**: the panel is live (CPSC); a volt stick doesn't
  prove dead (HSG85 para 54); the wiring the law allows a householder in England is lesson 22's, and
  this course teaches none of it (decision 7).
- **Mechanism to explain:** too much current heats a wire, and the breaker or fuse opens before the
  wire gets hot enough to start a fire (CPSC 1989's statement of what they are for; the lesson adds no
  thermal detail a source doesn't give); the GFCI compares current out and back (CPSC 099); the RCD by
  gate G-E1 or decision 7's fallback; why a volt stick can mislead (HSG85 recommends against it for
  proving dead; the lesson gives HSE's recommendation and no electronics).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a GFCI in a US bathroom trips when a hair dryer is plugged in; test,
  reset, try again without the dryer; if it holds, the dryer is the suspect (by G-E5's sources). (b)
  wrinkle: an RCD in a UK kitchen trips most nights around 3 a.m.; nothing obvious is on; the reader
  works through what the sources say (a fault in an appliance or the wiring), what they can safely do,
  and where it stops (a registered electrician).
- **Misconceptions to address:** breakers protect you from shock (CPSC); an AFCI and a GFCI do the

### 7. Heating: boilers, radiators and furnaces (~90 min)

- **Objectives:** identify a home's heating and hot water system (a combi, system or regular boiler;
  a furnace, boiler or heat pump; a storage or tankless water heater) and explain what its main parts
  do; diagnose a cold radiator as air or sludge, and describe bleeding it and restoring the pressure
  to the figure in the boiler's own instructions; explain why turning the thermostat up doesn't heat
  a home faster, what a TRV does, and what an AFUE figure leaves out.
- **Core ideas:** the reason to care: the heating is the most expensive system in the house to run and
  the one whose small faults (a cold radiator, falling pressure) most often become an engineer's call-out
  that wasn't needed. **UK boilers** (EST: combi, "don't use a hot water cylinder"; system, "a separate

  require a service engineer"; topping up "once or twice a year", more often suggests a leak; Vaillant

  top is air, cold at the bottom "could mean that sludge has built up"; heating off, cool (Vaillant:
  about an hour, two to be cautious), cloth, key anticlockwise, close when water runs steadily, don't
  overtighten, check and top up the pressure; repeated bleeding means a fault for the installer).

  21°C for most people; TRVs "work by sensing the air temperature around them", 0 to 6; flow
  temperature). **US systems** (DOE, as archived: furnaces heat air through ducts, boilers heat water;
  AFUE and duct losses "as much as 35%"; a leaking heat exchanger mixes combustion gases with house air,
  pointer to lesson 11; maintenance by "a heating system professional"; heat pumps serviced "at least
  once a year", filters "every 3 months", don't set back if it brings on backup heat; air conditioners,
  filters "every month or two" in season, 2 feet clear; ENERGY STAR, "Inspect, clean, or change air
  filters once a month", and the condensate drain; the reconciliation "check monthly, change when dirty
  and at least as often as the maker says", contested question 5 of Part A). **Tankless heaters** (DOE's
  flow and efficiency figures as archived). **Boiler repairs are gas work** (lesson 10).

  (Worcester Bosch's diagnosis; the lesson gives the diagnosis as the source gives it); a sealed system
  holds pressure, and bleeding lets water out with the air, which is why pressure drops (Worcester
  Bosch, Vaillant); a room thermostat and a TRV decide when heat stops, not how fast it comes (EST's
  account of each, in EST's words).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: in a UK flat with a combi, the bedroom radiator is cold at the top.
  Bleed it, watch the gauge fall from 1.2 to 0.8 bar, top it up by the boiler's instructions to the
  maker's figure. (b) wrinkle: a radiator is cold at the bottom and bleeding does nothing, and the
  pressure has needed topping up every fortnight since autumn: two separate faults (sludge; a leak), and
  both are the installer's. A US counterpart in the practice: a heat pump whose filter nobody has
  changed and whose owner turns the thermostat right down at night.
- **Misconceptions to address:** turning the thermostat up heats faster (EST); a radiator cold at the
  bottom needs bleeding (Worcester Bosch); 1 to 1.5 bar applies to every boiler (two makers, sealed
  systems, check your manual); an AFUE figure is what you get (DOE: duct losses).
- **Practice:** identify your system (boiler and flue type, cylinder, loft tanks; US: furnace or heat
  pump, filter size and date); read your boiler's pressure gauge cold and hot and compare with its
  manual; find and date your filter; bleed a radiator only if one is cold at the top (decision 1).
- **Images:** licensed photographs of a bleed valve and a pressure gauge (G-N4), or none.
- **Sources:** Part A §1 (EST boilers, DOE tankless), §5 (Worcester Bosch, EST controls, DOE furnaces,
  heat pumps, air conditioners, ENERGY STAR); Part B §3 (Worcester Bosch and Vaillant bleeding); gates
  G-W7, G-W10, and "Gate closures, lesson 06" in SOURCES.

  **US bans** (EPA: 1989 mostly overturned in 1991; March 2024 chrysotile, gate G-Z1; the ban doesn't
  make old material safe or require its removal). **Lead** (US: sale for homes banned 1978; "Approximately
  three-quarters" of pre-1978 homes contain some; the RRP rule binds paid work, "does not apply to
  homeowners doing RRP projects in their own pre-1978 homes" unless renting, childcare or flipping; the
  rule's open-flame ban, HEPA-shrouded power tools, heat gun "below 1,100 degrees Fahrenheit", and its
  6 and 20 square foot thresholds, as the rule for contractors; EPA's seven DIY practices, N-100
  respirator, misting, wet-dry paper; CDC: "No safe level of lead in children has been identified",
  3.5 µg/dL "not a health-based standard"; EPA's 2024 dust-lead change as a principle only). **UK lead
  dates** (the "who says which date" table: Defra withdrawn, BCF 1960s and 1992 and "Pre-1995", UKHSA
  "banned in the UK from 1992"); BCF: overcoating "the safest option", "Use chemical stripper or wet
  abrasive paper", no dry abrasive, no blowlamps, don't let paint burn; Defra's "below 450°C", with its

  employers, not you. **Radon** (EPA: "the number one cause of lung cancer among non-smokers, according

  pCi/L (150 Bq/m³), consider from 2; UKHSA: reduce above 200 Bq/m³, target 100, average home 20, two
  detectors over three months; the maps' caveat; no UK death figure, gate G-Z6).
- **Mechanism to explain:** asbestos harm is from fibres released by disturbing it, and it is cumulative
  with a long latency (HSE); children take in lead mostly by swallowing dust and flakes (BCF, UKHSA);
  radon comes from uranium in all rocks and soils (UKHSA). No deeper biology than the sources give.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a UK owner-occupier in a 1970s bungalow wants to fit a new ceiling
  light through an Artex ceiling and replace a corrugated garage roof. The law, then HSE's advice, then
  the labels (**Call someone** for both, by the advice); HSE's "should not be broken up" for AC sheets.

  pregnant; they also rent out the basement. The RRP rule doesn't bind them for their own rooms, EPA's

- **Misconceptions to address:** if you find asbestos you must remove it (HSE, EPA); the bans made old

### 21. Pests: find the cause, then control it (~80 min)

- **Objectives:** apply integrated pest management's four steps to a described pest problem; deal with
  mice or rats in the CDC's order (seal up, trap up, clean up), with its clean-up precautions, and state
  which consumer poisons each country allows and in what form; explain, from the two 2019 studies, why
  foggers fail against German cockroaches and what works instead; identify the signs of bed bugs,
  clothes moths, woodworm and (in the US) termites, and decide which need a professional.
- **Core ideas:** the reason to care: the spray can is the first thing people reach for and, for the
  commonest indoor pests, the least effective. **IPM** (EPA: thresholds, "Sighting a single pest does not
  always mean control is needed"; monitor and identify; prevent; control, "Effective, less risky pest
  controls are chosen first"; broadcast spraying "a last resort"). **Rodents** (CDC: droppings "pointed at
  one end and often contain hair"; "Mice can fit through a hole the width of a pencil (1/4 inch or 6
  millimeters in diameter)"; steel wool and caulk; "Traditional snap traps are recommended"; "Do not use
  glue traps and live traps", and why; traps in a T against the wall; pre-bait for rats; a week with no
  catches; clean-up, "Don't vacuum or sweep", 30 minutes' airing, gloves, "1 part bleach to 9 parts
  water", five minutes, double-bagged; hantavirus as a US concern, the clean-up applied anywhere as the
  course's step, gate G-R5). **Poisons** (EPA: second-generation anticoagulants "no longer are registered
  for use in products geared toward consumers", no pellets, ready-to-use bait stations; HSE: anticoagulants
  "present a higher risk ... than is normally acceptable", consider other methods first, amateur products
  "only within a bait box"; CRRU's stewardship as described, no pack sizes). **Glue traps** (CDC's advice;
  England's law by gate G-L14; whether they're humane is a value question). **Cockroaches** (UC IPM:
  "pesticides alone will not solve cockroach problems", baits better than sprays, avoid foggers which
  "may repel and disperse"; allergens and childhood asthma; DeVries 2019 abstracts: foggers "failed to
  reduce cockroach populations, whereas similarly priced gel baits caused significant declines", residues
  603 times baseline on average; 202-fold resistance and the kdr mutation in over 96%). **Bed bugs** (EPA:
  confirm first; don't throw everything out; encasements a year; hot dryer; freezing unreliable; "Bed bugs
  die when their body temperatures reaches 45°C (113°F)", and turning up the thermostat won't do it;
  "Never use a pesticide indoors that is intended for outdoor use"; the myths, including "no cases or
  studies that indicate bed bugs transmit diseases between humans"). **Clothes moths** (NHM: larvae eat
  keratin; "Freezing to -20°C for a week or heating fabric to temperatures of 49°C for over 30 minutes
  kills all life stages"; pheromone traps for monitoring). **Woodworm** (Babergh: sharp-edged holes and
  frass "the colour of freshly-cut timber" for an active attack; otherwise "no action is normally
  needed"; the tissue-paper test for a year; "a symptom of excess damp"; the commercial view seen only in
  snippets, so it is not stated, contested question 12 attributed). **Termites** (EPA: signs; dry soil;
  "hire a pest management professional who is licensed and trained"). **Checking a professional** (BPCA's
  criteria; US state pesticide agency, EPA).
- **Mechanism to explain:** why foggers fail (DeVries: resistance and dispersal, the abstract's
  findings); why moisture invites woodworm and termites (Babergh; EPA); why glue and live traps raise
  infection risk (CDC's reason).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: mouse droppings in a kitchen cupboard of a UK flat: identify, seal,
  snap traps, clean up by the CDC's method (labelled US guidance the course applies), and when a bait
  box is justified. (b) wrinkle: German cockroaches in a US apartment, where the tenant has already set
  off two foggers and the neighbours have them too. The studies, the baits, the landlord and a
  professional who follows IPM; a gap where the reader decides what the foggers did.
- **Misconceptions to address:** bug bombs clear cockroaches (DeVries; UC IPM); bed bugs mean a dirty
  home, spread disease, avoid light, or die when the heating is turned up (EPA); woodworm holes mean live
  woodworm (Babergh); sweep up droppings (CDC); outdoor pesticide is fine indoors (EPA).
- **Practice:** a pest walk-round (entry points as wide as a pencil; food storage; damp spots); a
  decision tree for three described finds, behind a checkpoint; the tissue-paper test on any old
  woodworm holes, started now and checked in the project's calendar.
- **Images:** licensed photographs of woodworm holes with frass and of mouse droppings (G-N4), captioned
  to teach the signs.
- **Sources:** Part B §5 (CDC, EPA rodenticides, HSE, CRRU, EPA IPM, EPA bed bugs, UC IPM, DeVries x2, EPA
  termites, NHM, Babergh, NHS, BPCA, NPMA note); gates G-L14, G-R4, G-R5.
- **Connects:** lessons 17 and 18 (damp and gaps); Cooking Fundamentals lesson 4 (food storage hygiene

- **Spaced review of:** lesson 18's moisture-first rule; lesson 17's outside walk-round.

  5. **The call list** (lesson 23): each trade you might need, and how you would check them in your

  stop list consistent with the reader's nation or state; repair write-up in the right order with a stop

## Neutrality notes

The course is not a 3.4 domain, but it has one economics question, one value question, several live
practical disputes and a standing risk of blaming occupants. The failures to guard against are **false
certainty about the law, silent picking between countries, silent picking between trade bodies, and
blame**. Every reviewer checks: that no rule is stated without its jurisdiction and its kind (law or
advice, decision 5); that neither country's advice is called wrong (decision 3); that the law and the
advice sit together where they differ (decision 8); and that no mechanism or method was added from
general knowledge (decision 13).

- **Occupational licensing** (lesson 22). Contested empirical (economics). Both sides in their proponents'
  own words (gates G-N2 and G-L3); the federal report's findings and its own endorsement of targeted
  licensing both reported; no side's evidence called weak in the course's voice; what would settle it
  said. **Full neutrality audit** (decision 17), checked against a licensing proponent, a deregulation
  proponent and a householder.
- **Householders doing their own electrical work** (lesson 22). Value question. The rules differ
  (established); ESF's position and the other side's (G-L4) described, not resolved. The course's own
  decision not to teach wiring is stated as a teaching judgement about what a page can check, not as a
  position on the value question.
- **The gas law and Gas Safe's "both parties" line** (lesson 10). Law stated exactly; the Register's
  interpretation attributed or omitted (G-G1). The Register is not criticised for saying it.
- **Rising damp** (lesson 18). Contested empirical (frequency and treatment). The PCA and the RICS-reported
  thesis, and the sceptics as quoted, each in their own words; the 2022 JPS signed by both as common
  ground; GOV.UK's "often misdiagnosed" as the official English line, attributed. The trade body's
  interest is stated only as the JPS itself frames declared interests, without insinuation; no verdict.
- **Flushable wipes** (lesson 4). WRc's dissent and the decision to end Fine to Flush, each attributed.
- **Hot water temperature** (lesson 8). Two established hazards; the trade-off described.
- **Bleach and mould** (lesson 18). Minor contested practice; each body attributed.
- **Asbestos cement removal; lead testing** (lesson 19). Law established; advisability a value
  judgement where every official source says the same; stated together.
- **Glue traps and woodworm** (lesson 21). Glue traps' humaneness a value question; woodworm's commercial
  treatment view not characterised from snippets.
- **Deposits** (lesson 23). California's law and England's guidance kept apart, neither called better.
- **Damp and blame** (lessons 17, 18). GOV.UK's no-blame statement first, then the adjustments; no
  sentence implies the occupant caused it.
- **Trade bodies, charities and makers.** ESF (a campaigning charity), the PCA (a trade body), WRc
  (which ran the scheme it defends), Thames Water (its commissioned survey), Dulux, Worcester Bosch,
  Vaillant, Bosch and Bristan (makers on their own products) are cited for what each says, labelled for
  what each is, and neither endorsed nor suspected in the course's voice.
- **Religion.** Nothing in SOURCES bears on any tradition's teaching; the course makes no claim either
  way.

## What downstream courses will need from this one

**Car Basics** (next, this term) can assume CO from engines in enclosed spaces (CPSC, CDC) and the job
labels; it owns the car. **Emergency Preparedness** (Term 24) can assume the shut-off map, the burst-pipe
and gas-leak orders for each country, frozen pipes, generators outdoors at 20 feet, and CO alarms, and
owns floods, storms and grid-down. **Tools and Making Things** (Term 23) can assume the small kit, OSHA's
power-tool rules, masks and their limits, the before-you-drill routine and ladders, and owns
woodworking and fabrication. **Energy: How the World Is Powered** (Term 9) can assume boilers, heat pumps,
AFUE and duct losses, and draught-proofing against ventilation. **Introduction to Law** (Term 12) can
assume building regulations in four UK nations and US licensing and permits as worked examples of how
one rule changes at every border, and the gas regulations' split between registration and competence.
**Economics courses later on the Core** can assume occupational licensing as one worked case, with the
federal report's findings and both sides' arguments, and should build on it rather than reteach it.
**Personal Finance** is earlier and is only pointed to. **Relationships and Family** and any course on
renting can assume the renter's pointers and GOV.UK's no-blame line on damp.
