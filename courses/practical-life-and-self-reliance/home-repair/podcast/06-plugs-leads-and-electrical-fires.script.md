S2: The usual word first.

S1: This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. The opening box says isolate the power before you open anything up, and in this lesson that has one everyday meaning. A plug is opened only when it's out of the socket.

S2: Last time was the box on the wall. And now?

S1: At the plug. Most of the electricity a household handles goes through a plug, often a lead or an adaptor, into something that heats or spins. That's where the small faults start, and where you can do the most without opening anything.

S2: Start with the plug, then.

S1: This part is British, because the course read no US source on plug protection. A UK plug carries its own fuse. Electrical Safety First, a UK charity that campaigns on electrical safety, says it will blow if an appliance or extension lead draws too much current, either because of an overload or a fault, and that this stops the cable and appliance overheating and causing a fire.

S2: And if it blows?

S1: One sentence matters. Replace it only with another of the same rating. A standard plug usually has a three amp or a thirteen amp fuse. Which is right for yours? The charity's own instruction settles it: check the maker's instructions. Changing a plug fuse for one of the same rating is, in the course's label, yours, on conditions. The plug out of the socket first, and the maker's rating. The course doesn't teach wiring a plug. On a moulded plug, the kind sealed onto its cable, the fuse sits in a small carrier and there's nothing else to open.

S2: Walk me through one.

S1: A rented flat in Staffordshire. A bedside lamp goes out, and the bulb is fine. The fuse in its moulded plug says three amps, and so do the lamp's instructions. Unplugged, new three amp fuse in, plugged back in, and it works. For a week. Then the new fuse blows too.

S2: So a bigger fuse.

S1: That's the one thing it never means. The rule is the same rating. One blown fuse can be a one-off. A second is the lamp telling you something is drawing too much current, and a table lamp isn't overloading anything. So the fault is in the lamp or its flex, and it isn't a fuse job any more. The charity's advice for fuses blowing is to unplug and contact the retailer, the manufacturer, or a qualified repair technician.

S2: Extension leads. I suspect I'm guilty here.

S1: Electrical Safety First says most leads are rated at thirteen amps, but some older ones and many cable reels may be only ten or less. The total of everything plugged in should stay within the lead's own rating. Don't plug one extension lead into another, as that could make the plug in the wall socket overheat. And unwind a cable reel fully if you need its full rating, because wound up its rating is typically as low as three or four amps. If nothing's tripped, that isn't the test. The lead has its own rating, often lower. All of this is UK advice. The course read no US source on extension cords.

S2: What should make me worried?

S1: The GOV.UK fire safety guide for England lists scorch marks, hot plugs and sockets, fuses that blow or breakers that trip for no obvious reason, and flickering lights. Electrical Safety First adds buzzing or crackling, which it calls arcing. The charity gives much the same signs two different answers on two pages. Its plugs and sockets pages say unplug and contact the retailer, the maker or a repair technician. Its questions page says get a registered electrician to check your electrics as soon as possible.

S2: So which is it?

S1: The course splits them itself, and says the split is its own, not the charity's. A sign on one appliance's plug or flex belongs to the appliance, for the retailer, the maker or a repair technician. A sign at a socket, a switch or a fitting belongs to the wiring, for a registered electrician. Both are Call someone. They're different someones. A kettle plug hot after every boil, stop using the kettle. A crackling socket, an electrician.

S2: Is there a law about checking the wiring?

S1: For landlords in England. This is general information, not legal advice, and the rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and between US states and cities, and they change. Regulations from twenty twenty require a landlord who grants what they call a specified tenancy to have the installation inspected and tested by a qualified person before the tenancy starts, then at intervals of no more than five years, or sooner if the last report says so. Existing tenants get a copy of the report within twenty-eight days of the inspection, and a new tenant before moving in. Some tenancies are excluded, such as one where the tenant shares a kitchen with the landlord or a member of the landlord's family. For an owner-occupied home, the charity recommends an inspection at least every ten years. That's advice, not law.

S2: And then the fire figures.

S1: In England, in the year to March twenty twenty-six, fire and rescue services attended twenty-three thousand, seven hundred and ninety-five accidental dwelling fires. The government's table puts six thousand, one hundred and forty-nine of them down to electrical distribution or other electrical appliances. That's about a quarter, on the course's own arithmetic. It leaves out cooking appliances, nine thousand, nine hundred and twenty-eight fires, because the table doesn't say which of those ran on electricity.

S2: I've heard almost half.

S1: Nothing the course read from the government shows that. A share that large depends on counting electric cooking, and the table can't separate electric cooking from gas. So any share you read needs its counting rule next to it. In the US, the National Fire Protection Association estimated an average of thirty-two thousand, six hundred and twenty home fires a year involving electrical distribution and lighting equipment, from twenty fifteen to twenty nineteen, with an average of four hundred and thirty civilian deaths a year.

S2: And do those count who did the work?

S1: No. They count the equipment involved and what contributed to ignition, not who installed or last worked on it, and the report puts none of them down to DIY. So they don't show that homeowners' own wiring started them, and they don't show the opposite either.
