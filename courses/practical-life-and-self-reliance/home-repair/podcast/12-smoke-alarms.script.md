In the year to March twenty twenty six, fire and rescue services found no smoke alarm at all in twenty three percent of the dwelling fires they attended, and twenty four percent of the deaths in those fires happened where there was none. S2: The law first, then.

S1: This is general information, not legal advice. The rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and between US states and cities, and they change. In England, the twenty fifteen Regulations require a landlord to make sure there's a smoke alarm on each storey with a room used as living accommodation. The course read no rule that requires an owner occupier in England to fit one in an existing home. Scotland is different. Every home, owned or rented, has had to meet a standard since February twenty twenty two, and the Scottish Government's factsheet says every home must have interlinked fire alarms. A smoke alarm in the living room or the room you use most, one in every hallway and landing, and a heat alarm in the kitchen, all on the ceiling, and interlinked, meaning if one goes off, they all go off.

S2: And Wales, Northern Ireland, the US?

S1: The course didn't read their smoke alarm law. In the US, the Fire Administration says your city, county or state may require a specific type of alarm, and to check with your local fire marshal.

S2: Then the advice.

S1: England's advice, from the government's Fire Kills campaign, is at least one on every level of your home, tested at least monthly. The ideal position is on the ceiling, in the middle of a room, and on the hallway and landing. Bedrooms too, for extra safety. And not in or near kitchens or bathrooms, where smoke or steam can set them off by accident. In a kitchen, you might consider a heat alarm. The Home Office guide for England adds, vacuum your smoke alarms every six months.

S2: And the American version?

S1: The US Fire Administration says every bedroom, outside each separate sleeping area, and every level, on the ceiling or high on the wall, and interconnected. Test every month, replace nine volt batteries at least once a year, and replace the alarms ten years from the manufacture date. The National Fire Protection Association adds at least ten feet, or three metres, from the stove.

S2: And the one that goes off when I make toast?

S1: England's advice and the US Fire Administration both say never take the battery out. For a false alarm while cooking, the US Fire Administration's steps are open a window or door and press the hush button, wave a towel at the alarm, and move the whole alarm several feet away from the kitchen. That last one, on the course's reading, is for a battery alarm, since moving a mains wired one means wiring. And Scotland goes further. Its factsheet says replaceable batteries cannot be used, because the sensors degrade over time, and there have been tragedies where batteries expired or people removed them. So in Scotland it's sealed long life battery alarms, which you can fit yourself, or mains wired ones fitted by a qualified electrician.

S2: So who does what?

S1: Testing, vacuuming and changing a battery are yours. Fitting or moving a sealed battery alarm is yours, on conditions: it's your home to repair, and if you rent, you tell the landlord instead. The maker's instructions. A ceiling you can reach safely. And nothing drilled until the lessons on what's in the wall and what's in an old ceiling. A mains wired alarm is call someone.

There, the battery reasons came to about sixteen percent, again by the course's addition, and the two siting reasons to about nineteen. And the commonest category in those fires, as every year since the year to March twenty eleven, was other. So in the fires that hurt people, batteries count for about as much as siting.

S2: And in America?

S1: The National Fire Protection Association's fact sheet is older and counted differently, US home fires in twenty oh nine to twenty thirteen. Where alarms were present but didn't operate, almost half, forty six percent, had missing or disconnected batteries. Dead batteries caused a quarter. But its categories aren't MHCLG's. It has no category for smoke that didn't reach the alarm. Neither source compares the two counts, and the course can't say how much of the gap that explains. What they show together is that keeping the battery in and putting the alarm where the smoke goes are separate pieces of advice.

S2: And the tumble dryer?

S1: MHCLG names tumble dryers as the source of five hundred and seventy nine accidental dwelling fires in England that year. The course read no UK official page on dryer care, so the routine is the US Fire Administration's. Clean the lint filter before and after each cycle, and make sure the outdoor vent covering opens when it runs. Those are yours. It also says to clean the back of the dryer.
