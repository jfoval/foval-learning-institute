S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Home Repair and Maintenance, lesson three. Taps and toilets.

S2: This one opens things up, so first the safety word.

S1: The course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas, follow your own country's steps in the box at the top of the full lesson. In either country, no flame and no electrical switch. And isolate the water before you open anything up.

S2: And the lesson itself?

S1: It's built to stop one mistake: buying a part before you know what's inside. A washer does nothing for a tap with no washer in it, and an American toilet part is a guess in a British toilet. The course read a method for two kinds. A washer tap has a washer, and washers wear. Southern Water, an English water company, says a dripping tap can have a few causes, but most of the time the washer has degraded. The other kind has a ceramic-disc cartridge instead. Bristan, a UK tap maker, says of one of its own single-lever sink taps that a drip for a short time after use is normal, from water left in the tap. A drip that continues is possibly the ceramic disc valves or cartridge. Its leaflet shows the cartridge cleaned, not a washer changed.

S2: How do I tell which I've got from the outside?

S1: No source the course read gives a test from the outside. What tells you is the paperwork, if you have the maker's leaflet or the maker's name is on the tap. Or what you find inside, with the water isolated. On the course's reading, a small plate, which Southern Water calls the jumper plate, and a washer under the tap top means a washer tap. A cartridge under the cover means ceramic. A nut on its own doesn't settle it, because Bristan's tap has one too. And the only method the course read that doesn't need the maker's leaflet is Southern Water's, for a tap whose cover unscrews to show a hexagonal nut. A tap that doesn't open that way, say a lever held on by a small screw, stays shut until you have its maker's instructions. That rule is the course's.

S2: Walk me through Southern Water's, then.

S1: It's written for a UK washer tap, and on Southern Water's advice it's Yours. Turn off the water at the stop tap. Turn the dripping tap on full, and a few drops may come out before it stops. Unscrew the cover, loosen the nut with a spanner, lift out the tap top, and lay the parts out in the order they came off. Lift out the jumper plate, undo the nut with pliers, and put in a new washer. Then reassemble.

S2: Any trap in that?

S1: One. Don't screw it back together too tightly, because you may damage the new washer. Then the water goes back on at the stop tap, and the drip should have stopped. If it hasn't, Southern Water says hire a plumber qualified to meet the regulations for working safely with drinking water.

S2: You skipped over turning the tap on full.

S1: Southern Water gives it without a reason, and the course uses it as your check. If the tap doesn't stop running after the stop tap's off, the water to that tap isn't off, and you don't open it. WaterSafe says a stop tap may take a few minutes, so give it that. But if a hot tap is still running hard well past that, say ten minutes later, stop. Turn the tap off, put the stop tap back on slowly, and either find out how that hot water is supplied, which comes in lesson seven, or ask a plumber. Southern Water doesn't say why a tap might keep running, and the course won't guess.

S2: What about the ceramic tap?

S1: Bristan's leaflet, for one of its own taps, shows the isolation valve on the pipe feeding it turned off, a small grub screw undone, the handle, the shroud and the nut taken off, and the cartridge lifted out and soaked until the scale's gone. It doesn't show fitting a new cartridge. But that's one maker's method for one maker's tap. For any other ceramic tap, it's Yours, on conditions: you have the maker's instructions for that tap, and any part they name. Without them, it's a plumber's job.

S2: And American faucets?

S1: America's Environmental Protection Agency, EPA, gives no step by step, and the course read none of the online tutorials it mentions, so it has no US faucet method. It's Yours, on conditions: the maker's instructions for your model. A showerhead leaking at the joint is simpler. EPA says some can be fixed by tightening it onto the pipe stem and using pipe tape. That's Yours. But a shower that drips when it isn't in use, EPA calls a valve leak, and sends it to an experienced handyperson or a licensed plumber. Call someone.

S2: Now the toilet that runs.

S1: The course read nothing that says one country's part fits the other's toilet. In an American toilet, the part EPA names is the flapper, the rubber seal that holds water in the tank. EPA says check it periodically and replace it at least every five years. A worn one can make the toilet flush on its own, or leak silently. That's Yours, on conditions: the flapper made for your toilet, and the water isolated first, which is the course's rule.

S2: And a British one?

S1: United Utilities, a water company, names modern push-button cisterns among the biggest causes of leaks in homes. On an older cistern, look at the overflow pipe through the outside wall. If it drips, United Utilities says the cistern is faulty and needs repairing. The course read no source on the parts inside a British cistern, so it gives no method. With the cistern maker's instructions, it's Yours, on conditions. Without them, Call someone, a WaterSafe approved plumber, where United Utilities sends its own customers.

S2: So say I'm in Britain, I've been reading an American website, the dye test shows my push-button cistern leaking into the bowl, and I'm about to buy a flapper.

S1: The flapper advice is EPA's, written for American toilets. Using the dye test on a British cistern is the course's own step, and it's done its job: you know it leaks. But nothing the course read says which part has failed, so a flapper is a guess. The next step is the maker's instructions, or a WaterSafe approved plumber. That's the whole lesson: name the fitting before you buy anything, use the method written for that fitting, and where no method was written for it, don't borrow one.

S2: The full lesson has a Victorian kitchen tap and an Essex bathroom with two faults, both worked through, one water company's allowance for water lost to a leak, and two exercises, one naming your own taps and toilets. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening.
