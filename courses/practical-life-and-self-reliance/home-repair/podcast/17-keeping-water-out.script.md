S2: The usual line first.

S1: This course is education, not a trade qualification, and it teaches no gas work and no wiring. And nothing today has you climb anything. The checks are done from the ground, from a window and inside the house.

S2: So where does the lesson start?

S1: With a damp patch on a bedroom wall. Often the water came from outside. A gutter spilling over, a downpipe leaking against the brick, soil or a patio built up against the wall. There's English government guidance, written for landlords, that names four kinds of damp: condensation, rising damp, traumatic damp from leaks or flooding, and penetrating damp, which it defines as water that gets into the building from outside due to defects in the walls, roofs, windows or floors. That fourth kind is today's.

S2: And why look outside first?

S1: Because those causes are cheap to check. The University of Minnesota Extension, a US university's public advice service, says first do the things that are easy and low cost.

S2: How much water comes off an ordinary roof?

S1: The extension service says that in a one inch rain, twelve hundred and fifty gallons fall on the roof of a two thousand square foot house. The course gives its own rule in metric, and it's the course's arithmetic, not the source's: each millimetre of rain on each square metre of roof is one litre of water.

S2: And all of that leaves at a handful of points.

S1: Which is why the extension service says a downspout without an extender or splash block is worse than no downspout at all. It puts a huge volume of the roof's water in one concentrated spot by the wall.

S2: So what do I actually check on a gutter?

S1: In Britain, the fullest list the course read is Historic England's maintenance checklist. That's England's guidance, written for older homes in England. The course read no equivalent for Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland, though on the course's reading its gutter and pipe checks suit any house. It says clear gutters after heavy leaf fall, at least every autumn and preferably more often. Does rainwater cascade over the gutter? And the most useful single instruction in the lesson: inspect while it is raining. For the pipes, check the back with a mirror, and look for staining or algae on the wall behind.

S2: And in the US?

S1: The University of Georgia's checklist says make sure gutters and downspouts discharge water two to three feet away from the house. Minnesota says extensions should discharge at least four feet beyond the wall. Both are US extension services, neither says why it chose its number, and the course won't guess. Its rule is well away from the wall, and your local extension service or building department may give a figure.

S2: Now, clearing the gutter. That means a ladder.

S1: So it's Yours, on conditions, and the conditions are the ladder lessons', fifteen and sixteen. HSE, Britain's Health and Safety Executive, says in its workplace guidance not to rest a ladder against weak upper surfaces such as plastic gutters, to spend no more than thirty minutes up a ladder at a time, and to keep six metres from an overhead power line. A long run of gutter can fail the thirty minutes on its own, and then it's Call someone, or the job split into short spells with a fresh set up each time. That split is the course's reading.

S2: Is there anything before the ladder?

S1: One check. HSE says asbestos may be in any home built or refurbished before the year two thousand, and it lists asbestos cement products, pipes, flues and roofs among them. It publishes a task sheet for trades on clearing debris from gutters on an asbestos cement roof, and that covers a gutter made of it, or a gutter that simply collects water from such a roof. Its advice to householders is not to try to repair or remove any asbestos materials yourself if you haven't had training for non licensed asbestos work. In the US, the Environmental Protection Agency says if in doubt, treat the material as if it contains asbestos and leave it alone. So if the gutter, or the roof it drains, might be asbestos cement, leave it, and go to lesson nineteen first.

S2: Walk me through a case.

S1: A nineteen fifties brick semi in the East Riding of Yorkshire. A damp patch high on a bedroom wall, in the corner under the eaves. On the next wet day the owner stands in the garden with a coat on and watches, and the gutter overflows in a sheet, straight down the wall over that corner. From an upstairs window there's moss and leaves near the outlet.

S2: Case closed, then.

S1: The likeliest cause, not a proven one. The next heavy rain after clearing it is the test. And before any ladder, the house was built before two thousand, so the owner checks what the gutter is and what drains into it. It's plastic, under clay tiles, so on the course's reading the asbestos cement question doesn't arise. That raises the ladder question instead, because you don't rest a ladder on a plastic gutter. It needs a strong resting point, such as the stand off device lesson fifteen showed, no overhead line within six metres, and a job of minutes, not an hour. Then clearing the outlet is Yours, on conditions, the ladder lessons' conditions. If the owner can't meet them, a gutter clearing firm is the call. If the gutter had been an old cement one, the label would be Call someone.

S2: What about the ground against the wall?

S1: In the US, Minnesota says slope the earth away from the foundation at least one inch per foot for at least six feet. In Britain, a house with a damp proof course has it low in the wall, and Historic England asks that ground levels be at least a hundred and fifty millimetres below any known one. The Property Care Association, the damp proofing firms' trade body, calls ground or a patio above it bridging, and says removing the bridge should hopefully be enough. Note the hopefully: it doesn't promise. Checking is Yours. Regrading soil or lowering a patio is Call someone, on the course's reading.

S2: And the roof itself?

S1: Look at it from the ground. Historic England says to inspect pitched roofs twice a year and after storms or high winds, with binoculars from the ground if you can't get safe access.
