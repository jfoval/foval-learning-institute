S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Home Repair and Maintenance, on the lesson called Gas: the smell and the valve.

S2: And since it's gas, the usual line first.

S1: This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. This lesson is what the gas companies tell you to do when you smell gas, and between the UK and the US it's different at the very first step.

S2: Different how?

S1: In the UK you ventilate and turn the gas off at the meter. In the US you leave first and touch nothing. So the lesson gives each country's steps on their own, and says follow your own country's, never the other one's. We'll go through them briefly. The full lesson has every step in the gas companies' own words.

S2: Great Britain first.

S1: One emergency number for all the networks: oh eight hundred, one one one, nine nine nine. National Gas says its guidelines apply to all gas users, and gives five lines. Don't smoke or light matches. Don't turn electrical switches on or off. Open doors and windows. Turn off the meter at the control handle unless the meter is in the cellar. And call the emergency number.

S2: And Cadent?

S1: Cadent runs the largest network, and gives fuller steps for its own customers. Doors and windows open, the gas off at the mains tap, and if the leak is in a cellar or basement, don't go in, and evacuate the building. No switches on or off, and no flame. Once you're safe, call. And wait for the engineer's all clear before going back inside.

S2: Those two cellar rules aren't the same, are they?

S1: No. Cadent's is about where the leak is. National Gas's is about where the meter is. They come apart when the meter's in the cellar and the smell is upstairs. The course's reading is to take both: if either the smell or the meter is in a cellar or basement, nobody goes down to the valve. And it adds a condition of its own. Only if you can reach the handle without going further into the smell.

S2: Do you stay in or get out?

S1: Neither British page says in so many words to leave, except for a leak in a cellar or basement. But Cadent says call once you're safe, and wait for the all clear before returning inside, which assumes you're outside. So the course's reading, for Great Britain, is switches alone, doors and windows open, the meter off if nothing stops you, then everyone out, the call made from outside, and nobody back in until the engineer says so.

S2: Northern Ireland has its own version.

S1: Its own number, oh eight hundred, oh oh two, oh oh one. The two pages the course read, SSE Airtricity and Phoenix Energy, both put the call first. Then, in SSE's order. The gas off at the meter, unless the meter is in a cellar or basement. If the smell is in the cellar or basement, get out of the building. Put out naked flames, and no smoking. Open doors and windows. Check whether an appliance has been left on unlit, or a pilot light has gone out. And no electrical switches on or off, including door entry systems. Both pages say never leave it to anyone else to call, and let the engineer straight in.

S2: Now the US.

S1: Con Edison, in New York: leave immediately and take others with you. Then call nine one one or the utility. Even if the odour isn't very strong, you still leave first. Don't assume someone else has already called. And don't light a match, smoke, flip a switch, ring a doorbell, or touch appliances or electronics, including your phone, because, in its words, doing so can produce sparks that might cause the gas to explode.

S2: The phone. I'd have got that wrong.

S1: It's the one page read that gives that reason. SoCalGas says evacuate and call from a safe location, and don't attempt to control the leak. Atmos says leave, and call from a safe distance. None of those three leak pages tells you to open windows or turn anything at the meter.

S2: So in the US you just never touch the meter.

S1: The lesson won't put it that way. SoCalGas has a separate earthquake and emergency page telling its customers to turn off the meter if they smell gas, and only if it's safe to do so. American utilities don't all say the same thing. Your own utility's page decides, and the course read three. So find yours now, and copy its emergency number from its website or your bill.

S2: Now a case.

S1: A split level house in Connecticut. The teenage son smells gas on the lower level, where the meter and the furnace are. A cousin visiting from England says, go down and turn it off at the meter, then open the windows. That's what we do at home.

S2: Which is right, in England.

S1: In Great Britain. This is Connecticut. Nobody goes down, and the windows stay as they are. Everyone leaves, now. The son doesn't stop to switch the light off, and nobody rings the next door bell on the way out. Then, from a safe place, whoever has a phone calls nine one one or the gas utility, rather than assuming someone else has.

S2: And the job labels?

S1: In Great Britain or Northern Ireland, turning the valve is yours, on conditions. In the US, on every leak page read, the meter is call someone, unless your own utility's page says otherwise. The call is yours, in both.

S2: What about when you can't smell anything?

S1: Natural gas has no smell of its own. Con Edison explains that a chemical called mercaptan is added, and many people compare it to rotten eggs. The smell is the alarm, and it can fail. SoCalGas says you may miss it if your sense of smell is diminished, or a cold has blocked it, or you've got used to it. And the odorant itself can be lost, which it calls odour fade. Atmos says how often: in certain rare situations. That's the US utilities' account of their own pipes. The UK pages the course read don't discuss it.

S2: So you listen as well as sniff.

S1: SoCalGas lists hissing or whistling, and dirt or water blowing into the air, among other signs. If you suspect a leak you can't smell, Con Edison says still find a phone away from the area and call. It also says most carbon monoxide detectors don't detect combustible gases. A carbon monoxide alarm is not a gas alarm.
