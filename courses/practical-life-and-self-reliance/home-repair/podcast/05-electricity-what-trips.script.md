S2: So. Nine at night, half the house goes dark, and a switch in a cupboard has dropped. What's it telling me?

S1: Either the circuit had more asked of it than it could safely carry, which you can usually clear, or something is faulty. And the devices in the box do two different jobs. Breakers and fuses look after the wiring. Southern California Edison, a US utility, says a breaker trips when a circuit overloads or shorts, and that this helps prevent fire and possible electrocution. Electrical Safety First, a UK charity, says the fuse in a British plug blows when too much current is drawn, which stops the cable and appliance overheating. And the US Consumer Product Safety Commission, CPSC, said that breakers and fuses function primarily to prevent the home wiring system from causing a fire. Primarily the wiring. Not you.

S2: Then what looks after me?

S1: In the UK, the residual current device, an RCD. In the US, the ground fault circuit interrupter, a GFCI. The lesson sets a puzzle. A kettle's flex is worn, and the live wire is touching the metal body. You pick it up with one hand while the other is on the tap. The kettle is drawing no more current than usual. Does the breaker trip?

S2: If nothing's overloaded, I suppose it has nothing to act on.

S1: That's it. And the device that does act doesn't know you're there. On CPSC's fact sheet, a GFCI compares the current going out with the current coming back. If they differ by as little as six thousandths of an amp, it cuts the power faster than a blink of an eye. Some current has found another path, in CPSC's example a person touching the appliance and a tap. The British page explaining the RCD didn't load when the course tried to read it, so the lesson gives you CPSC's account and doesn't claim a British source says the same.

CPSC says panel boxes are usually live even with the main breakers off. And the Health and Safety Executive's guidance for electrical workers doesn't recommend volt sticks for proving anything dead.

S1: It is, and pressing it is yours, in both countries. In the UK, HSE's leaflet for workplaces says use it regularly, without saying how often. S1: CPSC says it isn't working or wasn't installed correctly, and to contact a qualified electrician. For an RCD that doesn't trip, Electrical Safety First says get a registered electrician to investigate. CPSC also allows that consumers with adequate knowledge and skills may fit a GFCI receptacle. The course doesn't teach that, because it's wiring, so for you it's Call someone. That's the course's scope decision, not a claim that CPSC is wrong. And before you press, know what goes off, such as the freezer or equipment someone depends on for their health.

S2: Now the night it trips. What comes first?

S1: Is it you, or the network? National Grid Electricity Distribution says speak to your neighbours, or at night check the street lights. If the whole street is dark, both network operators the course read give one oh five as the number for a power cut. If it's only your house, National Grid and Edison share a shape. Take the load off first, by switching off or unplugging what's on the dead circuit, then put the switch back on. None of the sources says to keep trying.

S2: And if it trips again?

S1: Edison says a breaker that trips again could be tripping because of too many lamps and appliances plugged in, a damaged cord or plug, a short in a receptacle, switch or fixture, or faulty wiring. The first two you can see and deal with without opening anything. The last two are in the walls and fittings. So the course draws its line through the middle of the list. The first two are yours. The last two are Call someone, and so is any trip you can't pin on the first two.

S2: So if I unplug everything, reset it, and it goes again straight away?

S1: Then the plug-in half of the list is ruled out, and it's an electrician's job, with the device left off. Treating that one repeat as the signal is this course's reading of the sources, not a sentence any of them contains.
