The rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and between US states and cities, and they change.

S2: Where does it start?

S1: With damp going wrong in two directions. People scrub the mould and never find the water, so it comes back. Or they pay for a treatment aimed at a cause the house doesn't have. GOV.UK has guidance written for landlords and housing professionals in England, and it sorts damp into four kinds. Condensation, which it calls the most common form of damp. Penetrating damp from outside, which was last lesson. Traumatic damp, from leaks. And rising damp from the ground, which it says is usually found in older properties and is often misdiagnosed.

S2: Here's a test case. A mouldy patch low on a bedroom wall, behind a chest of drawers, in January. That's rising damp, surely. It's low.

S1: GOV.UK's own definition of condensation names low points on walls behind sofas or wardrobes. So height on the wall doesn't sort one from the other. Winter, behind furniture, a cold outside wall: that fits condensation. It's a first suspicion, not a diagnosis, because the kinds can combine.

S2: Where does the water come from?

S1: From living in the house. GOV.UK names showering, drying laundry and cooking. Whether it ends up on a wall depends on whether it can get out, which is ventilation, and on how cold the surfaces are, which is insulation and heating.

S2: That could easily turn into, you're cooking wrong.

S1: The lesson deals with that before any tip. The GOV.UK guidance says it is totally unreasonable to blame damp and mould in the home on lifestyle choices. Everyday tasks will produce moisture, and it puts the fundamental cause in the building. It sets a limit, too: tenants cannot be expected to reduce moisture levels if their home does not enable them to do so. Within that, the small changes its checklist has landlords raise with tenants, like a gap between furniture and outside walls and using the extractor fans, are yours as far as the home allows.

S2: Now the mould. How much can I clean myself?

S1: The two countries word it differently. The US Environmental Protection Agency gives a size: less than about ten square feet, and in most cases you can handle the job yourself. That's a little under a square metre, on the course's conversion. GOV.UK gives no measurement. It says most tenants could reasonably be expected to remove condensation and very small amounts of mould using an appropriate mould and mildew cleaner, and that larger areas of mould should only be addressed by qualified professionals. So a small patch on a hard surface is Yours, on conditions: small enough, on a surface you can clean without pulling anything apart, and with the moisture found. If you rent, it's yours too, and reported to the landlord. Anything larger, or behind wallpaper or panelling, is Call someone. And whatever the size, find the water. EPA's whole argument is in one sentence: the key to mold control is moisture control.

S2: Then rising damp, where the sources stop agreeing.

S1: How often it happens, and whether the usual treatment works, are disputed among surveyors, damp proofing firms and people who look after old buildings. It's a contested empirical question, so the lesson gives each side in its own words and doesn't decide it.

S2: Start with the case that it's real.

S1: The Property Care Association is the damp proofing trade body. Its account is that rising damp is ground water drawn up through brickwork by capillary action, normally stopped by a damp proof course. Where the barrier is absent or has broken down, it says it's often possible to install a remedial damp proof course. But its first remedy isn't a treatment. Where the course is bridged, by raised ground or a patio, removing the bridge should hopefully be enough. Two chartered surveyors, writing in the Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors' journal in twenty twenty, summarised a twenty seventeen doctoral thesis as concluding that rising damp is a real phenomenon that warrants treatment, and that the contemporary method of damp proofing, installed as it would be on a real construction site, does provide effective control. They also said rising damp could benefit from greater academic investigation. The course read their summary, not the thesis.

S2: And the case that it's rare?

S1: The sceptics' words here come from one report in The Architects' Journal, from June two thousand and nine. Stephen Boniface, a former chairman of the construction arm of that same Institution, said true rising damp is a myth, that chemically injected damp proof courses are a complete waste of money, and that the most likely causes of damp are moisture penetration and, most commonly, condensation. And Jeff Howell, a bricklayer and author, said, in the article's words, that trials in the laboratory confirm the falsehood. The course read the article, not his book.

S2: Those are years apart.

S1: And the lesson asks you to hold that both ways. The sceptics' quotations are from two thousand and nine, and the course didn't read any later statement of their case. The thesis is known only through the surveyors' summary, and the course didn't read any assessment of it by the other side.

S2: Is there anything both sides signed?

S1: A joint position statement from twenty twenty two, by the surveyors' Institution, Historic England and the trade body and adopted by conservation bodies. It never uses the words rising damp. It says moisture in old solid walled buildings may not be down to a construction failure. It says salts can influence a resistance meter. And it says deal with the obvious defects first, including ventilation, ground levels and lifestyle, then allow time for monitoring, and reassess before other treatments.

S1: The statement doesn't define it. Elsewhere its list for surveyors says occupation patterns can influence the moisture within the building, and it says nothing about blame. And GOV.UK's line stands beside it: blaming damp and mould on lifestyle choices is totally unreasonable.
