S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Home Repair and Maintenance, lesson two. Water in: supply, leaks and frozen pipes.

S2: The usual word first. What is this course?

S1: Education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas, follow your own country's steps in the box at the top of the full lesson, because Britain's and America's differ. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. And nothing in this lesson opens a pipe.

S2: So what does it do?

S1: It follows the water from the street to the taps. Most of what goes wrong with it happens where nobody looks. Under a lawn, in a crawl space, inside a toilet, in a pipe on an outside wall on the coldest night of the year.

S2: Start with the leak under the lawn. Whose problem is that?

S1: In England and Wales, the Consumer Council for Water splits the pipe at your property boundary. The water company is responsible for the main in the street, the pipe from the main to your boundary, the external stop tap in the footpath, and the meter if you have one. From the boundary inwards, the homeowner is responsible. If one pipe serves several properties, it's the joint responsibility of all the owners connected to it. This is general information, not legal advice, and the rules differ between the UK nations and between US states and cities, and they change. The course didn't research Scotland or Northern Ireland, and in the US it varies by utility, so ask yours.

S2: And if the leak's on my side?

S1: Call someone. A plumber, and the Consumer Council says arranging the repair is the homeowner's responsibility. If you rent, the person to tell is your landlord or whoever owns the home.

S2: What about weak flow? How do I know if my pressure's normal?

S1: WaterSafe, the plumbers' register run for the UK water industry, gives a test at the sink. With every other tap and appliance off, the first tap in the house should fill a four and a half litre bucket in thirty seconds. But it's only a guide, for a home with a suitable single service pipe, so a slow bucket doesn't prove the company's at fault. It says something's worth asking your water company about. The test itself is Yours.

S2: Now drips. Everyone ignores a drip.

S1: So here's a guess to make. A tap dripping once a second, day and night, for a year. How much water?

S2: A few hundred litres?

S1: Most people guess low. The US Environmental Protection Agency's WaterSense programme says more than three thousand gallons a year. Those are US gallons, so more than eleven thousand litres. Southern Water, an English water company, says a slow drip can waste up to ten thousand litres a year, though it doesn't define slow, and up to makes that a ceiling, not an average.

S2: Wait, a US gallon isn't a British gallon?

S1: No. On the course's conversion, a US gallon is about three point eight litres. An imperial gallon is about four and a half, and on the course's reading, that's WaterSafe's one-gallon bucket. Mix them up and your arithmetic is out by a fifth.

S2: How do I prove there's a leak before I start hunting for it?

S1: EPA gives two tests, and neither opens anything. The meter test: read your water meter before and after two hours with no water used at all. If the meter changes at all, you probably have a leak. Nobody flushes, nothing runs, and you need a meter. The dye test: a few drops of food colouring in the toilet tank, no flush, and if colour shows in the bowl after ten minutes, you have a leak. EPA wrote that for American toilets. Using it on a British cistern is the course's own step, because the question, does water get into the bowl without a flush, is the same whatever's inside. Both tests are Yours.

S2: Now a pipe freezes. On or off with the water while I thaw it?

S1: That depends on your country, and it's the lesson's clearest case of the course's question: law or advice, whose, and where? Both sides are advice. In the UK, the Met Office, and the Consumer Council for Water for England and Wales, turn the stop tap off first, then open the taps, and warm the pipe slowly. Hot water bottles or towels soaked in hot water, or on the Council's list a hairdryer on warm. In the US, the American Red Cross, a charity, says keep the faucet open, because running water helps melt the ice, and it doesn't mention turning the supply off. It lists a heating pad, a hair dryer, a space heater kept away from anything flammable, or hot towels.

S2: Which one's right?

S1: The course calls neither country's order wrong, and won't invent reasons the pages don't give. Neither UK page says in so many words why the stop tap goes off first. Follow your own country's. But one rule all three sources give: no naked flame, no blowtorch, ever. The Met Office says direct heat may damage the pipe and could start a fire. Thawing a pipe you can reach is Yours, though the Council's steps also say to contact a registered plumber before it bursts. If it's already split, it's lesson one's burst-pipe order, then Call someone.

S2: Put it together for me.

S1: A family of four in Missouri, in an older single-storey house over a crawl space. Their February bill says over fourteen thousand US gallons, above EPA's line of twelve thousand for a family of four in a colder month. They do the meter test on a Sunday morning, and it moves. They do the dye test on both toilets, and the bowls stay clear. Every tap and every pipe under the sinks is dry.

S2: So where's the leak?

S1: The Red Cross's list of pipes most at risk of freezing includes outdoor hose bibs, the outside taps, and pipes in crawl spaces. The father finds a hose still on the back wall from the autumn, and the inside valve to that bib never closed. Under the house, wet soil. The pipe split in a January freeze.

S2: And then?

S1: He closes the inside valve to the bib, the Red Cross's own advice, and it opens nothing, so that's Yours. Two more quiet hours, and the meter stays still. The split pipe is Call someone, a licensed plumber. What carries over is the order: prove the leak, rule places out from the cheapest check up, and let the meter tell you when you've found it.
