S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Home Repair and Maintenance, on the lesson called Who may do what: reserved work, and the argument about licensing.

S2: The usual lines first.

S1: This course is education, not a trade qualification, and it teaches no gas work and no wiring. And most of today is law, so this is general information, not legal advice. The rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and between US states and cities, and they change.

S2: So. Am I allowed to add a socket?

S1: That sounds like one question. The law in England, Wales and Scotland asks two. Must the work comply, meaning meet a legal standard, whoever does it? And must someone be told? A job can need no notice at all and still have to meet the standard.

S2: Start with England.

S1: Every piece of electrical work in an English home must meet requirement P one, in Part P of the Building Regulations. But only three kinds have to be notified. A new circuit, a replacement consumer unit, and any addition or alteration to existing circuits in a special location, which is the space around a bath or shower, or a room with a pool or a sauna heater. A kitchen is not one.

S2: So a socket on an existing kitchen circuit, in England?

S1: No notice, but it still has to meet P one. Cross into Wales and the same job is notifiable, because Wales kept an older, longer list, and a kitchen is on it. A registered electrician certifies it, or, if you do the work yourself, you tell building control before you start.

S2: And Scotland?

S1: Scotland uses building warrants, and its rules free any work to or in a house from a warrant, with exceptions such as altering the structure. A flat isn't a house under that definition. And exempt from a warrant is not exempt from the standards. Standard four point five, that an installation must not become a source of fire, applies either way. Northern Ireland's Building Regulations have no Part covering electrical safety, and Electrical Safety First, a UK charity, strongly recommends a competent registered electrician. That's advice, from a charity.

S2: Now America.

S1: Still general information, and notice the change in kind. In England, registration is a way of telling building control, not a permission to work. In the US places read, a licence decides who may do the work at all, and an owner's exemption is the way round it.

S2: What do the exemptions look like?

S1: Real, but conditional. Washington needs no licence for electrical work at your own residence, but the owner applies for an exemption, signs an affidavit before starting, and gets a permit and an inspection. And the exemption goes if the work is on a new building for rent, sale or lease, or the property is offered for sale within twelve months after obtaining it. In New York City, electrical work has to be done by a licensed electrical contractor, whoever owns the house.

S2: So where the law lets me, will the course teach me the wiring?

S1: No, and the reason is what a page can do. A page can't watch you or check the result. That's a teaching judgement. It isn't a claim that the work is unlawful where the law allows it, and it isn't a side in either argument that follows.

S2: Which is?

S1: Whether licensing tradespeople improves the quality and safety of their work, and at what cost. The course gives each side in its own words. A twenty fifteen US government report says the empirical research does not find large improvements in quality or health and safety from more stringent licensing. In only two out of the twelve studies was greater licensing associated with quality improvements. But it also says that, when designed and implemented carefully, licensing can offer important health and safety protections to consumers.

S2: Then the case for.

S1: SELECT, which calls itself Scotland's electrical trade association, says lack of regulation means anyone can claim to be an electrician, and these unqualified individuals put the people of Scotland at risk of injury and death. It asks for protection of the title, not a licence for every job. The Utah Plumbing and Heating Contractors Association argues for licensing proper, for plumbers. When plumbing is done wrong, the problems do not always show up immediately, and licensing creates a standard. Both answer the charge that a trade gains from limiting entry. Utah says advocating for the industry is its purpose, and that this is not about protecting territory for the sake of protecting territory, it is about protecting public safety. SELECT's then managing director says it has taken steps to ensure a variety of routes to qualification, so as not to leave people behind.

S2: And the case against licensing, or for less of it.

S1: The US Federal Trade Commission, speaking to Congress in twenty seventeen from the perspective of competition, says licensing restrictions may hamper entry, yet offer few, if any, significant consumer benefits, and may lead to higher prices, lower quality, and reduced consumer access. Its first test for any licence is whether there are significant and non speculative consumer health and safety issues, or other legitimate public policy purposes, that warrant it. The Institute for Justice, which says it believes all people have the right to earn an honest living without protectionist government interference, says licensing, by limiting entry, leads to higher earnings for licensees, and consumers pay the price through higher costs, limited access to services, or both. Both concede ground. The FTC says licensing, used appropriately, can protect consumers from harm. The Institute reports evidence that early twentieth century licensing of midwives reduced maternal and infant mortality.

S2: Where do they meet?

S1: Both cases accept licensing where there's a real health and safety risk, and so does the federal report. They disagree about how often that's true of a given trade, what counts as evidence, and who should have to prove it. Each side has a stake, and says so. Logic and Argument lesson eight gives the rule: an interest is a reason to check the evidence harder, not to call the claim false, and that applies to both sides equally.

S2: What would settle it?

S1: Comparisons of injury, fire or failure rates between places with different licensing rules for the same trade, other differences controlled for, or harm counts that separate qualified from unqualified work. Nothing this course read does either for electrical work in homes. And whether householders should do their own electrical work is a value question. The course reaches no verdict on that, or on licensing. It stops there.
