S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Home Repair and Maintenance, on the lesson called Ladders and working at height.

S2: The usual line first.

S1: This course is education, not a trade qualification, and it teaches no gas work and no wiring. And nothing in this lesson has you climb anything. Parts of it describe British and American workplace law, so this is general information, not legal advice. The rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and between US states and cities, and they change.

S2: Why a whole lesson on ladders?

S1: Because the most ordinary tool in a house puts a great many people in hospital. In the United States, one study counted about one hundred and thirty six thousand people a year treated in emergency departments for ladder injuries, between nineteen ninety and two thousand and five. That count comes from the consumer product side of the national system, and NIOSH says those estimates exclude work related injuries. So it's a great many people hurt on ladders off the job.

S2: And the rules for using one?

S1: Written almost entirely for workplaces. In Great Britain the law is the Work at Height Regulations two thousand and five, and there's no duty in them on a householder doing their own job at home. The detailed practice is the Health and Safety Executive's guide, and a short guide called LA four five five that the Ladder Association wrote with HSE, which says it's for employers. In the US, the rule the lesson reads is OSHA's ladder standard for construction, and its duties fall on employers. OSHA's QuickCard on portable ladders is workplace advice, not regulation.

S2: So legally, at home, I can do what I like.

S1: In law, neither country's ladder rules bind you on your own house. In advice, HSE's and OSHA's are still the most carefully specified ladder practice the course read. And RoSPA, the Royal Society for the Prevention of Accidents, a charity, gives the same core numbers for use at home. The course follows the advice. A short, low risk job on a ladder at home is yours, on conditions, and the conditions are the rest of the lesson.

S2: Start with whether to use one at all.

S1: HSE's guide opens by saying ladders and stepladders are not banned under health and safety law. They can be a sensible option for low risk, short duration tasks, though not automatically your first choice. HSE says short duration is not the deciding factor. Then a guide for time. If you'd be up a leaning ladder or stepladder for more than thirty minutes at a time, HSE recommends other equipment. And it wants ladders on firm, level, clean ground.

S2: Now the numbers.

S1: The angle first. HSE's is the one in four rule: one unit out for every four up, which it calls a seventy five degree angle. OSHA's rule for employers measures along the ladder instead: the foot about a quarter of the ladder's working length out from the top support. By the course's own arithmetic, that's about seventy six degrees for HSE's and about seventy five and a half for OSHA's, within half a degree of each other.

S2: And at the top?

S1: HSE says don't work off the top three rungs, and OSHA's workplace QuickCard says the same. If a job needs the top rungs, the ladder's too short for it.

S2: What about hands?

S1: HSE says keep three points of contact when climbing, and wherever possible at the work position. OSHA's rule for employers asks less: at least one hand on the ladder going up or down. Its own workplace QuickCard advises three points, two hands and a foot or two feet and a hand. The lesson says follow the card. And for overreaching, HSE's test: keep your belt buckle, or navel, within the stiles, the two side rails. If the next bit of the job would take you outside them, climb down and move the ladder.

S2: Is someone holding the bottom enough?

S1: HSE lists the ways of securing a ladder in order. Tie it, both stiles. If that's not practical, a ladder stability device. If not that, wedge it. And if none of those can be done, foot it, meaning someone at the bottom. HSE calls footing the last resort. RoSPA's home advice adds: consider having another person present, especially with leaning ladders. So the course teaches HSE's order, and RoSPA's point that it's worth not being alone. What it won't do is treat someone at the bottom as what makes a ladder safe.

S2: Give me a case.

S1: A two storey house on the Gulf Coast. A man wants to paint the frame of an upstairs window from a leaning extension ladder. The only place to rest it near the window is the plastic gutter. And a service cable runs from the street to the eaves, about two metres, six or seven feet, to one side of the window.

S2: The gutter's easy. HSE says don't rest a ladder against plastic gutters.

S1: Right. The Ladder Association's guide shows a stand off device, fitted by its maker's instructions, to rest it on the wall instead. The cable's harder. HSE says don't work within six metres horizontally of any overhead power line, unless it has been made dead or it is protected with insulation. Those are the only two ways out. HSE doesn't say whether the covering on a cable like this counts as protected with insulation, and the course read nothing that does, so its reading is to treat it as a line until its owner says otherwise.

S2: And in the US, where he actually lives?

S1: OSHA's workplace QuickCard says look for overhead lines before handling a ladder, and avoid a metal one near them, but gives no distance. The one federal rule on overhead lines the course read is part of OSHA's electrical rules for employers in general industry. Construction work has rules of its own, which the course didn't read. It keeps an unqualified worker at least ten feet from an unguarded, energized line of fifty kilovolts or below. So there are two distances, each written for workplaces: HSE's six metres, British guidance for workplaces, and OSHA's ten feet, a rule for employers. Neither binds him at home. Two metres is inside both.

S2: So what does he do?

S1: Not use the ladder there. Even with the gutter solved, the cable puts the job inside both distances. On this course's reading, a householder can't make someone else's cable dead, so it's call someone, and the first call is to whoever owns or runs the line, to ask whether it can be made dead or protected for the job. The course read no utility's page on how that's arranged.

It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.
