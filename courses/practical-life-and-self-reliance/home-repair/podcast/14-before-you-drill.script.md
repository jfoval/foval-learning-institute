S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Home Repair and Maintenance, on the lesson called Before you drill: cables, pipes and what's in the wall.

S2: The usual line first.

S1: This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas, follow your own country's steps in the box at the top of the full lesson, because Britain's and America's differ. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. And if a breaker, an RCD or a GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician.

S2: Where does the lesson start?

S1: With a survey. Electrical Safety First, a UK charity that campaigns on electrical safety, asked one thousand electricians about DIY work they'd been called to put right. The most common mistakes they reported were cutting through power leads, drilling into wiring, and trying to repair an electrical item while it was switched on. ESF doesn't give the survey's date, and the lesson says the list is what electricians reported, not a measure of how often DIY goes wrong. But a wall hides what's in it.

S2: So I buy a detector and scan the wall.

S1: A cable and pipe detector is sold for exactly that. The course found no official UK or US consumer page on detectors, so it uses a maker's guidance for its own devices. That's Bosch. Rings and watches off, the device flat on the wall, your other hand on the wall for grounding, several passes over the same place. And then the step that carries the whole thing: switch on electrical items while you scan, such as a light or a vacuum cleaner, because, in Bosch's words, a cable must have a current in order to be picked up by the device.

S2: Here's a test, then. Bright afternoon, every light off, nothing plugged in. I scan and get green everywhere. Is the wall clear?

S1: No. A cable with nothing drawing on it gives the detector nothing to find. Bosch's own summary of a green light is nothing was detected and you're good to go, but read that beside the current step. Green means nothing carrying current was found where you scanned. It doesn't mean there's no cable. Bosch doesn't say which items, so the course's reading is the room's lights and a lamp in the nearest socket. And your own detector's instructions decide.

S2: Do cables run anywhere predictable?

S1: Often straight up, down or across from sockets and switches. For UK homes, ESF puts that as a requirement. But the course didn't read the standard behind it, and ESF itself says that a quality cable detector can help track buried cables before you start work. The course read nothing about American walls. So in either country, the wall above, below and beside a socket or switch is the likeliest place for a cable, but not the only one.

S2: Then the power goes off before I drill?

S1: After the scan, yes. Bosch says switch off the power supply completely before you start drilling. For any DIY near wiring, ESF says switch off at your fuseboard, which is the consumer unit, and where possible only use battery powered tools. Then ESF's check that it's off: plug an appliance in or try switching on lights. That's ESF's test for non electrical work like drilling. It is not a way of proving a circuit dead for work on the wiring, which needs a proper tester and training. And ESF is British. In a US home, trying the lights is the course's reading of ESF's test, not an American body's advice.

S2: And the main switch is mine to touch?

S1: Yes. Everything behind the cover still isn't. The US Consumer Product Safety Commission says panel boxes are usually electrically live, even with the main circuit breakers turned off. And HSE's guidance for electrical workers doesn't recommend volt sticks for proving anything dead.

S2: What if I hit something anyway?

S1: A cable first. Stop, and leave that circuit off. A damaged cable in a wall is wiring, and the course's line is call someone, an electrician, whether or not anything tripped. If anyone has had a shock, First Aid and CPR lesson two, Danger, the call, and recognising cardiac arrest, has what comes first: the power off before you touch them, then the call.

S2: A gas pipe?

S1: That one differs by country. In Great Britain, lesson nine has what National Gas says: call the emergency number, day or night, even if no gas seems to be escaping. For Northern Ireland the course read no page on a struck pipe, so its reading is to follow lesson nine's Northern Ireland steps, which put the call first, and the number is in the box at the top of the lesson. In the US it read no utility page on a pipe hit inside a house either. Its reading is to treat it as a leak: leave, and call nine one one or your gas utility once you're outside. And don't try to deal with the pipe. SoCalGas, a US utility, says do not attempt to control the leak or repair a damaged pipe or meter.

S2: What about the wall itself?

S1: In Great Britain, HSE lists drilling into a wall among the things that can disturb asbestos. It says any building built or refurbished before the year two thousand may contain it, and buildings constructed after two thousand are unlikely to. Its guide, written for people at work, says to stop work immediately if you come across a material you think may contain it. In the US, EPA says you generally can't tell whether a material contains asbestos by looking at it, unless it's labelled. If in doubt, treat it as if it does and leave it alone. So anything you're unsure of in an older home, you don't drill. That stop is the course's line, built on HSE's and EPA's advice.

S2: Give me a case.

S1: A nineteen fifties house in the American Southwest. The homeowner wants a blind above a bedroom window. There's a light switch beside the window. The wall above has the same swirled texture as the ceiling. It's a bright afternoon, so the lights are off and nothing's plugged in on that wall. She scans above the window with a borrowed detector, gets green all the way, switches off the main breaker, and picks up the drill.

S2: Well, the scan told her nothing about cables. No current.

S1: That's one. What's the other?

S2: The texture? She can't tell what it is.

S1: That's the other, and it comes first. A nineteen fifties house, a textured finish she can't identify. EPA says if in doubt, treat it as asbestos and leave it alone. That stop comes before the scan matters at all. The blind waits until she knows what the texture is, which is lesson nineteen's subject.

S2: Anything else?

S1: She went straight from the breaker to the drill with no check that the power was off, and nothing for her eyes or the dust. But the texture stops the job before either matters. And that's the order of the lesson. What the wall is made of first. Then the scan, with current in the cables. Then the power off. Then the drill.

S2: The full lesson also has what to do if you hit a water pipe, a shelf put up start to finish on the Isle of Wight, what the dust from a plastered wall might really be, and a plan for a wall of your own. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.
