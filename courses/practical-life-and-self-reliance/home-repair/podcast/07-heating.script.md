S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Home Repair and Maintenance, on the lesson called Heating, boilers, radiators and furnaces.

S2: This one's near gas, so the course's word first.

S1: This course is education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. Everything this lesson gives you happens on the water side or the air side: the radiators, the pressure gauge, the filling controls the maker shows you, the filter slot. Anything behind the boiler's casing, or the furnace's burner, is somebody else's.

S2: Start in Britain. What have I got?

S1: The Energy Saving Trust, the UK's government-funded energy advice body, sorts boilers into three. A combi doesn't use a hot water cylinder; it heats water as you run the tap. A system boiler has a separate cylinder to store hot water. And a regular, or heat-only, boiler is like a system boiler with two extra header tanks storing cold water, usually in the loft.

S2: Then the gauge. I've got one and I've no idea what it should say.

S1: The number is a maker's. Worcester Bosch, for its own sealed systems, says between one and one and a half bar. Vaillant and Ideal give much the same, cold. Baxi gives one to two as its normal operating range. None of them is a standard, and none speaks for anyone else's boiler. So the number for yours is the one in your boiler's manual. And Worcester Bosch draws two lines. A rise of more than one bar when the heating is up to temperature means the expansion vessel may need repressurising, which will require a service engineer. And topping up is usually needed once or twice a year. Much more often, contact your installer.

S2: So how do I top it up?

S1: The lesson is careful here. Topping up is yours only on conditions, and only by your own boiler's user instructions. Those instructions have to give the user a filling method, and you follow them to your boiler's own figure. The filling controls have to be clearly identifiable, under or built into the boiler. The boiler is off and cold. And it's your home to repair, or your landlord agrees. If you rent, ask the landlord first.

S2: And if the loop is somewhere else, or I can't tell which valve is which?

S1: Then it isn't yours. Ideal's own guide is written for combis only. It says a system or heat-only boiler with its filling loop somewhere else goes to your installer or a heating engineer, and do not operate unidentified valves underneath the boiler. The lesson shows two makers' accounts only to show what such instructions look like, not to replace yours.

S2: What does the law say about the boiler itself?

S1: This is general information, not legal advice, and the rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and between US states and cities, and they change. In Great Britain the law requires anyone doing gas work to be competent, and anyone doing it as a business to be on the Gas Safe Register. Northern Ireland has its own regulations, with a regulation that mirrors that one. What a householder may do is what the appliance's user instructions give the user to do. So a repair, or a fault code the manual doesn't tell you to clear, is Call someone.

S2: Radiators, then. One's cold. Do I bleed it?

S1: First, where is it cold? Worcester Bosch says radiators that need bleeding are colder at the top than the bottom, because air rises and gathers there. Hot at the top and cold at the bottom could mean sludge has built up, and you might need the system cleaned. Bleeding does nothing for sludge.

S2: And bleeding itself is mine?

S1: Yours, on Worcester Bosch's and Vaillant's advice, for a radiator on a wet system with a bleed valve at the top, if it's your home to repair or your landlord agrees. In outline: heating off, and wait for the radiators to cool, Vaillant says about an hour, or two to be cautious. Key in, cloth under the valve, open slowly anticlockwise until it hisses, and close it when water runs, not too tight. Then check the gauge before the heating goes back on, because you've let water out with the air.

S2: Give me a case where it isn't that simple.

S1: A nineteen nineties house in Northamptonshire with a system boiler. The back bedroom radiator is hot along the top and cold along the bottom. The owner bled it anyway. Nothing hissed; water came straight out. And since October the pressure has needed topping up about every fortnight.

S2: That sounds like one problem.

S1: The lesson asks you to take them apart. The radiator is Worcester Bosch's sign of sludge, and a bleed that found no air rules out the one thing bleeding fixes. That's an installer or heating engineer. Topping up every fortnight is far more often than the once or twice a year that Worcester Bosch and Ideal call normal. Something is losing water, and finding it is Call someone. What the owner shouldn't do is keep topping up, because each top-up hides the leak for another fortnight. The course suspects the two may be connected, and says that's its own reading, not the makers'.

S2: Thermostats. I'll admit I turn it right up when I come in cold.

S1: The Energy Saving Trust says turning up your room thermostat won't make your home heat any faster. It turns the heating on until the room reaches the set temperature, then off. So on the course's reading, the setting decides when the heating stops, not how hard it works. The Trust suggests the lowest temperature you're comfortable at, for most people eighteen to twenty-one degrees.

S2: Now America.

S1: The Department of Energy's pages, cited as archived copies, put it in two sentences. Furnaces heat air and send it through ducts. Boilers heat water, for hot water or steam heating. The Department says a furnace or boiler should be maintained by a heating system professional, partly because a leaking heat exchanger can mix combustion gases with house air. And for a heat pump, one rule: do not set back its thermostat if that causes the backup heating to come on, because backup heat is usually less efficient and costs more to run.

S2: So what's mine on a furnace?

S1: The filter. ENERGY STAR, the US government's efficiency programme, says inspect, clean or change air filters once a month. The Department of Energy's pages give other intervals, so the course reads them together: check monthly, change it when it's dirty, and at least as often as the maker says. It's yours on conditions, the size your unit takes and the slot your manual shows. Nothing else on a gas furnace comes off for this job.
