S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Home Repair and Maintenance, on the lesson called Tools, dust and fumes: a small kit used safely.

S2: The usual line first.

S1: This course is education, not a trade qualification, and it teaches no gas work and no wiring. And where this lesson touches the law, it's general information, not legal advice. The rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and between US states and cities, and they change.

S2: So whose rules are we talking about today?

S1: Almost all of them were written for people at work. OSHA, the US Occupational Safety and Health Administration, wrote its tools booklet for employers and employees, and its respirator and silica rules bind employers. HSE, the Health and Safety Executive in Great Britain, wrote its dust sheets for employers. The course read no rule on masks or goggles at home, so using this advice in your own house is the course's reading. As the lesson puts it, the dust does not know whether you are being paid.

S2: Start with the tools. What actually hurts people?

S1: OSHA puts it in one sentence. The greatest hazards posed by hand tools result from misuse and improper maintenance. A chisel used as a screwdriver, where the tip may break and fly off. A hammer with a loose, splintered or cracked handle, where the head may fly off. On the course's reading, a tool like that is out of use until it's repaired or replaced.

S2: And power tools?

S1: OSHA's list is mostly habits. Never carry a tool by the cord. Guards must never be removed while the tool is being used. And disconnect tools when changing blades, bits and cutters. OSHA doesn't define disconnect, but a line just above it uses the word for taking a cord out of the socket, so the course reads it as the plug, not the switch. OSHA says nothing about cordless tools, so taking the battery out is the course's reading, with the tool's manual first. A sound power tool, used for its own job by the maker's instructions with the guard on, is yours, on conditions, and OSHA's list is the conditions.

S2: Now dust. Why its own section?

S1: Because it's slow. HSE says construction dust is not just a nuisance; it can seriously damage your health and some types can eventually even kill. It names three kinds. Silica dust, from materials like concrete, mortar and sandstone. Wood dust, MDF included. And general dust, from things like plasterboard. On silica, HSE says the dust that gets deep into your lungs is too fine to see with normal lighting, and that silicosis continues to develop after exposure has stopped and is irreversible.

S2: So a job that looks clean isn't evidence of anything.

S1: Right. And HSE gives the controls in an order, and the order is the lesson. First, make less dust: different materials, less powerful tools, other methods. Second, stop it getting into the air. Water, the whole time, because HSE says just wetting the material beforehand does not work. Or extraction on the tool to a unit of the right class, not a general vacuum. Third, and only then, a respirator. HSE calls it the last line of protection.

S2: Does it matter where you work?

S1: HSE says the more enclosed a space, the more the dust will build up. But it also says do not assume that dust levels will be low when working outside with high energy tools. So outside helps, and it doesn't let you skip the rest.

S2: Tell me about the masks.

S1: In the UK, HSE says FFP one, two and three respirators reduce the dust you breathe by factors of four, ten and twenty, and its sheets set twenty for construction and wood dust. In the US, NIOSH certifies respirators, and an N ninety five filters at least ninety five percent of airborne particles. Those are different measures, and the lesson doesn't convert one into the other. In the UK, read the FFP grade. In the US, read the N ninety five and the NIOSH approval.

S2: Let's do the case.

S1: A man in New Mexico with a full beard is laying a short path. On a cold, windy Saturday he cuts concrete pavers in his attached garage with the big door pulled most of the way down, using a borrowed handheld masonry saw, cutting dry. He's wearing a one strap mask from a box that says nuisance dust. Afterwards he plans to strip an old door in the same garage with a tin of paint remover. The label says methylene chloride.

S2: I count three problems. Silica cut dry in an enclosed space. The mask. And the stripper.

S1: That's the three. On the silica, OSHA's construction rule makes employers use a saw that feeds water to the blade, with a respirator on top indoors or in an enclosed area. That rule binds employers, not him. It's there to show how seriously OSHA takes this job. The mask fails twice. A nuisance dust mask isn't a respirator, and even a real one has to seal to the face. HSE says a respirator that depends on face fit is useless if you have a beard or thick beard stubble.

S2: And would a proper mask help against the stripper fumes?

S1: No. HSE says FFP masks are designed to filter out dust only, and must not be used against harmful gases and vapours. A vapour isn't a particle. The US Environmental Protection Agency says that during paint stripping, levels may be one thousand times background outdoor levels. What helps is air: outdoors, an exhaust fan, or windows open.

S2: And methylene chloride itself? I thought that was banned.

S1: Careful with that word. EPA says it's converted to carbon monoxide in the body. In twenty nineteen it issued a rule prohibiting its manufacture, processing and distribution, retailers included, for consumer paint and coating removal, and in twenty twenty four a wider rule covering all consumer uses. Those rules are about supply. For a tin someone already has, EPA's rule page says it's encouraging all consumers to stop using it. Its older indoor air page still says to use it outdoors when possible. The course follows the rule page, and its answer is not to use it. And it read no UK rule on methylene chloride at all.

S2: Batteries.

S1: The London Fire Brigade's advice is written for phones and other devices, so applying it to a tool pack is the course's reading. Use the charger it came with, and never cover a charger. If a battery starts swelling: stop charging immediately, turn off the device, do not remove the casing, and contact the manufacturer or a certified technician. A swollen pack is call someone.
