S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Home Repair and Maintenance, on the lesson called Pests: find the cause, then control it.

S2: The usual line first.

S1: This course is education, not a trade qualification, and it teaches no gas work and no wiring. That comes up today, because pests get in where pipes and cables come into a house. The course's line is to note those gaps, and not to fill or pack round a gas pipe, a meter or a cable yourself. That goes to the landlord, a qualified gas engineer, Gas Safe registered in the UK, or an electrician.

S2: So where does the lesson start? Because my first move with a mouse is a can of something.

S1: That's the move the regulators and researchers it read put last, or rule out. The US Environmental Protection Agency calls the approach integrated pest management. Four steps. Set a threshold, meaning decide what level of problem calls for action. Monitor and identify, so you know what the pest actually is. Prevent. And only then, control. And even then, less risky controls first, like traps. EPA writes the threshold for growers, about pests as an economic threat to crops, so using it in a house is the course's reading. One mouse is a reason to find its way in, not yet a reason for poison.

S2: Mice, then. What does the lesson do with them?

S1: It uses the steps of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the US public health agency. Seal up, trap up, clean up. Applying CDC's steps anywhere is the course's reading, not a UK body's advice. Sealing: a mouse can fit through a hole the width of a pencil, about six millimetres. Small holes get steel wool held in with sealant. Trapping: CDC recommends snap traps, set on the floor with the baited end against the wall, checked daily. And it says do not use glue traps or live traps, because they can scare the rodents into urinating, which can increase your chance of getting sick.

S2: And the clean up? I'd sweep.

S1: That's where most people get it wrong. CDC says don't vacuum or sweep rodent urine, droppings or nesting materials, because that can put tiny droplets containing viruses into the air. Instead, open the doors and windows for thirty minutes and leave the room. Rubber or plastic gloves. Spray the droppings and urine with a household disinfectant, or one part bleach to nine parts water, until very wet, and let it soak for five minutes, or as the label says. Then wipe up with paper towels.

S2: Now the poisons. Who's allowed to use what?

S1: This is general information, not legal advice. The rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and between US states and cities, and they change. In the US, EPA says every pesticide label carries a line making it a violation of federal law to use the product in a way the label doesn't allow. In other words, the label is the law, and the label says who may use it. In Great Britain and Northern Ireland, the Health and Safety Executive says members of the general public should never use products meant only for professional users. So a rodent problem that needs a professional product is call someone, in both countries.

S2: And glue traps. I gather this is where it gets complicated.

S1: Nation by nation, then. England first, and as general information. Since the thirty first of July twenty twenty four, setting a glue trap in England to catch a rodent is an offence. A licence can be given, but only to a pest controller, for public health or public safety, and only if there's no other satisfactory solution.

S2: Wales?

S1: As general information again. Since the seventeenth of October twenty twenty three, it's an offence to set a glue trap in Wales where it's likely to catch any animal, or to use one to kill or take any animal. Animal there means any vertebrate, not only rodents. And Wales has no licence route. The same Act left the new glue trap offences out of the licensing power.

S2: So wider than England, and no way round it.

S1: That's it. And Scotland, as general information too, since the first of July twenty twenty six. Using, supplying or possessing a glue trap without reasonable excuse is an offence, unless authorised, and authorisation is only for protecting public health where no other method of rodent control is practicable. The course didn't read the scheme itself. Northern Ireland, the course read no glue trap law at all. And in the US it read none, only CDC's advice against them.

S2: What about the little sticky traps for insects? People use them to watch for cockroaches and moths.

S1: That's the wrinkle, and it's open in all three nations, not just one. England's and Scotland's Acts define a glue trap by what it's capable of being used to catch, and Wales's offence turns on where one is set to catch a vertebrate. Whether a small insect trap falls inside them, the course can't say from what it read. Its line, in England, Wales and Scotland alike, is to ask your council first.

S2: Cockroaches, then. What's wrong with a bug bomb?

S1: Two studies from North Carolina State University, in twenty nineteen, and the course read the abstracts only. The first tried foggers in twenty infested homes, against gel baits costing about the same in ten. The foggers failed to reduce the cockroach populations. The gel baits caused significant declines. And after fogging, pesticide residues on horizontal kitchen surfaces were on average six hundred and three times the level before, with a median of eighty five times.

S2: So no fewer cockroaches, and a lot more pesticide in the kitchen.

S1: Yes. The second study asked why. Cockroaches from apartments where foggers had failed were about two hundred and two times more resistant to a common fogger chemical than a laboratory strain. The authors say their results suggest that resistance is a major factor. That word suggest is theirs, and it stays.

S2: Is resistance the whole story?

S1: There's a second reason, from a different source. The University of California's guide says foggers and sprays may repel and disperse cockroaches to other areas without killing them, which in a block of flats means next door. That the two reasons work together is the course's reading. What UC says works is sanitation and sealing first, then baits placed near where they hide. And patience. It may take seven days or longer before you see fewer.
