And drains add a hazard of their own: never mix drain chemicals.

S2: Why start with drains at all?

S1: Because of dinner. Thames Water, one of England's water companies, says pouring fats, oils and grease down the kitchen sink is one of the leading causes of blockage-related internal flooding. That's its phrase for sewage coming back up inside a home.

S2: Start with the bend under the sink.

S1: That bend is a trap, and it's there to hold water. The City of Superior, in Wisconsin, calls the water it keeps the trap seal, and says it stops sewage odours, gases and vermin getting into the room. England's statutory guidance on drainage, Approved Document H, gives a sink trap a seal at least seventy five millimetres deep, and says a trap should keep at least twenty five under working and test conditions. This is general information, not legal advice, and it's England's guidance; the rules differ across the UK nations and US states, and they change.

S2: So what happens when a basin isn't used for a couple of months?

S1: Most likely a smell of drains, with nothing blocked. The American Society of Home Inspectors describes it: in a rarely used bathroom, the trap can dry out and let sewer gas seep through, though a damaged pipe is also possible. Its fix: pour water into the drain to refill the trap, and run water in fixtures occasionally. That's Yours. If the smell comes back with the trap kept topped up, the other possibility is a damaged pipe, and that's Call someone.

S2: Where does the vent come in?

S1: Approved Document H says stacks should be ventilated, so the seals in the traps aren't lost to pressures in the pipes. And the home inspectors give an analogy. Turn a gallon jug upside down and it goes glub, glub, glub. Poke a hole in the other end and it pours out smoothly. Vent piping lets air into the system so drains flow easily, and lets sewer gas out.

S2: So a gurgling sink means a vent problem?

S1: Careful. The jug is an analogy for why a drain needs air, not a diagnosis of your sink, and none of the sources read says what a gurgle in a particular drain means. Thames Water's signs of a blockage are a toilet that's hard to flush, a sink or bath that empties very slowly, and a bad smell. A gurgle alone isn't on the list. A gurgle with slow draining is.

S2: What should go down, then?

S1: Thames Water's rule is the three Ps: pee, poo and toilet paper. Nothing else gets flushed. And fat goes in the bin. Portland, Oregon's environmental services bureau says fat doesn't break down in water, sticks to pipes, and gets thick and sticky when it cools. So let it cool and bin it, and wipe pans with paper first. Portland says don't wash grease down the drain at all.

S2: And flushable wipes?

S1: Thames Water says wipes block pipes, even the ones labelled flushable. There was a certification scheme, Fine to Flush, and it ended at the start of March twenty twenty four. WRc, which ran the testing, disagrees about the cause: it says plastic wipes that don't break up are the primary cause of blockages, not certified flushable products. But even WRc says plastic-free baby and cleaning wipes may not break up either. No source read tells you to flush one.

S2: Now my sink's blocked. Whose problem is it?

S1: For its own area in England, and again this is general information, not legal advice, Thames Water says property owners are responsible for the pipes in their homes and the ones connecting to its sewers. A blockage only in your home is yours, and it advises a plumber. It may be the company's if there are public sewers in the area and the problem's outside your boundary or more than one property is affected. In the US, who owns the pipe from the house to the city's main varies by city and utility, which the course didn't research, so check your own city or utility's page.

S2: And what can I do before calling anyone?

S1: WaterSafe, the UK register of approved plumbers, run for the water industry, gives an order for a sink with standing water in it. Scoop out as much water as you can. If you see food, grease or hair, pull it out with rubber gloves on. Pour in very hot water, not boiling, because boiling water can damage PVC pipes. Then a plunger, about five to ten pumps. Then baking soda and vinegar. Then a plumber's snake. Then the trap itself, with a bucket underneath, taken off and cleaned. And if it still backs up, stop, because the clog may be deeper and need a professional.

S2: So which of that is mine?

S1: The first seven steps are Yours, and the last is Call someone. But one condition, and it's the course's, not WaterSafe's. The order assumes only water and waste are in the sink. If a chemical drain cleaner has already gone in, don't start it. Not the scooping, not your hands, not the hot water, not the plunger, not the baking soda and vinegar, not the snake and not the trap.

S2: And the chemical cleaners themselves?

S1: The course's sources don't say never use one. Poison Control, run by the National Capital Poison Center in the US, calls them effective, and also says they must be used with great caution, because many contain highly concentrated corrosive chemicals. They can splash back if poured from too high, or if too much goes into a completely blocked drain. So as a job, it's Yours, on conditions: read the label, wear goggles and rubber gloves, ventilate the room, pour carefully and close to the drain, don't put too much into a drain that's completely blocked, and add nothing else. Every one of those conditions is Poison Control's, a US source, and none of them is law in either country. And never mix cleaning products. Poison Control says an acid toilet cleaner with bleach makes irritating chlorine fumes, and ammonia with bleach releases chloramine. America's Centers for Disease Control and Prevention says never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners.
