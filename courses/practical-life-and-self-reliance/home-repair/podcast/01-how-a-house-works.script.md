S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're starting the course called Home Repair and Maintenance, with its first lesson. How a house works, and where it switches off.

S2: First, what is this course, and what isn't it?

S1: It's education, not a trade qualification, and not legal advice about your own home. It teaches no gas work and no wiring. If you smell gas, the first steps differ between Britain and the US, and the box at the top of every lesson gives your country's, so follow those. In either country, don't light a flame or touch an electrical switch. And if a breaker, an RCD or a GFCI keeps tripping, leave it off and call an electrician.

S2: So where does it start?

S1: With a burst pipe. When one goes, there's one valve that stops the water to the whole house, one switch that cuts the power to every circuit, and one handle that shuts off the gas. Found beforehand, a burst costs a mop and a plumber. Found by torchlight under a bulging ceiling, it can cost a ceiling, a floor and the wiring between.

S2: Why those three?

S1: The lesson treats a house as four flows. Water in, under pressure. Water out, by gravity, through traps whose plug of water keeps sewer air out. Power in, through protective devices. And fuel in, with what's left after burning going out by a flue or chimney. The drains have no shut-off, and the flue has no switch. That leaves three controls.

S2: Water first. Where do I look?

S1: In the UK, WaterSafe, the approved-plumber register run for the water industry, calls it the internal stop tap, usually under the kitchen sink. Three facts, all WaterSafe's. Clockwise closes it. It may take a few minutes for the water to stop altogether. And turn it on and off slowly, never force it, because too much force can damage it. If it won't turn or won't shut off, call a plumber. There's a second stop tap in the pavement, and the Consumer Council for Water says that one is the water company's. In an emergency, the one inside is the one you reach first. And the Met Office, as advice, not law, says check your stop tap is working every six months.

S2: And in America?

S1: The lesson's American source on the valve is WSSC Water, one public utility in Maryland, describing its own customers' houses, not a national rule. It calls it the main shut-off valve, and in a house with a basement it's typically near the front foundation wall. A round wheel handle closes clockwise and may take two or more full turns. A lever takes a quarter turn. And where a home has fire sprinklers, WSSC says shut off the plumbing supply and leave the sprinkler pipe live, unless that's what's leaking.

S2: Now the power.

S1: A consumer unit in Britain, a panel in America. The main switch or main breaker cuts the power to the house's circuits, and a householder's work on the box stops there. The US Consumer Product Safety Commission says panel boxes are usually electrically live, even with the main circuit breakers turned off. And a pocket volt stick that stays silent proves nothing. Britain's Health and Safety Executive, in guidance for electrical workers, doesn't recommend one for proving a circuit dead.

S2: So what's mine?

S1: The front of the box. The switches, the test button and the labels. Behind the cover is somebody else's.

S2: And the gas?

S1: In Great Britain, Cadent, the largest gas distribution network, describes the valve on its own network's meters as a red handle near the pipe, turned a quarter turn to switch off. Find it and look, but don't turn it to see if it moves. Cadent says once it's off, don't turn it back on until you're advised it's safe. The American utilities the course read, Con Edison, SoCalGas and Atmos Energy, give a different first step: leave, and call from a safe distance. So in an American house, the course's advice is to know where the meter is and then leave it alone. It read no American instructions for turning one off.

S2: Isn't gas work simply illegal for the rest of us?

S1: That overstates it. This is general information, not legal advice, and the rules differ between the UK nations and between US states, and they change. In Great Britain, regulation three of the Gas Safety Installation and Use Regulations of nineteen ninety eight requires anyone who does gas work to be competent, and requires employers and the self-employed to be on the Gas Safe Register. Registration binds businesses; competence binds everyone. But the advice isn't in doubt. The Health and Safety Executive strongly advises a Gas Safe registered engineer, and Gas Safe says a householder can do the tasks the maker's user instructions give the user.

S2: Now the pipe bursts. What's the order?

S1: In the UK, the Met Office's: stop tap off, boiler off, open the taps to drain the pipes, soak up what you can, and call a WaterSafe plumber. The Consumer Council for Water gives the same order, and says photograph the damage for your insurer. Neither says why the stop tap comes first. The course's reading is that a towel can't keep up with a pipe still being fed. The course read no American agency's order. WSSC says once the main valve is closed, open a faucet on the highest level to relieve pressure. Calling a plumber after that is the course's line, not WSSC's.

S2: And if the water reaches the electrics?

S1: The Met Office says don't touch them, and switch them off at the fuse box. Electrical Safety First, a UK charity, adds a condition: switch off at the consumer unit only if it is safe to do so, and get a registered electrician if there's any sign of water inside it. Neither says what makes it safe, so this is the course's own reading, not a quotation. Go to the box only if it and the wall around it are dry, with no water on it, in it or running towards it, and you can reach it without standing in water or touching anything wet. Otherwise keep clear and call an electrician. For an American panel that reading is the course's own; it read no American source on it.

S2: Last thing. How will the course tell me what I can do myself?

S1: Every rule gets three questions. Who says it, is it law or advice, and where does it apply? And every job gets one of three labels. Yours. Yours, on conditions, such as only if the box and the wall are dry. And Call someone. Where the law allows more than the advice, as with gas in Great Britain, and again this is general information, the course gives the law first and the label follows the advice. So gas work is Call someone, though registration doesn't bind a householder.
