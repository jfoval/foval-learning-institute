S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Home Repair and Maintenance, on the lesson called Stepladders, and what the ladder injury figures count.

S2: The usual line first.

S1: This course is education, not a trade qualification, and it teaches no gas work and no wiring. And as last time, HSE's guidance and OSHA's rule are written for workplaces, and bind nobody working on their own home. This is general information, not legal advice. The rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and between US states and cities, and they change.

S2: A stepladder feels like the safe one, though. It stands up on its own.

S1: That's the feeling the lesson starts from. But Great Britain's workplace guidance counts a stepladder's treads as a working platform. So here are its rules. HSE says check all four feet are on the ground and the steps are level, locks engaged. Carry only light things and don't overreach. Face the work where you can, rather than side on. Avoid side loading, like drilling side on into brick. And don't stand and work on the top three steps unless there's a suitable handhold.

S2: Does the flat bit at the very top count as one of the three?

S1: Yes. HSE's words include a step forming the very top of the stepladder. OSHA's construction rule for employers is absolute about the very top: the top or top step of a stepladder shall not be used as a step. And OSHA's QuickCard, which is advice, says don't use a stepladder as a single ladder or partly closed. So folding one flat and leaning it on a wall is out.

S2: What about three points of contact? You're standing still up there.

S1: HSE's version for a stepladder is two feet and one hand, or, when both hands need to be free for a brief period, two feet and the body supported by the stepladder. And the lesson says that brief period is doing a lot of work.

S2: Because some jobs need both hands for longer.

S1: HSE names three: putting a box on a shelf, hanging wallpaper, and installing a smoke detector on a ceiling. It doesn't ban them. It says the decision needs to be justified, and gives six things to weigh. The height of the task. Whether a handhold is there before and after. Whether it's light work. Whether it avoids side loading. Whether it avoids overreaching. And whether the stepladder can be tied. HSE gives no scoring and no threshold.

S2: Let's run one.

S1: A man in a two storey maisonette in Inverclyde wants to fit a sealed battery smoke alarm to his landing ceiling. The Scottish Government says of sealed battery alarms that you can fit these yourself, and mains wired ones must be fitted by a qualified electrician. His is battery, so the fitting can be his, if it's his home or his landlord agrees. The landing is narrow, and the only place the stepladder fits puts it side on to the spot, at the top of the stairs.

S2: Light work, yes. A handhold before and after, yes, if he can hold the steps. But side on, and next to the stairs.

S1: Those are the doubts. HSE says avoid side on where possible. And on the course's reading, a stepladder by the top of a flight of stairs has a longer fall beside it than its own height. HSE's ladder pages don't mention stairs. A stepladder with its feet on the stairs themselves fails HSE's plainer rule, all four feet on the ground and level. So the change is to stand the steps on the flat of the landing, facing the work, and put the alarm where that's possible within whatever siting rule applies. Then it's yours, on conditions. If the steps can't stand that way, it's call someone.

S2: Now the injury figures. The lesson's careful here.

S1: Every figure is American, because the course found no British figure it could trace to a primary source. Numbers attributed online to RoSPA couldn't be traced to any RoSPA page it read. So no American figure should be read as a British one.

S2: The most quoted one: in an American study, where the place was recorded, ninety seven point three percent of ladder injuries were in non occupational settings. So only about three percent happen at work?

S1: No. That's from D'Souza and colleagues, and the course read the abstract only. They estimated an average of one hundred and thirty six thousand, one hundred and eighteen emergency department cases a year from nineteen ninety to two thousand and five, selected using ladder product codes on the consumer product side of the national system. The ninety seven point three percent were in settings such as homes and farms. But NIOSH says those consumer product estimates exclude work related injuries. So the figure says where the injuries in that count happened. It can't tell you what share of all ladder injuries happen at work.

S2: And that count went up, didn't it?

S1: By more than half over the period. But the abstract says the rate per hundred thousand people rose by almost twenty seven percent, less than the count, because the population grew. And a rising count doesn't tell you whether ladders got more dangerous or more people used them. You'd need injuries per ladder use, and no study the course read gives that. The data are also twenty one to thirty six years old.

S2: Anything more recent?

S1: The Consumer Product Safety Commission's staff estimate for the product group Ladders, stools, is two hundred and twenty seven thousand emergency department injuries in twenty twenty, related to, but not necessarily caused by, those products. The group includes stools, so it may not count the same things as D'Souza, and one is a single year where the other is an average. CPSC says the analysis was not reviewed by the Commission.

S2: And the idea that a short fall can't do much harm?

S1: NIOSH studied work related ladder falls in the US in twenty eleven: one hundred and thirteen deaths and an estimated thirty four thousand nonfatal injuries treated in emergency departments. The fall height was recorded for about a third of those. Of that third, nearly ninety percent were from under sixteen feet, and six to ten feet was the commonest. These are workers in one year, not householders. And it shows low falls hurt people often. It doesn't show a low ladder is riskier than a high one, because the study didn't count how often people use each.

S2: So every number comes with what it counts.

S1: And a count on its own doesn't tell you the risk. For that you'd need injuries per ladder use.
