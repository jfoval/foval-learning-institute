  # --- Knife work and size (L1) ---
  - q: >-
      A recipe for a quick vegetable soup simmers everything for about 12 minutes. You're cutting
      the parsnips and swede for it. Going by this course, what size should the pieces be?
    options:
      - Large dice, about 2 cm, so that the pieces keep their shape all through the simmer
      - Any size at all, as long as the parsnip pieces and swede pieces all match each other
      - Small dice, about 6 mm, so the pieces soften in the time they'll have in the pot
      - Mixed sizes, so the small pieces thicken the soup while the larger ones hold firm
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The size should match the time the piece will spend in the heat. Heat enters from the surface,
      and a piece twice as thick takes something nearer four times as long, so 2 cm chunks would
      still be hard in the middle after 12 minutes. Matching pieces is half the rule; they also have
      to match the time. And mixed sizes give you lesson 1's opening pan of badly cut carrots again,
      with some pieces overdone and some raw.

  # --- How heat gets into food (L2) ---
  - q: >-
      Broccoli florets steamed over boiling water are tender in a few minutes. The same florets on
      a tray in a 150°C oven, 50 degrees hotter, are still firm after the same time. What explains
      it?
    options:
      - Steam hands heat over far faster than air, and gives up stored heat as it condenses
      - The steam rising off the pan is well over 100°C, so it's really the hotter of the two
      - The oven's dial must be wrong, since air 50 degrees hotter would cook them faster
      - Oven air is too dry to carry heat at all, so the florets warm only by radiation
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Temperature isn't rate. Engineering ToolBox's typical coefficients run from 0.5 to 1,000 for
      air and from 5,000 to 100,000 for condensing steam, and steam carries a large load of extra
      heat that it hands over when it condenses on something cooler. Steam over an open pot sits at
      the boiling point, not above it. The oven isn't wrong: air at 150°C is at 150°C, and it does
      carry heat by convection, just slowly.
  - q: >-
      In the US, you see flames inside the oven while a tray of potatoes is roasting. What do the
      NFPA and the CPSC say to do?
    options:
      - Open the door a crack and throw a cup of water over the tray to put the fire out
      - Open the door and lay a damp tea towel over the tray to smother the flames on it
      - Open the door, lift the tray out in oven gloves, and carry it over to the sink
      - Keep the oven door shut and turn off the heat, since opening it lets in more air
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Both say keep the door shut and turn off the heat: the fire needs air, and opening the door
      gives it some. Water on hot fat flashes to steam and throws it about, and the CPSC says never
      use water on a cooking fire. A fire service calls the damp tea towel "outdated advice". And
      the sources on pan fires say not to move or carry a burning pan. If the fire grows, the NFPA
      says get out and close the door behind you, and the CPSC's list opens with calling the fire
      department.

  # --- Seasoning (L3) ---
  - q: >-
      You've made a courgette and herb soup and haven't salted it yet. It tastes flat: not sour,
      not heavy, just of very little. What does lesson 3 say to reach for first?
    options:
      - A squeeze of lemon, since a flat taste in a vegetable soup is a call for acid
      - A spoon of cream, since a soup that tastes of little is short of body and fat
      - Salt in pinches, tasting between, since an unsalted dish tastes of too little
      - More herbs, simmered longer, since a flat soup is short of its own flavourings
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Salt is the first thing to check, and an under-salted dish doesn't taste of too little salt;
      it tastes of too little of everything. Acid is the fix for a dish that's salted and still
      heavy or cloying. Fat is for a dish that's thin in the mouth. And more herbs add more of
      something the soup can't yet show off, because the salt that brings flavour forward isn't
      there.
  - q: >-
      A supplement company gives 40 people who say MSG makes them ill a bowl of broth, tells them
      it contains MSG, and 30 of them report symptoms. Why can't this show that MSG caused them?
    options:
      - It can, since three reactions in every four people is far too many to be chance
      - There's no placebo they couldn't tell apart, no one was blinded, and no retest
      - The one flaw is the food, since MSG should be tested on an empty stomach only
      - Forty people is too few, and a larger group told the same thing would settle it
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The fair test gives the substance and a placebo in a form nobody can tell apart, with neither
      the people nor the researchers knowing which is which, and repeats it. Here everyone was told
      what they were eating, so expectation can't be separated from MSG. In Geha's trial, 13.1%
      reacted to the placebo alone. A bigger group told the same thing only repeats the flaw at a
      larger size, and changing the food leaves the missing placebo missing.

  # --- Food safety (L4) ---
  - q: >-
      A cook holds chicken in a water bath until the centre has sat at 60°C (140°F) for 20
      minutes. By the FSA's list of time and temperature pairs and FSIS's table for chicken, has it
      had enough?
    options:
      - No; the FSA pairs 60°C with 45 minutes, and FSIS's table asks 25 to 35 there
      - Yes; 20 minutes is ten times the 2 minutes the FSA asks for, so it's plenty
      - Yes; any time held above 55°C kills Salmonella, however short the time is
      - No, and no time would do, since chicken is safe only at 74°C (165°F) or above
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Heat kills at a rate, so a lower temperature needs a longer hold. The FSA's list gives 45
      minutes at 60°C, and FSIS's chicken table, drawn in lesson 4's chart, needs 25.2 minutes at
      140°F for 1% fat and 35 minutes for 12% fat. The FSA's figure alone settles it. The 2 minutes belongs to 70°C, not 60°C. 165°F is where the kill is
      instant, not the only way to reach it. The tables are there to show why the rules are what
      they are; a home cook follows the consumer rule.
  - q: >-
      With one board and one knife, you're making raw sausages cut into lengths and roasted with
      potato wedges, and a coleslaw of raw cabbage and carrot. Which order fits both countries'
      advice?
    options:
      - Sausages first, to get them in the oven, then wipe the board and do the rest of it
      - Potatoes, then sausages, then the coleslaw on the other side of the same board
      - Coleslaw, then potatoes, then sausages; then wash hands, then the board and knife
      - Coleslaw last, on the same board, rinsed under the cold tap after the sausages
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      FSIS would give raw meat a board of its own. With one board, the FSA's rule is
      vegetables and fruit first and raw meat last, and the coleslaw
      matters most because nothing will cook it. After the sausages, hands first, since everything
      they touch next picks up what's on them, then the board and knife in hot soapy water, and
      anything else touched with raw meat on your hands. A wipe or a cold rinse isn't washing, and
      the other side of the board is still the board the raw meat was on.
  - q: >-
      In the US, at 10 pm, you've made about 4 litres of bean and sausage soup and you want to go
      to bed. What does FSIS's guidance point you to?
    options:
      - Leave the pot covered on the hob overnight, and put it in the fridge first thing
      - Put the whole deep pot straight in the fridge, since FSIS lets hot food go in
      - Leave it on the counter to reach room temperature first, then fridge it after
      - Divide it into shallow containers and put them in the fridge now, hot if need be
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      FSIS says hot food can go straight into the fridge, and that a large pot of soup or stew
      should be divided into small portions in shallow containers first, because a big pot takes a
      long time to cool. Its limit for food out of the fridge is 2 hours, so overnight on the hob is
      far past it. The whole deep pot is the right door and the wrong depth. And waiting for room
      temperature is closer to the UK's approach, which still wants food in the fridge within one
      to two hours.
  - q: >-
      A cook with no thermometer barbecues chicken drumsticks, cuts into the thickest one, and
      finds no pink meat, clear juices and plenty of steam. What does each country's guidance say about
      that check?
    options:
      - Both accept it, since clear juices show that the centre has passed 74°C (165°F)
      - The UK's FSA accepts those cues without a thermometer; US agencies want 165°F
      - Neither accepts it, since both say only a thermometer shows chicken is cooked
      - The US accepts it but the UK doesn't, since the FSA wants 70°C for 2 minutes
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The FSA's advice, if you don't have a food thermometer, is to cut into the thickest part and
      check there's no pink meat left, the juices run clear and it's steaming hot. The CDC says you
      can't tell whether food is safely cooked by its colour and texture, except seafood, and the
      FSIS chart gives 165°F by thermometer. The FSA's 70°C for 2 minutes is the rule the cues are
      a way of checking, not a reason to reject them.

  # --- Eggs (L5) ---
  - q: >-
      You have some eggs that have sat in the fridge for weeks and some bought this morning. You
      want poached eggs for breakfast and hard-boiled ones for a salad. By FSIS's account, which
      eggs go where?
    options:
      - The older ones for poaching, since a thinner white wraps round the yolk more easily
      - The new ones for both, since an older egg is likely to have gone off by this point
      - The older ones for both, since their bigger air cells help them set faster in water
      - The new ones for poaching, as the white holds together; the older ones for boiling
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      FSIS says an ageing egg's white gets thinner and its yolk flatter, and that the grades with
      the firmest whites are best for frying and poaching; a thin white spreads through the water
      before it sets. The white also turns more alkaline with age, which loosens it from the
      membrane, so FSIS says older eggs make better candidates for hard cooking. Old isn't the same
      as unsafe, and nothing read here says a bigger air cell makes an egg set faster.

  # --- Searing, sautéing and pans (L6) ---
  - q: >-
      You salt 1 kg of diced pork shoulder for tacos just before it goes in, and tip all of it into
      one 26 cm frying pan. It comes out grey and dry, and a friend blames the pork. What's the
      likelier cause?
    options:
      - A crowded pan; the water had to boil off first, and the inside cooked all the while
      - Salting it just before cooking, since that is what drew the juice out of the pork
      - Too hot a pan, since fierce heat seizes the proteins and wrings the pork out dry
      - Pieces cut too small, since diced pork dries out in any pan, and only large cuts sear
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      A crowded pan loses heat and gains water faster than it can boil it off, so the pork simmers
      in its own juice. While the water goes, heat keeps travelling into the middle, so by the time
      anything browns the inside has overcooked. That's why the rule about crowding is a rule about
      meat. Salting right before cooking is the timing lesson 6 recommends. A hot pan is part of the
      fix, and small pieces brown well in batches, as a stir-fry does.
  - q: >-
      You're simmering a tomato and red wine ragù for three hours. You own a well-seasoned bare
      cast-iron skillet, a bare aluminium pot, and a clad pot with stainless steel on the inside.
      Which does lesson 6 point to?
    options:
      - The cast iron, since well-seasoned iron stands up to acid for any length of time
      - The clad pot, since the stainless steel that faces the food won't react with acid
      - The bare aluminium, since it spreads heat best and so it won't catch at the bottom
      - The cast iron, soaked overnight after, so that the dried-on sauce lifts off easily
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      ATK says aluminium reacts with acidic foods and stainless steel doesn't, which is why a clad
      pan puts the steel on the inside and the aluminium in the middle for spreading heat. Bare
      aluminium does spread heat well; three hours of tomato and wine is what the warning is about. Lodge's
      rule for cast iron is not to leave acidic ingredients in it for more than about 45 minutes,
      and three hours is far past that. And Lodge calls soaking cast iron "a recipe for rust".

  # --- Roasting, carryover and mince (L7) ---
  - q: >-
      In the US, a restaurant's written procedure counts the minutes after a beef roast leaves the
      oven towards its time at a killing temperature. At home, you want to take a beef rib roast
      out at 139°F and let the rest carry it to 145°F. Can you?
    options:
      - Yes; carryover does the same job at home, and the rest counts under both codes
      - Yes, provided it then rests three minutes, as the chart says for whole cuts
      - No; the consumer chart wants 145°F read before the roast comes off the heat
      - No, since carryover adds no more than a degree or two to a roast's centre
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The FSIS consumer chart says to reach its temperatures "before removing food from the heat
      source", and then adds the 3-minute rest for whole cuts. The Food Code's note that holding
      time "may include post-oven heat rise" is written for restaurants. So at home the roast comes
      out at 145°F or above, and carryover can take it higher. Carryover isn't small, either: Gritzer's
      pork chop in lesson 7 climbed 15°F in under three minutes. The question is which rule lets it
      count, not whether it happens.
  - q: >-
      You serve lamb chops pink, and you want to make burgers from minced lamb and serve them the
      same way. What do the US and UK rules both say about the burgers?
    options:
      - Cook them through, to 160°F in the US and with no pink in the UK, as they're mince
      - Serve them pink like the chops, since lamb counts as a whole-cut meat in both places
      - Serve them pink if they're seared hard, since the sear kills what was on the outside
      - Cook them until brown all the way through, since both say brown means a burger is safe
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      A whole cut's bacteria are on its outside, where the sear reaches, which is why a chop can be
      pink. Mincing moves whatever was on the outside all through the meat. FSIS gives minced meat
      160°F (71.1°C), and the FSA lists mince and burgers among the meats to cook through with no
      pink. Colour won't settle it either: FSIS says ground beef may brown before it's safe, and the
      FSA says a burger's colour can't be the only check.

  # --- Water, steam and alcohol (L8) ---
  - q: >-
      A stew uses 330 ml of beer at 5% alcohol, stirred in and simmered for an hour, and serves
      four. By the USDA's table, about how much of the beer's alcohol ends up in each bowl?
    options:
      - None at all, since an hour at a simmer is enough to cook all of the alcohol off
      - About 1 ml, since 16.5 ml went in, a quarter of it stays, and it's split four ways
      - About 3 ml, since three quarters of it stays, as it does for alcohol that's flamed
      - About 4 ml, the whole 16.5 ml split four ways, since the stew's lid keeps it all in
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      330 ml at 5% is 16.5 ml of alcohol. The table gives 25% retained after an hour's simmering, so
      about 4 ml stays in the pot, or about 1 ml a bowl. No row in the table reaches zero; even 2½
      hours leaves 5%. The 75% belongs to flaming, a different row. And nothing read here says a
      lid keeps all of it in. Whether a millilitre matters is for the guest to decide, which is why
      you tell them what's in the dish.

  # --- Rice and beans (L9) ---
  - q: >-
      In the UK, you cook a large pan of rice on Tuesday evening, to eat some at Wednesday lunch
      and the rest on Saturday. What does the FSA's advice point you to?
    options:
      - Fridge it within two hours, as cooked leftovers keep three to four days in a fridge
      - Leave it covered in the pan to cool overnight, then fridge it on Wednesday morning
      - Fridge it within the hour, then freeze Saturday's share on Thursday to be safe
      - Chill Wednesday's within the hour, and freeze Saturday's within an hour of cooking
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The FSA says to chill leftover rice ideally within one hour, keep it in the fridge no more
      than one day, and freeze it within an hour of cooking if you're freezing it. So Wednesday
      lunch is inside the day and Saturday's share goes in the freezer on Tuesday evening. Three to
      four days is the US general rule for leftovers, not the UK's rice rule. The FSA names the pan
      as the place not to leave rice to cool. And by Thursday the rice is past its one day.
  - q: >-
      A cook soaks dried red kidney beans overnight, drains them, and simmers them gently in a
      tomato and squash stew on the hob for three hours without it ever boiling. By the US FDA's
      procedure, which lesson 9 follows, what should they change?
    options:
      - Boil the soaked beans hard in fresh water for at least 30 minutes, then add them in
      - Nothing, since three hours of gentle simmering does more than any 10-minute boil
      - Soak them for a full 24 hours next time, since the soaking removes the bean toxin
      - Bring the sauce up to the boil once as the beans go in, and then simmer them as before
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Soaked beans are still raw, and soaking hydrates a bean without cooking it. The toxin needs a
      boil, and the Bad Bug Book reports slow-cooked casseroles at 75°C or less failing to destroy
      it; a gentle simmer isn't a boil. The FDA's procedure is to soak, pour the water away, and
      boil in fresh water for at least 30 minutes. In the UK, the FSA gives no boiling time, but it
      says not to slow cook raw kidney beans and that tinned ones are safe, so nothing there
      contradicts the FDA's figure. A brief boil falls short of 30 minutes. The tomatoes
      in from the start will also slow the softening, on ATK's account.

  # --- Stock and the big pot (L10) ---
  - q: >-
      The same hot stew is cooled in four containers. Which one's middle is likeliest to still be
      warm after two hours?
    options:
      - A tin 5 cm deep, uncovered, standing in a sink of iced water and stirred now and then
      - A tin 5 cm deep, loosely covered, cooling on the counter next to an open window
      - A tub 15 cm deep with its lid pressed on tight, put straight into the fridge
      - A tin 5 cm deep, loosely covered, put into the fridge on a shelf of its own
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Depth mattered most in the Minneapolis study, an ice bath helped too, and the shallow foods
      that still cooled too slowly were being cooled above 41°F or were partly or wholly covered.
      The Food Code says a tightly covered container transfers heat more slowly, and that
      commercial fridges are designed to hold food cold, not to cool large masses; this course reads
      a home fridge the same way. The deep, sealed tub has all three against it, and the iced,
      stirred tin has everything in its favour.

  # --- Bread (L11) ---
  - q: >-
      A flatbread baked in a rimmed tray uses 750 g of flour, 600 g of water and 15 g of salt. In
      baker's percentages, what are its hydration and salt, and what do they suggest?
    options:
      - 44% water and 1.1% salt, a stiff dough, and low in salt by King Arthur's range
      - 125% water and 2% salt, wetter even than King Arthur's no-knead at about 101%
      - 80% water and 2.5% salt, above King Arthur's range, as salt is set against water
      - 80% water and 2% salt, wet enough to spread flat without the tray walls round it
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Flour is 100% and everything else is divided by the flour: 600 ÷ 750 is 80% and 15 ÷ 750 is
      2%, inside both of King Arthur's salt ranges. ATK's 80% test loaf spread flat and wide with
      nothing to hold it, so the tray is doing the job King Arthur's pot does. Dividing by the whole
      dough gives 44% and 1.1%, flour over water turns 80% into 125%, and salt over water gives
      2.5%.

  # --- Reading a recipe (L12) ---
  - q: >-
      A mushroom soup recipe says: "Sauté the mushrooms in two batches. Simmer for 25 minutes. Add
      1 teaspoon of salt. Cool leftovers in shallow tubs, no more than 5 cm deep." Which should you
      keep exactly as written, whatever your kitchen?
    options:
      - The two batches, since the order of work in a recipe is the part that never changes
      - The shallow tubs, since they guard against a germ that no amount of tasting catches
      - The 25 minutes, since the writer timed the soup, and a simmer is a simmer anywhere
      - The teaspoon of salt, since the writer tested the soup with that particular amount
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Cooling in shallow containers is a safety rule both countries give, against Clostridium
      perfringens, and the soup can't tell you if you got it wrong. The batches are a cooking rule,
      and mushrooms are the case where lesson 6 found crowding matters least. The 25 minutes was
      the writer's pan and hob. And a teaspoon holds different amounts of different salts, so you
      taste instead.

This test covers all twelve lessons. **Twenty questions, and 70 percent to pass**, which is
fourteen.

Nearly every question gives you a dish, a pot or a plan that isn't in any lesson and asks you to
apply what the course taught to it. Lesson 4 has four questions, because it's the one the rest of
the course leans on.

Food safety rules differ between the US and the UK. Where a question asks about one country's rule,
it names the country. Where it doesn't, the right answer is the same under both, so answer it from
the rules you know rather than guessing which country is meant.
