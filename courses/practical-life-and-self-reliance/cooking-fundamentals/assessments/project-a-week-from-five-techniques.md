This course was organised by method rather than by recipe, on the bet that a method travels to the
next dish and a recipe doesn't. This project is where you find out whether the bet paid off for
you. You pick a handful of techniques, plan a week of dinners from them, cook it, and write down
what happened.

It takes a week of your cooking, which you'd be doing anyway, plus about four and a half hours of
your own time on top: an hour and a half to choose the techniques and write the plan and the
storage plan, ten minutes a day on the log, and about two hours for the write-up at the end. Lesson
12's second exercise was a fifteen-minute first run at the plan, so if you did it, you've started.

## What you hand in

One plain text or Markdown file, in six parts: the techniques, the plan, the storage plan, the
log, the dinner without a recipe, and the report. Expect something between 1,500 and 2,500 words,
most of it log. Photographs are welcome and earn nothing; the writing is what's marked.

A meat-free plan is fully eligible, and so is a plan built around food from any kitchen in the
world. Nothing here asks you to eat anything you don't, and nothing here is a view on what's
healthy. That's Nutrition: The Evidence's subject.

## Section 1: five techniques, and what each depends on

Choose no more than five techniques from this course. Fewer is fine. For each, write one or two
lines: what the technique is, which lesson taught it, and the mechanism it depends on.

The mechanism is the part that is marked. "Roasting vegetables (lesson 7)" is a name. "Roasting
vegetables (lesson 7): a dry surface with room around it climbs past water's boiling point and
browns, and a crowded tray steams instead (lessons 2 and 6)" is a technique you could carry to a
vegetable you have never roasted. That is the level to aim for, one technique at a time.

The techniques have to be enough to cook the whole week, including at least one way of making
something in quantity: a braise, a pot of beans, a pot of rice or another grain, or a stock. If the
beans are red kidney beans, lesson 9's boil in fresh water is part of the technique, and so is the
reason for it.

## Section 2: the plan

At least five dinners for one week, using only the techniques in section 1.

At least two of them have to be batch items: something cooked once and used in at least two
different dinners. A braise that becomes Monday's dinner and Thursday's filling counts. So does a
pot of beans that turns up in a soup and a salad, or a stock that becomes a sauce on one night and
a soup on another.

Build it the way lesson 12 did. Put the evenings you can't cook in first. Put the batch cooking on
the day or two you have time. Leave one dinner loose, so that a late night or a missing ingredient
bends the plan without breaking it. Then write it as a table: day, dinner, techniques used, batch
items used and the day each one was cooked.

End the section with one sentence: where you expect the week to break. Lesson 12's exercise asked
for the same prediction. Don't change it once the week starts, because the report checks it.

## Section 3: the storage plan

This is the section that can fail outright, so it gets the most care.

For each batch item, five lines, written before you cook, under the rules of the country you live
in:

1. **How it cools**, and how fast.
2. **How long it keeps** in the fridge.
3. **What gets frozen, and when.**
4. **How it thaws.**
5. **How it's reheated.**

Then check the plan against the storage plan, day by day, the way lesson 12 checked the Sunday
braise. Mark every day where a batch item would be past its limit, and fix it with the freezer or
a fresh pot. A plan that needed fixing and says so is better than one that was never checked.

Three things fail this criterion however good the rest is: an error on cooked rice, an error on
cooling a big pot, or an error on how many days leftovers keep. They're singled out because the
dish can't tell you about any of them. The figures are in lesson 4's table and in lessons 9, 10 and
12, and here they are in one place.

| | US (FSIS, CDC) | UK (FSA) |
|---|---|---|
| Cooling a big pot | divide it into small portions in shallow containers; hot food may go straight into the fridge; in within 2 hours, or 1 hour above 90°F (32°C) | divide it into smaller portions in containers; cool first, and into the fridge within one to two hours |
| Cooked rice | the general rules above and below | cool it quickly, ideally within an hour; don't leave it in the pan or rice cooker to cool; no more than one day in the fridge; freeze it within an hour of cooking; reheat once |
| Leftovers in the fridge | 3 to 4 days | 48 hours, or freeze them |
| Freezing | freeze leftovers within 3 to 4 days | freeze leftovers as soon as possible, cooled first |
| Thawing | fridge, cold water changed every 30 minutes, or microwave; cook straight away after cold water or microwave | fridge, or the microwave's defrost setting directly before cooking; use within 24 hours of fully defrosting |
| Reheating | to 165°F (74°C); sauces, soups and gravies to a rolling boil | steaming hot all the way through, and only ever once |

In both countries, this course's depth for those containers is no more than about 5 cm (2 inches),
the benchmark lesson 10 took from the FDA's code for restaurants and the Minneapolis cooling study.
Neither country's home advice gives a depth, so the 5 cm is this course's recommendation, not a rule
from either table.

If you live somewhere else, follow your own country's regulator, as lesson 4 said, and name it at
the top of the section. Then give the same five lines under its rules, with a link to where you
found each one.

Two more things go in this section.

**Anyone at higher risk.** If anyone eating is pregnant, 65 or over, a young child, or has a
weakened immune system, say what changes. Lesson 4's callout is the list: mince cooked all the way
through, no raw dough or batter, your country's rule on runny eggs, and your country's list of
chilled ready-to-eat foods to heat or avoid. Lesson 12's application of it to a batch week is to
serve leftovers reheated until steaming hot, not cold. If anyone is severely immunocompromised,
lesson 4 says to ask their doctor what they can eat, and the plan says what the answer was.

**Anyone with a food allergy.** If you cook for anyone with one, say how you handled it, in terms
of what lesson 4 taught. Heat doesn't help: the FSA says kitchen temperatures won't remove an
allergen's allergenic potential. Amounts too small to see can matter, so taking the allergen off a
plate is not enough. And cross-contact, the FDA's name for an allergen getting into food by accident,
happens through shared boards, spoons and pans, and cooking afterwards doesn't undo it. So the plan
has to say which of two routes you took: the allergen left out of the whole batch, or their portion
made first, with clean tools and a clean pan, and kept apart from then on. Say whether you asked
them what they can eat, since the FSA says they "will have the best understanding of their specific
allergy". And say what went on the tub's label besides the date. The US names nine major allergens
and the UK fourteen, and lesson 4 lists both; check your sauces and condiments against the list for
your country.

If nobody eating has an allergy or is at higher risk, say that in one line. The line still counts,
because it shows you asked.

## Section 4: the log

Cook the week, and write in the log the same day, not at the weekend.

For every dinner, three lines:

1. **What you cooked**, which techniques, and which batch item came out of the fridge or freezer,
   with the date it was cooked.
2. **One seasoning adjustment**, and why. Not "added salt" but "tasted flat, not heavy, so salt
   first, three pinches until the beans tasted of beans" (lesson 3). If you tasted and changed
   nothing, write down what you tasted for and why it didn't need anything, which is a decision
   too.
3. **What went wrong, if anything.**

Across the week, at least one thing will go wrong. When it does, explain it by mechanism: what
happened in the pan or the pot, and which lesson's mechanism predicts it. "The onions burned"
is what happened. "The pan was too hot for the amount in it, and with little water to boil off, the
surface climbed fast and scorched" is a mechanism. "Bad luck" and "my hob" are not explanations
unless you say what the hob did.

Keep a storage record alongside it: the time each batch item came off the heat, the time it went
into the fridge or freezer, and the day it was eaten. It is two numbers a day, and it is the evidence
for section 3.

## Section 5: one dinner without a recipe

Cook one of the week's dinners from a method and a ratio, with no recipe open. Then write it up.

1. **The method**, written the way lesson 12's first exercise wrote a recipe's: the techniques in
   order, each with the lesson it came from and the mechanism it depends on.
2. **The ratio** you anchored it to, where the ratio came from (a lesson, and whose figure it
   was), and what you had that fixed the amounts: one egg, 300 g of flour, whatever was in the
   fridge.
3. **Every tasting point**, and what you decided at each: what you tasted for, what you added, how
   much, and whether it worked. At least two tasting points; most dishes have three.

This section is marked on reasoning, not on how the dinner turned out. A dish that came out too
sharp, with a clear account of which tasting point missed it, scores better than a good dish with
no account at all.

## The report

Last, a short report, about 400 to 600 words, answering three questions.

1. **Where did the week break?** Put your prediction from section 2 next to what happened. Say
   whether you were right, and if you weren't, what you didn't see coming.
2. **Which batch item worked hardest?** Which one saved you the most cooking, and did its storage
   plan hold?
3. **What would you change** for next week, and why? One change, specifically enough that you'd
   know in a week whether you'd made it.

## Rubric

| Criterion | What full marks look like |
|---|---|
| Techniques and their mechanisms | No more than five techniques, each with the lesson it came from and a mechanism stated correctly |
| The storage plan is correct | Every batch item has all five lines, each right for the reader's own country, the plan is checked day by day, and higher-risk eaters and allergies are answered, if only in one line. **An error on cooked rice, on cooling a big pot, or on leftover days fails this criterion outright**, whatever the rest of the plan does |
| Batch items used inside their limits | The log and storage record show each batch item eaten, frozen or thrown out inside its limits, and any slip is recorded as a slip |
| Seasoning adjustments logged with a reason | Every dinner has one adjustment, or one tasting that needed nothing, with the reason in the terms lesson 3 taught |
| The no-recipe dinner is reasoned | The method with its lessons, a ratio with its source, and a decision at each tasting point |
| A failure explained by mechanism | At least one thing that went wrong, explained by what happened to the food and which lesson predicts it, not by luck |

Higher-risk eaters and allergens are marked inside the storage plan: a plan that cooks for someone
with an allergy and does not say how it was handled hasn't met that criterion.

## If the week goes wrong

It will, in some way, and that's what the log is for. A week where the braise burned and you
explained why is a good project.

The one kind of going wrong that has a rule attached is a batch item that runs past its limit. If
that happens, it goes in the bin, not in the dinner, and the log says so. That costs you nothing on
the storage-plan criterion if the plan itself was right, and it's worth writing up in the report,
because it tells you where your week really breaks.

**The one thing not worth doing is writing the log from memory at the end of the week.** You'll
remember the dinners and forget the tasting points, and the tasting points carry two of the six
criteria.

## A note on what this project is not

It isn't a diet or a meal plan anyone should follow. It says nothing about what's healthy, and it
doesn't teach managing a food allergy; lesson 4's section on allergens is the most this course
offers, and the person with the allergy knows their needs better than any course. It's a test of
whether you can cook from methods instead of recipes, and keep food safe while you do.
