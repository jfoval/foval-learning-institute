S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Cooking Fundamentals, on the lesson called Searing and sautéing, and the pan sauce.

S2: I'm guessing this is the one about getting a brown crust.

S1: It is, and it starts with a failure. Four chicken thighs, skin down, in a pan a little too small for them. For the first minute they sizzle loudly. Then the sound drops to a mutter, liquid pools around them, and five minutes later the skin is pale, wet and soft. Give the same thighs room and the skin goes deep brown and crisp, with brown bits stuck to the pan you can make a sauce from.

S2: Same chicken, same heat. So what changed?

S1: How much water was on the surface, and how fast it could leave. Lesson two gave two facts. A wet surface can't get much above a hundred degrees Celsius, and browning gets going fast at about a hundred and forty Celsius, two hundred and eighty five Fahrenheit, which the course still treats as a rough mark rather than a switch. Cold, wet food takes heat out of the pan and gives up water, and the pan has to boil that water off before the surface can climb past a hundred. With one or two pieces and room, it flashes off. Crowd the pan and there's more cold food, more water, and less open pan for the steam to escape from. The pan cools, the water collects, and the food simmers in its own juice.

S2: So the pan couldn't stay hot. What does that cost you?

S1: In meat, the lesson says the real cost isn't just colour. While you wait for the water to go, heat keeps travelling into the middle. A thin piece can be cooked through, or past it, before its surface is ever dry. So you end up choosing between a pale outside and an overcooked middle.

S2: What are the fixes?

S1: Four, and all of them are about water. Dry the surface with paper towels. Give it room, and cook in batches if it won't fit. Heat the pan with its fat first, until the fat shimmers, rippling like air over a hot road. And salt at the right time. Salt draws water out of meat the way lesson three showed it drawing juice from a tomato, so a steak salted a little while ago has a wet surface. Given longer, the meat takes that liquid back in. So salt right before it goes in the pan, or well ahead, overnight being best, and not in between.

S2: Walk me through the chicken, then. The good version.

S1: Pat the thighs dry, salt them, and lay them skin down in shimmering oil with a gap between each. Leave the skin alone until it's deep golden and lets go of the pan, then turn them. Cook to temperature, not to time, by your country's rule. In the US that's a hundred and sixty five Fahrenheit, seventy three point nine Celsius, in the thickest part, away from the bone. In the UK, the cues are to cut into the thickest part and check there's no pink meat left and the juices run clear, and that the meat is steaming hot. Then look at the pan. Those brown patches are fond, the browning reaction's work, concentrated, and they're the whole flavour base of what comes next.

S2: How does it become a sauce?

S1: Pour off most of the fat, leaving a spoonful, and soften a shallot in it. Pour in stock and scrape the fond up as it bubbles. That's deglazing. America's Test Kitchen says use low salt stock, because reducing can make it overwhelmingly salty. Boil it down until it's thicker. Then turn the heat off or right down, which the lesson marks as its own instruction, not Test Kitchen's, and whisk in cold butter a piece at a time. Test Kitchen says cold butter makes a sturdier emulsion, more resistant to separation. Taste, salt, and a squeeze of lemon if it's heavy.

S2: Emulsion. Like the mayonnaise last time.

S1: The same idea. Fat from the butter held as fine droplets through the reduced stock. And the lesson is straight about a gap here. No source it read says what happens if you boil the finished sauce hard. The course's research notes expected it to split, but that was reasoning, not a test, so the practice has you boil a spoonful and see.

S2: Now, mushrooms. I've been told never to wash them and never to crowd them.

S1: And the lesson says both rules are wrong for most mushrooms. Test Kitchen weighed six kinds before and after a one minute soak. The ones without exposed gills took up almost no water. Open gilled ones soaked up as much as twenty five percent of their weight. So rinse closed ones just before cooking if they're dirty, and brush or wipe the open kinds. Then crowding. In two thousand and nine Dave Arnold, who ran the Cooking Issues blog, set up a deliberately unfair test. Soaked mushrooms in one extremely crowded batch, dry ones in three roomy batches, identical salt and oil. The crowded batch went soupy at first, then the water boiled off, and he wrote that they looked as good and tasted better and less oily than their dry cousins.

S2: That seems to break everything you just said.

S1: The lesson calls it one test in one kitchen, tasted by the people who ran it, so a strong hint rather than a law. But the reason is worth having. The crowding rule protects the inside of the meat, which overcooks while you wait for the water. Test Kitchen says mushrooms are virtually impossible to overcook. So waiting costs a mushroom some time and nothing else. A mushroom has no inside to protect.

S2: And steak? There must be myths there.

S1: The lesson looks at three, and none survived as usually stated. First, searing seals in the juices. Test Kitchen cooked eight rib eyes in two batches, one seared first and one seared last, and both lost nearly the same amount, around twenty two percent of their weight. The lesson calls eight steaks a small test, but it fits the physics. A crust is a dried, browned layer, not a waterproof skin.

S2: And the others?

S1: Flip it only once. A twenty twenty six Serious Eats article reports J. Kenji López-Alt's burger test. Flipped every fifteen seconds, they browned just as well as burgers flipped once, cooked in about two thirds of the time, and had roughly forty percent less overcooked meat around the edges. But the article is careful. Flipping once won't ruin your steak, and the differences are noticeable but a bit subtle. So flip as often as you like, or once if you're busy.
