S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Cooking Fundamentals, on the lesson called Seasoning by taste.

S2: I'll start with the question I've always had. Why does salt make things taste better, instead of just salty?

S1: The lesson starts with the cook Samin Nosrat, whose book Salt, Fat, Acid, Heat is built on this kind of observation. Taste a slice of plain tomato, then the same tomato with a few grains of salt. The second tastes more of tomato, not more of salt. She explained it on NPR. The salt draws the juices out, osmosis starts, and the aromatic compounds in those watery cells come to the top, where they're more available for you to breathe in.

S2: Then why can't I just follow the teaspoon in the recipe?

S1: Because a teaspoon isn't a measurement of salt. Nosrat said that if she means one teaspoon of the kosher salt she uses at home, and you use a box of iodized table salt, what you use will almost be equivalent to twice as much. The reason, which the interview doesn't spell out and the lesson labels as its own explanation, is that a spoon measures volume and your tongue measures salt. Big or flaky crystals leave air between them.

S2: And when it's salted and still tastes wrong?

S1: The course sorts the common problems into three, and it says plainly that this is its own working tool, not something a source defines. Flat, cloying or heavy, and thin. Acid is the usual fix for cloying. Nosrat explains it with lemonade: too much sugar is cloying, so you increase the acid for tart contrast. Anything tart counts as acid, lemon, lime, vinegar, yogurt. Using that same contrast against richness, a heavy stew, a soup with no edges, is the course's extension of her point.

S2: And the third one, thin?

S1: Thin can mean it lacks body, which fat fixes, or it lacks savouriness. That taste has a name, umami, and a molecule behind it, glutamate. What matters to a cook is that most glutamate in food is bound into protein, and it's the free form that has been shown to enhance flavour. So by the Australian and New Zealand regulator's table, beef carries more than seven times as much glutamate in total as tomatoes, but tomatoes have about four times as much free glutamate. And the savoury seasonings cooks reach for all over the world, soy sauce, fish sauce, Parmesan, are high in free glutamate. Most of them are salty too, so a splash adds salt as well.

S2: Which brings us to MSG. A lot of people think it makes them ill.

S1: The lesson takes that seriously, starting with where the belief came from. In April nineteen sixty eight a doctor in Maryland wrote to the New England Journal of Medicine describing a racing heart, weakness and numbness after meals at restaurants serving Northern Chinese food. According to the science writer Sam Kean, he offered three possible culprits, and MSG was only one of them. Newspapers ran stories about Chinese Restaurant Syndrome. Kean points out that MSG had been in American canned soups and TV dinners for decades without complaint. But the lesson is careful here. Kean's piece is an argued history, written with open scorn for the scare, and the course uses him for the sequence of events, not for his explanation of the symptoms.

S2: So what happened when it was actually tested?

S1: The fair test is double blind. The substance one day, a placebo another, in a form you can't tell apart, neither you nor the researchers knowing which, and then you repeat it. The trial the lesson takes was led by Raif Geha, published in two thousand, and its authors called it the largest to date. A hundred and thirty people who believed they reacted to MSG got five grams of it one day and a placebo on another, both without food.

S2: And?

S1: Fifty of them, thirty eight and a half percent, reacted to MSG and not to the placebo. Seventeen reacted to the placebo only. Nineteen reacted to both.

S2: That sounds like MSG did something.

S1: On a first test, in that group, it did produce more reported symptoms than placebo. And bigger doses gave more responses, which is the strongest finding for people who report reactions. But then they retested. Only about half, nineteen of the thirty seven retested who had reacted to MSG but not placebo, reacted the same way the next time. When the most consistent reactors were tested again, two responded to MSG and not placebo. Those two were then given MSG three times and placebo three times, this time with food, and each responded to only one of the MSG challenges. The authors' summary says the responses weren't observed when MSG was given with food.

S2: So how does the lesson land?

S1: As a general claim, that MSG in ordinary food makes people ill failed the tests designed to catch it. Whether a few people have a small, short lived reaction to large doses on an empty stomach is still open at the margin. And the mirror claim goes too far as well. People who feel unwell after a meal aren't imagining it. The Australian and New Zealand regulator's advice to anyone who suspects a sensitivity is appropriate clinical testing. The symptoms can be real while the explanation is wrong.

S2: The lesson ends on garlic oil. Why that?

S1: Because it's the one seasoning job here that can put someone in hospital. The danger is botulism, a rare but serious illness caused by a toxin made by the bacterium Clostridium botulinum. It can form spores that aren't killed by cooking, and the spores may already be in the garlic. Oil keeps the air out, garlic is low in acid, and at room temperature the spores can germinate, multiply and make the toxin. The only thing on that list you control at home is the temperature.

S2: So what are the rules?

S1: The US National Center for Home Food Preservation says garlic in oil should be made fresh and kept in the fridge at forty degrees Fahrenheit or lower for no more than four days, or frozen for up to several months. In the UK, the Food Standards Agency says bottling things like garlic in oil at home can be risky, and the page the course read gives no day limit. Its general advice is to keep chilled food below five degrees Celsius. Keeping to the US four days in a UK fridge fits inside that, and the lesson is clear that this is the course's suggestion, not an FSA rule.

S2: And a squeeze of lemon in the jar doesn't make it safe?

S1: No. There's a tested acidifying method from the University of Georgia, and it specifically says not to swap its citric acid for vinegar or lemon juice. And tasting can't help you here. In the Food Standards Agency's words, you can't smell or taste bacteria which could make you ill.

S2: The full lesson has what fat does for flavour, with Nosrat's garlic cooked in water and in oil, a pot of dal seasoned in four stages, a sweetcorn soup that's salted and still wrong, and why the avocado pit doesn't keep guacamole green. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Taste as you go.
