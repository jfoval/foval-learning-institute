S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Cooking Fundamentals, on the lesson called Water and steam: boil, simmer, poach, steam, braise.

S2: After searing and roasting, water sounds a bit tame.

S1: The lesson's case is that tame is the point. Put a chicken breast in a frying pan and the bottom is at the temperature of the metal, far hotter than water can get, which is what lets it brown. Put the same breast in water at a bare simmer and nothing touching it is hotter than about eighty five Celsius. A pan gives you browning and asks you to watch it. Water gives you a temperature that holds still on its own, so you can leave a pot for hours and know what it's doing.

S2: Why does it hold still?

S1: Two facts from lesson two. Water has a ceiling. At sea level it can't get hotter than a hundred Celsius, two hundred and twelve Fahrenheit, and the ceiling drops as you go up. Turn the flame up under a boiling pot and you get more steam, not hotter water. And water hands heat over fast. So it delivers a lot of heat, but never more than about a hundred degrees of it. The outside of the food can't overshoot the water around it. A potato left too long will fall apart eventually, but it won't burn. The flip side is lesson six's. Nothing wet browns fast, so a braise gets its colour before the water goes in.

S2: Boil, simmer, poach. Are those really different, or just words for hot water?

S1: The lesson calls them a scale of temperature you can see. America's Test Kitchen gives the two main marks. A boil is two hundred and twelve Fahrenheit, with large bubbles vigorously rising and continually breaking the surface. A simmer is a hundred and eighty to a hundred and ninety Fahrenheit, about eighty two to eighty eight Celsius, with small bubbles occasionally breaking the surface. Poaching sits below that, and the course hasn't read a source that puts a number on it. So it's a working description: hot and steaming, moving a little, with few or no bubbles breaking. Your thermometer can tell you where your own poach sits.

S2: Give me a dish.

S1: A poached chicken breast. Bring your liquid to a simmer, season it, put the breast in, then turn it down below a simmer. The lesson says plainly that the figure it suggests is the course's choice, not a source's: about eighty Celsius, a hundred and seventy six Fahrenheit. Check the liquid with your thermometer, not just the meat. Then cook to your country's rule in the thickest part. In the US that's a hundred and sixty five Fahrenheit, seventy three point nine Celsius. In the UK, the middle should reach seventy Celsius for two minutes, or an equivalent, and the cues for chicken are to cut into the thickest part and check there's no pink meat left and the juices run clear.

S2: What does the low water buy you, apart from patience?

S1: A wide margin. In a hot pan the outer layers keep cooking hard while the centre catches up, so a minute's inattention costs you. In water at eighty, the outside can't go past eighty, only a few degrees above a seventy four degree centre, so a few extra minutes change little. And you keep the broth.

S2: Now the tough cuts. Why does a shin of beef take hours when a steak takes minutes?

S1: Connective tissue. Cuts like shoulder, shin and cheek have a lot of it around the muscle fibres, and it's chiefly collagen. Cooked briefly, it stays tough. Cooked long enough in a wet pot, it dissolves into gelatin, which is soft and gives the liquid its body. A two thousand and five review by E. Tornberg puts temperatures on it. Nearly every temperature in its figures for the muscle proteins is at or below sixty Celsius. Collagen starts to give way between fifty three and sixty three, and with further heating it can dissolve into gelatin. The course read that paper's abstract, not the whole review.

S2: So by the time the collagen goes, the meat's already set.

S1: That's the lesson's reading of the two sets of figures side by side, and it labels it as its own, not the paper's. A braised shin is, by steak standards, well done all through. What makes it tender isn't the fibres staying soft, it's the collagen between them dissolving. And that takes time. A twenty nineteen study held meat at sixty Celsius for up to twenty four hours and found the connective tissue's strength diminished with cooking time, with a slow, gradual rise in the collagen that had given way. Again, the course read the abstract.

S2: The lesson's braise uses a whole bottle of wine. Which raises the question everyone asks. Doesn't the alcohol cook off?

S1: Some does. The best figures are in a table the US Department of Agriculture published in two thousand and seven, as the percentage of the alcohol you added that's still there. Stir it into something boiling and take it straight off the heat, eighty five percent. Flamed, seventy five percent. The lesson points out that a figure you'll often see attaches eighty five to flaming, and that's a misreading. Simmered fifteen minutes, forty percent. Thirty minutes, thirty five. An hour, twenty five. Two hours, ten. Two and a half hours, five percent. No row reaches zero, and the lesson says to treat them as rough guides, since the table can't know your pot.

S2: What does that mean in a bowl?

S1: A seven hundred and fifty millilitre bottle at thirteen and a half percent holds about a hundred and one millilitres of alcohol. Serve six. Pull the braise at thirty minutes and about thirty five millilitres is left, about six a serving, the alcohol in roughly three tablespoons of the wine in each bowl. Cook it two and a half hours and about five millilitres is left, under one a serving, the alcohol in a little over a teaspoon of the wine.

S2: Small, but not none.

S1: That's how the lesson puts it. And whether it matters is a question the course doesn't answer, because it depends on why the person at your table avoids alcohol. Religious reasons, recovery, a medicine, a pregnancy, or simply not drinking, and each may draw the line in a different place. The course gives you the number and leaves the choice to the cook and the guest. Tell them what's in the dish and how long it cooked, and let them decide. And a braise made with stock and a little acid instead of wine is still a braise.
