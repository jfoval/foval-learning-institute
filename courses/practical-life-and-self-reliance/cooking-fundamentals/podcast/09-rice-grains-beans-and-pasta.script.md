S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Cooking Fundamentals, on the lesson called Rice, grains, beans and pasta.

S2: That's most of my cupboard. Is there anything that ties them together?

S1: One mechanism. These are the dry starches of the world's kitchens, and in every one of them hot water gets into hard starch and softens it. A grain of raw white rice is a small, hard pack of starch in tightly organised granules that water can't get into at room temperature. Heat it in water and the granules take up water, swell, and then begin to break open. That swelling is called gelatinisation, and it's what turns a pebble into something you can eat. In a study of ten rice varieties it began between fifty seven point nine and sixty four point four Celsius, about a hundred and thirty six to a hundred and forty eight Fahrenheit, depending on the variety. A simmer is far above that, which is one reason rice doesn't need a hard boil, just enough water reaching the middle of the grain for long enough.

S2: Then the rice I actually worry about. Leftovers.

S1: The lesson opens there. Cook a pot of rice on Sunday evening, leave it in the pan with the lid on while you eat, and fry it on Monday. It's a sensible use of leftovers, and done that way it's also how a pot of rice can make someone ill. The germ is Bacillus cereus. The Food Standards Agency says uncooked rice can contain its spores, a dormant, tough form of the bacterium, and that the spores can survive being cooked. The FDA's reference on foodborne germs, the Bad Bug Book, says it grows best between twenty eight and thirty five Celsius, about eighty two to ninety five Fahrenheit, and can grow anywhere from four to forty eight Celsius, about thirty nine to a hundred and eighteen. A covered pot cooling on the hob spends hours in that range.

S2: But on Monday I heat it up. Doesn't that kill it?

S1: It kills the bacteria, not what they've made. The vomiting kind of illness comes from a toxin they make in the food, called cereulide, and the book says it's stable after heating at a hundred and twenty one Celsius, about two hundred and fifty Fahrenheit, for thirty minutes. The course's reading, from water's ceiling of a hundred Celsius in an open pot, is that no reheating in a pan or a microwave comes near that. So the agency puts it this way: "It's not the reheating that causes the problem, but the way the rice has been stored before it's reheated."

S2: So what are the rules?

S1: The two countries differ. The UK has a rule for rice, and it's strict. Chill it as quickly as possible, ideally within one hour, dividing it into smaller portions to help, and don't leave it to cool in the rice cooker, steamer or pan. Keep it in the fridge no more than one day until reheating, never reheat it more than once, and make sure it's steaming hot all the way through. The US pages the course read give no rule for rice alone, so it falls under the general ones. Into the fridge within two hours, or one hour above ninety Fahrenheit, thirty two Celsius. Hot food can go straight in, in shallow containers. Leftovers keep three to four days, and you reheat to a hundred and sixty five Fahrenheit, seventy four Celsius.

S2: Why does the UK single out rice?

S1: The pages don't say, and the course won't guess. They agree on the direction, cool it fast and keep it cold. And the step that fails in both countries is the one people do without thinking: leaving the rice in the pot, lid on, to cool on the stove.

S2: Beans, then. I know red kidney beans come with a warning.

S1: Raw red kidney beans contain a lectin, a kind of protein, called phytohaemagglutinin, and it's a genuine poison. The Bad Bug Book says as few as four or five raw beans can trigger symptoms. It usually begins with extreme nausea and vomiting within one to three hours, and recovery is usually rapid, within three to four hours after onset, though some people have needed hospital. The outbreaks it describes are British, seven in the UK between nineteen seventy six and nineteen seventy nine, and it says the illness is usually caused by eating raw, soaked beans.

S2: Soaked. So soaking doesn't make them safe.

S1: Soaking hydrates a bean. It doesn't cook it. And a slow cooker is the danger. The book says several outbreaks have been linked to beans cooked in slow cookers, and that casseroles cooked in them often reached only seventy five Celsius or less, which isn't enough to destroy the toxin. The Food Standards Agency says you should not slow cook raw red kidney beans, as it does not destroy the toxin and may increase its toxicity. Neither page explains the "may increase", and the course won't try to. So both countries' regulators say the same thing: don't slow cook them raw.

S2: I grew up with boil them hard for ten minutes.

S1: That was NHS advice. The version of its page reviewed in twenty fifteen said to soak them for at least twelve hours, then boil them vigorously in fresh water for at least ten minutes and simmer them for around forty five to sixty. By the version reviewed in twenty twenty one the numbers had gone, and the NHS has since removed the page. The ten minutes did have a real source. The Bad Bug Book reports that Bender and Readi found boiling the beans for ten minutes, at a hundred Celsius, completely destroyed the toxin. And then it tells consumers something else: boil them for at least thirty minutes.

S2: Why three times as long, if ten did the job?

S1: That's lesson four's time plus temperature. Ten minutes at a hundred destroyed the toxin in a study. In your kitchen, a pot of cold beans takes time to come back to a boil, a big bean's centre lags its outside, and a gentle simmer isn't a boil. The thirty minutes buys the margin. The FDA's consumer advice, which it says comes from studies by British scientists, is to soak the beans at least five hours, pour the water away, and boil them in fresh water for at least thirty minutes. Or skip all of it. The Food Standards Agency says tinned beans are safe, because they've been pre-soaked and boiled.
