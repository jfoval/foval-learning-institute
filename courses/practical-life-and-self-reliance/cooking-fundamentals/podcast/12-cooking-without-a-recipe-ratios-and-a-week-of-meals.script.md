S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Cooking Fundamentals, on the lesson called Cooking without a recipe: ratios and a week of meals.

S2: I'll admit I'm a recipe follower. Heat the wok for one minute, stir-fry for two. I like being told.

S1: That's the card the lesson opens with. It was written for the lesson, in the shape most stir-fry recipes take, so it could be taken apart. It isn't anyone's tested recipe. The point is that those minutes are a report of what happened in one kitchen, with one burner, one pan, broccoli cut to one size and prawns that started at one temperature. Change any of that and the minutes stop being true, but the dish still works.

S2: So what is a recipe, if it isn't the minutes?

S1: The lesson sorts it into four kinds of thing. The method, meaning the steps and each step's reason, like browning meat in batches for a dry surface. The ratios, how much of one thing to another. The tasting points, where you stop and decide whether it needs salt or acid. And the numbers for one kitchen: minutes, medium heat, a large onion, a teaspoon of salt. The method and the ratios travel. The numbers for one kitchen don't.

S2: What goes wrong with the stir-fry card, then?

S1: Three things. It assumes a wok, and most people have a frying pan. America's Test Kitchen warns that even a skillet over high heat won't get hot enough to quickly stir-fry either large amounts or large pieces of food, and says to cook the protein in batches if necessary. The course's reading is that three hundred grams of cold prawns in one go is the large amount they mean. Second, the times. The prawns are done when they're firm and opaque, not after two minutes, and the one minute for the sauce is shorter than Test Kitchen found cornstarch needs, which is a couple of minutes to reach full thickness. Third, there's no tasting point, and three tablespoons of soy sauce carry most of the salt.

S2: You keep saying ratio. Is that a real system or a kitchen habit?

S1: Michael Ruhlman built a book on it, called Ratio. The course read its contents and its publisher's description, not the book itself. The description says that when you know a culinary ratio, it's not like knowing a single recipe, it's instantly knowing a thousand. His ratios are by weight. For pasta, his blog gives two parts egg to three parts flour, and in a reply to a reader there he added: always weigh them out of the shell.

S2: But eggs aren't all the same size.

S1: That's where the ratio beats the recipe. You anchor it to what you have. Crack one egg into a bowl on your scale. Say it weighs fifty seven grams. The flour is one and a half times that, about eighty six grams. It fits whatever egg you happened to have, which a recipe that says one large egg, a hundred grams of flour can't do.

S2: So I can change anything, as long as I taste?

S1: Not anything. Ask the course's question of each instruction: what is this rule protecting? Most fall into two groups. Cooking rules protect something you can check yourself, a texture, a colour, a flavour, and the dish tells you if you got it wrong. Safety rules protect against something you can't see, smell or taste. Boil soaked kidney beans for at least thirty minutes. Cool a stew in shallow containers. Those come from regulators, and they travel exactly as they are.

S2: The second half is a week of meals. What's the idea?

S1: If you know five techniques well, you don't need a new recipe every night. The lesson's week is for two people, from a braise, rice, a stir-fry, a tray of roasted vegetables and a vinaigrette. On Sunday you braise about one and a half kilos of pork shoulder, which makes about eight portions, four dinners for two. On Monday you cook a pot of rice. The braise comes back Monday over rice, Wednesday in flatbreads and Thursday with rice. Tuesday is fried rice from Monday's pot. Friday is a stir-fry with fresh rice.

S2: Does it hold up?

S1: In the US, yes. Leftovers keep three to four days, so Thursday is the braise's fourth day, right at the far end. The rice is eaten three days after cooking at most. Cool everything in shallow containers into the fridge within two hours, and reheat to a hundred and sixty five degrees Fahrenheit, with the braise's liquid brought to a rolling boil. And FSIS says to freeze leftovers within three to four days, and freezing Thursday's portion on Sunday takes the pressure off the end of the week.

S2: And in the UK?

S1: Two dinners break. The UK gives leftovers forty eight hours, so the braise runs out on Tuesday evening, and Wednesday's and Thursday's portions are past it. Cooked rice keeps no more than one day, so Tuesday's fried rice is right at the limit and Thursday's rice is two days past it. And if the braise sits in one pot that gets reheated Monday and again Wednesday, the rule to reheat only once is broken too.

S2: So how do you rescue it?

S1: The freezer. On Sunday, divide what's left into three tubs of two portions. Cool them, and within two hours of cooking, Monday's goes in the fridge and Wednesday's and Thursday's go in the freezer. On Tuesday after dinner, move Wednesday's tub to the fridge. The only thawing time the course read is from FSIS, a full day for a pound of minced meat or boneless chicken breasts, and applying that to a portion of braise is the course's step. On Wednesday, check it's thawed right to the middle, because the FSA says partially defrosted food may not cook evenly. If it isn't, finish it in the microwave on defrost and reheat it straight away. The FSA gives the microwave as a way to defrost directly before cooking, and using it to finish a slow fridge thaw is the course's application of that. Reheat it once, until steaming hot. It's inside the FSA's twenty four hours after defrosting. After Wednesday's dinner, move Thursday's tub to the fridge the same way. The rice for Thursday is a fresh pot, or a share frozen within an hour of cooking on Monday.
