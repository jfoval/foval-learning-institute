S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Cooking Fundamentals, on the lesson called What makes people ill, and the rules that stop it.

S2: Food safety. I'm expecting a list of numbers.

S1: There are numbers, but the lesson starts with a puzzle. Two chicken breasts. One is poached very gently, and a thermometer in its centre reads a hundred and fifty degrees Fahrenheit, sixty five point six Celsius, and stays there for five minutes. The other is on a hot grill, and its centre reads a hundred and fifty too, on its way up. Same meat, same reading. By the tables the US regulator publishes for meat plants, the first has probably had enough heat to be safe. Nobody can say that about the second.

S2: Why not? Same number.

S1: Because a reading is a moment, and safety takes time. Heat kills bacteria at a rate, not like a switch. At a given temperature, a fixed fraction dies in each stretch of time, so the count falls tenfold, then tenfold again. Food scientists count kills in those steps, called log reductions. A seven log reduction is ten million to one. Start with ten million Salmonella and one is left.

S2: And hotter means faster.

S1: Each tenfold step comes faster. So a lower temperature does the same job as a higher one if you hold it longer. Here's the figure that surprises people. The US consumer rule for chicken is a hundred and sixty five Fahrenheit, seventy four Celsius. But the Food Safety and Inspection Service's twenty twenty one guideline, written for small meat plants, says chicken held at a hundred and forty five Fahrenheit, sixty two point eight Celsius, gets a seven log Salmonella kill in eight point four minutes at one percent fat, or thirteen minutes at twelve percent fat. At a hundred and sixty five the time is zero.

S2: So a hundred and sixty five isn't where chicken becomes safe.

S1: It's where it becomes safe instantly, which is what makes it a good rule for a thermometer. One reading, no clock. Back to the breasts. At a hundred and fifty the table asks for two point seven to four point two minutes. The poached one sat there for five, so if the reading was right, it got its kill. The grilled one didn't stay anywhere.

S2: What does the UK say?

S1: Seventy degrees Celsius for two minutes, or its equivalents, running from sixty Celsius for forty five minutes to eighty for six seconds. The same shape, points on a curve. But every UK pair asks for more time than the US chicken table at the same temperature. The two lists aren't measuring the same thing, one is Salmonella in chicken and the other is the middle of any food, and the lesson is honest that the pages it read don't say how the UK pairs were worked out. So it doesn't explain the gap.

S2: And how you check differs too?

S1: The US says use a thermometer. The CDC says you can't tell if food is safely cooked by checking its colour and texture, except for seafood. The UK accepts either. The Food Standards Agency gives the thermometer first, and then, if you don't have one, cues. The food should be steaming hot, and for chicken, cut into the thickest part and check there's no pink meat left and the juices run clear. The lesson points out the cues aren't careless. They check the right place, the thickest part, the last to heat.

S2: Now the big one. Don't wash your chicken.

S1: The lesson says the research behind that says something more useful than don't. First, a lot of cooks wash chicken. In a twenty fifteen US web survey, nearly seventy percent said they washed or rinsed raw poultry. Every regulator read points the same way. The UK says never, because washing can splash Campylobacter onto hands, surfaces, clothing and equipment. The US regulator says it's not recommended as the safest method, and the CDC adds, if you choose to wash chicken, do so as safely as possible.

S2: What was the research?

S1: A USDA study of three hundred people who said they usually washed their chicken. About half were first sent the USDA's messages advising against it. Then everyone was filmed cooking chicken thighs dosed with a harmless strain of E. coli the researchers could trace, and making a salad.

S2: And the washers got it on the salad.

S1: That's what most people guess. Among the people who weren't sent the messages, twenty six percent of washers got the bacteria onto the salad lettuce, and thirty one percent of non washers. Among the people who were sent them, it ran the other way, thirty percent for washers and fifteen for non washers. Either way, not washing didn't keep the salad clean. What washing did add was the sink. Sixty percent of washers had bacteria in their sink afterwards, and fourteen percent still did after they'd tried to clean it.

S2: So the sink mattered more than the splash.

S1: The authors said so themselves. Germs carried to the sink from the chicken, the packaging or people's hands were, in their view, a larger concern than splashing onto the counter. They recommended the message focus on keeping sinks clean, and on hand washing and sanitising surfaces. The lesson calls that the course's question at work. What is don't wash chicken protecting? The sink and the salad.

S2: Why do people wash, though? It can't just be habit.

S1: The lesson is clear the reasons are real, and they are not ignorance. Carolyn Desalu, writing for Food52, grew up in a Belizean and Nigerian family where, in her words, washing meat is the start to well prepared food. Her cousin Bobbi, who is eighty three, remembers a Belize City market where you saw someone handling your meat with their bare hands, and that was more than enough reason to wash it at home. Bobbi cleans her sink and counters afterwards with the leftover lemons, to avoid cross contamination. The chef Julius Jackson, in the US Virgin Islands, gives history as part of the reason, and his own method is practical about the risk: a really large bowl, to prevent splashing, and try to avoid the sink. Researchers at Drexel University found cooks wash from a desire to control the process, a lack of trust in chicken processing, and habit.

S2: So if I'm going to wash anyway?

S1: The lesson says the microbiology is settled and whether to keep washing is the cook's call, and it gives the version that does least harm. Make the salad first. Clear the sink before you start, which is the course's own addition. Wash in a large bowl, or run the water gently. Treat the wash water, lemon included, as raw chicken juice. Then clean and sanitise the sink, and wash your hands for twenty seconds. Desalu ends her piece still washing, but planning to sanitise with a bleach based solution instead of vinegar.

S2: The full lesson has what a lemon or vinegar wash actually does to Salmonella, why a good smell tells you nothing about leftovers, why pork is where the two countries differ most, the rules for cooling, thawing and date labels, what to heat or avoid when cooking for someone at higher risk, and a side by side table of the US and UK rules. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Mind the sink.
