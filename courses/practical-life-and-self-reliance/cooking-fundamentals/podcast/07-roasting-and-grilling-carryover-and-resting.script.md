S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Cooking Fundamentals, on the lesson called Roasting and grilling, carryover and resting.

S2: Last time was the surface of the meat. I'm guessing this is the inside.

S1: The centre, where doneness and safety are decided. And it opens with a test. In a test published in twenty twenty five, Daniel Gritzer of Serious Eats took a pork chop an inch and a half thick, about four centimetres, off the heat when its centre was fifteen degrees Fahrenheit short of his target, about eight Celsius. You'd expect to have undershot. He wrote that it reached a hundred and forty Fahrenheit, sixty Celsius, and threatened to pass it in under three minutes. Off the heat, and the centre kept cooking.

S2: Carryover.

S1: When a roast comes out, its layers are at different temperatures. The surface is near the oven's heat, the centre is whatever your thermometer reads, and there's a slope between them. Heat flows down that slope both ways. Some leaves into the kitchen. Some keeps travelling in, so for a while the centre goes on rising even as the whole piece cools. You met it in lesson five, with the boiled egg that kept cooking after it left the water. Nobody plunges a roast into cold water.

S2: How far does it climb? I've heard a few degrees.

S1: The usual advice, as Gritzer sums it up, is to pull meat roughly five to fifteen degrees Fahrenheit short and rest it five to thirty minutes, and steaks and chops five to ten short. His chops didn't fit. His verdict was that if you follow the conventional advice, in many cases you'll blow right past your target. So he won't offer a rule. It depends on the meat, the method, the heat, and the size and shape of the cut, and he thinks a good thermometer and some trial and error are probably your best bet. The lesson adds that these are one cook's measurements on one cut, so the practice has you measure your own.

S2: Here's what I want to know. If the meat keeps cooking, does that count for safety?

S1: The lesson says cooks often get this backwards, and it depends on which rule you follow. The US consumer chart says cook to its minimum temperatures, measured with a thermometer, before removing food from the heat source. So the minimum is reached in the oven, and carryover takes you above it. A US pork roast can come out at a hundred and forty five Fahrenheit and climb to a doneness you like better. It can't come out at a hundred and thirty five and count on the rest to get there.

S2: Then what's the three minute rest I've seen for pork?

S1: For whole cuts of beef, pork, veal and lamb, the chart says a hundred and forty five Fahrenheit, sixty two point eight Celsius, and rest for at least three minutes. That rest is part of the safety rule. When the USDA lowered its figure for pork in twenty eleven, its statement, as CIDRAP reported it, said that during that time the temperature stays the same or continues to rise, killing pathogens. The FDA's Food Code, which is written for restaurants, goes further and lets a roast's holding time include the heat rise after the oven. A home cook following the consumer chart doesn't do that. And the UK's rule is seventy Celsius for two minutes in the middle of the food, or an equivalent. The Food Standards Agency page the course read doesn't say whether time out of the oven counts towards those two minutes, so the lesson doesn't say either.

S2: Now resting. The reason I've always heard is the juices.

S1: That the fibres relax and reabsorb juice, and if you cut too soon it runs onto the board. And that had evidence. More than fifteen years ago J. Kenji López-Alt cooked steaks to a hundred and twenty five Fahrenheit, about fifty two Celsius, and sliced one every two and a half minutes. As Gritzer describes it, the unrested steak gushed juice, and by ten minutes the meat was hardly losing any. The course read Gritzer's account, not the original.

S2: That sounds convincing.

S1: Think about what else changed. Every steak came off at the same temperature, but the one cut at once was cut while its outer layers were at their hottest, and the one cut at ten minutes had finished carrying over and started to cool. So the test compared temperatures as well as resting times. In Gritzer's words, temperature changes during resting were not controlled for.

S2: So someone controlled for it.

S1: Chris Young, a chef and food scientist, one of the co-authors of the original Modernist Cuisine books. And the lesson flags an interest. He now makes a thermometer, and part of his case is that carryover is bigger than cooks think and needs tracking. His tests are in a video the course hasn't watched. Gritzer's summary is that when seared meat is sliced at the same final internal temperature, resting has no measurable effect on juice loss at all. Young's explanation, in Gritzer's paraphrase, is that hotter meat has higher vapour pressure pushing liquid out when it's cut. So a rested steak loses less because it's cooler.

S2: And Gritzer's own test?

S1: Whether anyone could taste it. Chops cut from one pork loin, unsalted, all aimed at the same final temperature, some sliced at once and some rested. Four tasters with their eyes closed, over thirty rounds, each picked the juicier of a rested and an unrested sample. They picked the rested one sixteen times. No better than a coin flip. His target was a hundred and forty Fahrenheit, below the US figure for pork, so the lesson is clear this was a tasting test, not a recipe.

S2: Is that settled, then?

S1: No, and the lesson says so. It's contested, the evidence is moving, and the new view rests on a narrow base: one publication's writers, Young's video, and thirty rounds by four people. Gritzer asks himself whether it's the last word, and answers, probably not. The juice on the old board was real. What the re-test answers is what caused it. So resting survives, as temperature control. In Gritzer's words, resting meat is best understood as a method of temperature control, not juice retention. Pull it early, rest it only long enough for carryover to bring the centre to your target, and watch the thermometer, not a clock. A fixed fifteen minutes can carry a small cut well past where you wanted it.
