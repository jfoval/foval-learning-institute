S1: And I'm John. Today we're starting Cooking Fundamentals, with its first lesson. Your knife, your board, and mise en place.

S2: A knife lesson. I'll be honest, I thought a knife was just how you make food smaller.

S1: That's the idea the lesson wants to take away from you. Picture a carrot cut badly. Some pieces are thin coins, some are thick chunks, and they go into a hot pan together. Five minutes later the coins are brown at the edges and the chunks are still crunchy in the middle. And there's no heat setting that fixes it. You eat half raw chunks or scorched coins. The mistake was made on the board, before the pan was even hot.

S2: So the board is where cooking starts.

S1: That's the claim. The size you cut sets how long a piece takes to cook. And before it gets to why, the lesson says what the course is. It's organised by method rather than by recipe, because a method travels to the next dish and a recipe doesn't. And it asks one question of every rule it meets. What is this rule protecting, and does it apply here?

S2: I'll hold on to that. Where do we start, the knife?

S1: How you hold it. Most beginners hold a chef's knife by the handle, like a hammer. America's Test Kitchen, whose knife guide the lesson leans on, grants that it feels secure, but says it doesn't give you the control over the tip that precise cuts need. What they teach instead is the pinch grip. Your thumb and forefinger pinch the blade right where it meets the handle, and the other three fingers wrap the handle. It puts your hand closer to where the edge is working, and that's where the control comes from.

S2: And the other hand? That's the one I'd worry about.

S1: That's the one that matters more for safety. It's called the claw. Fingers curved on top of the food, thumb tucked behind them, pressing down to hold it still. The flat of the blade rests lightly against your knuckles, and your fingertips are curled back behind them. After each cut, the claw walks back a step, and the size of that step is the size of your slice.

S2: So if I picture it, the thing nearest the blade is a knuckle, not a fingertip.

S1: Exactly. The edge can only reach what sticks out in front of it, and in a claw that's a flat wall of knuckle. If your fingertips are ahead of your knuckles, you've gone back to holding the food like a pen.

S2: Okay. Now the part I actually want. Why does size matter so much? Twice as thick, twice as long, surely.

S1: It's the natural guess, and it's wrong. Heat arrives at the surface and has to travel inward, and the centre is the last part to get hot. Douglas Baldwin, writing about cooking meat in a water bath, puts it plainly: a steak that is twice as thick takes about four times longer to cook.

S2: Four times. For a steak, though. Not a carrot.

S1: And the lesson says exactly that. Carrying Baldwin's rule from meat to a carrot is the course's own inference from the same physics, not a measurement of carrots. So treat four times as a rough guide. The direction is what you can rely on. If most of your carrot is at about six millimetres and a few pieces are at twelve, then when the small ones are soft, the big ones have had only about a quarter of the time they need. Now here's the course's question getting its first outing. Ask it of cut evenly. What is it protecting? Pieces that go into the heat together finishing together. Does it apply here? For one vegetable in one pan, yes. But put a firm vegetable and a soft one in the same pan at the same time, and the purpose points somewhere the wording doesn't.

S2: Because it's even time you want, not even size.

S1: So the firmer vegetable gets cut smaller. And again the lesson labels it. That's the course's inference from the mechanism, not a rule any source states, and your own pan is the test. The other answer, of course, is to put the firm one in first.

S2: Let's talk sharpness. Everyone says a sharp knife is safer than a dull one. Is that true?

S1: The lesson's answer is, better than folklore, but not what the confident version implies. The mechanism is easy to state. A dull edge skids on a tomato skin instead of biting, you push harder, and when it goes, it goes with more force behind it. What's actually been measured is the force. In two thousand and three, researchers measured fifteen professional meat cutters in two plants, and sharper blades needed statistically significantly less grip force and less turning effort at the wrist.

S2: Less force. But did anyone count cuts?

S1: That's the gap. The step from more force to more injuries is a mechanism that OSHA, the US workplace safety regulator, and America's Test Kitchen both state. The course found no study that counted injuries with sharp and dull knives. So it's professional consensus with a measured mechanism behind it, not a counted result. Which is still a good reason to keep your knife sharp.

S2: And keeping it sharp is the steel? The rod that comes with the set?

S1: That rod is usually called a sharpening steel, and America's Test Kitchen calls the name a misnomer. As you cut, microscopic bits of the edge get bent to either side. The edge is still there, it's just knocked out of line, and a steel pushes it back. That's honing. Sharpening is a different job. It removes metal to make a new edge, and that takes a sharpener or a stone.

S2: How do I know which one my knife needs?

S1: The paper test. Hold a sheet of copy paper by one end and draw the knife through it from heel to tip. If it snags, hone it, with light pressure, four or five strokes a side, and try again. If it still snags, it needs sharpening. When the edge itself has worn away, only a sharpener or a stone brings it back.

S2: And mise en place? It's in the title.

S1: French for everything in its place, as the Escoffier School translates it. Read the recipe all the way through, get the tools out, measure the ingredients, and do all the cutting before any heat goes on. Once a pan is hot, things happen on the pan's schedule. If you're cutting while something cooks, you're cutting in a hurry with your eyes on the stove. Doing it first means the knife work happens calmly, with your attention on your hands.
