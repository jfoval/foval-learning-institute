S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Cooking Fundamentals, on the lesson called Stock and sauces.

S2: Stock has always sounded like restaurant work to me. Why make it at home?

S1: The lesson starts from what you already have. Lesson seven left you a chicken carcass, and lesson eight a braise and a pot of poaching broth. And a stock does two jobs that come from different things. The first is body, that slightly thick, lip coating feel that makes a good broth different from salted water. That comes from gelatin. Lesson eight showed collagen dissolving into gelatin with long, moist heat, and America's Test Kitchen puts the stock version in one clause: the bones and meat contain lots of collagen, which breaks down to form gelatin. That's why stock recipes ask for the bony, sinewy parts of a bird. The second job is savouriness, the taste lesson three called umami.

S2: And the usual rule is never let it boil. Is that real?

S1: It's real, but look at what it protects. Test Kitchen tested it on a French boiled beef dish, pot au feu, and drew the conclusion for stock: boiling causes soluble proteins and rendered fat to emulsify into the liquid. At a simmer the fat doesn't emulsify and the stock stays clearer, and in the test the scum simply settled to the bottom of the pot. It's lesson five's emulsion again. A hard boil beats the fat into fine droplets, and the stock goes cloudy and a little greasy.

S2: So a boiled stock is worse.

S1: Cloudier and greasier. The test was about clarity and scum. Nothing in it says a boiled stock is less safe, and nothing the course read says it tastes worse. So the course's question from lesson one, what does this rule protect, has a plain answer here: the look and the texture. If you don't mind a cloudy stock for a stew, the rule matters less than it sounds.

S2: Now, the lesson says the dangerous part of a stock comes after it's cooked.

S1: It does. A big pot of stock, soup or stew, cooked perfectly safely, can make people ill the next day because of the hours it spent cooling. The germ is Clostridium perfringens, and the FDA's handbook on foodborne germs, the Bad Bug Book, tells it in three steps. First, cooking doesn't get rid of it. It makes spores, and the spores of some strains can survive boiling water for an hour or longer in a relatively protective medium, a cooked meat medium, for example. Second, as the food cools, the spores come back to life and grow fast. The book gives a doubling time under ten minutes for the growing cells, depending on the temperature and the food. And it says that during cool down, at a hundred and nine to a hundred and thirteen Fahrenheit, about forty three to forty five Celsius, it can reach harmful levels much more quickly than other bacteria.

S2: And the third?

S1: It takes a lot of them. Illness follows eating more than a million cells or spores per gram of food, typically about sixteen hours later, with cramps and diarrhoea that in most people last twelve to twenty four hours. The course does some arithmetic of its own here, and says so. A doubling every ten minutes is six an hour, a sixty four fold rise. Three hours is eighteen doublings, more than a two hundred and fifty thousand fold rise. A real pot doesn't sit at the ideal temperature the whole time, so it grows less than that. But it shows why an hour or two matters.

S2: What's the rule, then?

S1: The FDA Food Code, the model code US states adopt for restaurants and shops, has two stages. It isn't a legal rule for your kitchen, but its reasoning is physics, so it's a useful target at home. Cooked food goes from fifty seven Celsius, a hundred and thirty five Fahrenheit, down to twenty one Celsius, seventy Fahrenheit, within two hours, and to five Celsius, forty one Fahrenheit, within six hours in total. It calls that first two hour cool a critical element, and it names the range to get through quickly, twenty one to fifty two Celsius, seventy to a hundred and twenty five Fahrenheit, as ideal bacterial incubation temperatures. It gives two examples. Food that reaches seventy in one hour has five hours left for the rest. Food that makes forty one within the six hours, but took three hours to reach seventy, may not be adequately controlled.

S2: So the total doesn't matter. The time in the warm stretch does.

S1: That's the point. And three things decide how fast a pot gets through it. Depth, most of all. The code's new benchmark is two inches, about five centimetres, and it cites a study of two hundred and twenty four recipes logged by Minneapolis inspectors in restaurant kitchens. Depth had a highly significant effect. The growth there was estimated with existing models, not measured in the food, but depth is depth. Second, an ice bath, which the same study also found significant. Third, no tight lid. The code says a tightly covered container transfers heat more slowly, and asks for loose covers while food cools.

S2: Make it concrete. I've made a huge stew on a Sunday.

S1: Say six litres in one pot about twenty four centimetres across. By the course's arithmetic that stands about thirteen centimetres deep. At five centimetres the same stew needs two big roasting tins, or a tin and a few tubs. Then the countries differ. In the US, the Food Safety and Inspection Service says hot food can go straight into the fridge, or into an ice or cold water bath first, and that a large pot of soup or stew should be divided into shallow containers before it goes in. In the UK, the Food Standards Agency says don't put hot or warm food in the fridge. Cool it at room temperature and get it in within one to two hours. Neither agency's page explains its view on the fridge door, and the course won't guess. Both agree on what matters most: not one deep pot.

S2: And how long does it keep?

S1: Three to four days in the US. In the UK, eat it within forty eight hours or freeze it. So freeze the portions you won't reach, on the day you make it. To reheat, the US agency says bring sauces, soups and gravies to a rolling boil. The UK agency says steaming hot, and only ever reheat once.

It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.
