S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Cooking Fundamentals, on the lesson called Bread.

S2: Bread always feels like a craft you either have in your hands or you don't.

S1: Some of that feel is real. But a lean loaf is flour, water, salt and yeast, and the lesson's claim is that a good crusty loaf can be made with about twenty minutes of your hands, a pot with a lid and a day of waiting.

S2: Start with the four, then. What does each one do?

S1: Flour and water make the structure. Wet wheat proteins link into a network, gluten, which America's Test Kitchen says traps air bubbles and stretches as the dough bakes. Yeast makes the gas, carbon dioxide, and the gluten traps it. King Arthur Baking's reference page on yeast says there is almost no reproduction occurring in bread dough, and the rise is almost entirely gas production. So a rise is yeast working, not yeast growing.

S2: And salt is just for taste?

S1: It does more. King Arthur's page on salt says it tightens the gluten, so the dough holds gas better, and it slows the yeast. Leave it out and, in the page's words, the dough is slack and sticky, and bread volume is poor. The yeast eats through the sugars quickly, so the crust comes out pale and dull.

S2: So where does kneading come in?

S1: J. Kenji López-Alt at Test Kitchen has a picture for it. Think of the proteins in flour as bundled up balls of yarn you're trying to tie into one longer piece. Balled up yarn won't tie. You have to straighten it first, and kneading is usually how that's done. But there's a second way. Flour carries its own enzymes, and in López-Alt's words they act like scissors, cutting the balled up proteins into smaller segments that are easier to straighten. Given time in a wet dough they do some of the untangling for you, and the more water there is, the more efficiently they work.

S2: So a long enough rest in a wet enough dough and you skip the kneading.

S1: That's no-knead bread. The best known version is Jim Lahey's, from the Sullivan Street Bakery in Manhattan. You mix flour, a tiny amount of yeast, a little salt and water, leave it twelve to eighteen hours, shape it, and bake it in a heavy covered pot. Its dough is eighty five percent water, and with that much water and that long a rest, López-Alt found, the enzymes had cut the proteins small enough to stretch out and link up during fermentation and a brief turning step, with no kneading.

S2: Couldn't you make it drier, so it's easier to handle?

S1: At seventy percent water and the same long rest, his loaf came out dense and chewy, with rubbery pockets of unleavened flour. His fix was just fifteen extra seconds of kneading, and it let him cut the minimum rest from twelve hours to eight. So ask the course's question: what does knead for ten minutes protect? An organised gluten network. A wet dough with a long rest gets there another way.

S2: Eighty five percent of what, though?

S1: Of the flour's weight. That's baker's percentages. The flour is always a hundred percent, and everything else is divided by the flour's weight. Seven hundred grams of water to a thousand grams of flour is seventy percent, which bakers call seventy percent hydration. Salt is the one ingredient with a near standard figure. King Arthur's reference page gives one point eight to two percent of the flour's weight. Martin Philip, writing on King Arthur's blog, gives one point eight to two point two. Both come from one company, and the course didn't read a second baking authority on it. Two percent sits inside both.

S2: Walk me through one.

S1: Michael Ruhlman gives bread as five parts flour to three parts water. Three divided by five is sixty percent hydration. His post names salt but gives no amounts for salt or yeast, so the lesson adds them. Take five hundred grams of flour. Water at sixty percent is three hundred grams. Salt at two percent is ten grams. And one teaspoon of instant yeast, which King Arthur's weight chart gives two teaspoons as six grams, so one is three, which makes zero point six percent. That yeast figure is the course's choice for a kneaded loaf meant to rise in a few hours, not a standard. So the formula reads: flour, a hundred. Water, sixty. Salt, two. Yeast, zero point six.

S2: And the loaf the lesson has you bake?

S1: King Arthur's no-knead recipe. Six hundred grams of bread flour, six hundred and five of water, fourteen of table salt and a quarter teaspoon of instant yeast. The course does the arithmetic. The water comes to about a hundred and one percent, slightly more water than flour. The yeast is about zero point one three percent, around a fifth of the five to three loaf's, which goes with a ten hour rest. And the salt is about two point three percent, a little above both of King Arthur's own ranges.

S2: More water than flour. How do you even shape that?

S1: You don't. You stir it, leave it, stir it down and scoop it into a greased pot, where it rises and bakes without being shaped or moved. That the pot is what lets such a wet dough work is the course's reading. The recipe doesn't explain it.

S2: The lesson ends on raw flour. I thought raw dough was only a problem because of egg.

S1: The CDC says flour doesn't look like a raw food, but most flour is raw. It names E. coli and Salmonella, says grinding grain and bleaching flour don't kill harmful germs, and says germs are killed only when flour is baked or cooked. The UK's Food Standards Agency says getting ill from uncooked flour is rare, because it's mostly used in food to be baked or cooked. But its advice is plain: you should not eat flour or flour products such as pastry and doughs that have not been cooked thoroughly or are not sold as ready to eat. So bread dough is covered by both, egg or no egg.

S2: Do the two countries agree, then?

S1: On eating raw dough, yes. Where they differ is in how strongly they put it about play dough. The CDC's list includes not letting children play with or eat dough made with raw flour, crafts included. The FSA doesn't say not to play with it. It gives handling advice for crafts and play, and says children and older people may need extra care. Either way, nobody eats it, and everything the flour touched gets washed afterwards.
