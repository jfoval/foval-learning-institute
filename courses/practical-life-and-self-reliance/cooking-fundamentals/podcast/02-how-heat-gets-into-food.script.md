S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Cooking Fundamentals, on the lesson called How heat gets into food.

S2: Where does it start?

S1: With something you've probably done without thinking. You open an oven at two hundred degrees Celsius, about three hundred and ninety Fahrenheit, reach in and pull out a tray. Your arm is in air twice as hot as boiling water for a second or two, and nothing happens to it. Now imagine putting the same hand into a pan of water at a rolling boil, for the same second or two.

S2: I wouldn't.

S1: Nobody would. The oven is hotter, and the water is the one that hurts. That puzzle is the whole lesson in small.

S2: So what's going on?

S1: Temperature tells you how hot something is. It doesn't tell you how fast it hands that heat over. The rate depends on the temperature difference and on what's doing the carrying, and engineers put a number on the second part, called the heat transfer coefficient. The ranges a free engineering reference gives are wide, but air's range starts far below water's.

S2: How much slower are we talking?

S1: The lesson does a sum, and it's clear that it's the course's own arithmetic, with figures picked from inside those ranges, not measured on a hand. Say your skin is at about thirty Celsius. Still oven air is a hundred and seventy degrees hotter, but it carries heat so poorly that it delivers about seventeen hundred watts on each square metre. Boiling water is only seventy degrees hotter than your skin, but take a middling figure for moving water and you get about seventy thousand. About forty times the oven's rate. Water churned by a rolling boil can sit higher, about two hundred times.

S2: From a liquid a hundred degrees cooler.

S1: Yes. And the sum counts only the air. The oven's hot walls also radiate heat onto your arm, which narrows the gap. It doesn't come close to closing it.

S2: Okay. Once the heat reaches the food, what then?

S1: It goes in from the surface, and inside a solid piece of food the only way it travels is conduction, layer by layer. Each layer has to warm before it passes heat on to the next. So time doesn't grow in step with thickness. It grows much faster. Lesson one gave Douglas Baldwin's rule of thumb: twice as thick, about four times as long. He's writing about food in a water bath held at one temperature, and a pot of boiling water is a version of that.

S2: And the practice has you test it.

S1: Two slices from the same potato, one a centimetre thick and one two centimetres, into the same boiling pot. Most people expect the thick one to take twice as long. By Baldwin's rule it's closer to four times. And the lesson adds that in an oven the gap between thin and thick is smaller, because air is slow to hand heat over. It labels that as the course's reading of the physics, not a measurement.

S2: Which does something to recipes, doesn't it.

S1: It does. A recipe that says boil for twelve minutes is really saying, boil pieces about the size I cut them for about twelve minutes. So ask the course's question. What is boil for twelve minutes protecting? A cooked centre. Does it apply here? Only if your pieces are the size the writer's were, starting from the temperature theirs did. A thicker cut, a bigger potato or a frozen piece each breaks that. On frozen, the US Food Safety and Inspection Service says cooking meat and poultry straight from frozen is safe, and takes about fifty percent longer.

S2: Here's one I've always wondered about. Why does a boiled potato never go brown?

S1: Because water has a ceiling. At ordinary pressure it can't get hotter than its boiling point, which is a hundred Celsius, two hundred and twelve Fahrenheit, at sea level, and lower the higher up you are. Add more heat and you get more steam, not hotter water. And while there's water on the surface of food, that surface sits near a hundred too.

S2: And browning needs more than that.

S1: Browning is mostly the Maillard reaction, between amino acids and sugars. A Kansas State extension page puts its start at about a hundred and forty Celsius, two hundred and eighty five Fahrenheit. A twenty twenty five review of the chemistry is more careful. Temperature, time and water all change the reaction, and the higher the temperature, the faster it goes. So the lesson says treat a hundred and forty as a rough mark for fast browning, not an on switch.

S2: So the surface has to dry out first.

S1: That's the order the review gives for baking. The crust dries, then it browns. A boiled potato is surrounded by water, so it never browns. The same potato cut into chunks and roasted browns once its surface water has gone.

S2: Then the lesson turns to fire.

S1: In the United States, the National Fire Protection Association reports that from twenty seventeen to twenty twenty one, cooking was the leading cause of reported home fires and home fire injuries, and unattended cooking was the leading factor. Oil warns you first. If you see wisps of smoke or the oil smells, turn off the burner. And never water. Water sinks under hot oil, flashes to steam, and throws burning oil up and out.

S2: So what do you do?

S1: This is where two sets of official advice differ, and neither is careless. The US Consumer Product Safety Commission says slide a lid over the flames, turn off the heat, and leave the lid on until the pan cools, and its list opens with calling the fire department. The London Fire Brigade says don't tackle it yourself and don't move the pan. Turn off the heat only if it's safe to, leave the room, close the door, warn others, and call nine nine nine.

S2: Those sound opposite.

S1: Closer than they look. The NFPA's own page also says just get out and close the door, and its research found that more than half of non fatal injuries happened when people tried to control the fire themselves. So the lesson's fair reading of both is this. A lid kept by the pan can smother a small fire that has only just started. Anything bigger, or any doubt at all, means heat off if you safely can, everyone out, door closed, and call your emergency number. Never carry the pan anywhere. And the old damp tea towel over the pan? Essex's fire service calls it outdated advice.

S2: The full lesson has why steam burns worse than boiling water, how to check your thermometer in a glass of ice water, why microwaves don't cook from the inside out, what to do in the first minutes after a burn or scald, and the argument over gas hobs and children's asthma, with each side in its own words. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Stay with the pan.
