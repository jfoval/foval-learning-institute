S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Cooking Fundamentals, on the lesson called Eggs, gentle heat and the first emulsion.

S2: Eggs. I'd have said that's the easy one.

S1: The lesson opens with a picture you'll know. Crack an egg into a very hot pan. The edge bubbles, browns and goes stiff, and the yolk is still sitting there raw and cold on top. Crack one into a gentle pan and the white sets tender, the yolk warms through slowly, and you get to choose when to stop. The lesson says that difference is most of what there is to cooking an egg, and it rests on one fact. An egg is several proteins, and they set at different temperatures.

S2: So where do they set?

S1: Two sources, a few degrees apart. The Egg Board, which is the US egg industry's promotion body, gives the white as setting between about sixty two and sixty five degrees Celsius, a hundred and forty four to a hundred and forty nine Fahrenheit, and the yolk between sixty five and seventy Celsius, a hundred and forty nine to a hundred and fifty eight Fahrenheit. The UK's Institute of Food Science and Technology gives rounder numbers, sixty for the white and sixty five for the yolk. Neither claims to be exact. What they agree on is the order. The white starts first, and the yolk follows about five degrees Celsius later. That gap is why you can have a set white and a runny yolk at all.

S2: Then why does the white on a hot pan go rubbery? If it's set, it's set.

S1: Because the white isn't one protein. A twenty twenty three laboratory study lists two main ones. The smaller, about twelve percent of the white's protein, gave way around sixty three degrees Celsius. The big one, about fifty four percent, not until around seventy six. So the white first sets on the smaller protein, and most of it doesn't let go until well above where the yolk sets. The next step the lesson flags as the course's own reading of those laboratory figures, not something either source says about a fried egg. A white held in the low sixties is a soft, just set gel. Take it well past seventy five and it's firmer. Take it far past that, like the edge on a smoking pan, and it's rubbery.

S2: So high heat doesn't cook an egg better, it just cooks the outside too far.

S1: The temperature the egg reaches decides what you get, and the time it takes getting there decides how evenly. For a fried egg, moderate heat fixes most of it, and the other fix is to heat the top as well. A lid for the last minute or so, which traps steam over the egg, or hot fat spooned over it.

S2: Now the part people argue about. Runny eggs. Are they safe?

S1: Here the two countries give different answers. The germ is Salmonella Enteritidis, and the US regulator says it can get into an egg before the shell even forms. So a clean, cold, uncracked egg can carry it, and you can't wash it off, see it or smell it. Heat removes it. The US says cook eggs until the yolks are firm, and it advises everyone, not only people at higher risk, against raw or undercooked eggs, homemade mayonnaise included. For dishes that need raw egg it gives two routes. Pasteurised eggs, which some grocery stores carry and which you can't make at home. Or heat the eggs in liquid over low heat, stirring, to a hundred and sixty Fahrenheit, seventy one Celsius, before they meet anything else.

S2: And the UK?

S1: The UK changed its advice in twenty seventeen, after a report from an expert group the year before. That change was about eggs with the British Lion mark, and today's advice names a second scheme too, Laid in Britain. Those eggs can now be eaten raw or lightly cooked, even by young children, people who are pregnant and older people. The Food Standards Agency pointed to the Lion scheme's controls, from vaccinating hens to keeping eggs cool in transport, and said more than ninety percent of UK eggs were produced under it at the time. Two limits come with it. It doesn't cover people who are severely immunocompromised. And other eggs, a duck egg or an imported one, should still be cooked thoroughly for vulnerable groups.

S2: So which country is right?

S1: The lesson says neither is wrong. It isn't a disagreement about Salmonella or about heat. It's two regulators judging two egg supplies. The parts of the UK report the course read compare Lion eggs with other eggs, not with American ones, and nothing the course read puts the two countries' eggs side by side. So it can't tell you which supply is safer, and it doesn't call either rule too careful or too relaxed. If you live somewhere else, follow your own regulator.

S2: Which brings us to mayonnaise.

S1: The yolk's other job. Oil and water don't mix. An emulsion keeps them mixed for a while, the oil broken into tiny droplets sitting in a little water. But droplets that touch will merge, so something has to coat each one. Yolk does that. The Egg Board credits lecithin, a molecule with one end that sits happily in oil and the other in water, so it settles on the boundary. Marc Anton, a researcher at France's national agricultural research institute, credits mostly the yolk's tiny particles of fat wrapped in proteins and phospholipids, which spread over each droplet as a film. Either way, every droplet is wrapped, and the wrap keeps it apart.

S2: Then why does it break?

S1: Anton separates the failures. Droplets clumping, or drifting to the top, are reversible, and a simple stir deals with them. Droplets merging, which he calls coalescence, is irreversible. The film ruptures and the emulsion is destroyed. That's the greasy, runny mayonnaise, and whisking it harder won't bring it back. The usual cause is speed. As the Egg Board puts it, the oil has to go in slowly so the lecithin can coat the droplets. At the start there's no emulsion yet, so oil poured fast arrives as a pool. Add it a few drops at a time until the sauce thickens, and then you can move to a thin stream.

S2: Put it together for me. I'm making mayonnaise, and one guest is pregnant.

S1: In the US, the advice against homemade mayonnaise made with raw egg names pregnant women among the particularly vulnerable. So use pasteurised eggs, or heat the yolks in liquid to a hundred and sixty Fahrenheit, seventy one Celsius, before any oil goes in. America's Test Kitchen's recipe, the one the lesson follows, does exactly that, heating the yolks with water and lemon juice in the microwave. In the UK, raw yolks from Lion or Laid in Britain eggs are fine for her, and any other eggs get the stricter rule. And in either country, if a guest is severely immunocompromised, ask what their doctor has told them. The same sauce, made safely both ways.
