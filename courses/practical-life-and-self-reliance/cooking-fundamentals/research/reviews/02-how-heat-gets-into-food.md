1. **[high] F1, altitude quiz item is wrong by FSIS's own figures.** Stem: "You live about 1,500 m
   above sea level ... 207°F in a pan of boiling water", key "probably reading correctly". FSIS's
   "High Altitude Cooking" page: "With each 500-feet increase in elevation, the boiling point of
   water is lowered by just under 1 °F. At 7,500 feet, for example, water boils at about 198 °F."
   1,500 m is about 4,900 feet, so about 203°F; a thermometer reading 207°F there reads about 4°F
   high. The item was built on the thermometer page's "at least 2 ... perhaps ... 5 degrees F",
   which understates the drop above about 2,500 feet. The body and the exercise had the same gap:
   a reader in a mountain town told to expect 207 to 210°F would conclude a good thermometer was
   faulty. Fix: stem to 750 m (2,500 feet); body quotes the high-altitude page and says the 5°F
   figure fits modest heights; exercise gives the reader the rule to predict their own boil.
2. **[high] F2, Baldwin's four-times rule graded outside its scope.** Quiz item 2 applied "twice as
   thick takes about four times as long" to roasting cauliflower in an oven, and the misconception
   list gave "closer to four times" unconditionally. The body itself says Baldwin writes about a
   water bath. The square law holds when the surface is held near the medium's temperature (water,
   high coefficient); in oven air, with the low coefficient the lesson has just taught, heating is
   partly surface-limited and the thin-to-thick ratio is nearer proportional. The key ("a good
   deal more than twice as long") is not safe for 1.5 cm against 3 cm slices in an oven. Fix: item
   rewritten as simmered sweet potato, with the hard-boil distractor refuted by water's ceiling
   (taught in the lesson); misconception and objective scoped to water; one labelled sentence in
   the body saying the gap is smaller in an oven.
3. **[medium] F3, "Why a hotter oven isn't a shortcut" overstated.** "The centre plods on at close
   to its old pace" and "very little time saved" are stronger than the physics: a hotter oven does
   raise the driving difference at the centre too (roughly a quarter faster for 180 to 230°C on a
   simple estimate), just much less than at the surface. Labelled as the course's reasoning, which
   is right, but the reasoning was too strong. Fix: softened in the section and the misconception.
4. **[medium] F4, oven-hand arithmetic omits radiation.** Drafter's flag (1): the sums check.
   10 × 170 = 1,700; 1,000 × 70 = 70,000, 41 times; 5,000 × 70 = 350,000, 206 times; "about 40 to
   200" is fair, and the figures are inside the page's ranges and labelled as the course's choice.
   But the body later says an oven roast gets "radiation from the hot walls", and a 200°C wall
   radiates onto skin at a rate of the same order as the convective 1,700 W/m² (Stefan-Boltzmann,
   course's own estimate, not printed). The "40 to 200 times" compares water with oven air only,
   which the predict question does say, but "That is why the oven doesn't hurt" reads as the whole
   story. Fix: one sentence saying the sum counts only the air, the walls narrow the gap, and it
   is not closed. No figure printed for the radiation.
5. **[medium] F5, gas section: Kashtan 2024 misdescribed and uncaveated (neutrality).** The lesson
   put Kashtan's 4.0 ppb under "What's measured" and called the 50,000 cases a model "from its
   measured exposures". The abstract (rechecked): exposure is estimated "using emissions and
   concentration measurements from >100 homes, a room-specific indoor air quality model,
   epidemiological risk parameters, and statistical sampling"; the paper's own words are "likely
   causes ~50,000 cases". So the exposure is an estimate and the 50,000 is the same kind of
   attributable calculation as Gruenwald's, which the lesson caveated ("takes the link as causal")
   while leaving Kashtan's, which had the last word, bare. Fix: section retitled "What's measured,
   and what's estimated from it"; Kashtan described by its method; the 50,000 given in the
   authors' full sentence with the same caveat as Gruenwald's.
