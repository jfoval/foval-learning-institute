# Outline: Cooking Fundamentals

*Stage 2, 2026-09-24. Built from `research/SOURCES.md` (the synthesis and Parts A to D). Sequenced
for how the ideas build. Not a sensitive domain under standards 3.4, and `sensitive_domain` stays
false: the course's contested questions are few, narrow and named below, and its safety content is
regulators' rules rather than disputed science. Two exceptions to the default single reviewer are
set under "Neutrality notes".*

## How many lessons, and why

**Twelve.** SOURCES' first two findings decide the shape. **Safety is time plus temperature**, and
**most of the kitchen rules experts correct are about water and heat and share one mechanism**. So
the course teaches the mechanisms early (knife and evenness, heat, taste, time plus temperature)
and then spends its methods lessons applying them. Each later lesson re-uses an earlier mechanism
rather than stating a new rule. The taxonomy row says "technique over recipes", and the leading
professional text's move from organisation by food to organisation by method (Part A 1.5) supports
building the course around methods, with foods as the worked examples.

1. **Your knife, your board, and mise en place.** Grips, the claw, the cuts as cooking sizes,
   boards, a sharp knife and why; scope, equipment and the course's question.
2. **How heat gets into food.** Three modes, temperature against rate, thickness and time, water's
   ceiling, the thermometer, microwaves, fire, steam and burns, the gas stove.
3. **Seasoning by taste.** Salt by weight and by taste, acid, fat as texture and carrier, umami and
   MSG, balancing in stages; garlic in oil.
4. **What makes people ill, and the rules that stop it.** Time plus temperature, clean, separate,
   cook, chill, washing chicken, cooling and leftovers, dates, higher-risk people, the US and UK
   side by side.
5. **Eggs: gentle heat and the first emulsion.** Set temperatures, five egg cookeries, the green
   ring, mayonnaise, the US and UK on runny eggs.
6. **Searing and sautéing, and the pan sauce.** Dry surfaces, crowding, salting ahead, the searing
   and flipping and room-temperature myths, pans and their care, nonstick, stir-frying, fond.
7. **Roasting and grilling: carryover and resting.** Oven and grill heat, carryover, resting as
   temperature control, whole cuts, rolled joints, pork, mince, roast potatoes, acrylamide.
8. **Water and steam: boil, simmer, poach, steam, braise.** Why water is gentle, collagen, the
   braise, slow cookers, alcohol retention, salt and the boiling point.
9. **Rice, grains, beans and pasta.** Gelatinisation, absorption against excess water, rinsing and
   arsenic, salted soaks, kidney beans, rice and *Bacillus cereus*, pasta in less water.
10. **Stock and sauces.** Gelatin, simmer not boil, cooling a big pot and *C. perfringens*,
    starch-thickened sauces, vinaigrette, the pan sauce revisited.
11. **Bread.** Flour, water, salt, yeast; gluten by kneading or by time; baker's percentages and
    the 5:3 ratio; one no-knead loaf; raw flour.
12. **Cooking without a recipe: ratios and a week of meals.** Reading a recipe for its method,
    ratios, batch cooking, freezing and thawing, the storage plan. The project starts here.

**Food safety is lesson 4, and that is the constraint that fixed the order.** No lesson before it
handles raw meat, poultry, fish or raw egg: lesson 1 cuts vegetables, lesson 2 heats water,
vegetables and bread, lesson 3 seasons a soup. Lesson 4 comes after heat rather than first (where
the professional syllabi put it, Part A "What the field considers essential") because time plus
temperature cannot be taught before the reader knows why the centre of a thick piece lags the
surface and how to read a thermometer, both of which are lesson 2. **Salting meat ahead moves out of
the seasoning lesson into lesson 6**, where its mechanism (a wet surface will not brown) lives and
where the reader has had lesson 4.

**Eggs move up from eighth to fifth.** The home-cook curricula and the science courses both teach
eggs early as the model protein (Leiths puts egg cookery third, López-Alt's book opens with it,
Part A final section), and here eggs do three jobs before meat arrives: they show protein setting at
different temperatures, which lesson 7's carryover depends on; they introduce the emulsion, which
lesson 6's pan sauce reuses; and they are the first place lesson 4's US and UK difference changes
what a reader actually cooks.

**Specific hazards are taught where the food is cooked, not all in lesson 4.** Part A recommends
this ("place it early but short, and then weave cooling, holding and temperatures into the stock,
egg and meat lessons", as City & Guilds does), and it is the only way lesson 4 stays inside one
sitting. Lesson 4 teaches the principle, the four steps and the side-by-side rules; the hazards are
placed as follows: garlic in oil (3), runny and raw eggs (5), pork, rolled joints, burgers and
kebabs (7), slow cookers (8), rice and kidney beans (9), big pots cooling (10), raw flour (11),
thawing, freezing and canning (12).

**Why not fewer.** Heat and safety cannot share a lesson without one of them losing its mechanism.
The dry-heat methods split in two because searing turns on the surface and roasting on the centre
(carryover), and each has its own myth. Bread is its own craft and the outcomes promise a loaf.
**Why not more.** Vegetables have no lesson of their own: every syllabus has one, but here they are
the worked examples in lessons 1, 2, 6, 7 and 8, and a separate lesson would repeat those
mechanisms on new foods. Fish has no lesson either: the only fish-specific material read is the
temperature charts (Part B 1.1, 1.2), which lesson 4 carries, and fish appears as an example in
lessons 6 and 8.

**Lesson length.** Most should land between 55 and 85 measured minutes, remembering that measured
minutes include the exercises and the Sources list. **Lesson 4 is planned at about 90** and earns it:
it is the safety-critical lesson, it carries two countries' rules side by side, and the hazards
have already been moved out to shorten it. **Lesson 9 is planned at about 85** with a named seam if
its draft runs past 100: rice before the seam, beans and pasta after.

## What is deliberately left out, and said so in lesson 1

- **Every health claim about what to eat.** Nutrition: The Evidence, earlier this term, owns them.
  When salt comes up, one sentence points to Nutrition lesson 7 ("Salt and sugar"); when processed
  meat or browned meat comes up, Nutrition lesson 8 ("Processed food and meat"); alcohol and health,
  Nutrition lesson 10. The alcohol retention table in lesson 8 is a cooking fact, not a health
  claim, and is stated as one.
- **Baking and pastry beyond one lean bread.** Cakes, pastry, flatbreads and sourdough are named in
  lesson 11 as the next craft and not taught.
- **Butchery beyond cutting up and carving a chicken** (Pépin's videos, lesson 7).
- **Deep frying beyond its fire safety** (lesson 2).
- **Preserving and home canning.** Emergency Preparedness owns food storage for outages and
  canning; lesson 12 gives the CDC's single instruction (use the USDA *Complete Guide to Home
  Canning* and no other recipe, Part B 5.4) and stops.
- **Special and religious diets, and food allergy management.** Named as things the course does
  not teach; the week project must work for a vegetarian (see decision 9). Allergens get the
  minimum SOURCES requires (gate G1).
- **Treating injuries.** First Aid and CPR, later this term, owns it. Lesson 2 gives the NHS and Red
  Cross first response for a burn, because it was read; lesson 1 gives **no** first aid for a cut,
  because no cut first-aid page was read, and points there.
- **The appliance itself.** Home Repair and Maintenance.
- **Pressure cooking**, unless gate G7 is closed (see below).
- **Cost.** Not researched. No lesson claims cooking saves money, or says what anything costs.
- **Standards 2.5's note**, "education, not personalised advice", is given once in lesson 1, in the
  scope section, as the standards ask, and the higher-risk callout (decision 3) carries the part of
  it that matters most.

## Ten decisions, settled here so no lesson re-opens them

**1. The US and the UK side by side, every time a rule applies, and neither called wrong.** SOURCES
finding 3 and Part B 7: the two regulators disagree on how doneness is judged, pork, hot food into
the fridge, leftovers, reheating, rice, thawing and raw eggs. Lesson 4 carries the full table as the
course's reference. Every later lesson that applies a rule gives both, in the form "the US says …;
the UK says …", and says where the sources explain the difference (the Lion scheme for eggs) and
where they do not. Readers elsewhere are told in lesson 4 to follow their own country's regulator
and that these two are shown because both were read. **Consumer figures and Food Code figures are
never mixed in one sentence** (40°F and 140°F consumer; 41°F and 135°F retail, Part B 2.6).

**2. Safety is time plus temperature, taught once and applied after.** Lesson 4 teaches it with
the FSIS 2021 tables, the FSA equivalence list and the Food Code roast table (Parts B 2.1, 3.1,
6.1), and draws the chicken table as a chart. Later lessons apply it in a sentence ("lesson 4's
table says …") rather than re-deriving it. The thermometer is introduced and calibrated in lesson
2, placed correctly in lesson 4, and used in every lesson that cooks meat, poultry, eggs or a
leftover.

**3. One callout for higher-risk people, in lessons 4, 5 and 7, word for word.** Draft text, to be
checked clause by clause at Stage 4 against FSA "Burgers" and the burger guidance (Part B 3.7),
the FSA fact checker on eggs (3.5), CDC raw flour (4.2) and CDC Listeria risk groups (4.2):

> :::callout Cooking for someone at higher risk
> Food poisoning is more dangerous for anyone who is pregnant, aged 65 or over, a young child, or has a weakened immune system. If you're cooking for someone in one of these groups, cook burgers and other mince all the way through, don't give them raw dough or batter, and follow your country's rule on runny eggs in lesson 5. If they are severely immunocompromised, ask their doctor what they can eat.
> :::

If gate G4 is closed, a clause on chilled ready-to-eat foods may be added. Lesson 4 is where the
reasons are given; the callout is a pointer, so that a reader arriving at lesson 5 or 7 from a
search engine meets it. A check in the build, like Nutrition's and Strength and Fitness's, would be
worth adding when lesson 4 lands; it is not added now.

**4. No health claims about what to eat.** See the boundary above. Browning and acrylamide get one
paragraph in lesson 7, stated as a contested question in both sides' words (Part D 3f), with a
pointer to Nutrition lesson 8's treatment of how to read a cancer-hazard claim; Nutrition does not
cover acrylamide itself and the lesson must not say it does. "Microwaving keeps more vitamins"
(FDA, Part C 10h) is stated once, in lesson 2, as the FDA's statement about cooking methods, not
as dietary advice.

**5. Examples from several cuisines in every methods lesson.** SOURCES "Open concerns": every book
and syllabus read teaches the French-derived method list. Each lesson from 3 to 12 draws its
worked examples from at least two traditions besides French and American, and the lesson plans
below name candidates. The classical cut names in lesson 1 are introduced as one tradition's
vocabulary for sizes, which is what they are. **No lesson calls a dish authentic or a technique
proper.** Where a named dish is an example, the lesson makes no claim about how it is "really" made
beyond what a source read supports.

**6. The course's question: what is this rule protecting, and does it apply here?** Memory added
the sample, Nutrition "instead of what?", Strength and Fitness "trained whom, for how long,
measured on what?". **A kitchen rule is a mechanism compressed into an instruction**, and it fails
when it is carried to a case its mechanism does not cover. "Don't crowd the pan" protects a dry
surface on meat, and fails for mushrooms (Part C 6, Arnold); "sear to seal in juices" protects
nothing (Part C 1); "don't wash chicken" protects the sink and the salad, which is why the USDA's
own authors moved the message to hands and sinks (Part D 3e). Introduced in lesson 1 (even pieces
protect even cooking), named in lesson 2, used on every myth after, and turned on recipes in lesson
12. This is what "technique over recipes" means in practice, and it is the transfer test's skill.

**7. Read-level discipline, and the unread line.** A lesson may not cite, name or quote anything on
the `<!-- unread: -->` line, and the build fails on the words. Concretely: no lesson names
McGee's book (his 2009 *New York Times* pasta column was read in full and may be cited), López-Alt's
book (link his YouTube roast-potato video with a label that leaves out its first two words),
the CIA's textbook (so the 9th-to-10th edition reorganisation stays in this outline and out of
lessons), Pépin's book (his videos are fine), ATK's science book or its numbered concepts (ATK's
free articles read in Part C are fine), Lahey's book (Lahey may be named as the baker whose method
ATK's article describes), Brown's textbook (so the carryover range it supplies to Wikipedia cannot
be cited, see lesson 7), Bittman (the word itself: the 2006 column is not named), Verhoeven
(the knife-sharpening experiments), or Augustin (the alcohol figures are cited to the USDA table).
**Quotations marked [T] or "fetch tool" in SOURCES are re-checked against the page before a lesson
prints them.** **Wikipedia is never a cited source** (standards 2.2): where SOURCES leans on it, the
lesson plans below say what replaces it.

**8. Units.** Every temperature in both scales, **in the source's own unit first with its own
rounding** (FSIS "145°F (62.8°C)"; foodsafety.gov "145°F (63°C)"; FSA "70°C"). Weights in grams
first with ounces in brackets; volumes where the source used them (US recipes, the ATK bean brine),
converted. Ratios and bread by weight only.

**9. Equipment, and the vegetarian path.** Lesson 1 states what the course assumes: one chef's
knife, a board, a heavy frying pan, a pot with a lid, a sheet pan, an instant-read thermometer
(FSIS: only about 15% of people consistently use one, Part B 1.9), and a kitchen scale for lessons
3, 11 and 12. Lessons 7 and 11 need an oven, and say so at the top. Every lesson that uses meat
also offers a meat-free practice that exercises the same mechanism (mushrooms or tofu for searing,
vegetables for roasting, beans for braising), and the project can be done without meat.

**10. Dated figures are quarantined.** Each dated figure sits in one sentence with its date, so a
later session can update it without re-opening the teaching: CDC's 9.9 million (2019 estimates,
published 2025) beside the older 48 million with CDC's own "cannot meaningfully" compare; the FSA's
2.4 million (new models due by early 2027); the FDA Food Code 2026; NFPA fire figures (2017 to 2021
edition); PFAS law (moving monthly, dated "as of September 2026"); the 2025 resting revision.

## Lesson sequence

### 1. Your knife, your board, and mise en place (~55 min)
- **Objectives:** apply a pinch grip and the claw to cut an onion and a carrot into even pieces,
  and explain why evenness is a cooking decision; identify the small, medium and large dice,
  batonnet and julienne by size and say which a dish calls for; explain what the evidence for "a
  sharp knife is safer" is and what it is not, and set up a stable board and a mise en place;
  say what this course covers, leaves out and assumes.
- **Core ideas:** OSHA's knife rules verbatim (Part D 1: let a falling knife fall; never leave a
  knife in a sink; put it down when interrupted), with the note that "cut away from the body" is
  written for boning and the home version is "keep the guiding hand out of the line", which the
  claw does; ATK's pinch grip and claw (D 1); a flat side down and a damp towel under the board
  (Kentucky, D 1, which repeats ATK and is not independent of it); the Escoffier School sizes,
  converted (D 1, using 6 mm for a small dice); knife injuries in US emergency departments, Smith
  2013 (434,259 a year; 66% fingers or thumbs; kitchen knives 36% **of the injuries whose knife type
  was recorded**, data to 2008); McGorry 2003 (grip force, meat cutters, mechanism not injury
  counts, C 9); wood against plastic boards (Ak, Cliver and Kaspar 1994; Cliver 2006; FSIS "either",
  C 10i, D 3g) with a forward pointer to lesson 4's separate board; honing and sharpening (gate
  G10); the course's question introduced (decision 6); scope, equipment and the education note.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why a piece's size sets its cooking time (previewing lesson 2: heat
  enters from the surface, so thicker pieces take disproportionately longer); why a dull blade needs
  more force and more force means less control.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: an onion diced start to finish, each cut with its reason (ATK's
  "How To Chop an Onion" and Pépin's knife-skills video embedded). (b) wrinkle: a round, rolling
  vegetable (carrot, sweet potato, a knob of ginger), where the first job is to make a flat side,
  and a pan of unevenly cut carrot where the small pieces scorch while the large ones are raw: the
  rule "cut evenly" is protecting even cooking, which is the course's question on its first outing.
- **Misconceptions:** "a dull knife is safer"; "catch a falling knife"; "plastic boards are more
  hygienic than wood"; "the classical cuts are for show".
- **Practice:** dice one onion and one carrot, measure ten pieces of each with a ruler and record
  the spread; set up a board and a mise en place for a simple recipe and photograph it.
- **Sources:** D 1, C 9, C 10i, D 3g, A 4 (videos).
- **Connects:** Time Management (mise en place is planning), Habits and Self-Discipline (practice
  that becomes automatic). Leads to lesson 2, which explains why size sets time.
- **Spaced review of:** none within the course; Logic and Argument's habit of asking what a claim
  rests on, applied to "a sharp knife is safer".

### 2. How heat gets into food (~75 min)
- **Objectives:** explain conduction, convection and radiation, and identify which dominates in a
  given method; explain why temperature and the rate of heat transfer differ, using the oven-air
  and boiling-water calculation, and why thickness sets cooking time; calibrate a thermometer and
  explain where it goes and why; state what to do for a pan fire, an oven fire and a burn, including
  where US and UK fire services differ.
- **Core ideas:** the three modes (C 7); heat-transfer coefficients and the worked arithmetic (1.7
  kW/m² against 325 kW/m², about 200 times, stated as the researcher's arithmetic from Engineering
  ToolBox ranges, C 7); steam's latent heat and why steam burns are severe; Baldwin's "a steak that is
  twice as thick takes about four times longer" (B 6.2); frozen food takes about 50% longer (FSIS,
  B 1.5); water's 100°C ceiling and why browning needs a dry surface (C 6, gate G9 for the figure);
  altitude lowers the boil (FSIS thermometer page, B 1.9); thermometer types, calibration in ice
  water and placement (B 1.9); microwaves heat the outer layers and the inside by conduction (FDA,
  C 10h); fire: NFPA figures (dated), unattended cooking, US lid-and-heat-off against the London
  Fire Brigade's leave-and-call, NFPA's own "Just get out!", never water or flour, oven door shut
  (D 2a); burns: 20 minutes under cool running water, no ice, butter or creams, when to go to
  hospital (NHS, Red Cross, D 2b), then a pointer to First Aid and CPR; oil smoke as the warning
  sign (NFPA, FSIS, D 2a, 2c); gas stoves and indoor air in one short section (D 3c).
- **Mechanism to explain:** heat flow depends on the medium as much as its temperature, which is
  why you can reach into a 200°C oven but not into boiling water; the centre of a piece is the last
  to heat because heat has to be conducted in from the surface.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: the oven hand and the pot of water, calculated. (b) wrinkle: two
  slices of potato, one 1 cm and one 2 cm thick, in the same pot: the reader predicts the second
  takes twice as long, and it takes much longer, which is why a recipe's time fails for a thicker
  piece and a thermometer does not.
- **Misconceptions:** "microwaves cook from the inside out"; "a hotter oven cooks everything
  proportionally faster"; "water or flour will put out a pan fire"; "put butter or ice on a burn";
  "a damp tea towel over the pan" (UK services no longer advise it; no date given).
- **Practice:** calibrate your thermometer in ice water by the FSIS method, then in boiling water,
  and note your altitude; the potato-thickness test, timed; write a fire plan for your kitchen
  (where the lid is, where the exit is, your emergency number).
- **Sources:** C 6, C 7, C 10h, B 1.5, B 1.9, B 6.2, D 2, D 3c.
- **Connects:** lesson 1's even pieces. Leads to lesson 3, and lays the ground for lesson 4's time
  plus temperature.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's question, now named: "cut evenly" protects even heat.
- **Neutrality:** the gas stove section is contested (decision in "Neutrality notes").

### 3. Seasoning by taste (~65 min)
- **Objectives:** explain what salt does to flavour and why a teaspoon of one salt is not a
  teaspoon of another; apply acid, fat or umami to a dish that tastes flat, heavy or thin, and say
  which problem each fixes; identify glutamate-rich ingredients from several cuisines and state what
  the double-blind evidence shows about MSG; season a dish in stages by tasting.
- **Core ideas:** Nosrat's own words from the NPR interview (salt drawing juices from a tomato;
  salts differing "almost … twice as much" by volume; fat "mostly about texture" and a carrier; acid
  as balance) and the book's organising idea from its publisher (A 2.1; **nothing else may be
  attributed to the book**, and the widely quoted line about salt's impact is snippet only);
  weighing your own teaspoon of salt; FSANZ's free glutamate table (Parmesan, tomatoes, peas, soy

  for cooks who will wash (CDC's steps, D 3e); cooling (FSIS hot food straight in against the FSA's

  3.2, 2.5); fridge temperatures; leftovers 3 to 4 days against 48 hours; reheating to 165°F or

### 5. Eggs: gentle heat and the first emulsion (~70 min)
- **Objectives:** explain why white and yolk set at different temperatures and use it to cook a
  boiled, scrambled, fried and poached egg and an omelette to a chosen doneness; explain the green
  ring and prevent it; explain what an emulsion is and why mayonnaise breaks, make one, and rescue a
  broken one; state the US and UK advice on runny and raw eggs and who it applies to.
- **Core ideas:** white proteins begin setting before the yolk does (gate G9 for the figures);
  sous-vide and onsen-style eggs at 63 to 64°C as the demonstration; the green ring as iron sulphide,
  harmless, prevented by not overcooking and cooling fast (UNL, C 8b); don't wash shop eggs, keep
  them in the carton in the main compartment (B 1.6, 1.8); the float test tells age, not safety
  (FSA, B 3.5); the US "cook until yolk and white are firm", egg dishes to 160°F, and pasteurised
  eggs (B 1.2, 4.2); the UK's Lion and Laid in Britain eggs safe raw or lightly cooked even for
  higher-risk groups, except the severely immunocompromised (B 3.5); the Food Code's 145°F for 15
  seconds and its 3D reduction (B 2.1); emulsions (C 8c, gate G8); hollandaise named without
  temperatures.
- **Mechanism to explain:** proteins unfold and bond as they heat, different proteins at different
  temperatures, so gentle heat gives a set white and a runny yolk and fierce heat gives rubber;

  fast.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: soft- and hard-boiled eggs by time, with the cooling step that

  shakshuka (eggs poached in a sauce), Japanese rolled omelette, Chinese steamed egg custard, a
  Spanish tortilla.
- **Misconceptions:** "if it floats it's unsafe"; "the green ring is a sign of a bad egg"; "wash
  eggs before using them"; "high heat cooks eggs faster, so it's better".
- **Practice:** cook four eggs to four doneness levels and log times and heat; make mayonnaise with
  eggs that suit your country's rule, split a small batch on purpose, and rescue it.
- **Sources:** C 8b, C 8c, B 1.2, 1.6, 1.8, 2.1, 3.5, 4.2, A 4 (Pépin poaching and omelette videos).

### 6. Searing and sautéing, and the pan sauce (~85 min)
- **Objectives:** explain why a wet or crowded pan steams instead of browning, and apply it (dry the
  surface, salt at the right time, give pieces room, preheat); evaluate "searing seals in juices",
  "bring meat to room temperature first" and "flip only once" against the tests; choose a pan by its
  material and care for it, including cast iron and nonstick; make a pan sauce from fond and explain
  why cold butter holds it together.
- **Core ideas:** fast browning needs a surface well above 100°C (C 6, G9); ATK on crowding (C 6);
  Arnold's crowded wet mushrooms (C 6); ATK's rinsed mushroom test, closed against open gills (C
  10b); the ATK eight-steak test, about 22% loss either way (C 1); salting ahead (C 3a, see the
  unverified-figures table); room temperature (Kenji via Wilson, and the 2-hour rule, C 10e);
  flipping (C 10f); pan materials by conductivity and heat storage (C 7); clad stainless (ATK, C 7);
  cast iron: Lodge on soap, soaking, the dishwasher and long acidic simmers (C 10g, D 3b); nonstick:
  the three questions, both positions on the third, and the practical rule (D 3a; decision 10 for
  the law); aluminium and dementia (Alzheimer's Society, D 3f), one paragraph; stir-frying as the
  same mechanism in a different pan (cook in batches; no claims about a wok beyond what was read);
  fond, deglazing, reducing and cold butter (ATK, C 8f).
- **Mechanism to explain:** water on the surface pins it at 100°C until it evaporates, and cold,
  crowded food releases water faster than the pan can boil it off; the cost of waiting for the water
  to go is an overcooked interior in meat, and nothing in a mushroom.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: chicken thighs seared skin side down, checked with the
  thermometer, with a pan sauce of stock, reduced and finished with cold butter. (b) wrinkle:
  mushrooms, where "don't crowd" and "don't wash" both fail, because the rule protects a dry surface
  on meat and a mushroom loses its water before it browns anyway (the course's question). A
  stir-fry of beef or tofu with vegetables, cooked in batches, as the third example.
- **Misconceptions:** "searing seals in the juices"; "let the steak come to room temperature";
  "flip it only once"; "never use soap on cast iron"; "cast iron is naturally non-stick"; "nonstick
  pans put chemicals in your food" and its mirror "nonstick is fine at any heat"; "aluminium pans
  cause Alzheimer's".
- **Practice:** sear two batches of mushrooms or tofu, one crowded and one with room, and log time
  and colour; make a pan sauce and deliberately boil a spoonful of it after the butter goes in.
- **Sources:** C 1, C 3a, C 6, C 7, C 8f, C 10b, C 10e, C 10f, C 10g, D 3a, D 3b, D 3f.
- **Connects:** lesson 2's water ceiling, lesson 3's salt, lesson 4's raw meat handling, lesson 5's

### 7. Roasting and grilling: carryover and resting (~75 min)

  rare steak and a rare burger are different; roast potatoes and vegetables, and state the two
  positions on acrylamide.
- **Core ideas:** roasting and grilling as convection and radiation (C 7); FSIS's "oven temperature
  no lower than 325°F" (B 1.3); the Food Code roast table and its "holding time may include post-oven
  heat rise" (B 2.1); carryover as the reason to pull early, measured by the reader because no
  citable range was read (decision 7); resting: Gritzer 2025 and Ibrahim, with Chris Young, taught as
  the current best view (C 2, contested, evidence moving); the FSIS 145°F plus 3 minutes, the 2011

- **Sources:** C 2, C 7, B 1.3, 1.9, 2.1, 3.1, 3.7, D 3d, D 3f, A 2.3, A 4.

### 9. Rice, grains, beans and pasta (~85 min)
- **Objectives:** explain starch gelatinisation and use it to cook rice by absorption and in excess

  rinsing (ATK) and Gray 2016 (rinsing barely moves arsenic and strips enrichment; excess water cuts
  inorganic arsenic by 40 to 60% and enriched vitamins by 50 to 70%, C 8a), with the honest note that
  this matters where rice is enriched; ratios (G6); ATK's salted soak, about 1.4% (C 3b); acid (G13);
  kidney beans: the lectin, "as few as four or five raw beans", onset 1 to 3 hours, slow-cooker

- **Core ideas:** the CIA's "ratios and formulas" in its first course (A 3.4); Ruhlman's ratios, the
  directly read ones (pasta dough 3:2, pancakes) and the rechecked ones (vinaigrette, pie dough,
  biscuit) (A 1.4); thawing and refreezing (FSIS: fridge-thawed food may be refrozen; UK: use within
  24 hours of defrosting, B 1.4, 1.5, 3.2); freezer times as quality, not safety (FSIS 3 to 4 months;
  FSA meats best within 2 to 3 months); garlic in oil (lesson 3 review); canning (CDC's single
  instruction, B 5.4); dates and smell (lesson 4 review, G3); cooking for others and allergens
  (G1). The course's question turned on recipes: which step is the recipe's rule protecting?
- **Mechanism to explain:** a recipe is a set of ratios, a method and tasting points written for
  one kitchen; the ratio and the method travel and the times do not (lesson 2's thickness).
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a stir-fry recipe taken apart into its method, its sauce ratio and
  its tasting point, then cooked with different vegetables. (b) wrinkle: a week plan built on a
  Sunday braise and a Monday pot of rice, which works under US rules (3 to 4 days) and fails under
  UK ones (48 hours for leftovers, 24 hours for rice), and the freezer schedule that fixes it.
- **Misconceptions:** "good cooks don't measure"; "leftovers are good for a week"; "never refreeze
  anything that has thawed"; "a recipe's cooking time is a fact".
- **Practice:** rewrite three recipes you own as methods; begin the project.
- **Sources:** A 1.4, A 3.4, B 1.4, 1.5, 3.2, 5.4, D 3h.
- **Connects:** Time Management (a week planned backwards from Sunday), Habits (a routine that
  survives a bad week). Closes the course; points to the project, First Aid and CPR, Emergency
  Preparedness and Nutrition.
- **Spaced review of:** every lesson's mechanism, through the week plan.

## Assessments

- **Course-end test:** 20 items, at least 13 application. Blueprint: lesson 1 (1), 2 (2: rate against
  temperature, fire response), 3 (2: a flat dish fixed, MSG evidence), 4 (4: time plus temperature
  from the chart, order of work, cooling, the two-country difference), 5 (1), 6 (2: crowding with a
  case not in the lesson, pan care), 7 (2: carryover, the rolled joint or burger), 8 (1: alcohol
  retention), 9 (2: rice storage, kidney beans), 10 (1: the big pot), 11 (1: baker's percentages), 12
  (1: a recipe read for its method). **Every safety item either names the country whose rule it asks
  about or has one correct answer under both.** New cases throughout; no worked example's food or
  numbers reused. Key sequence irregular, all four positions used, option lengths within about 15
  characters.
- **Project: "A week from five techniques."** Deliverable: a plan, a log and a short report.
  1. Choose no more than five techniques from the course and name the mechanism each depends on.
  2. Plan at least five dinners for one week that use only those techniques, including at least two
     batch items cooked once and used twice (a braise or a pot of beans, a pot of grains, a stock).
     A meat-free plan is fully eligible.
  3. Write the storage plan for your country: for each batch item, how it will be cooled, how long
     it keeps in the fridge, what gets frozen and when, how it is thawed, and how it is reheated.
     If anyone eating is in a higher-risk group, say what changes. If you cook for anyone with a
     food allergy, say how you handled it (G1).
  4. Cook the week and keep a log: what you cooked, one seasoning adjustment per meal, one thing
     that went wrong and why (by mechanism).
  5. Cook one of the dinners without a recipe, from its method and a ratio, and report what you
     decided at each tasting point.
  **Rubric:** techniques chosen and each mechanism stated correctly; **storage plan correct against
  the reader's country's rules (an error on rice, big-pot cooling or leftover days fails this
  criterion outright)**; batch items used within their limits; seasoning adjustments logged with a
  reason; the no-recipe dinner reasoned from method and ratio; the report explains at least one
  failure by mechanism rather than by luck.

## Neutrality notes

Not a sensitive domain, and most of the course is established technique and regulators' rules. The
contested and value questions are few, and each is placed:

- **Washing raw chicken** (lesson 4). The microbiology is established; keeping the practice is a
  value question for the cook. The practitioners' case is stated in terms they would recognise
  (texture, cleanliness, family practice, distrust of processing, and the acid wash as part of
  preparation), gate G2 governs how far the lesson may describe named traditions, and the lesson
  teaches the harm-reduced version without mockery. **Lesson 4 gets the neutrality audit** in
  addition to its reviewer, for this and for the US and UK framing.
- **US against UK** (lesson 4 and throughout). Not a scientific dispute: two regulators writing for
  different food systems and habits. Present both, never pick one silently, never call one
  over-cautious or lax. Pink pork is described as a difference in how cautiously advice is phrased,
  with the FSA's own 60°C for 45 minutes as the evidence that the microbiology is shared.
- **MSG** (lesson 3). Established that the general syndrome claim fails double-blind testing;
  contested at the margin (Geha 2000). The history of the name is stated plainly, including the
  stigma it carried, in Kean's account. People who report symptoms are not mocked; the lesson says
  what the challenge studies found about them.
- **Gas stoves and indoor air** (lesson 2). Established that burners add NO2 and some benzene and
  that ventilation reduces it; contested whether this causes a measurable share of childhood asthma.
  Gruenwald 2022's authors' affiliation (RMI), the American Gas Association's statement and Li
  2023's declared industry work are each stated once, as interests, without insinuation; ISAAC's
  null result and Kashtan's measurements are given. The advice is the same on every reading. Kept
  short. **Lesson 2 gets the neutrality audit on this section only.**
- **Nonstick and PFAS** (lesson 6). Three questions kept apart: migration from an intact pan
  (established low, FDA), overheating (established), and whether PTFE cookware should be sold (value
  with a contested core). The Cookware Sustainability Alliance and the ban's proponents are each
  quoted in their own words; both kitchen choices are presented as defensible. Law dated "as of
  September 2026".
- **Resting meat** (lesson 7). Contested, with the evidence moving; the 2025 revision is taught as
  the current best view and its narrow base (one publication plus Chris Young) is said.
- **Acrylamide** (lesson 7). Contested empirical at the level of human risk; EFSA and the FSA
  (precaution from animal evidence) and Cancer Research UK and Spiegelhalter (no reliable human link)
  each in their own words, both true to their method. One paragraph.
- **Wooden against plastic boards** (lesson 1). Contested, leaning to "either, if kept clean".
- **Cuisines** (decision 5). No tradition's technique presented as the proper one; no dish called
  authentic; the French names of the cuts introduced as vocabulary.
- **Alcohol, meat and religious food rules.** Value questions the course does not adjudicate. The
  alcohol retention figures are given so a reader can decide (lesson 8); every practice has a
  meat-free path (decision 9); the course says nothing about what anyone should or should not eat.

## What downstream courses will need from this one

**First Aid and CPR** (this term) can assume a reader knows the first response to a kitchen burn and
to a pan fire, and must teach the treatment of cuts, which this course deliberately does not.
**Home Repair and Maintenance** (this term) can assume the ventilation advice for gas hobs and that
a reader has used and calibrated a thermometer. **Emergency Preparedness** can assume the danger
zone, time plus temperature, both countries' fridge and freezer figures, freezing not killing germs,
and the CDC's one instruction on canning, and owns food storage in an outage. **Gardening and
Growing Food** can assume a reader can cook what they grow, and the FSIS rule on rinsing produce
under running water. **Relationships and Family** can assume a reader can plan and cook a week of
meals for a household, including for higher-risk members.
