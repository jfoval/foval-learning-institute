Here's a recipe card of the kind you'll find anywhere. "Heat the wok for 1 minute. Add the prawns
and stir-fry for 2 minutes. Add the broccoli and pepper and stir-fry for 3 minutes. Add the sauce
and cook for 1 minute more." It reads like a set of facts, but it's a report of what happened in
one kitchen, with one burner, one pan, broccoli cut to one size and prawns that started at one
temperature. Change any of those and the minutes stop being true, while the dish still works,
because the dish was never really in the minutes.

This lesson is about where the dish is. The first half takes recipes apart into what travels and
what doesn't, so that you can cook without the card and change it to suit what's in the fridge.
The second half plans a week of meals around a handful of techniques, and that's where lesson 4's
food-safety rules do their most practical work: food cooked once and eaten three times has to be
cooled, stored, frozen, thawed and reheated, and the US and the UK have different rules for most
of those steps.

## What a recipe is made of

Lesson 1 gave you the course's question: what is this rule protecting, and does it apply here?
This lesson asks it of recipes, which are rules in a row, and sorts what's in them into four
kinds.

**The method.** The order of the steps, and each step's reason. "Brown the meat in batches" is
lesson 6's dry surface. "Simmer, covered, until tender" is lesson 8's collagen. These travel,
because each rests on a mechanism, and the mechanism works the same in any kitchen.

**The ratios.** How much of one thing to how much of another: two parts egg to three of flour,
three of oil to one of vinegar. These travel too, and they scale. Double everything and a ratio
stays true, which is why bakers write bread as percentages (lesson 11).

**The tasting points.** The moments where a cook stops and decides: is it salted enough, does it
need acid? A good recipe marks them ("taste and adjust"); many leave them out.

**The numbers for one kitchen.** Minutes, "medium heat", "a large onion", "1 teaspoon salt". These
are the writer's measurements of their own food, and they don't travel. Lesson 2's thickness rule
is why for times, and lesson 3's salts, one nearly twice as salty as another by the spoon, are why
for salt. America's Test Kitchen (ATK) gave the general reason in lesson 9: "set-in-stone ratios
fail the home cook", because evaporation depends on "the size and shape of the cooking vessel, how
tightly the pot is covered, and the source and intensity of the heat."[20]

Professional schools start here too. The Culinary Institute of America's first course,
[Culinary Fundamentals](https://catalog.ciachef.edu/culinary-skills/culs100), lists tasting first
and promises "an introduction to the concepts of ratios and formulas".[1]

:::predict A recipe for a pot of dal says: "Simmer 1 cup of red lentils in 3 cups of water for 25 minutes, until soft. Add 1 teaspoon of salt." Before reading on, sort each instruction into method, ratio, tasting point, or a number for one kitchen.

"Simmer until soft" is method, with its own test built in. "1 cup of lentils to 3 of water" is a ratio, though only a rough one, because some of the water is there to replace what boils off, and lesson 9 showed that depends on your pot. "25 minutes" is a number for one kitchen: the writer's lentils, pot and hob. "1 teaspoon of salt" is one too, because it depends on the salt. The recipe has no tasting point at all, and it needs one, which is what lesson 3's dal was about: salt in stages, then taste.
:::

## A stir-fry, taken apart

Here's the recipe from the top of the lesson in full. It's written for this lesson, in the shape
most stir-fry recipes take, so that it can be taken apart; it isn't anyone's tested recipe, and
nothing here claims it is how the dish is made anywhere in particular.

> **Prawns with broccoli and pepper** (serves 2). 300 g raw peeled prawns. 1 head of broccoli, in
> florets. 1 red pepper, sliced. 2 cloves of garlic and a thumb of ginger, minced. 3 spring onions,
> sliced. For the sauce: 3 tablespoons soy sauce, 4 tablespoons water, 1 tablespoon rice wine, 1
> teaspoon sugar, 1½ teaspoons cornstarch. 1 teaspoon sesame oil. Heat the wok for 1 minute. Add
> oil and the prawns and stir-fry for 2 minutes. Add the broccoli and pepper and stir-fry for 3
> minutes. Add the garlic, ginger and sauce and cook for 1 minute. Stir in the sesame oil and spring
> onions. Serve with rice.

Read it with this course behind you and three problems show up.

The first is the pan. The recipe assumes a wok, and most home cooks have a frying pan. ATK's
technique for stir-frying without one warns that "Even a skillet set over high heat won’t get hot
enough to quickly stir-fry either large amounts or large pieces of food", and says: "If necessary,
cook the protein in batches to avoid overcrowding the pan, which will cause the protein to steam
rather than sear."[8] This course's reading is that 300 g of cold prawns in one go is the large
amount ATK means, and the likely result is lesson 6's puddle.

The second is the times. "2 minutes" for the prawns means nothing without their size and how cold
they were. What the prawns need is to be done, and the two countries describe it in almost the
same words: in the US, cook shellfish "until flesh is pearly or white, and opaque"; in the UK,
"prawns, scallops, crab, and lobster flesh should become firm and opaque".[9][10] The broccoli and
the thinner, softer pepper get the same 3 minutes, and lesson 1's rule says they can't both finish
on time unless they were cut to match. And "1 minute" for the sauce is shorter than ATK found
cornstarch needs: "a couple of minutes to achieve maximum thickness".[7]

The third is what's missing. There's no tasting point. Three tablespoons of soy sauce carry most
of the dish's salt, and nobody tastes the sauce until it's on the plate.

### The same recipe, as a method

Rewrite it as what it is: lesson 6's stir-fry method, a sauce built to a ratio, and two tasting
points.

1. **Everything ready before the heat goes on** (lesson 1): broccoli florets cut smaller than the
   soft pepper strips, so they finish together, and the sauce mixed cold with the cornstarch
   stirred in, which is the slurry ATK describes: "first mix it with cold liquid to form a slurry
   before adding the thickener to hot liquids to prevent clumping."[7]
2. **Taste the sauce now**, while you can still fix it: acid if it is flat and heavy, water if it's
   too salty (lesson 3).
3. **Dry the prawns and cook them in batches** until opaque and firm, and set them aside.
4. **Vegetables next, hardest first**, then the aromatics briefly. Test a piece; do not count
   minutes.
5. **Prawns back, sauce in** (give it a stir first), and toss until it bubbles
   and thickens, a couple of minutes. **Taste again**, and adjust.

Now the ratio. The sauce is 8 tablespoons of liquid, which is half a cup, with 1½ teaspoons of
cornstarch, which is half a tablespoon. That's the proportion lesson 10 took from Michael Ruhlman's
cornstarch-thickened sauce: about a tablespoon of cornstarch to a cup of liquid, by this course's
division of his recipe.[6] So what you carry away is not "3 tablespoons of soy sauce". It's
half a cup of seasoned liquid for two, a tablespoon of cornstarch per cup, tasted before it goes in.

:::predict You make it again with what's in the fridge: green beans, a handful of mushrooms and some pak choi instead of broccoli and pepper. Before reading on, how would you cut each, and in what order would they go in?

Green beans are dense, like broccoli, so they're cut short (a few centimetres) and go in first. Mushrooms go next, and lesson 6's crowding rule matters less for them: in Arnold's one test, crowded mushrooms still browned once their water had gone. Pak choi has thick stems and thin leaves, so this course's suggestion is to split it, stems in with the mushrooms and leaves at the very end, where they wilt in seconds. Nothing on the recipe card said any of this; the method did.
:::

Tofu, cubed and patted dry, goes where the prawns went for a meat-free version.

## Ratios: the part of a recipe that travels

Michael Ruhlman built a book on this idea, *Ratio: The Simple Codes Behind the Craft of Everyday
Cooking* (2009). This course has read the book's catalogued contents, its publisher's description
and Ruhlman's own blog posts, not the book itself. The description puts the claim plainly: "When
you know a culinary ratio, it’s not like knowing a single recipe, it’s instantly knowing a
thousand."[2] The catalogue says the book gives thirty-three ratios and has a section called "The
all-important scale": his ratios are by weight.[2]

Here are the ones this course has seen, and where.

| Pasta dough | 3 flour : 2 egg | Ruhlman's blog[3] |

*The last three are how the book's publisher describes it, which isn't the book, so treat them as
the book's figures at second hand. Ratios for stock and pie dough are also attributed to the book,
but this course hasn't found either on a page by Ruhlman or his publisher, so it does not print
them as his.*

### Anchoring a ratio to what you have

A ratio is most useful when one ingredient is fixed and you build the rest around it. Ruhlman's
[pasta post](https://ruhlman.com/pasta-agnolotti-and-the-power-of-ratios/) puts it in one line:
"once you have the pasta ratio, you can make pasta using one egg, two, or three, or more. Just
weigh them, 2 parts egg, 3 parts AP flour."[3] (AP is all-purpose flour, plain flour in the UK.)
In a reply to a reader who asked about shells, he added: "always weigh them out of the shell".[3]

So crack one egg into a bowl on your scale. Say it weighs 57 g. The flour is one and a half times
that, 57 × 1.5, which is about 86 g. That's the whole recipe, and it fits whatever egg you
happened to have, which a recipe that says "1 large egg, 100 g flour" cannot do. His post kneads
the dough until smooth, about 10 minutes, and rests it covered for 20 minutes to an hour; lesson
11 explained what that does to the gluten.[3]

The [pancake post](https://ruhlman.com/pancake-ratio/) makes the same point: ratios "scale to
what you have or need. Say you have only one egg."[4] His ratio is 2 parts liquid, 1 of egg, ½ of
butter and 2 of flour, and he calls the result "a fairly thick batter", to be thinned with an ounce
or two more milk if you like. So the ratio is a starting point, and he adds: "No matter how many
times I make pancakes, I always check the ratio."[4]

:::checkpoint You want pancakes and have exactly 150 g of flour left. Using the ratio, how much milk, beaten egg and butter do you need?

Flour is 2 parts, so one part is 75 g. Milk is 2 parts: 150 g, about 150 ml. Egg is 1 part: 75 g, so beat your eggs together and weigh out 75 g. Butter is half a part: about 38 g. The sugar, baking powder and salt aren't in the ratio, so scale them from his recipe: its 8 ounces of flour is about 227 g, and 150 g is about two-thirds of that. The grams are this course's arithmetic.
:::

where you start, and the tasting points are where you make it yours.

## What a recipe's rules are protecting

Turn the course's question on a recipe's instructions one at a time, and most fall into one of two
groups.

**Cooking rules** protect a result you can check yourself: a texture, a colour, a flavour. "Cook
the beef in two batches" protects a dry surface (lesson 6). "Add the tomatoes once the beans have
softened" protects beans from acid, which slows softening (lesson 9). If you know what one of these
protects, you can change it for your kitchen, and the dish will tell you whether you were right.

**Safety rules** protect against something you can't see, smell or taste, so the dish cannot tell
you whether you got them right. "Boil the soaked kidney beans for at least 30 minutes" protects you
from a toxin (lesson 9). "Cool the stew in shallow containers" protects against *Clostridium
perfringens* (lesson 10). These come from regulators, and they travel exactly as they are.

A recipe prints both kinds in the same voice, as though "simmer for 20 minutes" and "boil for 30
minutes" were the same sort of sentence. Telling them apart is most of what reading a recipe for
its method means.

The UK's Food Standards Agency (FSA) seems at first to say something different. Its cooking page
says: "When following a recipe take care to follow the instructions for cooking times and
temperatures carefully."[10] Its first top tip is to "always follow cooking temperature guidelines
according to the recipe or packet instructions carefully".[10] Taken at its word, that is advice to
trust the recipe's clock. The sentence sits under the page's table of safe temperatures and times,
though, and the same page tells you to check that food is cooked with a thermometer or by cues
such as steaming hot and no pink, not by the clock. This course's reading is that the FSA is
warning against cutting a time short, and that it and this lesson agree on the part that matters:
a recipe's time is where you start, and the check is what tells you the food is done.

:::checkpoint Sort these four instructions from a recipe for a bean and sausage casserole: "Brown the sausages in batches." "Soak the dried red kidney beans overnight, then boil them hard in fresh water for 30 minutes before they go in." "Bake for 1 hour 30 minutes." "Cook until the sausages reach 160°F (71°C)." Which can you change for your kitchen, and which not?

The batches are a cooking rule: they protect a brown surface, and a bigger pan might let you do it in one. The 1 hour 30 minutes is a number for the writer's oven and dish, so you'd check the sausages and the beans rather than the clock. The 30-minute boil is a safety rule from the FDA, and it stays. The 160°F is the US rule for minced meat, which sausages are made from (lesson 7), and it stays too; in the UK, the equivalent is sausages cooked all the way through, with no pink, steaming hot.
:::

## When to follow the recipe to the letter

A few recipes are the whole point of not adapting, and they share a feature: what goes wrong can't
be tasted, seen or smelled, and the only protection is a method someone has tested.

**Home canning.** The CDC's [page on home-canned foods](https://www.cdc.gov/botulism/prevention/home-canned-foods.html)
is the most direct instruction in this course: "Use only recipes and cookbooks that follow the
steps in the USDA guide." "Do not use other recipes, even if you got them from a trusted friend or
family member."[14] And it says why the usual checks are useless: "You cannot see, smell, or taste
the toxin that causes botulism."[14] This course doesn't teach canning; Emergency Preparedness
covers keeping food for the long term, and the USDA's guide is where to start.

**Flavoured oils kept at room temperature.** Lesson 3's garlic oil lives in the fridge, "for no more than 4 days".[15] The tested
method for one that keeps out of it, from the University of Georgia, warns: "Do not replace
the citric acid solution with vinegar or lemon juice, as they have not been tested and may result
in an unsafe final product."[16] There the ratio is itself the safety rule.

**A regulator's time or temperature written into a recipe.** The kidney bean boil is one; the
160°F for egg dishes in the US (lesson 5) is another. You can change everything around them, but
not them.

A stir-fry that's a little too salty tells you, and you add water next time. A home-canned jar of
green beans that went wrong tells you nothing.

## Planning a week from a few techniques

If you know five techniques well, you don't need a new recipe for every night. You need a plan
that uses the same few techniques in different combinations, with some things cooked once and
eaten more than once. That's batch cooking, and every batch is a leftover, which is where the two
countries' rules differ most.

Time Management's lesson on a week that survives put the fixed points in first and reserved slack
before anything else. This course applies that order to food: the evenings you cannot cook first,
then the batch cooking on the day you have time, then quick dinners that use what the batch made,
and one dinner left loose for the night the week goes wrong.

A batch item is anything cooked in quantity once and used across several meals: a braise (lesson
8), a pot of beans or rice (lesson 9), a stock (lesson 10). Each needs a storage plan, written
before you cook, with five lines:

1. **How it cools.** Into shallow containers no more than about 5 cm (2 inches) deep (lesson 10).
   In the US, into the fridge within 2 hours, hot if you like; in the UK, cooled first and in the
   fridge within one to two hours. Cooked rice in the UK is cooled faster, ideally within an hour
   (lesson 9).
2. **How long it keeps in the fridge.** 3 to 4 days in the US; 48 hours in the UK, and cooked rice
   no more than one day (lessons 4 and 9).
3. **What gets frozen, and when.**
4. **How it thaws.**
5. **How it's reheated.** In the US to 165°F (74°C), with sauces, soups and gravies brought to a
   rolling boil; in the UK until steaming hot, and only ever once (lesson 4).

The next section works a week through, and it is built to fail under one country's rules.

## A Sunday braise and a Monday pot of rice

Here's a week for a household of two, from five techniques: a braise, rice by absorption, a
stir-fry, a tray of roasted vegetables and a vinaigrette. On Sunday afternoon you braise about 1.5
kg of pork shoulder with onions and stock (lesson 8's method); for a meat-free version, soaked
chickpeas braised with the same vegetables, as lesson 8 did, and not kidney beans of any colour.
It makes about eight portions, four dinners for two. On Monday you cook a pot of rice.

| Day | Dinner | Batch items used |
|---|---|---|
| Sunday | The braise, with roasted carrots and onions | braise, cooked today |
| Monday | Braise over rice | braise; rice, cooked today |
| Tuesday | Fried rice with egg and greens | Monday's rice |
| Wednesday | Braise in flatbreads, with a salad and vinaigrette | braise |
| Thursday | Braise with rice | braise; Monday's rice |
| Friday | Prawn or tofu stir-fry, with fresh rice | none |

:::predict Before reading on, check the plan against your own country's rules. On which days, if any, is a batch item past its limit?

In the US, none. The braise is cooked on Sunday, so Thursday is its fourth day, the far end of FSIS's 3 to 4. The rice is cooked on Monday and eaten on Tuesday and Thursday, three days at most. In the UK, two dinners break. The braise's 48 hours run out on Tuesday evening, so Wednesday's and Thursday's portions are past it. Cooked rice keeps no more than one day in the fridge, so Tuesday's fried rice is right at the limit and Thursday's rice is two days past it. And if the braise sits in one pot that is reheated on Monday and again on Wednesday, the UK's reheat-once rule is broken as well.
:::

### Under the US rules

The plan works as written, provided the cooling does: shallow containers into the fridge within 2
hours, hot if need be, and each reheat to 165°F, with the braise's liquid brought to a rolling
boil, FSIS's rule for "sauces, soups and gravies".[13] Thursday is the braise's last day, so if
anything slips, it has run out of days. FSIS says to "Freeze leftovers within 3-4 days", and
freezing the Thursday portion on Sunday takes the pressure off the end of the week.[12]

### Under the UK rules, and the freezer that fixes it

The fix is to make the freezer do what the fridge can't. The FSA calls a freezer "a pause button",
and says that leftovers "should be frozen as soon as possible".[11] Here's the week, day by day.

- **Sunday.** As soon as dinner is served, divide the rest of the braise into three tubs of two
  portions, not one pot. The FSA says freezing in individual portions "will help you defrost only
  what you need", and each tub can then be reheated once.[11] Cool them, since the FSA wants warm
  dishes cooled before they go in the freezer, and within two hours of cooking put Monday's tub in
  the fridge and Wednesday's and Thursday's in the freezer, flat if you can.[11] Label each with
  what it is and the date.
- **Monday.** Monday's tub, reheated once until steaming hot: about 24 hours after cooking, well
  inside the 48. Cook the rice, and get the leftover rice cold within the hour, spread thin
  (lesson 9). Tuesday's share goes in the fridge. For Thursday, either plan a fresh pot or put a
  second share in the freezer, also within an hour of cooking.[17]
- **Tuesday.** Fried rice, heated until steaming hot all the way through. It's about 24 hours
  after the rice was cooked, which is the limit, so it cannot slip to Wednesday. It's also the
  rice's one reheat, so nothing of it is kept. After dinner, move Wednesday's braise from the
  freezer to the fridge.
- **Wednesday.** The braise has had about a day in the fridge. FSIS's thawing page, the only
  source read here with a thawing time, gives a pound of minced meat or boneless chicken breasts
  "a full day";[22] a flat portion should be quicker than a deep one (this course's reasoning,
  below). Before you reheat it, check that it's thawed right through to the middle, because the
  FSA says "Partially defrosted food may not cook evenly and this means that harmful bacteria
  could survive the cooking process."[11] If
  it is not, finish it in the microwave on the defrost setting and reheat it straight away. The FSA
  gives the microwave as its other defrosting route, "directly before cooking"; using it to finish
  a slow fridge thaw is this course's application of that.[11] Reheat once, until steaming hot.
  Because it went into the fridge only a day ago, it is inside the FSA's 24 hours after
  defrosting. After dinner, move Thursday's tub to the fridge.
- **Thursday.** The braise as Wednesday. The rice is a fresh pot, twenty minutes' work, or the
  frozen share, moved to the fridge on Wednesday evening and, in the FSA's words, reheated "until
  it is steaming hot before eating immediately".[17]

It's the same week, the same food and the same techniques. What changed is when things went into
the freezer, and that was decided on Sunday, before the first portion was eaten. That's why the
storage plan is written before you cook, and why the thawing is timed to the evening before: move
a tub too early and the 24-hour clock can run out before you eat it.

:::checkpoint A US reader runs the same plan, but cooks the braise on Saturday instead of Sunday. What changes?

Thursday becomes the braise's fifth day, past FSIS's 3 to 4, so the Thursday portion has to be frozen. FSIS says to freeze leftovers within 3 to 4 days, so any day up to Tuesday or Wednesday is inside that, though Saturday or Sunday is better for quality. It thaws in the fridge, or in cold water or the microwave and then gets cooked straight away, and it's reheated to 165°F. Wednesday's portion is on day four, still inside the rule but at the edge.
:::

## What the freezer does, and what it does not

Both countries agree on what makes a batch plan work. FSIS, on its [freezing
page](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/freezing-and-food-safety):
"Food stored constantly at 0 °F will always be safe. Only the quality suffers with lengthy freezer
storage."[12] The FSA, on its [page about chilling and
freezing](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/how-to-chill-freeze-and-defrost-food-safely/how-to-chill-freeze-and-defrost-food-safely):
"A freezer acts as a pause button - food in a freezer won’t go bad and most bacteria cannot grow
in it."[11] (0°F is about -18°C, the figure the FSA gives for a freezer.)

Pause is the right word, because freezing doesn't kill germs (lesson 4). Once thawed, FSIS says,
"you must handle thawed items as you would any perishable food."[12] The two countries then set
different clocks. FSIS's leftovers page says of frozen leftovers thawed in the fridge: "After
thawing, the food should be used within 3 to 4 days or can be refrozen."[13] The FSA says to use
food "within 24 hours after it’s been fully defrosted".[11]

### Freezer times are for quality

The storage times both regulators give are about how good the food will be, not whether it is
safe. FSIS: "Because freezing keeps food safe almost indefinitely, recommended storage times are
for quality only." Its chart gives soups and stews 2 to 3 months.[12] The FSA says "Meats are
usually best eaten within 2-3 months", and that freezer burn affects quality, "rather than the
safety".[11] FSIS's leftovers page gives frozen leftovers 3 to 4 months, a month more than its own
chart,[13] which tells you how precise these figures are meant to be.

### Freeze it flat and fast

FSIS says to freeze food as fast as possible, for quality: "Ideally, a food 2-inches thick should
freeze completely in about 2 hours." And: "Never stack packages to be frozen. Instead, spread them
out in one layer on various shelves, stacking them only after frozen solid."[12] That's lesson 2's
thickness rule again: a thin, flat package loses its heat far faster than a deep one, so the same
5 cm that cools a stew safely in the fridge freezes it quickly. By the same physics a flat bag
should thaw faster too, which helps with the UK's one-day clock; that last point is this course's
reasoning, not a figure from a source.

Some things do not freeze well. FSIS names "mayonnaise, cream sauce and lettuce", and gives the
reason for the first two on the same page: slow freezing makes large ice crystals, and "Emulsions
such as mayonnaise or cream will separate and appear curdled."[12] Those are lesson 5's emulsions.
So in a batch week, freeze the braise and the stock, and make the salad and its dressing on the
day.

### Refreezing

"Never refreeze anything that has thawed" is one of the most repeated kitchen rules, and the US
regulator does not say it. FSIS: "Once food is thawed in the refrigerator, it is safe to refreeze
it without cooking, although there may be a loss of quality due to the moisture lost through
thawing. After cooking raw foods which were previously frozen, it is safe to freeze the cooked
foods."[12] The limits are in the same passage: nothing left out of the fridge longer than 2
hours, or 1 hour above 90°F (32°C), goes back in, and food thawed in cold water or the microwave
is cooked before it's refrozen.[12][13]

The UK page read for this course says less. It allows the second case: "you can freeze food again
once cooked, but you’ll only be able to reheat it once after that".[11] It doesn't say whether raw
food thawed in the fridge can be refrozen raw, so this course gives no UK rule for that. If you're
in the UK, cooking it and then freezing it is the route the page does cover.

FSIS also allows reheating a casserole straight from frozen, which "will take approximately one and
a half times as long".[12] The FSA page gives no rule on that; its advice is to defrost fully
first, for the reason in Wednesday's step.[11]

## Cooking for other people

A batch cooked for a household, or taken to a friend, carries lesson 4's two extra hazards into
every portion.

**Allergens.** Heat does not remove an allergen, and scraping it off a plate isn't enough; the FSA
says "an extremely small amount can be enough to cause an allergic reaction."[18] So if anyone
eating the week has a food allergy, decide before you cook whether the whole batch leaves the
allergen out, or their portion is made first with clean tools and a clean pan. Ask them first,
since your guest "will have the best understanding of their specific allergy", and label each tub
with what went in as well as the date, which is the FSA's advice on "keeping a note of the
ingredients or the food packet itself used in the dish".[18] A stir-fry sauce made in bulk with
soy sauce and sesame oil puts two allergens from both countries' lists into every portion. Those
steps for a batch plan are this course's application of the FSA's advice.

**Someone at higher risk.** Lesson 4's callout applies to every batch, including its rule on runny
eggs if the fried rice gets a fried egg on top. Lesson 4 also showed that *Listeria* grows in the
fridge, and that heating a chilled food until steaming hot deals with it in both countries' advice.
This course's application of that to your own leftovers is to serve them to someone in these groups
reheated until steaming hot, not cold.

**The label, not your nose.** Every tub gets a date, and the date decides, not the smell. FSIS
says the germs that make people ill "do not generally affect the taste, smell, or appearance of a
food. In other words, one cannot tell that a pathogen is present."[19] The FSA's advice to "label
food you freeze so you don’t end up with Unidentified Frozen Objects (UFOs)" is a joke with a
serious end:[11] an unlabelled tub has no clock.

## What people get wrong

**"Good cooks don't measure."** The Culinary Institute of America teaches "ratios and formulas" in
its first course,[1] Ruhlman checks his pancake ratio every time,[4] and bread is written by weight
because salts differ spoon for spoon (lesson 11). What's true in the saying is lesson 3's point:
you can taste your way back in a soup, and you can't in a dough, so measure the dough.

**"Leftovers are good for a week."** Not under either country's rules: 3 to 4 days in the US, 48
hours in the UK, and one day for cooked rice in the UK.[13][10][17] The freezer is how a batch
lasts a week.

**"Never refreeze anything that has thawed."** FSIS says food thawed in the fridge can be refrozen
uncooked, with some loss of quality. The limit is food that has been left warm.[12] The FSA page
read here allows refreezing once the food is cooked.[11]

**"A recipe's cooking time is a fact."** It's the writer's measurement of their own food in their
own pan (lesson 2). Test the food. Safety times are the exception, because they're rules, not
estimates.
