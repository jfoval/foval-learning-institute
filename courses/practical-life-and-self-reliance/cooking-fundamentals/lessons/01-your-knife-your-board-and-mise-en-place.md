Cut a carrot badly and put it in a hot pan. Some pieces are thin coins and some are thick chunks.
Five minutes later the coins are brown at the edges and the chunks are still crunchy in the middle,
and there's no heat setting that fixes it. You can take the pan off and eat half-raw chunks, or
leave it on and eat scorched coins. The mistake was made on the board, before the pan was hot.

That is the first thing this course wants you to see. A knife is not only a way of making food
smaller. The size you cut sets how long a piece takes to cook, so the board is where cooking
starts. This lesson teaches how to hold a knife and guide it safely, the sizes cooks use and what
their names mean, why a sharp edge is easier to control and how to keep one, which board to use,
and how to set up your work before the heat goes on.

## What this course is, and what it assumes

Cooking Fundamentals teaches the handful of things most cooking is built from: knife work, how
heat moves into food, seasoning by taste, the food-safety rules that matter, and the main ways of
cooking with dry heat and with water, then eggs, grains and beans, stocks and sauces, and one loaf
of bread. It is organised by method rather than by recipe, because a method travels to the next
dish and a recipe does not.

**What you will need.** One chef's knife, a cutting board, a heavy frying pan, a pot with a lid, a
sheet pan (a flat, rimmed baking tray) and an instant-read thermometer. A kitchen scale helps in lessons 3, 11 and 12, and
lessons 7 and 11 need an oven. The thermometer may be the surprise on that list. The US Food Safety
and Inspection Service (FSIS) says only about 15% of people consistently use one,[13] and lesson 2
shows why a thermometer tells you things a recipe's time cannot. Every practice that uses meat also
has a meat-free version that exercises the same idea.

**What it leaves out.** It makes no claims about what's healthy to eat; Nutrition: The Evidence,
earlier in this term, covers that, and this course points back to it when salt or browned meat
comes up. It doesn't teach pastry or cakes, deep frying beyond its fire safety, butchery beyond a
chicken, home canning, special or religious diets, or managing food allergies. It does not treat
injuries either. If you cut yourself, First Aid and CPR, later in this term, is the course that
teaches what to do, and this lesson deliberately doesn't try. Nothing here says what anything costs.

This course is education, not advice for your own kitchen or your own health. Where the rules
differ between countries, and for food safety they often do, it gives the US and UK rules side by
side and tells you to follow your own country's regulator.

**The course's question.** Every kitchen rule is a mechanism squeezed into an instruction, and
rules fail when they are carried somewhere the mechanism does not reach. So this course asks one
question of every rule it meets: *what is this rule protecting, and does it apply here?* You'll
use it for the first time before this lesson is out.

## Holding the knife

Most beginners hold a chef's knife by the handle, like a hammer. It feels secure, and [America's
Test Kitchen's knife guide](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/1362-a-guide-to-using-your-chef-s-knife) (ATK from here on) grants that, but says the handle grip "doesn't offer the control over the tip
that is needed to make precision cuts."[1]

What ATK teaches instead is the **pinch grip**: "Pinch the blade where it meets the handle with your
thumb and forefinger. This grip, our go-to for most cutting tasks, allows you to choke up on the
blade to get leverage over the whole length of the knife."[1] Your other three fingers wrap the
handle. It feels odd for a day or two, because your thumb and finger are on the steel, but it puts
your hand closer to the edge's work, and that is where the control comes from.

Your other hand matters more for safety. ATK calls its position the claw: "Curve your fingers into
a claw on top of the food, making sure to tuck your thumb behind them and pressing down to hold the
food in place. This position allows your knuckles to help guide the side of the blade while your
fingertips stay out of harm's way."[1] The flat of the blade rests lightly against your knuckles,
your fingertips are curled back behind them, and after each cut the claw walks back a step. The
size of that step is the size of your slice. ATK notes that with practice the claw moves back in
even steps, which is what guides the knife to even slices.[1]

Most cuts are not a straight chop downward. ATK's guide describes a rocking motion for small items,
tip on the board, pushing down and forward, and for large items lifting the whole blade to start
each cut.[1] Either way the blade moves forward or back as it goes down, which is what lets it
slice rather than crush.

:::predict Your claw is in place and the knife is sliding down your knuckles. What part of your guiding hand should be closest to the blade, and what part should be furthest away?

The knuckles are closest, touching the flat of the blade. The fingertips are furthest, curled back behind the knuckles, and the thumb is tucked behind the fingers. The edge can only reach what sticks out in front of it, and in a claw that's a flat wall of knuckle rather than a fingertip. If your fingertips are ahead of your knuckles, you've gone back to holding the food like a pen.
:::

## A board that doesn't move

A knife slipping is one danger. The board slipping is another, and it's easier to fix. The
University of Kentucky's knife-skills handout says that if your board moves around, put a damp
towel or paper towel under it, or buy an anti-slip mat.[3] Its grip and claw advice repeats ATK's
almost word for word, so it isn't an independent second opinion, but it is the source this course
has for the towel.

The food has to be stable too. A round thing on a flat board rolls, and a rolling onion under a
moving blade is how people get cut. ATK's rule: "Create flat, stable surface by cutting food in
half or removing thin slice from bottom. Hold food with claw grip."[1] Every round vegetable starts
this way, and you will see it twice in the examples below.

## An onion, cut by cut

This is [ATK's written method](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/229-how-to-cut-an-onion), for taking a whole onion to a medium dice, with the reason for each cut.[2]

1. **Halve it through the root.** Cut from the top down through the root end, "pole to pole". Then
   peel it and trim the top. The skin comes off more easily once the onion is cut.[2] The halving
   also gives you the flat side.
2. **Lay it flat side down.** Now it can't roll.
3. **Leave the root on.** ATK says many chefs cut off both ends first, "However, for the average
   home cook, we find that leaving the root intact makes it easier to keep the layers together as
   you make each cut."[2] The root is the hinge that holds everything while you work.
4. **Make a few horizontal cuts** from the top end towards the root, keeping your guiding hand flat
   on top of the onion, and stop before the root.
5. **Make several vertical cuts**, again up to but not through the root.[2]
6. **Turn it so the root is at the back, and slice across** the earlier cuts, with your claw
   guiding the blade. The dice falls away as you go, and you stop at the root and throw it out.

The spacing of your cuts decides the size. For chopped or diced onion, ATK leaves a quarter to half
an inch between cuts, about 6 to 13 mm; for minced, an eighth to a quarter of an inch, about 3 to 6
mm.[2] (The millimetres here and throughout are this course's conversions.) ATK gives the reason
the size matters in one line: "The goal when chopping an onion is to obtain evenly sized pieces
that will cook at an even rate."[2]

## Why size is a cooking decision

ATK's general knife guide gives the aim of all cutting as avoiding crushed food and creating
"even-size pieces that cook at the same rate and/or ensure even flavor distribution throughout the
dish."[1] The first half of that is the one that changes what happens in the pan, and the reason
is how heat gets into food.

Heat arrives at the surface of a piece and has to travel inward, and the centre is the last part
to get hot. A thicker piece does not just have more distance to cover. Douglas Baldwin, writing
about cooking meat in a water bath, puts the relationship plainly: "a steak that is twice as thick
takes about four times longer to cook and cool!"[6] Lesson 2 explains why it works that way. For
now, the consequence is what matters. Carrying Baldwin's rule from meat to a carrot is this
course's inference from the same physics, not a measurement of carrots, but it predicts
what the pan of badly cut carrots showed: double the thickness and you do not get twice the time,
you get something nearer four times.

:::checkpoint You've diced a carrot for a soup base. Most pieces are about 6 mm, but a few came out at about 12 mm. Using the rule above as a rough guide, how does the time the big pieces need compare with the small ones, and what will you taste when the small ones are done?

Twice the thickness means roughly four times the time, not twice. So when the 6 mm pieces are soft, the 12 mm pieces have had only about a quarter of the time they need, and you'll bite into firm, raw-tasting carrot in the middle of an otherwise cooked dish. Treat the factor of four as a rough guide: it comes from meat in a water bath, and a carrot in a pan is not the same situation. The direction is what you can rely on.
:::

### A carrot, and the rule's first test

A carrot is the harder case, because it's round, it tapers, and it rolls. The first job isn't a cut
for size at all. It is making a flat side, by halving the carrot along its length or taking a thin
slice off one side, and laying it on that face.[1] Only then does the claw have something steady
to hold.

The taper causes the second problem. Chop a whole carrot into chunks straight across and a chunk
from the thick end can be three or four times as wide as one from the tip, so the same chopping
gives you big pieces and small ones. This course's fix is to cut the thick end in half or quarters
lengthways first, so the pieces you cut across it come out about the size of the ones from the thin
end. Ginger and sweet potato are the same
problem at different sizes: flat side first, then size the pieces to match.

Now ask the course's question of "cut evenly." What is it protecting? Pieces that go into the
heat together finishing together. Does it apply here? For a single vegetable in a single pan, yes,
and that is the carrot. But when a firm vegetable and a soft one go into the same pan at the same
time, the rule's purpose points somewhere its wording doesn't. Even *time*, not even *size*, is
what it protects, so the firmer vegetable gets cut smaller. That's this course's inference from
the mechanism, not a rule any source states, and your own pan is the test of it. The other
solution, of course, is to put the firmer vegetable in first.

## The names for sizes

Kitchens use a set of names for standard cuts. They come from French classical cooking, and
they're one tradition's vocabulary for sizes, which is how to treat them. Other cuisines have their
own shapes and their own words, and nothing in this course calls one tradition's cuts the proper
ones. What the names give you is a shared language for "how big", and a way to read a recipe
precisely.

Here are the sizes the Escoffier School of Culinary Arts gives, in its inches, with this course's
metric conversions.[4]

| Name | Size (Escoffier) | About |
|---|---|---|
| Brunoise | 1/8 in cubes | 3 mm cubes |
| Small dice | 1/4 in cubes | 6 mm cubes |
| Medium dice | 1/2 in cubes | 12 to 13 mm cubes |
| Large dice | 3/4 in to 1 in cubes | 19 to 25 mm cubes |
| Julienne | 1/8 × 1/8 × 2 1/2 in | 3 × 3 mm, about 6 cm long |
| Batonnet | 1/4 × 1/4 × 2 1/2 in | 6 × 6 mm, about 6 cm long |

Look at the pairs. A brunoise is a julienne cut across into cubes, and a small dice is a batonnet
cut across. That suggests the order of work: slice to a thickness, cut the slices into sticks,
and cut the sticks into cubes. The exact millimetre matters less than your pieces matching each
other.

Home recipes mostly use different words for the same idea. ATK's own recipe vocabulary runs:
"Minced" means 1/8-inch pieces or smaller, "Chopped fine" 1/8 to 1/4 inch, "Chopped medium" 1/4 to
1/2 inch and "Chopped coarse" 1/2 to 3/4 inch.[2] So when an ATK recipe says "chopped medium", it's
asking for roughly Escoffier's small to medium dice, about 6 to 13 mm. Other publishers may use
these words differently, and a recipe that does not define them leaves you to judge.

Which size does a dish want? Follow the same logic as the carrots. The size should match the time
the piece will spend in the heat. A long braise can take large pieces that would never cook through
in a quick sauté, and a sauce cooked for ten minutes needs its onion small enough to soften in ten
minutes.

:::predict A recipe for a quick tomato sauce, simmered for 15 minutes, says "1 onion, chopped fine." Another, a beef stew cooked for over two hours, says "2 onions, chopped coarse." Before reading on, why might both authors be right?

Because each size matches the time. In 15 minutes, an onion cut to 3 to 6 mm has time to soften; a coarse chunk would still be crunchy. In two hours, a fine chop would collapse into the stew, while 13 to 19 mm pieces soften and keep some shape. The size is an instruction about cooking time, written in the language of cutting.
:::

## Sharp, and staying sharp

The standard advice is that a sharp knife is safer than a dull one. OSHA, the US workplace safety
regulator, puts it in its [guidance for young restaurant workers](https://www.osha.gov/etools/young-workers-restaurant-safety/food-prep): "Keep knives sharpened and in good
condition; dull knives tend to slip and may cause injuries."[7] ATK gives the mechanism: "a dull
blade requires more force to do the job and has a higher chance of slipping and missing the
mark."[1] A dull edge skids on a tomato skin or an onion's outer layer instead of biting, you push
harder, and when it finally goes, it goes with more force behind it. ATK adds that the usual result
of a dull knife is crushed or bruised food, not a cut, which is a second reason to keep an edge.

How good is the evidence? Better than folklore, but not what the confident version implies. What
has been measured is force. In 2003, researchers at the Liberty Mutual Research Center for Safety
and Health measured 15 professional meat cutters in two plants. Sharpness affected their grip force, the turning effort at the wrist (the paper's "cutting
moments") and cutting time, "with sharper blades requiring statistically significantly lower peak and mean
cutting moments, and grip forces than dull knives".[9] Those cutters were already
gripping hard: their mean grip force was 28.3% of their maximum, and their peak 72.6%.[9] That is
the evidence for "a dull knife takes more force." The next step, from more force to more cuts, is
the mechanism OSHA and ATK both state. This course found no study that counted injuries with sharp
and dull knives. So treat "a sharp knife is safer" as professional consensus with a measured
mechanism behind it, not a counted result. That is still a good reason to keep your knife sharp.

What is counted is how many people get cut. Gary Smith's study of US emergency departments from
1990 to 2008 estimated an average of 434,259 knife injuries a year, about 1,190 a day.[8] Fingers
or thumbs were injured in 66% of cases.[8] Among the injuries where the type of knife was recorded,
kitchen knives accounted for 36%, behind pocket and utility knives at 47%, but the type was
recorded for only about 2.5 million of the 8.25 million injuries, so 36% is a share of that subset,
not of all knife injuries.[8] Those figures are old, and they are about all knife injuries, not
only cooking. What they tell you is that fingers and thumbs are where it happens, which is exactly
what the claw protects.

### Honing and sharpening are different jobs

The rod sold with most knife sets is usually called a sharpening steel. ATK calls that name a
misnomer,[1] and explains why. As you cut, "the force of repeated cutting bends microscopic pieces of
the blade to either side, making it harder to push the blade through the food, which in turn makes
it feel dull to the user."[10] The edge is still there; it has just been knocked out of line.

A steel fixes that. In ATK's words, honing "repositions (or “trues”) the edge of a blade that is
slightly out of alignment to restore sharpness. Sharpening trims and reshapes the blade by removing
metal that is blunted or too far out of alignment for honing to work."[1] So a steel straightens
and a sharpener or a stone grinds. ATK's companion page adds that honing takes about a minute
against five to sharpen, and "doesn’t remove much metal from the blade".[10] Note "much": the claim
is little, not none.

How to hone, from ATK's guide.[1] Hold the knife by the handle. Stand the steel upright with its
tip on the counter. Put the heel of the blade against the top of the steel, tip of the knife
pointing slightly up, at a steady angle of about 15 to 20 degrees depending on the knife. Sweep the
blade down and towards you so the whole edge touches the steel, then do the other side. Four or
five strokes a side is enough. The pressure should be light: ATK says, "Despite what many cooks
believe, proper honing requires very little pressure."[10] Doing it this way, with the steel
planted and the blade moving down and towards you, keeps the edge travelling away from both hands.

How do you know which one a knife needs? ATK's paper test.[1][10] Hold a sheet of copy paper by one
end and draw the knife through it from heel to tip. If it snags, hone it and try again. If it still
fails, it needs sharpening. ATK's rule of thumb is to hone every time you start to cook, and to
test weekly if you cook often.[1] If your knife came without a steel, the paper test still tells
you whether it needs sharpening, and ATK's advice when honing fails is to "run it through a
sharpener."[1] This course doesn't teach sharpening; follow the instructions that come with your
sharpener or stone.

## The rules about knives, and what each protects

OSHA's knife guidance is written for teenagers working in restaurants, but most of it transfers
straight to a home kitchen.[7] Here are the rules in its own words. OSHA states them without
reasons, so the reason after each one is this course's reading of what it protects.

- "Let a falling knife fall. Do not try to catch it." A falling knife can turn as it drops, and you cannot
  see which way the edge is facing. Your reflex is to grab, which is why the rule has to be
  learned. Step back instead.
- "Do not store knives and other sharp objects in sinks between periods of use." A knife under
  soapy water is invisible, and the next hand into the sink finds it by touch.
- "When interrupted, stop cutting and place the knife down on a secure surface." Your eyes go to
  whoever interrupted you, and the claw stops being a claw.
- "Carry knives with the cutting edge angled slightly away from your body, with the tip pointed
  down to your side." And don't hand a knife over: "Place a knife that you are handing to someone,
  down on a clean surface, and let the other person pick it up."

One OSHA rule needs the course's question. It says, "Cut in the direction away from the body. Keep
your fingers and thumbs out of the way of the cutting line."[7] This course reads that first
sentence as written for trimming and boning in a restaurant, where a blade drawn towards you can end in your
stomach. At a home board, the blade travels down into the board and forward or back along it, not towards
you, and the part that protects you is the second sentence: keep your guiding hand out of the line the blade will travel. That's what the
claw does. When you are paring something in your hand, though, the first sentence applies in full.

## Wood or plastic

Plenty of people believe plastic boards are more hygienic than wood, on the grounds that wood is
porous and bacteria hide in it. It was tested in 1994 at the University of Wisconsin-Madison by
Nese Ak, Dean Cliver and Charles Kaspar. They put *E. coli*, *Listeria* and *Salmonella* on boards
of nine hardwoods and four plastics, new and used.[11] Clean wood absorbed the bacteria within 3 to
10 minutes, and at low and moderate doses they "generally could not be recovered after entering the
wood". At a high dose some survived for 12 hours, but their numbers were "reduced by at least 98%,
and often more than 99.9%". On plastic, bacteria survived and multiplied overnight if the board
stayed moist. The authors concluded: "These results do not support the often-heard assertion that
Plastic cutting boards are more sanitary than wood".[11]

Their companion paper found new plastic easy to clean, but knife-scarred plastic hard to clean by
hand, especially with chicken fat on it.[11] Cliver's later review repeated that knife-scarred
plastic is "very difficult to clean and disinfect".[11] On the other side, studies of wooden boards
in Hong Kong wet markets, cleaned only by scraping, found harmful bacteria on them. That shows poor
cleaning fails on wood as it does on anything; those studies did not compare wood with plastic.[11]

So this is a contested question that leans one way, and the way it leans is "either." FSIS says
so: "Consumers may choose either wood or a nonporous surface cutting board such as plastic, marble,
glass, or pyroceramic. Nonporous surfaces are easier to clean than wood."[12] Wash either kind with
hot soapy water and dry it, and replace a plastic board when it's deeply scored. The step that
matters more than the material is the one FSIS adds: one board for produce and bread, and "a
separate one for raw meat, poultry, and seafood."[12] Lesson 4 is about that, including how to
manage with one board.

## Mise en place

Mise en place is French for, in the Escoffier School's translation, "everything in its place."[5]
The school gives the goal as having "everything ready to go when it comes time to cook, bake, or
assemble your final dish."[5] In practice it's four steps, in this order:

1. **Read the recipe all the way through first**, method as well as ingredients.[5]
2. **Get the tools out**: the board steadied, the knife honed, a pan, a spoon, a bowl for scraps.
3. **Get the ingredients out and measure them**, each into its own bowl or small pile.[5]
4. **Do the prep**: wash, peel, and cut everything to its size before any heat goes on.

Here's why this belongs in a lesson about knives. Once a pan is hot, things happen on the pan's
schedule rather than yours: whatever is in it keeps cooking while you look for the next
ingredient. If you're cutting while something cooks, you're cutting in a hurry,
with your eyes on the stove, which is the interrupted-knife problem OSHA's rule is about. Doing the
cutting first means it happens calmly, at a board that isn't moving, with your attention on your
hands.

The school also lists the ways mise en place goes wrong. Prepping too far ahead is one, because
"vegetables can lose their crispiness, herbs may wilt".[5] Another is uneven cutting, for the
reason this lesson has already given: "Uniformity is important when slicing and chopping
ingredients as it helps the food to cook evenly."[5] A third is skipping the full read-through and
finding a missing step halfway.

:::checkpoint You're making a stir-fry: rice already cooked, then garlic, ginger, a carrot, a pepper and some spring onions, with a sauce of soy sauce and a little sugar. The recipe says the whole thing cooks in six minutes. Which of these jobs has to be done before the pan goes on, and which could be left?

All of it has to be done first. Six minutes of cooking leaves no gap to mince garlic, julienne ginger, cut the carrot thinner than the pepper so they finish together, slice the spring onions, or mix the sauce. The only thing that can wait is washing up. A fast dish is where mise en place matters most, because the cooking gives you no time to catch up.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"A dull knife is safer, because it can't cut you as badly."** A dull knife does less damage per
stroke, but it needs more force, and more force is what makes a blade skid off the food and into
your hand. The force difference is measured; the injury difference is professional consensus built
on it.[1][7][9]

**"Catch a falling knife before it hits the floor."** It's the reflex, and it's the wrong one. Let
it fall and step back.[7]

**"Plastic boards are more hygienic than wood."** The lab work doesn't support it, knife-scarred
plastic is hard to clean, and FSIS says either will do. Keeping raw meat on its own board matters
more than the material.[11][12]

**"The classical cuts are for show."** Some are used for looks, but mostly they're a size
vocabulary, and size sets cooking time. A recipe that says "small dice" is telling you how long the
pieces will take.[1][4]

**"A steel sharpens a knife."** It realigns an edge that has bent over. When the edge itself has
worn away, only a sharpener or stone brings it back.[1][10]

## Practice

:::exercise Cut, measure and set up
About 45 minutes for the cutting, the test and the setup, not counting the cooking in step 4. You'll need a ruler with millimetres and a sheet of copy or printer paper.

1. **Dice one onion** by the six steps above, aiming for a medium dice. Then pick out ten pieces at random, measure each across its longest side with a ruler, and write down the smallest and the largest. That gap is your spread.

2. **Dice one carrot** to a small dice, about 6 mm, starting by making a flat side. Measure ten pieces the same way and write down the spread. Which vegetable came out more even, and why? (Look at which one fought the flat side.)

3. **Do the paper test** on your knife. Hone it if it snags and you have a steel, and test again. Write down which it needed: nothing, honing, or sharpening.

4. **Set up a mise en place** for any simple recipe you'd like to cook this week: read it through, steady your board with a damp towel, get out the tools, and measure and cut every ingredient into its own bowl before you turn anything on. Photograph the setup, then cook it.

5. **Keep the photo and the two spreads.** Lesson 2 asks you to cut pieces to two thicknesses and time them, and you'll want to know how even your cutting was when you started.
:::

## Connections

Time Management's third lesson measured how far people's predicted times fall short of their
actual ones on real tasks. Cooking is a good place to test your own ratio: time your mise en place
against your guess, and you'll probably find the cutting takes longer than you thought, which is
another reason to do it before the pan is hot. Habits and Self-Discipline taught that a behaviour
repeated in a stable context becomes automatic; the claw is meant to become one of those, which
is why it's worth practising slowly until your hand does it without being asked.

Logic and Argument's habit of asking what a claim rests on did the work in the section on sharp
knives. "A sharp knife is safer" turned out to rest on a measured force difference and a mechanism,
not on counted injuries, which is enough to act on and worth knowing.

Lesson 2 explains why size sets cooking time: heat enters from the surface, and thickness changes
how long it takes to reach the centre. You'll measure it yourself with two slices of potato. The
course's question comes back there with a name, and in every lesson after.
