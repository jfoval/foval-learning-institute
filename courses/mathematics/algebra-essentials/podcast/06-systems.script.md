S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: Lesson five dodged something. The theatre problem had two unknown numbers in it, standard tickets and concessions, and the way round it was to call the concessions the letter c, and write the standard tickets as three lots of the letter c, so only one letter ever appeared.

S2: And that works when?

S1: When one quantity is described directly in terms of the other. Often it isn't. You know two facts about two quantities, and neither fact hands you one in terms of the other. Then you need both facts written down as equations, and a way of working with the pair.

S2: So what is a pair of equations claiming?

S1: Take these two. X plus y equals ninety. And twelve x plus seven y equals eight hundred and eighty.

S2: And each one on its own?

S1: Has an enormous number of solutions. The first is satisfied by x equals one and y equals eighty nine. And by x equals twenty and y equals seventy. And by x equals ninety and y equals nought. And by infinitely many other pairs.

S2: So what's the question?

S1: Asking for a solution of the pair, which is what people mean by a system, is asking for the one pair of numbers that satisfies both. That's the whole idea, and everything else in the lesson is machinery for finding it.

S2: Is there a picture?

S1: There is, and the picture makes it obvious. Each equation is a straight line, in the sense of lesson four: the line is made of all the pairs that satisfy it. Two lines that aren't parallel cross at exactly one point, and that point is on both lines, so its two coordinates satisfy both equations. Draw them carefully and you can read that point off, which is fine for a rough answer. The algebra is for when you want it exactly.

S2: Method one.

S1: Substitution, which is the method from lesson five made general. Get one letter alone in one equation, then put what it equals into the other, which leaves an equation in a single unknown. And that's lesson three.

S2: Give me the situation.

S1: A theatre sells adult tickets at twelve dollars and child tickets at seven. One evening it sells ninety tickets and takes eight hundred and eighty dollars. How many of each?

S2: Name the letters first.

S1: As numbers, which is lesson five's first step. Let x be the number of adult tickets, and y the number of child tickets. Then the two facts. Ninety tickets in all: x plus y equals ninety. And eight hundred and eighty dollars in all: twelve x plus seven y equals eight hundred and eighty.

S2: Get one letter alone.

S1: The first equation is the easy one. Take x off both sides, and y equals ninety minus x.

S2: And substitute.

S1: Everywhere the second equation says y, write ninety minus x instead. So it becomes twelve x, plus seven times bracket ninety minus x close bracket, equals eight hundred and eighty.

S2: And now it's lesson three.

S1: Expand the bracket. Seven times ninety is six hundred and thirty, and seven times minus x is minus seven x. So twelve x plus six hundred and thirty minus seven x equals eight hundred and eighty.

S2: Collect the x terms.

S1: Twelve x minus seven x is five x. So five x plus six hundred and thirty equals eight hundred and eighty. Take six hundred and thirty off both sides: five x equals two hundred and fifty. Divide by five: x equals fifty.

S2: And the other letter.

S1: Put x equals fifty back into whichever equation is simpler. That gives y equals ninety minus fifty, so y is forty.

S2: And now the step people skip. Why is it the one that gets skipped?

S1: Because after all that work the answer feels earned, and it isn't earned until it survives both equations. So check in both, not one. The first: fifty plus forty is ninety. The second: twelve times fifty is six hundred, seven times forty is two hundred and eighty, and six hundred plus two hundred and eighty is eight hundred and eighty. Both hold, so it's fifty adult tickets and forty child tickets.

S2: Try me. Take the pair, y equals two x minus one, together with three x plus y equals fourteen.

S1: And the first equation has y alone already, so the substitution is done for you. Three x, plus bracket two x minus one close bracket, equals fourteen.

S2: Collect the x terms. Five x minus one equals fourteen. Add one to both sides. Five x equals fifteen. Divide by five, and x equals three.

S1: And the other letter is two times three, which is six, minus one. So it's five.

S2: Check it in both.

S1: The first: two times three is six, and six minus one is five, so five equals five. The second: three times three is nine, and nine plus five is fourteen.

S2: And there's a second method.

S1: Elimination. Sometimes neither equation gives a letter up easily, and substitution drags fractions in. Elimination goes at it from the other side: combine the two equations so that one letter disappears.

S2: Show me one where it works with no preparation.

S1: Four x plus y equals fourteen, together with two x minus y equals four. Look at the plus y in the first and the minus y in the second, then add the two equations, left side to left side and right side to right side.

S2: The left gives four x plus y, plus two x minus y. The y and the minus y cancel, so that's six x. The right gives fourteen plus four, which is eighteen.

S1: So six x equals eighteen, and x equals three. One letter has gone, which is the whole trick. Then put three back into the first equation. Four times three, plus y, equals fourteen. So twelve plus y equals fourteen, and y equals two.

S2: And check in both.

S1: The first: twelve plus two is fourteen. The second: two times three is six, and six minus two is four. Both hold.

S2: And adding two equations is allowed because?

S1: Because of the balance from lesson two. The second equation says that two x minus y, and four, are two names for the same number. So adding that number to the left side of the first equation, and adding four to its right side, is doing the same thing to both sides. Elimination isn't a new rule. It's the old rule with a cleverer choice of what to add.

S2: And if the letters don't cancel as they stand?

S1: Then you multiply one equation, or both, by whatever it takes to make them cancel. The lesson's coffee and tea pair needs that: nothing cancels as it stands, so you double the whole of the first equation once, which makes its coffee term match the other's, and then subtract. Multiplying an equation through by a number is legal for the usual reason: it does the same thing to both sides. The whole of both sides, which is where people slip.

S2: So which method do I use?

S1: Substitution when one equation already has a letter alone, or nearly. Elimination when neither does and the coefficients will cancel with a little work. And neither is more correct than the other.

S2: And there's a case we haven't touched at all. What happens when both letters vanish.

S1: Then read what's left. If you're left with a false statement, nought equals seven, the lines are parallel and there's no answer. If you're left with a true one, nought equals nought, the two equations are the same line and every point on it works. Neither is a mistake, and the second one isn't an error message. It means the two facts you were given were the same fact.
