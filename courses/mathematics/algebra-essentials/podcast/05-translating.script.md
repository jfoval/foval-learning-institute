S2: And that step is the problem.

S1: That step is where the most reliable error in the whole subject lives, and the lesson is about it. So here's the problem, put to engineering freshmen at an American university by John Clement and his colleagues.

S2: Go on.

S1: At a university there are six times as many students as professors. Write an equation using S for the number of students and P for the number of professors. And if you're listening, say your answer out loud before either of us does. The lesson stops the reader dead at this point and tells them to write it down, because the whole thing turns on what your own hand does.

S2: Give me a second, then.

S1: Take it.

S2: Six times S equals P.

S1: And that's the common wrong answer, and you're in large company. In Clement's study thirty seven percent of the engineering students got this wrong, and of the wrong answers, sixty eight percent were exactly that reversal.

S2: So what's right?

S1: S equals six times P. And being wrong there isn't a sign of anything about you. It's a sign of a habit that a great deal of schooling installs.

S2: Show me the habit.

S1: Read the sentence and watch the order in which the pieces arrive. Six times as many students as professors. Six, then students, then professors. Write those down in that order and you get six times S equals P.

S2: So the equation is a transcription of the word order.

S1: That's the whole mechanism, and it's called word order matching. And it isn't stupidity. It's a reasonable strategy that happens to be wrong, because the order of words in a sentence and the structure of an equation are different things that look alike.

S2: Is there more than one thing going on?

S1: There's a second mechanism behind the same wrong answer, and it's worth knowing because people defend the answer with it. It's called static comparison. You picture the room: six students standing next to each professor. The six goes with the students because there are six of them in the picture.

S2: And what's wrong with that?

S1: An equation isn't a caption on a picture. It's a claim that one number is equal to another number, and it's either true or false when you put the numbers in.

S2: So how do I settle it?

S1: With the one habit to keep if you forget everything else in this lesson. Put numbers in. Pick a small, easy number for one quantity, work out the other from the sentence in plain English, and then see whether your equation holds.

S2: Do it.

S1: Suppose there are six professors. The sentence says six times as many students, so there are thirty six students. Now test both candidates. Take six times S equals P, with S thirty six and P six. Six times thirty six is two hundred and sixteen, and the right side is six. Two hundred and sixteen equals six.

S2: False.

S1: Now S equals six times P. Thirty six equals six times six. Thirty six equals thirty six.

S2: True. And that settles it without any argument about what the words feel like.

S1: Which is the point. The correct equation is S equals six times P.

S2: Is there a way to see why?

S1: The lesson draws it as two bars. The bar for the bigger group is the longer one, and the six has to sit on the side of the smaller number, to lift it up to the size of the larger.

S2: So the multiplier goes with the group there are fewer of.

S1: Which feels backwards until you've said it out loud a few times.

S2: And the lesson says there's a habit that prevents the trouble before it starts.

S1: And it costs one line of writing. Write down what each letter stands for, as a number, in words.

S2: Not, S is students.

S1: That's a label on a thing, which is lesson one's misconception coming back for a second try. Write, S is the number of students. P is the number of professors.

S2: Does that actually help?

S1: It does. Once the letters are numbers, the question, does the six go with the students, stops being askable at all. Six times a number of students is a number of students, and not a number of professors. And the question, is two hundred and sixteen equal to six, is obviously answerable.

S2: Give me another one to try.

S1: A car park holds five times as many cars as buses. The naming's done for you. Let the letter c be the number of cars, and the letter b the number of buses. Write the equation, and test it with four buses.

S2: The number of cars is the number of buses multiplied by five, so I'll write c equals five times b. Four buses means twenty cars. Does twenty equal five times four?

S1: Twenty equals twenty. True. And test the reversal while you're there. Five times c equals b would be five times twenty, which is a hundred, equals four.

S2: A hundred equals four. False.

S1: And notice what the check did. You didn't have to remember which side the multiplier goes on, or trust a rule, or argue about what the words feel like. You picked a number, worked out the other from the English, and let arithmetic decide.

S2: So the check is the method.

S1: The check is the method, and the rule is what you notice afterwards.

S2: Does this happen with other shapes of sentence?

S1: It happens with more than and fewer than, and there it catches people who'd never fall for the students and the professors. Maya is seven years younger than Ben. Write that, with M for Maya's age and B for Ben's.

S2: The word order offers M minus seven equals B.

S1: So test it. If Ben is thirty, Maya is twenty three. Twenty three minus seven is sixteen, and the right side is thirty. Sixteen equals thirty, which is false.

S2: And the right one?

S1: M equals B minus seven. Twenty three equals thirty minus seven. That holds.

S2: What's the English doing there?

S1: Something sly. Maya is seven years younger mentions Maya and the seven close together, so the hand wants them close together in the equation. But the seven is the size of the gap, and a gap comes off the larger number, not off Maya's.
