S1: At a university there are six times as many students as professors. Write an equation using S for the number of students and P for the number of professors.

S2: Six S equals P.

S1: And that's the common wrong answer, and you're in large company. In Clement's study thirty seven percent of the engineering students got this wrong, and of the wrong answers, sixty eight percent were exactly that reversal.

S2: So what's right?

S1: S equals six P. And being wrong there isn't a sign of anything about you. It's a sign of a habit that a great deal of schooling installs.

S2: Show me the habit.

S1: Read the sentence and watch the order in which the pieces arrive. Six times as many students as professors. Six, then students, then professors. Write those down in that order and you get six S equals P.

S2: So the equation is a transcription of the word order.

S1: That's the whole mechanism, and it's called word order matching. And it isn't stupidity. It's a reasonable strategy that happens to be wrong, because the order of words in a sentence and the structure of an equation are different things that look alike.

S2: Is there more than one thing going on?

S1: There's a second mechanism behind the same wrong answer, and it's worth knowing because people defend the answer with it. It's called static comparison. You picture the room: six students standing next to each professor. The six goes with the students because there are six of them in the picture.

S2: And what's wrong with that?

S1: An equation isn't a caption on a picture. It's a claim that one number is equal to another number, and it's either true or false when you put the numbers in.

S2: So how do I settle it?

S1: With the habit from lesson one, and if you forget everything else in this lesson, keep this one. Put numbers in. Pick a small, easy number for one quantity, work out the other from the sentence in plain English, and then see whether your equation holds.

S2: Do it.

S1: Suppose there are six professors. The sentence says six times as many students, so there are thirty six students. Now test both candidates. Take six S equals P, with S thirty six and P six. Six times thirty six is two hundred and sixteen, and the right side is six. Two hundred and sixteen equals six.

S2: False.

S1: Now S equals six P. Thirty six equals six times six. Thirty six equals thirty six.

S2: True. And that settles it without any argument about what the words feel like.

S1: Which is the point. The correct equation is S equals six P.

S2: Is there a way to see why?

S1: The lesson draws it as two bars. The bar for the bigger group is the longer one, and the six has to sit on the side of the smaller number, to lift it up to the size of the larger.

S2: So the multiplier goes with the group there are fewer of.

S1: Which feels backwards until you've said it out loud a few times.

S2: You said there's a habit that prevents the trouble before it starts.
