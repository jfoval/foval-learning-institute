S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Algebra Essentials, lesson two. Equals means the same size.

S1: And the lesson opens with a question, so let me put it to you the way it puts it to the reader. Eight plus four, equals, box, plus five. What number goes in the box? And if you're listening, stop and try it before either of us says anything.

S2: My hand wants to write twelve.

S1: Hold that. Researchers put that exact question to school students from first grade to sixth. What share do you think got it right, and did the older children do better?

S2: I'd guess the sixth graders beat the first graders.

S1: Fewer than ten percent got it right in any grade, and the older children did no better than the younger ones. The natural guess is the one the study overturned.

S2: So it isn't about ability.

S1: Being older didn't help, which tells you this isn't a hard sum. Getting it wrong is about what people think the equals sign means. And two wrong answers dominate. Twelve, and seventeen.

S2: Where do those come from?

S1: The same place. If you've spent years doing arithmetic, you've seen the equals sign thousands of times in one particular shape. Eight plus four equals what. Six times seven equals what. Twenty minus three equals what. In every one of those, the sign sits just before the answer.

S2: So it starts to feel like an instruction.

S1: Meaning, and the answer is. Read the question that way and the box is obviously where the answer goes, so it's twelve. Read the whole line as one long sum and you get seventeen.

S2: And the right reading?

S1: The sign means the two sides are the same size. That's all it has ever meant. It isn't a button that says compute. It's a statement that what's on the left and what's on the right are the same amount.

S2: So say the question that way.

S1: Eight plus four is the same size as something plus five. Twelve is the same size as something plus five. So the something is seven.

S2: And once I read it like that?

S1: Some things that used to look wrong stop looking wrong. Seven equals three plus four is a perfectly good equation. Seven is the same size as three plus four.

S2: That does look odd written down.

S1: And if it felt slightly uncomfortable, that discomfort is the old reading of the sign still operating, and noticing it is worth more than any rule you could memorise. Twelve equals twelve is an equation too, and a true one. Five plus five equals three plus seven is an equation, and true. Five plus five equals three plus six is an equation, and false.

S2: Equations can be false.

S1: And instructions cannot. Which is another way of seeing that the sign isn't an instruction.

S2: Try me. Nine plus six, equals, fifteen plus zero. True or false?

S1: True. Fifteen is the same size as fifteen.

S2: And this, written all on one line. Nine plus six, equals, fifteen, equals, twenty plus five.

S1: Not a sensible thing to write, even though every individual piece looks familiar. It claims fifteen is the same size as twenty five, because if nine plus six is fifteen and fifteen is twenty plus five, then fifteen equals twenty five.

S2: But that's how people write out their working.

S1: A great many people do, using the sign to mean, and then I did this next. Each line of working needs its own equation.

S2: All right. There's a picture that carries the rest of the course.

S1: A pair of scales. An equation is a balance that's level. Whatever is on the left pan weighs the same as whatever is on the right pan. That's what the sign is claiming.

S2: And what can I do to it?

S1: Take the same amount off both pans and it stays level. Add the same to both and it stays level. That's the whole reason the moves in lesson three are allowed, and it's why doing something to one side only isn't a move at all.

S2: So the rule everyone gets taught.

S1: Whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other. And not because a teacher said so. Because the equation is a claim that the two sides are the same size, and if you change one side and not the other, the claim you started with isn't the claim you have any more. The balance tips.

S2: Do the first question with the scales.

S1: Eight plus four, equals, box, plus five. Take five off both pans and you get seven equals box. Which is the answer. And that's the whole method of lesson three, arrived at by looking at a picture.

S2: How do I check an equation with a letter in it?

S1: An equation with a letter in it is a claim that may be true for some numbers and false for others, and you can always check by substituting. Take three times n, plus one, equals ten.

S2: Is it true when n is two?

S1: Substitute. Three times two is six, plus one is seven, and seven isn't ten. So no. Is it true when n is three?

S2: Three times three is nine, plus one is ten. Yes.

S1: And that's what solving will mean in lesson three. Finding the values that make the claim true. And notice you already have a way to check any answer you ever produce, which is to put it back in. Checking isn't an optional extra step in algebra. It's the thing that makes the rest of it safe.

S2: What about n equals four?

S1: Three times four is twelve, plus one is thirteen, not ten. And no other number works, because if three times something plus one is ten, then three times that something is nine, and only one number times three is nine.

S2: Is that usual?

S1: Most equations you'll meet in this course are like that, true for exactly one number, though lesson six meets some that aren't.

S2: So nineteen minus nine. The box is ten.

S1: That people are much more likely to read the sign as a claim when they meet it with operations on both sides, and much more likely to read it as an instruction when they only ever meet it in the shape, sum equals answer.
