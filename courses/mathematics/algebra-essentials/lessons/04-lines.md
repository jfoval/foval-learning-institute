quiz:
  - q: >-
      A window cleaner charges a fixed call-out fee plus an amount per window. For eight windows
      the bill is $46, and for twelve windows it is $62. What is the rate per window, and what
      is the call-out fee?
    options:
      - >-
          Five dollars a window and a six dollar fee, since five eights and six is forty six
      - >-
          Four dollars a window and a six dollar fee, since sixteen over four is four
      - >-
          Sixteen dollars a window and no fee at all, from the change between the bills
      - >-
          Four dollars a window and a fourteen dollar fee, from the rise over the run
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Two points on a line give the rate: the bill rose sixteen dollars while the windows rose
      by four, so the rate is four dollars a window. Then work back to the fee: eight windows at
      four dollars is thirty two, and the bill was forty six, so the fee is fourteen. Check the
      other point: twelve windows at four is forty eight, plus fourteen is sixty two. Both fit.
      Option B gets the rate right and then forgets to work the fee back from a real point.
      Option A fits the first bill exactly, since five eights plus six is forty six, and fails the second, since five twelves plus six is sixty six rather than sixty two. One point never settles a line. Option C reports
      the change in the bill as though it were the rate per window.
  - q: >-
      Two lines are drawn on the same axes. One has a slope of 3 and the other a slope of 30.
      What can you say for certain?
    options:
      - >-
          The second is ten times steeper, so it will always be above the first line
      - >-
          Nothing at all, since slopes cannot be compared between two different lines
      - >-
          The second climbs ten times as fast, and neither number means much alone
      - >-
          The second is ten times steeper and must therefore have a larger intercept
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Slope is a rate, so a slope of thirty climbs ten times as fast as a slope of three, and
      that comparison is fair here because the question says the two lines are on the same axes
      with the same units. What neither number means on its own is anything at all: three could
      be dollars per hour, miles per gallon or degrees per minute, and the number is meaningless
      until the units are named. Option A adds a claim about position that slope cannot support,
      since a steeper line starting far lower can sit below the other for a long stretch. Option
      D invents a link between rate and starting value; they are independent. Option B
      overcorrects: on shared axes the comparison is exactly what slope is for.
  - q: >-
      A tank is filled from a hose and the water level is plotted against time. The line is
      straight. What does that tell you, and what would a curve have meant?
    options:
      - >-
          The tank is full, and a curve would mean the hose had been turned off
      - >-
          The tank is cylindrical, and a curve would mean it was wider at the top
      - >-
          The level is rising, and a curve would mean the level was falling instead
      - >-
          The hose runs at a steady rate, and a curve would mean the rate changed
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Straight means the rate is the same everywhere on the line, so the level rises by the same
      amount in every equal stretch of time. A curve would mean the rate changes as you go,
      either speeding up or slowing down. Option B is the interesting distractor and it is half
      right in the real world: the shape of the tank does affect how fast the level rises for a
      given flow, so a tank that widens at the top would give a curve even with a steady hose.
      But the question asks what the straight line tells you, and the answer is about the rate
      rather than the vessel. Option C confuses a curve with a downward slope, which is a
      different thing: a falling line is still straight.
  - q: >-
      A plan is `y = 25 + 0.06m`. Someone needs the bill to be $40. How do you find the minutes,
      and what is the answer?
    options:
      - >-
          Divide forty by 25.06, which gives about one and a half minutes of calls
      - >-
          Multiply forty by 0.06 and add twenty five, giving twenty seven point four
      - >-
          Take twenty five off, then divide by 0.06, giving two hundred and fifty
      - >-
          Take twenty five off, then multiply by 0.06, giving ninety cents of calls
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Solving `25 + 0.06m = 40` is lesson three's method: take twenty five off both sides to get
      `0.06m = 15`, then divide both sides by 0.06 to get `m = 250`. Check it: `25 + 0.06 × 250`
      is `25 + 15`, which is forty. Option D undoes the multiplication in the wrong direction,
      and the units give it away, since a number of minutes should not come out in money. Option
      A invents an operation out of the two numbers in the equation. Option B works forwards
      rather than backwards and answers a question nobody asked.
  - q: >-
      Printer A costs $180 plus 4 cents a page. Printer B costs $40 plus 8 cents a page. What
      does the crossing point of their two lines mean, and where is it?
    options:
      - >-
          At three thousand five hundred pages printer A becomes cheaper to buy outright
      - >-
          At three thousand five hundred pages both cost three hundred and twenty dollars
      - >-
          At one hundred and forty pages the difference in purchase price has been paid off
      - >-
          At three thousand five hundred dollars the two printers have printed the same number
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Set the two costs equal and solve: `180 + 0.04p = 40 + 0.08p` gives `140 = 0.04p`, so `p =
      3,500`. At that many pages both cost three hundred and twenty dollars, which is the check.
      The crossing point is where the answer to which is cheaper changes: below it the cheap
      printer wins on total cost, above it the expensive one does. Option A states half of that
      and states it as though the purchase price changed, which it does not. Option C uses the
      difference in purchase prices as though it were a page count. Option D swaps the two axes,
      putting dollars where pages belong.
  - q: >-
      A line is drawn through data on children's heights against age, and the equation comes out
      as `height = 50 + 6a` in centimetres. What should you be careful about with the fifty?
    options:
      - >-
          It is the height at age zero, which may or may not be a sensible real quantity
      - >-
          Nothing, since the intercept is always the value of the thing being measured
      - >-
          It is the slope rather than the intercept, since it is the larger of the numbers
      - >-
          It should be ignored, since only the rate matters when a line is fitted to data
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The intercept is the value where the line meets the vertical axis, which here is the
      height at age zero, and whether that is meaningful depends entirely on the situation.
      Fifty centimetres is a plausible newborn length, so in this case it happens to mean
      something. In many fitted lines the value at zero is impossible or absurd, and the honest
      habit is to ask rather than to assume. Option B states the assumption the question is
      warning against. Option D throws out a number that is often meaningful and is always part
      of the equation. Option C misidentifies which number does which job, and the size of a
      number never tells you that.

## Three ways of saying the same thing

Take the taxi from lesson 1's practice: $3.50 to get in, $1.20 a mile. That relationship can be written three ways.

**As a sentence.** The fare starts at three dollars fifty and grows by a dollar twenty for every mile.

**As a table.**

| Miles (`d`) | Fare |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | $3.50 |
| 2 | $5.90 |
| 5 | $9.50 |
| 10 | $15.50 |

**As an equation.** `fare = 3.50 + 1.20d`

These are not three topics. They are one relationship in three costumes, and being able to move between them is most of what this lesson is for. The table came from the equation by substituting, which is lesson 1. The graph below comes from the table by plotting.

## What the two numbers are

Look at the equation and the picture together, because each number in one is a feature of the other.

**The 3.50 is where the line starts.** It is the fare at zero miles, which is the flag charge you pay for getting in. On the graph it is the height of the line where it meets the vertical axis. Its usual name is the **intercept**.

**The 1.20 is the rate.** It is how much the fare goes up for each extra mile, and on the graph it is how steeply the line climbs. Its usual name is the **slope** or **gradient**.

That is the whole of it. Any relationship of the form `y = starting value + rate × x` is a straight line, and any straight line can be written that way. The general form you will meet everywhere is:

`y = mx + c`

where `m` is the rate and `c` is the starting value. The letters are a convention, not a law; some countries write `y = mx + b`. What matters is which number does which job.

:::checkpoint The phone plan from lesson 1 was `12 + 0.05m`. What is its starting value, what is its rate, and what does each one mean in the situation?
The starting value is 12 and it is the monthly charge you pay before making a single call, so it is the height of the line at zero minutes. The rate is 0.05, which is five cents per minute, and it is how steeply the cost climbs as you talk. Notice that the rate is small, so the line is nearly flat, which is a true fact about the plan: minutes are cheap and the fixed charge dominates until you have talked for a long time. At two hundred and forty minutes the calls have cost twelve dollars, and only then does the variable part match the fixed part.
:::

## Reading a slope in the situation's own units

The slope of a line is a number, and on its own it means very little. What makes it useful is that it always has units, and the units come from the situation.

For the taxi, the slope is 1.20 **dollars per mile**. For the phone plan it is 0.05 **dollars per minute**. If you plotted the distance a car travels against time, the slope would be **miles per hour**, which is a thing you already know how to think about: speed is a slope.

To find a slope from any two points on a line, take the change in the up-and-down direction and divide by the change in the across direction. From the two marked points on the graph, at two miles the fare is $5.90 and at five miles it is $9.50:

change in fare divided by change in miles = `(9.50 - 5.90) / (5 - 2)` = `3.60 / 3` = **1.20 dollars per mile**

Which is the number that was in the equation all along. Any two points on the line give the same answer, and that is what makes the line straight: **the rate is the same everywhere on it.** A relationship where the rate changes as you go is not a straight line, and this course does not cover those.

:::checkpoint A savings account starts with $20 in it and $5 is added every week, with no interest. Write the equation, and say what the graph of it looks like at week zero and how steeply it climbs.
`total = 20 + 5w`, where `w` is the number of weeks. At week zero the line is at a height of 20, since that is the money already there. It climbs by 5 dollars per week, so it is five times steeper than a line rising a dollar a week. Notice how little of this is about algebra: the equation is a way of writing down something you could have said in a sentence, and the graph is a way of seeing it.
:::

## Building a line from a situation

**The situation.** A printer costs $180 to buy, and each page costs 4 cents to print.

**The equation.** The cost that does not depend on pages is 180, and the rate is 0.04 per page. So `cost = 180 + 0.04p`.

**The table**, by substituting, which is lesson 1's skill doing a job here.

| Pages (`p`) | Cost |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | $180 |
| 500 | $200 |
| 2,000 | $260 |
| 5,000 | $380 |

**The question the picture answers instantly.** Someone offers you a different printer at $40, with pages at 8 cents. Which is cheaper? It depends on how much you print, and the two lines cross at the point where the answer changes. Setting the two costs equal and solving is lesson 3's method:

`180 + 0.04p = 40 + 0.08p`

Take `0.04p` off both sides: `180 = 40 + 0.04p`. Take 40 off both: `140 = 0.04p`. Divide by 0.04: `p = 3,500`.

So at three and a half thousand pages the two printers cost the same, and below that the cheap printer wins while above it the expensive one does. That is what solving an equation means when you have a picture: **finding where two lines cross.**

:::predict Check that answer before trusting it. What does each printer cost at exactly 3,500 pages?
The first is `180 + 0.04 × 3500`, which is `180 + 140`, so $320. The second is `40 + 0.08 × 3500`, which is `40 + 280`, so $320 as well. The two agree, so 3,500 is right. This is the same substitution check from lesson 3, and it is worth noticing that it works identically here even though the question arrived as a shopping decision rather than as an equation.
:::

:::checkpoint A second taxi firm charges $2.80 to get in and $1.40 a mile. Here is its table, and the equation worked to the last line. The fixed part is 2.80. The rate is 1.40 per mile. So the fare is `2.80 + ` something. Finish the equation, then use it to find the fare for five miles.

| Miles | Fare |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | $2.80 |
| 2 | $5.60 |
| 10 | $16.80 |

`fare = 2.80 + 1.40d`, and at five miles that is `2.80 + 7.00`, which is $9.80. Notice that this firm is cheaper than the first one for short trips, because its flag charge is lower, and dearer for long ones, because its rate is higher. The two lines cross somewhere, and finding where is exactly the printer question below.
:::

## What people get wrong

**Reading a slope without its units.** A slope of 1.20 means nothing until you say dollars per mile. Steeper does not mean bigger unless the two lines measure the same things.

**Thinking the intercept is always the answer to something sensible.** In the taxi it is a real charge. In a line fitted to data, the value at zero can be meaningless or impossible, and you should ask before using it.

**Confusing the rate with the total.** The taxi's slope is 1.20, and the fare after five miles is 9.50. The slope is not the fare; it is how fast the fare grows.

**Expecting every relationship to be straight.** Straight means a constant rate. Compound growth, the shape of the savings curve you may meet elsewhere, is not straight, because its rate depends on the current amount.

:::exercise Practice
1. A gym charges $30 to join and $22 a month. Write the equation for the total cost after `m` months, and give the cost at 6 months.
2. What is the starting value and what is the rate in `y = 8 + 3x`?
3. A line passes through the points (0, 20) and (5, 45). What is its slope, and what is its equation?
4. A phone plan is `y = 15 + 0.02m`. How many minutes make the bill $25?
5. Two plans: plan A is `20 + 0.10m`, plan B is `35 + 0.04m`. At how many minutes do they cost the same?
6. A slope is 4. Say what it means if the axes are hours and dollars, and what it means if they are miles and litres.
7. Sketch `y = 6 + 2x` for `x` from 0 to 5. Mark the starting value on your sketch, and mark one step of the slope.
:::

:::checkpoint Answers to the practice set, once you have done all six
1. `30 + 22m`, and at six months `30 + 132`, which is $162.

2. Starting value 8, rate 3.

3. Slope is `(45 - 20) / 5`, which is 5, and the equation is `y = 20 + 5x`.

4. Take 15 off both sides to get `10 = 0.02m`, then divide, so 500 minutes. Check: `15 + 0.02 × 500` is 25.

5. Set them equal: `20 + 0.10m = 35 + 0.04m`, so `0.06m = 15`, so `m = 250`. Check: A gives `20 + 25`, which is 45, and B gives `35 + 10`, which is 45.

6. Four dollars per hour in the first case, and four litres per mile in the second, which would be an extraordinarily thirsty vehicle. The number is the same and the meaning comes entirely from the units.

7. It starts at a height of 6 and climbs 2 for every 1 across, so it passes through (0, 6), (1, 8) and (5, 16).
:::
