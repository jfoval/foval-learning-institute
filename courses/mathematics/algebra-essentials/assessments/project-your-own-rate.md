Every worked example in this course belonged to somebody else. A taxi, a phone plan, a printer, a theatre selling tickets. This is the same work done on a rate that is actually yours, and it fits on one page.

The brief: **find one real rate in your own life, write it as an equation, draw it, use it to answer a question you actually have, and then check the answer against reality.** The last step is the point of the project, and the rubric weights it accordingly.

## Step 1: find a rate you can measure

You need something that changes at a steady amount per unit of something else. Anywhere a bill, a quantity or a total goes up by roughly the same amount each time, there is a rate.

Places people find one on the first look:

- A phone, internet or utility bill with a standing charge and a usage charge.
- A pay rate: hourly work, a per-item rate, a mileage reimbursement.
- A subscription with a joining fee, or a membership with a monthly charge.
- Fuel: litres or gallons per hundred kilometres or miles, from two full-tank readings.
- A recipe that scales, where the base is fixed and the rest is per person.
- A commute: minutes per mile, from a few trips.
- Anything you are saving up for, at a fixed amount put aside each week.

**Two things to check before you commit to it.** First, you must be able to get **real numbers**, from a statement, a meter, a payslip or your own measurement, not from memory or estimation. Second, at least **two data points**, because one point never settles a line. If you only have one, take a second measurement before you go on.

## Step 2: the six things on the page

**1. The situation, in one sentence, with the units named.** What is being measured, in what units, against what.

**2. Your data.** A small table of the real numbers, and where each one came from. Three rows is plenty. Say which are measured and which you worked out.

**3. The equation.** Name each letter as a **number**, in words, not as a thing: "`m` is the number of minutes", not "`m` is minutes". Then write `total = starting value + rate × something`, with your own numbers in it, and say what the starting value and the rate mean in the situation, in the situation's own units. If your relationship has no starting value, say so; a rate with nothing fixed attached to it is a perfectly good line through zero.

**4. The graph.** Drawn by hand on paper is entirely fine and takes two minutes. Label both axes with the quantity and its units. Mark your data points, draw the line, and mark the starting value where the line meets the vertical axis.

**5. The question, and the answer.** One question you genuinely want the answer to, of the kind this equation can answer. "How many minutes before this plan costs more than that one?" "How much will I have by March?" "At what mileage does the cheaper car stop being cheaper?" Show the solving, step by step, the way lesson 3 does it, and substitute your answer back into your own equation to check the arithmetic.

**6. The check against reality.** Take your answer, or a prediction the equation makes, and test it against something real: a later bill, a next measurement, a figure you can look up, a third data point you did not use to build the line. Write down what the equation predicted, what actually happened, and the size of the gap.

## Step 3: say what the gap means

This is the part that matters, so give it three or four sentences rather than one.

Your prediction will not be exactly right. The interesting question is why, and there are three quite different answers.

**The arithmetic was wrong.** Check the substitution again. This is the easiest of the three to find and the least interesting.

**The line is right but the reading was imprecise.** Meters, statements and stopwatches all have slack in them. A prediction two per cent out from a rate measured off two fuel fills is doing well.

**The relationship is not actually straight.** This is the useful discovery, and it happens more often than a maths course usually admits. Your phone bill has a tier you did not know about. Your fuel consumption depends on whether the trip is town or motorway. The savings rate slipped in the month you had a car repair. A straight line means the rate is the same everywhere, and finding out that yours is not is a real result, not a failed project. Say what you now think the relationship does instead.

**An equation that disagrees with the world is the most useful thing a beginner can find**, because up to that point the algebra has only ever been checked against itself.

## How to tell whether it is finished

Seven checks, and the last one is worth more than the rest.

1. **The numbers are yours and you can say where each came from.** Not illustrative, not remembered, not from an example in the course.
2. **There are at least two measured points**, and the rate was worked out from them rather than assumed.
3. **Every letter is named as a number**, in words, on the page.
4. **The rate has its units written next to it**, and they are the situation's own units. A slope of 4 means nothing; 4 litres per hundred kilometres means something.
5. **Both axes are labelled** with the quantity and its unit.
6. **The answer was substituted back into the equation** and the check is shown.
7. **The prediction was tested against something real**, the gap is stated, and you have said which of the three explanations you think it is.

## What usually happens

Three things happen to most people, and each of them is the project working.

**Getting two clean data points takes longer than the algebra does.** Bills are laid out to be paid rather than to be read, and the standing charge is often on a different page from the usage. This is the ordinary condition of using maths on real things.

**The starting value turns out to mean something you had not thought about.** People are often surprised by how much of a bill is fixed, and the equation is the first place they see it as a number rather than as a feeling.

**The check in step 6 is the part you will want to skip**, because by then the page looks finished. Do it anyway. Every worked example in this course came out exactly right, and none of your future ones will.

## Afterwards

Keep the page somewhere you will find it, and when the next bill or the next measurement arrives, put the point on the graph. Two things can happen. It lands on the line, in which case you have a working model of something in your life and can use it to answer the next question without redoing any of this. Or it does not, and then you know something changed, and roughly when.

That is what people who use algebra actually do with it. Not solving for `x` on demand, but keeping a rough model of something and noticing when the world stops agreeing with it.
