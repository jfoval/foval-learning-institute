S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Digital Literacy, on the lesson called What services keep about you, and how to look.

S2: And this one opens with a confession, doesn't it?

S1: It does. Twelve lessons have been about machines you can look inside. This one is different. What a service keeps about you is the thinnest-evidenced thing in the course, not because nobody has studied it, but because what any particular company holds is visible only to that company.

S2: So what can a lesson actually do?

S1: It doesn't tell you what's kept. It teaches you to look, and then tells you honestly what people find when they do.

S2: Where does it start?

S1: With a study from twenty fifteen. Researchers sat twenty-eight people down and asked them to draw the internet on paper. On deletion, they split into two camps. One held that once something is online, it's there forever. The other held a vague belief that deleting works.

S2: Those sound like opposites.

S1: They look like opposites and they fail the same way. Both are guesses about a question nobody had tried to answer, and neither camp could say who held copies. And the answer is partly available. Every large service has an export, most have an activity record, and looking takes an afternoon.

S2: So what does an export promise?

S1: The course read Google's own help page on downloading your data, and nothing else of Google's, on the nineteenth of September twenty twenty-six. It says building the archive could take from a few minutes to a few days, depending on how much is in your account, and that most people get the link the same day. It arrives as a zip file, or a T G Z file, which may need additional software on Windows. Archives larger than the size limit you chose are split into multiple files. It expires in about seven days. And you can download each archive five times.

S2: That's a lot of detail.

S1: Five concrete facts. What do they say about what's inside?

S2: Nothing. They're all about the parcel.

S1: All delivery. How long, what it's wrapped in, when it's split, how long it sits on the doorstep, how often you can collect it. Not one word about contents, readability or completeness. It isn't a criticism of the page, but it means the documentation can't tell you what a service holds, and the archive can.

S2: So what do people find when they open one?

S1: A study from twenty twenty-four had thirty-three participants explore their own data from Amazon, Facebook, Google, Spotify or Uber. The course read the abstract, not the paper. They started by saying what questions they hoped the exports would answer. And the finding, in the authors' words: while participants hoped to learn either about their own usage of the platform or how the company collects and uses their personal data, these questions were often left unanswered.

S2: Often left unanswered. That's bleak.

S1: It isn't the whole of it. The same abstract describes nostalgia at some records, and shock and anger about what others meant for privacy. On the course's reading, one archive, both reactions. And afterwards many of them hoped to ask the company to erase some, but not all, of their data.

S2: Why are people surprised by their own data, though? They put it there.

S1: Most of what a service holds about you is observed rather than provided. What you typed into a box is provided. What you opened, when, from where, and for how long, is observed. Nobody typed that in. It piles up as a by-product of the service working at all.

S2: And that's the bigger pile.

S1: Usually, because it's what makes the product function and what makes it worth money. A recommendation needs what you watched, not what you said you liked. A map needs where you went. So saying you never posted anything isn't an answer. Not posting removes you from the provided column and leaves the observed one untouched.

S2: Back to deletion, then. Who holds copies?

S1: The lesson splits deletion into four things on four timescales, and it says plainly that this split is the course's own way of organising the question, not a finding from a source. Removed from your view. Removed from the company's active systems. Removed from backups. And removed from everyone they already shared it with, where your deletion is a request that has to travel, if it travels at all.

S2: So I delete a photo and it disappears from my account. Which of the four has happened?

S1: The first, and only the first. Disappearing from your account is evidence about your account. It's the one sense of deletion that reports on itself. The other three can't be observed from where you're standing.

S2: Is looking even a good idea? What's the evidence?

S1: And the lesson answers that against itself. A twenty twenty-one survey of one hundred and fifty-three participants looked at Google's My Activity dashboard. Again, abstract only. Exposure made them significantly more likely to be less concerned about data collection and to view it more beneficially, and only twenty-five percent said they'd change any setting or behaviour.

S2: So looking calms people down, and few say they'd change anything.

S1: The authors' own conclusion, in their words: it remains unclear if such transparency tools actually improve end user privacy by sufficiently assisting or motivating users to change or review data collection settings.

S2: Then why does the lesson still send me to look?

S1: Because the alternative is the twenty fifteen participants, confident in both directions and unable to say who held copies. Looking replaces a guess with a fact about your own account. The course's position is that knowing is worth having even where the evidence that it changes behaviour is weak, and the lesson says outright that this is a judgement, not a finding.

S2: But the study does change something.

S1: It changes the instruction. Looking isn't the exercise. Writing down what you'll do differently, before you know what's in it, is the exercise.

S2: And Priya?

S1: A constructed case. She opens the activity record on the service she uses most, expecting searches. She finds those, and also a list of every place her phone has been, going back years, because a traffic feature she uses on her commute needs it. She didn't know it was kept. She uses the feature. Switching the record off would break it.

S2: So what should she do?

S1: The lesson won't say. That's a value question, and a course that told her to switch it off would be deciding for her with less information than she has. What it gives her is the shape. Write down what she gave up and what she got for it, both, in her own words, on the same line. Written together, they turn a default into a choice she can revisit.
