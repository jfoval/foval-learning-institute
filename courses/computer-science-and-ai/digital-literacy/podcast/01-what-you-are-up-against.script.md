S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're starting Digital Literacy, with its first lesson. What you are up against, and what this course can honestly do.

S1: And it's an unusual first lesson, because it opens by arguing against the thing you'd expect a course like this to promise.

S2: Which is what, that understanding computers makes you safer?

S1: That's the one. And the lesson doesn't think the evidence supports it.

S2: Start with why people refuse security advice, then. Because that's usually put down to laziness.

S1: And it isn't laziness. Rick Wash interviewed thirty three people, published in twenty ten, and screened out anyone with computer training. What he found is that people hold what he calls folk models. Coherent pictures of what a threat is. Four of viruses and four of hackers, and everybody held at least one of each.

S2: Give me one.

S1: The most consequential is what he calls the big fish model. Nine of the thirty three. Attackers go after the rich and the important. And if that's your picture, then you're not a target, so this isn't your problem.

S2: Which is a perfectly sensible conclusion.

S1: Given the picture, it's the right conclusion. And that's the finding. The advice isn't being rejected out of laziness. It's being rejected rationally, given a wrong picture.

S2: Are there others?

S1: There's one Wash calls viruses are buggy software, also nine of thirty three. A virus is something you have to download and run, so if you're careful what you click, antivirus is unnecessary. And there's the graffiti model, eight people, where attackers are young technical people showing off, which produces fatalism. If they're going to get in, they're going to get in.

S2: So different wrong picture, different wrong response.

S1: And that's why this course builds the picture of the machine and the network before it says a word about passwords. Fix the picture and the advice stops being arbitrary.

S2: You said the lesson argues against its own premise. How?

S1: Two results, and the first one starts out sounding like an argument for the course. In twenty fifteen, four researchers sat twenty eight people down and asked them to draw the internet. Fourteen drew a single box. You send a request in, an answer comes back, nothing in between. The other fourteen drew something with layers in it, and those people named more parties who could see their data, about four on average against about two and a half.

S2: Which is the case for understanding the thing.

S1: So far. And then the same study cuts the other way. They found no direct relationship between technical background and the protective actions people actually took. Knowing more predicted noticing more. It did not predict doing more.

S2: And the second result?

S1: The second is newer and more uncomfortable. A team published it in twenty twenty five. An eight month randomised experiment at a university health system, more than nineteen thousand five hundred employees, ten simulated phishing campaigns, and real control groups who got no training.

S2: What did it find?

S1: No significant relationship between having recently completed the annual awareness training and the chance of failing a simulation. The embedded training, the page you land on after you click, produced a small but statistically significant improvement of about two percentage points in absolute terms. And more than fifty six percent of employees clicked a phishing link at least once, trained or not.

S2: That's a course arguing against itself.

S1: It's a course being honest about what it can claim. And I should say the lesson is careful here in the other direction too. The authors don't claim people can't learn to spot a fake message. Earlier laboratory and small scale studies found training does improve how accurately people sort real messages from fakes, and a study of seven thousand and eighty nine Korean organisations found a correlation between time spent on training and fewer incidents. Both of those are set out by the twenty twenty five authors themselves, in their own paper, against their own result. What they claim is narrower. The training products currently deployed, at the intensity organisations deploy them, don't move the outcome much. Those are two different claims.

S2: So if the course won't promise safety, what does it promise?

S1: Two things, and there's no third. The first is that it changes what you can diagnose. When something goes wrong, a working picture of where files live and who sits in a network path is the difference between a person who can say what failed and a person who can only say that it broke.

S2: And neither study touched that.

S1: Neither study touched it. They measured protective behaviour. Did you click, did you turn the thing on. Diagnosis is a separate skill and nobody has shown it to be unteachable.

S2: And the second promise?

S1: That it supports the handful of choices you make once. A password manager. A passkey. Multi factor authentication turned on. Updates applied automatically. A backup you've actually restored from.

S2: Each of those is one afternoon.

S1: Each of those is a decision made once, and it then keeps working while you're tired and distracted and in a hurry. Vigilance is the thing that's been measured and found wanting, because vigilance has to be produced fresh every time.

S2: That's a real distinction. Setup against attentiveness.

S1: And the phishing result is specifically about attentiveness.

S2: The lesson also has you write something.

S1: A security plan, and it's five questions. What do I have that's worth protecting. Who might want it. How likely is it they'll try. How bad is it if they succeed. And how much trouble am I willing to go to.

S2: That last one sounds like the important one.

S1: It's the question that stops a plan being a fantasy. And the lesson reaches one honest conclusion a plan is allowed to reach, which is that some threats aren't worth defending against. If a national intelligence service wants your photographs, you're not going to stop them, and a plan that pretends otherwise will collapse and take the achievable parts with it.

S2: Does that plan come back?

S1: It comes back in lessons eight, ten, eleven and thirteen. It's the spine of the course.

S1: We've skipped the plan worked all the way through on one ordinary person, which is the part that shows you how to do it. And there's a case at the end about an attacker not one of Wash's thirty three respondents could even conceive of, which is worth the lesson on its own.
