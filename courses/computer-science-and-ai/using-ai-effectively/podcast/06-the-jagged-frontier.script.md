S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Using AI Effectively, lesson six. The jagged frontier.

S2: And the lesson opens with two jobs.

S1: Both of which an experienced conveyancer would call routine. One: turn these eight pages of survey notes into a two-page letter for a client. Two: state the standard notice period for this kind of lease.

S2: And one of them goes wrong.

S1: One is fine and the other is a liability, and the lesson's claim is that the reason people pick wrong isn't carelessness. That reason is the whole of the lesson.

S2: Where does the name come from?

S1: The consulting study from lesson one. Its authors set out to describe the uneven impact of artificial intelligence capabilities, where AI assistance improves performance for some tasks but worsens it for others, even within the same knowledge workflow and with a seemingly similar level of difficulty. And they called it a jagged technology frontier.

S2: Same workflow, same apparent difficulty, opposite results.

S1: That's it. And the scope: seven hundred and fifty-eight management consultants at Boston Consulting Group, using GPT four, with the data gathered in twenty twenty-three. On the eighteen tasks inside the frontier, the ones with AI completed twelve point two percent more and finished twenty-five point one percent faster, graded twenty-nine point nine percent higher with the system alone and thirty-three point nine with the system plus a prompting overview, against a control mean of four point three seven out of ten.

S2: And outside it?

S1: The AI groups were right sixty percent and seventy point six percent of the time, against a control group's eighty-four point five.

S2: Now, I want to challenge the obvious reading. The hard task was the one it failed, surely.

S1: That's what the lesson spends a paragraph correcting, and it's the most important paragraph in it. The outside task wasn't easy. It was built out of the sort of business cases BCG uses in its own hiring, deliberately, with the aim of creating a task at which knowledge workers would excel but AI would struggle, at least without extensive guidance and human intervention. And the control group still only got it right eighty-four point five percent of the time.

S2: So it was hard.

S1: It was hard. It was chosen because it's the kind of hard that people are comparatively good at. Difficulty is one axis and the frontier runs across it at an angle.

S2: Which explains something I recognise.

S1: The lesson says exactly that. Your own experience of these systems feels contradictory: you hand over something demanding and it comes back excellent, you hand over something trivial and it comes back wrong. That isn't the system being erratic. It's you sorting by the wrong property.

S2: And there's the third group again, from lesson one.

S1: And here it matters differently. Inside the frontier, the group with the prompting overview did best of the three. Outside it, that same group fell furthest: sixty percent correct, twenty-four and a half points below the control, against thirteen point nine points for the group with no overview.

S2: And the study's own sentence about that task.

S1: Read it again. It was built to be one AI would struggle with, at least without extensive guidance and human intervention. The overview group is the group that had the guidance. It didn't rescue them, and on that task it went the other way.

S2: So being good at asking isn't the same skill.

S1: Skill at asking and skill at knowing when to ask are two different competences. They don't arrive together, and the first without the second is worse than neither, because it produces confidence exactly where confidence is misplaced.

S2: All right. So how do I find the line?

S1: And here the lesson is careful. The study names the frontier and doesn't tell you how to find it. What comes next is the course's own answer, reasoned from lesson two rather than measured, to be held as a working rule you can test.

S2: Understood. Go on.

S1: Go back to the mechanism. It produces text, one piece at a time, each piece chosen given everything in front of it, with no document to consult. So: reliable when the answer is constrained by what you supplied. Rewriting, shortening, changing register, translating, structuring notes, generating options, drafting from material you provided.

S2: And unreliable in how many kinds?

S1: Three. The answer has to match a fact outside the request, like a notice period or a price or a deadline. Or it turns on counting, position or exact arithmetic, which sits below the pieces the system works in. Or it depends on something nobody has written down: your organisation's real appetite for risk, what your director will actually agree to, why the last attempt failed.

S2: Is there one question that does it?

S1: Is the answer constrained by what I supplied, or does it have to match something outside it? Run the two conveyancing jobs through it. The letter is entirely constrained by the eight pages. The notice period has to match the law.

S2: But plenty of products search the web now.

S1: The lesson owes you that complication and takes it here. Nothing goes and looks is true of a plain chat window and isn't true of every product. When a search tool runs, some facts outside the request get brought inside it. That doesn't move the line. It moves particular tasks across it, and only when the tool actually ran and actually found the right thing. So the sorting rule stands with one extra question on the fact cases: did it go and look, and can I see what it found?

S2: Give me a pair to sort.

S1: Two requests. One: draft a thank-you note to a donor who gave five hundred pounds last March. Two: which of our donors gave over five hundred pounds last March?

S2: The note is constrained. Everything needed is in the request.

S1: And the question is a fact outside it. The answer lives in your donor records, and unless something goes and gets them, nothing in the process has them.

S2: Can I move it?

S1: Paste the donor list in. Now the answer is constrained by what you supplied and the task has crossed the line. Though it's landed close to the second unreliable kind, because picking out everyone above a threshold is a filtering job, and filtering is counting's cousin. So check what it returns, and ask whether anything's missing, which is the harder check.

S2: And that's a general move.

S1: It's worth more than the categories. An unreliable task often becomes a reliable one if you supply the thing the answer has to match.

S2: Most real work isn't one or the other, though.

S1: Most real work is mixed, and the skill is the join. It's usually small and specific: the figures, the dates, the names, the one claim about a rule. Knowing which they are before you start is much faster than checking everything afterwards.

S2: Last thing. People draw this line wrong. Give me the two worst ways.

S1: By difficulty, which is the commonest and is the study's whole point. Naming the third word of a sentence is trivial for a person and sits on the unreliable side, because position is below the pieces the system works in.

S2: And the second?

S1: By assuming better prompting protects you. The overview group. That's the rule this lesson exists to break, and it's the one held by people who have taken a course.

S2: And if somebody says the frontier moves, so why learn where it is?

S1: It does move, and a better model shifts where the boundary falls on the counting cases and on some of the fact cases. What doesn't move is the reason a task sits on one side, which is whether the answer is constrained by what you supplied. That question survives a better model, and the question is what you're learning.
