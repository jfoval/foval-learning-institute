S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Using AI Effectively, lesson nine. When not to use one.

S2: Which is an unusual lesson for a course like this to contain.

S1: The lesson says so in its first line. A course that teaches you to use something has an obvious temptation, which is never to say when to stop. And it says this lesson exists because the best-evidenced study in the course's research is about exactly that, and it would be a dishonest course that put it in a callout.

S2: Tell me about the study.

S1: Twenty twenty-five, a field experiment published in PNAS, with nearly a thousand high school mathematics students. During practice sessions the students were in one of three conditions. Two with access to GPT four, and one with no access at all.

S2: What were the two?

S1: GPT Base was a standard chat interface. GPT Tutor was the same model with prompts designed to safeguard learning, giving teacher-written hints rather than answers.

S2: And the third group matters.

S1: It's the comparison that makes the rest of it mean anything. Without it there's nothing to be seventeen percent below.

S2: Go on, then. During practice.

S1: Both helped, and the guarded one helped far more. Grades improved forty-eight percent for GPT Base and a hundred and twenty-seven percent for GPT Tutor.

S2: That's an enormous gain.

S1: It is. Now the other half. When access was taken away, the unguarded group was worse off than students who'd never had it at all, by seventeen percent in grades for GPT Base.

S2: Worse off than never having had it.

S1: And the authors' own summary of why. Without guardrails, students attempt to use GPT four as a crutch during practice problem sessions, and subsequently perform worse on their own.

S2: What about the guarded group?

S1: They didn't suffer that. The paper says the negative effects were largely mitigated by the safeguards in GPT Tutor.

S2: So which numbers do I carry?

S1: All of them, and the lesson is firm about this. The forty-eight and the seventeen travel together, always, because either one on its own is being used to make an argument the study doesn't support. And the hundred and twenty-seven travels with them, because it's what turns this from a verdict on the technology into a question about how it's used.

S2: And the scope.

S1: High school mathematics, practice problems, GPT four, published twenty twenty-five. One study, one subject, one age group.

S2: Now the part I want. Why would using a tool during practice leave somebody worse off afterwards than never having used it?

S1: The mechanism comes from Term one of the Core. How to Learn Anything taught that reconstructing something from nothing is what builds the kind of memory that lasts, and that a study method which feels fluent is often the one teaching least. Rereading a clear answer raises fluency and does little for what you'll still have next month.

S2: And asking for the answer removes the reconstructing.

S1: At exactly the moment when the struggle was doing the work. The unguarded group spent their practice sessions reading answers rather than producing them, and got grades in those sessions that reflected the tool rather than themselves. Same hours, none of the retrieval. And the feeling of following a clear worked answer is very close to the feeling of understanding one.

S2: Which is the fluency illusion.

S1: With a much better interface.

S2: Most listeners aren't doing maths homework, though.

S1: And the lesson says that, and then says what follows is its own reasoning from the study rather than a finding, and labels it. Here it is. The cost appears when the doing was the point.

S2: Unpack that.

S1: Sometimes the output is the point. A letter you need, a summary somebody will read, a first draft you'll rewrite anyway. Hand those over, check them, get on with your day. Sometimes the doing is the point. You're learning something. You're building a judgement you'll need later, in a room, without the system.

S2: And in between?

S1: Sometimes it's neither, and the honest answer is that nobody can tell you. Whether to trade the skill for the hour is a question about what you want your life to look like, and the course describes the trade and declines to make it.

S2: Give me the concrete version.

S1: A trainee claims handler, first year, learning to spot the problems in a claim file. Constructed case, but work it through. She has a system open, and two ways to use it. First way: she reads the file, writes down what she thinks is wrong, and then asks, here's the file and here's what I flagged, what have I missed and why would somebody flag it?

S2: What had she flagged?

S1: Three things. The incident date is a Sunday and the policy is a commercial one. The repair quote has no VAT line. The claimant's address on the form doesn't match the address on the policy. What comes back adds two she didn't have. The claim was notified eleven weeks after the incident, and the policy wording has a notification window in it. And the quote is from a firm that shares a surname with the claimant.

S2: And the next file?

S1: She catches the late notification herself, and she misses the surname again.

S2: And the second way of using it?

S1: She pastes the file in and asks what's wrong with it. Back come five things, the same five. She reads them, agrees, and sends the file on. Next file, she pastes in.

S2: Same tool, same fifteen minutes.

S1: The first builds the thing she came to the firm to acquire, which is a trained eye. Three flags, then five, then the two she missed becoming one. The second produces the same five flags every week and builds nothing, and it works right up to the morning she's in front of somebody with a question the list didn't cover.

S2: And notice what the first way is.

S1: It's the guarded condition, run by her rather than by a designer. She attempted first and asked for what she missed, which is hints after effort rather than answers instead of it.

S2: Does this lesson think declining is the right answer?

S1: No, and it's careful in both directions. The standards body names over-reliance, which it calls excessive deference to automated systems, and in the same section it names the opposite failure: human experts may be unnecessarily averse to these systems, and thus deprive themselves or others of their beneficial uses.

S2: So the course is against both.

S1: Declining isn't a mistake and it isn't a virtue either. It's a decision with a cost on each side, and the point of the lesson is to make the cost on the less obvious side visible enough to weigh.

S2: And the exercise asks you to act on that.

S1: Two of them. One asks you to stop something, and to write down what you'll do instead and how much longer it'll take. The other is the harder half, which is redesigning a task you intend to keep, so that you still do the part that builds the judgement.

S2: That's the episode. We've left out a case where handing the work over costs nothing at all, a checkpoint, and both exercises in full. The full lesson is at Foval Learning Institute dot org. Thanks for listening.
