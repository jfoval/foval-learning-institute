S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're starting a course called Using AI Effectively, with its first lesson. What this is for, and what the evidence actually says.

S2: And I want to start where the lesson starts, because it's a strange opening for a course.

S1: It is. It opens by saying that the best evidence anybody has about whether these systems make people faster is two randomised trials, both careful, and they point in opposite directions.

S2: Give me the first one.

S1: A study run with Boston Consulting Group, published in twenty twenty-six. Seven hundred and fifty-eight consultants, eighteen realistic consulting tasks, three conditions: no AI, GPT four, and GPT four plus a short overview on how to prompt it. The ones with AI completed twelve point two percent more tasks, finished them twenty-five point one percent faster, and their work was graded substantially better.

S2: How much better?

S1: Graders were working on a scale of one to ten. The control group averaged four point three seven, and the two AI groups came in twenty-nine point nine and thirty-three point nine percent above that. And the authors checked it wasn't just tidier writing. They report the effects are not driven by superficial spelling or grammar improvements.

S2: That sounds like the case for the tools, made properly.

S1: It is. Now the second one. In twenty twenty-five a group called METR ran a randomised trial with sixteen experienced open-source developers, on two hundred and forty-six real tasks, in repositories they had been working in for years. Tasks were randomly assigned to allow or forbid AI tools.

S2: And before you tell me, the lesson stops you here and asks you to guess. So let me guess. Faster.

S1: The developers guessed faster too. Beforehand, they predicted the tools would make them twenty-four percent quicker. Allowing the tools made the work take nineteen percent longer.

S2: Longer.

S1: Nineteen percent longer. And there's a third number, which is the one I'd carry out of this lesson if I carried one. When those same developers were asked afterwards, having in fact been slowed by nineteen percent, they estimated they'd been sped up by twenty.

S2: They were wrong about it while it was happening to them.

S1: Twice, in the same direction. And they weren't being dishonest. Everybody judges a tool by how it feels to use, and this is the one trial that checked the feeling against the clock.

S2: All right. So how do two careful trials come out opposite?

S1: Look at what each one measured. The consultants were doing eighteen tasks written for the experiment, new to them that afternoon. The developers were working in code they had written and maintained for years. The lesson's reading is that the gains show up on work near the edge of what a person can already do, and the losses show up on work they'd already mastered.

S2: Why would that be?

S1: Think about what using one of these costs. You write the request, you read what comes back, you check it, you correct it or you throw it away and start again. That cost is roughly constant. What changes is what you're comparing it against. If doing the work yourself would have taken an afternoon, the reading and checking is small against that. If you could've done it yourself in eleven minutes and done it right, the reading and checking is most of eleven minutes and you've added a step for nothing.

S2: And I should say, is that the studies' explanation or the course's?

S1: The course's, and the lesson says so in as many words. Neither set of authors puts it that way. It's offered as the best available account of why two careful trials came out opposite, to be held the way you'd hold any reasonable explanation nobody has tested.

S2: Is there anything in the studies themselves that supports it?

S1: There is, and it's in how the first study built its hard task. The authors designed it with BCG, using as a starting point the type of business cases that BCG uses for its highly competitive job interviews, aiming at creating a task at which knowledge workers would excel but AI would struggle. So the task where AI made people worse was chosen precisely because people are good at it.

S2: What happened on that task?

S1: The control group, no AI, got it right eighty-four point five percent of the time. The two AI groups got sixty percent and seventy point six. A drop of about nineteen percentage points. Same people, same afternoon, same tool. The difference was the task.

S2: And this is the part I want to ask about, because there's a detail in the lesson that stopped me. The three groups.

S1: Yes. The consultants were split three ways, and the third group had GPT four plus that short overview on how to prompt it. Inside the frontier, on the tasks the system was good at, that group did best of all. On the task outside it, that group did worst. It scored sixty percent, a drop of twenty-four and a half points, against the other AI group's seventy point six, a drop of thirteen point nine.

S2: So the people who'd been taught to use it better were hurt most.

S1: On the wrong task, yes. And the lesson says that result is why the course is shaped the way it is. Lessons four and five teach you to ask for things well. Lesson six teaches you which tasks to ask about, and lesson nine is about putting the thing down. If you only take half the course, take the second half.

S2: So what is it actually promising? Not a speed-up, by the sound of it.

S1: No, and it says so. Two things. First, you'll be able to tell which kind of task you're on, which is lesson six. Second, you'll be able to check what comes back, which is lesson eight. And there's a line about the second one worth repeating. A person who can check an answer can use a tool they do not trust.

S2: And a warning about the studies themselves?

S1: An honest one. Consultants at one firm, on tasks written for the experiment, using GPT four, with data gathered in twenty twenty-three. And sixteen people on work they knew intimately, with tools from early twenty twenty-five. Neither is a claim about you.

S2: Which sounds like a reason to ignore them.

S1: The lesson says the opposite. What you do with a study that isn't about you is read what it measured and ask how far that reaches.

S2: That's the whole lesson in a sentence.

S1: Nearly. And it does the same thing to itself: it names a gap in its own evidence, which is that nobody has measured what happens to somebody with no technical background using one of these for ordinary work.

S2: Where does somebody start if they want to do something with this?

S1: The exercise, which takes twenty minutes. Two columns. Three tasks you use one of these for, with a reason each. Three you would not, with a reason each. Then mark every entry as a guess or a finding, and most people's are almost all guesses. Then pick the entry you're least sure about and write one sentence on what would change your mind.

S2: And if you can't write that sentence?

S1: Then you don't have a view. You have a habit. That's the lesson's line, not mine.

S1: Thanks for listening.
