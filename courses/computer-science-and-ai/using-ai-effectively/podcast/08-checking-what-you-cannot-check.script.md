S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Using AI Effectively, lesson eight. Checking an answer you could not have produced.

S2: And that title is the whole problem.

S1: Everything the course has taught so far assumes you can tell whether an answer is any good. Lesson six told you which tasks to be careful on, lesson seven told you what going wrong looks like. Both leave the same question standing, and it's the one that matters at your desk on a Tuesday. You asked because you couldn't produce the answer yourself. So how do you check it?

S2: If you could check it by reading it and knowing, you wouldn't have needed to ask.

S1: That's the whole difficulty, and it's why the honest answer has steps in it rather than a slogan. Four steps, cheapest first, and none of them requires you to know the answer.

S2: Before we take them, where do the four steps come from?

S1: I'm glad you asked, because the lesson says this plainly. They're this course's construction. Not a finding, not anybody's published method. They're assembled out of things that are sourced: lesson five's three readings, lesson three's variation between runs, one measured result about self-assessment, and ordinary practice. Use them as a working method, and if one lets something through, the useful thing to say afterwards is which step it was.

S2: Right. Step one.

S1: Before step one, actually. Decide how much checking this needs, and decide it from the consequence rather than from the clock. Two questions. What will this be used for? A note to yourself, a paragraph in a report somebody else signs, a figure that goes to a client, a decision that costs money. And what does being wrong cost? Embarrassment, an afternoon, a relationship, a regulatory problem.

S2: And write it down.

S1: As a sentence. This is going in an internal update, so twenty minutes and the figures have to be right. Writing it down isn't about discipline. It's that the budget decides which of the four steps you run, and without it you'll either check nothing or check until you're tired, and neither of those is a decision.

S2: Now step one.

S1: Check the parts that are independently checkable. Names, numbers, dates, quotations, citations, section numbers. Anything with an outside referent. They're cheap, and they're where failures concentrate, for lesson six's reason: they're the parts that have to match something outside the request.

S2: Is that enough?

S1: It's the best value in the procedure and it isn't sufficient, and the lesson says both in the same breath, because people who do it well often stop there. An answer can be built out of entirely accurate components and still not follow.

S2: Step two.

S1: Ask for the claims it's arguing from, in order, and read the seam. And it earns its place for a particular reason: you don't have to be able to produce the answer to notice that the third claim doesn't follow from the second. You aren't checking the claims. You're checking the joins, which is a much smaller job and is sometimes the whole of it.

S2: And step three?

S1: Ask it again, cleanly, in a fresh conversation rather than further down the same one, and be careful what you conclude. And asking the system to review its own answer belongs here too, in the middle of the procedure rather than at the end of it. If the two disagree, you've learned something solid. One of them is wrong, and you've been shown exactly where to look.

S2: And if they agree?

S1: You've learned much less than it feels like. Two runs of the same model on the same question agreeing tells you the system is consistent here. A system can be consistently wrong, and a consistent error is precisely the kind this step cannot see.

S2: Step four.

S1: Take it to something that isn't the system. A search engine, the actual document, the regulation itself, a colleague who knows. This is the expensive step, and it's the only one that can catch an error the system makes every time, because every other step stays inside the system and inherits whatever the system consistently gets wrong.

S2: Which is why the budget comes first.

S1: Step four is where the time goes, and the budget is what tells you whether this piece of work has earned it.

S2: Give me a case.

S1: The lesson has two, and both are constructed, figures included, so that you can watch which step catches what. Take the short one. A school business manager asks whether a particular equipment purchase can be spread across two budget years. Three paragraphs come back, confident, with one figure and a named policy document.

S2: And she checks.

S1: Step one, four minutes. The document exists under that name. The figure, a ten thousand pound capital threshold, is in it. Clean. Step two, three minutes. She asks for the claims in order. Three of them: this equipment counts as capital; capital purchases above the threshold may be spread; therefore this purchase may be spread. Read the joins. Clean. Step three, two minutes. Fresh conversation, same question, same answer, same document. Clean.

S2: Three clean steps.

S1: Step four, twenty minutes. She rings the finance office. That policy was replaced eight months ago, and the rule now runs the other way.

S2: So nothing inside the system could have caught it.

S1: Because the system wasn't confused about anything. It was consistent, and it was consistently working from a document that had been superseded. That's the case that justifies the expensive step, and it's the reason the expensive step exists.

S2: And the long case?

S1: A community transport charity, and its finance volunteer, reclaiming VAT on a minibus. Step one, eleven minutes, and it finds a figure of sixty percent that's nowhere in the notice the answer cites. Step two, six minutes, and the seam is inside the answer's second claim, which asserts that a seat count is what makes a vehicle qualify while the answer's own prose had said the test was about use. She doesn't need to know any VAT law to see that the load-bearing claim was asserted rather than derived.

S2: And step three?

S1: Four minutes, a fresh conversation, and the second answer gives a different percentage. Consistent on the conclusion, inconsistent on a figure. That does not tell her which percentage is right. It tells her where the uncertainty sits.

S2: And step four.

S1: An afternoon. She reads the notice and rings the charity's accountant, who says the answer is broadly right in direction, the percentage is wrong in a way that changes the sum, and there's a further condition nobody's answer mentioned at all.

S2: What did each step cost her?

S1: Eleven minutes, six, four, and an afternoon. And the accounting the lesson wants is this. The whole thing took an afternoon, and writing it from scratch would have taken her a week she hasn't got. The answer was worth asking for and it wasn't worth trusting, and those are two judgements rather than one.

S2: One thing that surprised me. Steps two and four found the same fault.

S1: From opposite sides. Step two could see that a claim had no support under it. Only step four could say what the right claim was. Which is the relationship between the cheap steps and the expensive one in one sentence: the cheap ones tell you where to spend the expensive one.

S2: Last thing. What's the mistake people make constantly here?

S1: Treating "ask it to double-check" as the end of the procedure. It's step three of four. It catches real things on familiar ground, and it stays inside the system, so treating it as the end leaves the whole of step four undone.
