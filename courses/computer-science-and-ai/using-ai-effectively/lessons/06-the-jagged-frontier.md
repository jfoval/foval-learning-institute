Two jobs, both of which an experienced person would call routine.

Turn these eight pages of notes into a two-page letter for a client.

Tell me the standard notice period for this kind of lease.

Hand both to one of these systems and one is fine and the other is a liability. Most people, asked in advance which is which, get it wrong, and the reason they get it wrong is the whole of this lesson.

## The line is not where difficulty is

The consulting study from lesson 1 gave this its name. The authors set out to describe "the uneven impact of artificial intelligence (AI) capabilities, where AI assistance improves performance for some tasks but worsens it for others, **even within the same knowledge workflow and with a seemingly similar level of difficulty**".[1] They called it a **jagged technology frontier**.

Hold on to that last clause. Same workflow. Same apparent difficulty. Opposite results.

Their eighteen inside-the-frontier tasks produced consultants who completed 12.2% more work, 25.1% faster, and were graded 29.9% and 33.9% higher than the control group depending on which AI condition they were in. The one task chosen to sit outside it produced consultants who were right 60% and 70.6% of the time against a control group's 84.5%.[1]

Nothing about the second task was harder for a person. It was built, in the authors' words, to be one "at which knowledge workers would excel but AI would struggle".[1]

So difficulty is the wrong axis, and using it is why people's experience of these systems feels contradictory. You hand over something hard and it comes back excellent. You hand over something trivial and it comes back wrong. That isn't the system being erratic. It's you sorting by the wrong property.

## The result that should change how you read the last two lessons

The consulting study had three groups, not two. No AI, the system, and the system plus a short overview on how to prompt it.

Inside the frontier, the group with the prompting overview did best of the three.

On the task outside the frontier, that same group did **worst**. Both AI groups fell below the control group's 84.5% correct, and the overview group fell furthest: down to 60%, a drop of 24.5 percentage points, against the other AI group's 70.6% and 13.9 points.[1]

**The people who had been taught to use the tool better were the ones most damaged by using it where it should not have been used.**

That result is almost never quoted, and you have just spent two lessons learning to ask well, so it is the right moment to say what it means. **Skill at asking is not skill at knowing when to ask.** They're two different competences, they don't come together, and the first without the second is worse than neither, because it produces confidence on exactly the tasks where confidence is misplaced.

This lesson is the second competence.

## What actually puts a task on one side

The study names the frontier and doesn't tell you how to find it. What follows is this course's answer, reasoned from lesson 2 rather than measured, and you should hold it as a working rule you can check rather than as a finding.

Go back to the mechanism. The system produces text, one piece at a time, each piece chosen given everything in front of it, with no document to consult. So:

**Reliable work: the answer is constrained by what you supplied.** Rewriting, shortening, changing register, translating, structuring notes, generating options, drafting from material you provided. Everything needed is in the request, and the test of the answer is reading it against what you sent.

**Unreliable work, in three kinds.** The answer has to match **a fact outside the request**, like a notice period or a deadline or a price. Or it turns on **counting, position or exact arithmetic**, which sits below the pieces the system works in, as lesson 2 showed with letters. Or it depends on **something nobody has written down**: your organisation's real appetite for risk, what your director will actually agree to, why the last attempt failed.

One question gets you there in about two seconds: **is the answer constrained by what I supplied, or does it have to match something outside it?**

Now run the two jobs from the top of this lesson through it. The letter is entirely constrained by the eight pages. The notice period has to match the law, and nothing in the process goes and looks.

:::predict Two more, before you read on. One: "draft a thank-you note to a donor who gave £500 last March." Two: "which of our donors gave over £500 last March?" Sort them, then say what the second one would need in order to move sides.
The note is constrained work. The question isn't, and it fails in the third way rather than the first: the answer depends on something nobody has written down anywhere the system can reach, which is your donor list.

What would move it: paste the donor list in. Now the answer is constrained by what you supplied, and the task has crossed the line, though it has landed close to the second unreliable kind, because picking out everyone above a threshold is a filtering job and filtering is counting's cousin. So check the ones it returns, and ask whether anything's missing, which is the harder check.

That is the general move, and it is worth more than the categories: **many unreliable tasks become reliable if you supply the thing the answer has to match.**
:::

## Most real work is mixed, and the skill is the join

A manager asks for a summary of a forty-page quarterly report.

Summarising is the clearest text-shape task there is, so this looks like an easy call. It isn't, because the report contains figures, and the summary has to carry them accurately. The prose is on one side of the line and the numbers are on the other, inside one request.

Handing the whole thing over produces a good summary with figures you cannot trust. Refusing to hand any of it over means writing the summary yourself for the sake of seven numbers.

**The split is the answer**, and it takes a minute: let the system produce the prose, then check every figure against the report yourself. You've kept the part that works and spent your attention on the part that doesn't, which is the whole of what this course is trying to teach you to do.

Look for the join in anything you are about to hand over. It's usually a small, specific thing: the figures, the dates, the names, the one claim about a rule. Those are what to check, and knowing which they are before you start is much faster than checking everything afterwards.

:::checkpoint A recruiter wants a shortlist of five from forty applications, with a reason for each. Where is the join?
The reasons are constrained work: each one is a claim about a document the system was given, and you can check it against that document in seconds.

The ranking isn't. "Best five" depends on what your team actually needs this year, which of last year's hires worked out, and what your director means by a strong candidate, and none of that is in the forty applications.

So the split is not "do the shortlist, check the reasons". It is the other way round. Let it summarise each application against criteria you supplied, and do the ranking yourself with those summaries in front of you.

Notice what that does to the work. You haven't saved yourself the decision, which is the part that needed you. You've saved yourself forty readings, which is the part that didn't.
:::

## Five ways people draw the line wrong

Each of these is a real rule somebody uses, and each fails on a case you can now build yourself.

**By difficulty.** The most common, and the study's whole point. Counting syllables in a limerick is easier for a person than writing the limerick, and the system's results go the other way.

**By subject, technical against creative.** Both directions of this rule are in circulation, which should tell you something. Rewriting a technical manual is constrained work; inventing a plausible-sounding statistic for a poem is not. The subject is not the property.

**By how confident the answer sounds.** Fluency is a property of the text, as lesson 2 said. The answer on the unreliable side is often the more confident one, because the shape of a confident answer is exactly what it can produce.

**By what happened last time.** One run is one draw, which lesson 3 showed, so a single good result on a fact question is not evidence that fact questions are safe here. The reason a task sits where it does is worth more than a sample of one.

**By assuming better prompting protects you.** The overview group. This is the rule this lesson exists to break, and it is the one held by people who have taken a course.

And one objection worth answering properly rather than listing. **"The frontier moves, so why learn where it is?"** It does move. What doesn't move is the reason a task sits on one side, which is whether the answer is constrained by what you supplied. A better model shifts where the reliable-unreliable boundary falls on the counting cases and on some of the fact cases. It doesn't change the question you ask, and the question is what you're learning.

## Practice

:::exercise Sort ten, predict first
Take 25 minutes.

1. Write down ten tasks from your own work. Real ones from the last fortnight. Mix them up: some you have handed over, some you never would, some you have wondered about.

2. **Before running anything, sort all ten** into reliable, unreliable and mixed, and beside each write which reason applies: constrained by what I supplied; has to match a fact outside; counting or position; depends on something unwritten.

3. Now pick the three you are least sure about and actually run them.

4. For each, write down what happened and whether your sorting was right. Where it was wrong, the useful question is not "was I wrong" but **which of the four reasons did I misapply?**

5. Keep the list. The course project asks for six trials with predictions, and this is four of them done.
:::

:::exercise Find the join in one real job
Take 20 minutes, on a piece of work you actually have to do this week.

1. Write the job down in one sentence.

2. Split it into parts, and mark each part with which side it sits on. Most jobs come apart into three or four.

3. For the unreliable parts, write down specifically what would have to be checked, and how long that would take.

4. Then the decision, in writing: **which parts are you handing over, which are you keeping, and what will you check?** If the checking would take longer than doing the part yourself, that part stays with you, and saying so is a result rather than a failure.

This is section four of the course project. Do it properly now and that section is written.
:::

:::exercise Get the baseline out
Take 10 minutes.

Find the two columns you wrote in lesson 1: three tasks you use one of these systems for, three you would not, each with a reason.

Read your reasons now. Mark every one that was really about difficulty, or about subject, or about what happened last time. Rewrite those reasons using the question from this lesson.

Then count how many entries moved sides. Lesson 11 asks for this list one last time, and the interesting thing by then will be how much of the movement happened here.
:::
