  - q: >-
      "Our assistant reduces time spent on reporting by up to 60%." Sort it, and say what would
      settle it.
    options:
      - Measurable, and a trial comparing matched teams with and without it over a real reporting cycle would settle it
      - Unmeasurable, because the words "up to" leave the claim compatible with any result at all, including no reduction
      - False, since no study of any professional task has ever found a time saving of anything like that size
      - Measurable, and the vendor's own published figures on its own customers would settle it if they were released

    explain: >-
      A time saving on a named task is exactly the kind of thing a trial can measure, and naming the
      trial is what turns a slogan into a claim. "Up to" is a weasel and it weakens the claim rather
      than making it unmeasurable, which is worth noticing because the two feel the same. The false
      answer asserts a survey of the literature nobody has done. And the vendor's own figures would
      be evidence and not a settlement, for the reason this whole course keeps giving.
  - q: >-
      A product page says its system "understands your documents". What kind of claim is that, and
      what should a reader do with it?
    options:
      - Measurable, and running a reading-comprehension benchmark on the reader's own kinds of document would settle it
      - False, since these systems do not understand anything at all and the word is being used loosely to suggest that they do
      - Unmeasurable as written, because nothing is specified that could come out one way or the other
      - True but uninformative, since every product of this kind already does the same thing as every other
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Nothing here could fail. No task, no comparison, no threshold, so there is no result that would
      contradict it. Calling it false settles the reasoning question this course declines, and calling
      it measurable imports a task the page never named. And "every product does the same thing" is a
      claim about the market that the sentence does not support.

      Three legal research products claimed retrieval had eliminated or avoided hallucination. Tested
      in 2024, they hallucinated between 17% and 33% of the time. What is the most accurate account
      of what the vendors did?

      - They lied, since the measured rates contradict the claim they published about their products
      - They made a claim too broad to be true of any system of that kind, which is a different failure
      - They were right about their own systems, and the test used queries their products were not built for
      - They described a goal rather than a state, which every product page does and nobody misreads

    explain: >-
      Retrieval did reduce the rate against a general chatbot, so the direction of their claim was
      right and its scope was not, and that is harder to spot than a lie because nothing in it is
      obviously untrue. Digital Literacy makes the same point about security marketing. The lying
      answer is simpler and less accurate. The wrong-queries answer is the response a vendor would

  - q: >-
      A reader has used one of these systems daily for a year and says he knows what it is good at.

    options:
      - A year is nowhere near long enough to form a reliable impression of a tool that is replaced every few months
      - Daily users are the most invested in the tool and are therefore the least able to judge it fairly
      - The one trial that measured it found experienced users wrong about their own speed, in both directions

      complication and not the reason. The invested answer is an accusation rather than an argument.

Nine lessons ago this course told you it would not promise to make you faster, because two randomised trials pointed opposite ways. Everything since has been about what these systems do and how to work them. This last lesson turns the same habits outward, onto the claims other people make about them, including the claims you will meet next week.

It's also the lesson to revise when the world moves. Everything in this course that can go stale is here on purpose, so that a reader in three years can read lessons 2 to 10 and know that the argument still holds.

## Three questions for any claim

**Is it measurable, unmeasurable, or false?**

This is **Digital Literacy**'s move, from its lesson on security tools, applied to a new subject. That course taught it on VPN marketing. The sorting is the same and the material is different.

*Measurable* means somebody could run something that would come out one way or the other. "Reduces drafting time by a third on standard contracts" is measurable: you could measure it.

*Unmeasurable* means nothing in the sentence could fail. "Understands your documents" names no task, no comparison and no threshold, so no result contradicts it. That isn't the same as false, and treating the two as the same is the commonest error in reading this material.

*False* means it contradicts something established. These are rarer than people think, because most marketing avoids them.

**What is missing?** For a capability claim, the two things that are almost always absent are the **model** and the **date**. This course has carried both in every sentence that needed them, and the reason applies to other people's writing as much as to this one: a sentence about what "AI" can do, with no model and no date, is a sentence about nothing in particular, and it can't be checked, revisited, or fairly disputed.

**Is it a benchmark result or a demonstration?** A benchmark is a set of questions somebody chose, scored the same way for everybody. A demonstration is one thing working once, in front of you. Both are worth something and neither is the other, and most writing on this subject slides between them without noticing.

## Four claims, sorted

**"Scored 92% on the bar examination."** Measurable, and already measured. What it doesn't tell you is whether the system can do a lawyer's work, because the examination is a set of questions somebody chose and the work isn't. Notice the two-step: the claim is true and the inference people draw from it is a different claim entirely.

**"Cuts the time your team spends on reporting by up to 60%."** Measurable, and unsettled, and the weasel is "up to", which makes the sentence compatible with a 0% reduction. What would settle it: matched teams, a real reporting cycle, the time measured rather than reported. What you have instead is a number with no study attached.

**"Understands your business."** Unmeasurable. Nothing in it could come out the other way.

**"Our system does not hallucinate."** This is the interesting one, and it needs its own section.

## The one that is not a lie and is not true

Three commercial legal research products were sold to lawyers on the claim that retrieval had dealt with the problem. Their providers said their methods were "eliminating" or "avoid[ing]" hallucinations, or guaranteeing "hallucination-free" legal citations.[1]

Tested in 2024 in a preregistered evaluation, they hallucinated "between 17% and 33% of the time".[1]

**And yet the vendors weren't simply lying**, which is what makes this worth a section rather than a sentence. Retrieval really did reduce the rate against a general chatbot; the study says so in the same breath.[1] The direction of their claim was right.

What was wrong was the scope. "Eliminating" is a claim too broad to be true of any system of that kind, and it was written in the present tense about a product you could buy. **That's a different failure from a lie and it's much harder to see**, because every individual thing in the sentence is defensible and the sentence as a whole isn't.

**Digital Literacy** found exactly this in a different market. When somebody evaluated sixteen consumer VPN products, most of them weren't lying either; they were making claims too broad to be true of any product of that kind. Two industries, one failure, and the reason to notice the pattern is that it will be the shape of the next claim you meet as well.

:::predict You see: "Our model now matches expert physicians on diagnostic reasoning." Sort it, and name what is missing before you read on.
Measurable, and probably already measured, since that phrasing usually points at a benchmark.

Missing, in order of how much it matters. **Which model**, because the claim is about one and will be read as being about all of them. **When**, because both the model and the benchmark move. **Which physicians and on what**, since "expert" is doing enormous work and diagnostic reasoning on written cases is not diagnosis. And **what "matches" means**, which is a threshold somebody chose.

Then the step that is not about the claim at all: even taken at its best, this is a benchmark result, and lesson 6 is the reason that does not transfer. The benchmark is a set of written cases with known answers. A morning in a clinic isn't.

Notice that you can do all of that without knowing any medicine. That's what makes this a transferable skill rather than an expert one.
:::

## Why your own year of using it is not evidence

The hardest part of this lesson is the part about you.

You have now spent this course forming impressions. You have run exercises, noticed things, built a sense of what your system is good at. That sense feels like data and it isn't.

Lesson 1 gave you the measurement and it lands differently now. Sixteen experienced developers, working in repositories they knew well, predicted the tools would make them 24% faster. They were 19% slower. Afterwards, having been slowed, they still estimated a 20% speed-up.[2]

**Twice wrong, in the same direction, by people with every reason to know.**

Here is the mechanism, and you can test it against your own week. **The waiting is short and the output arrives complete.** That is the part you experience as the cost. The reading, the checking, the correcting, the second attempt and the bit you ended up writing yourself all happen afterwards and are filed under other headings. The felt duration of using one of these systems is the wait, and the wait is genuinely fast.

So what would be evidence? The same thing it always is. **Time something.** Do a task the usual way and one like it the other way, and write down the minutes including the checking. A sample of two isn't a study, and it's enormously better than an impression, because it counts the parts your impression drops.

## Five things people believe about claims

**"The benchmark score means it can do the job."** A benchmark is questions somebody chose. Lesson 6 is about why that doesn't transfer to work.

**"It worked in the demonstration, so it will work for me."** A demonstration is a single run of a chosen case by somebody who chose it. Lesson 3 explains why a single run tells you little even when nobody is choosing.

**"The newest model is better at everything than the last one."** Better on average on chosen measures, which is what "better" usually means in these announcements. Whether it's better on your task is a thing to find out, and the frontier from lesson 6 is the reason it may not be.

**"I have used it a lot, so I know what it is good at."** The METR gap.[2] Use is exposure rather than measurement, and this is the belief this lesson exists to disturb.

**"The research is out of date, so there is no point reading it."** Capability figures do go stale fast. **Findings about people don't.** What happened to 758 consultants, sixteen developers and a thousand students are dated facts that stay true as history, and they are what this whole course is built on.

## Practice

:::exercise Sort one you actually met
Take 20 minutes.

1. Find a claim about these systems from the last week. A product page, a news article, a colleague, a post, a slide in a meeting. Write it down word for word.

2. **Sort it.** Measurable, unmeasurable, or false.

3. If measurable: what exactly would settle it? Write the study you would want, in two sentences. Who, doing what, compared with what.

4. Name what is missing. Model, date, task, comparison, threshold. "Not stated" is the usual answer, and that is the finding.

5. Then the question the whole course has been building to: **what would have to be true for this to apply to your work?** Use lesson 6's sorting to answer it.

You did a cold version of this in lesson 1. Dig that out and compare the two. The difference is what eleven lessons bought you.
:::

:::exercise Write the baseline again, from memory
Take 25 minutes. This is the last exercise in the course and it has a particular shape, so do it in this order.

1. **Do not look at your old list.** From memory, write two columns again: three tasks you would hand to one of these systems, three you would not, with a reason beside each.

2. Now, still without looking, mark each reason with which lesson it came from.

3. **Only now** get out the original from lesson 1, and the revised copy you made in lesson 6.

4. Put all three side by side and answer three questions in writing. Which entries moved, and when? Which reasons changed from a feeling to a mechanism? And is there anything in the original list you would now defend that you could not defend then?

The order matters and it isn't a formality. Retrieving before you look is what makes the comparison teach you something, which is **How to Learn Anything**'s argument used on this course's own material, at the end of it.
:::

## Connections

**Back.** This lesson is the whole course turned outward. Lesson 1's studies come back as claims to be read rather than results to be learned. Lesson 3's single draw is why a demonstration proves little. Lesson 6 is why a benchmark does not transfer. Lesson 7's measurement of the legal tools is the worked case in the middle of this lesson. And **Digital Literacy**'s sorting of security claims is the move itself, borrowed and pointed at a new market.

**Forward.** Out of the course. What you have isn't a list of what these systems can do, which would have been out of date by the time you finished reading it. It is a mechanism, a way of sorting tasks, a procedure for checking, a decision about when to stop, and a way of reading a claim. Those were chosen because they survive the models changing, and the test of whether that was the right choice is whether this course is still useful to you in three years.
