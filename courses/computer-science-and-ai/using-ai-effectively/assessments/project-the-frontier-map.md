Eleven lessons have given you an account of what these systems are doing when they answer, a way of sorting tasks by whether the answer has to match something outside the request, a procedure for checking an answer you could not have produced, and a reason to be careful about your own impression of whether any of it helped. This project is the first time you use all of it at once, on your own work.

The deliverable is one file: plain text or Markdown, roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words, in six sections, with the transcripts to back them.

The file being plain text is not an arbitrary rule. It's **Digital Literacy**'s reason used again: producing it exercises an earlier course, and when you finish you are holding a document you made rather than a score somebody gave you. Nobody else can write it, because every section is about your work and your own predictions.

Budget about twelve hours over two or three weeks. Most of that is section 3, and those trials are spread out on purpose, because six tasks run in one afternoon is six tasks chosen to be run in one afternoon.

## The thing that makes this project work

Every other course project in this institute can tell you when you are wrong. This one can't, because the course can't predict what your system will say to your question, and two readers doing the same exercise properly will get different answers.

So **no section below asks you for an answer.** Every section asks for a prediction, then the outcome, then the gap between them.

The gap is the deliverable. That is what makes this assessable without a key, and it is also the only form of this work that teaches you anything, for the reason lesson 11 gave: your sense of what these systems are good at is not evidence, and a prediction written down before the run is the cheapest thing that is.

## Before you start

Open a plain text file and keep it open. Call it `frontier.md`, and put it where you can find it.

Three habits, all from the course.

**Write the prediction first, in the file, before you send the request.** Not in your head. A prediction you didn't write down will quietly become whatever happened.

**Paste what actually happened.** Including the runs that went badly, and the ones where you sent a bad request and knew it. A frontier map with no surprises in it is a map somebody drew afterwards.

**Date and name everything.** Which system, which day. Lesson 2's whole argument for why is that a capability observation with no model and no date is an observation about nothing, and that applies to your own notes as much as to a vendor's homepage.

## 1. The baseline, rewritten

Lesson 1 asked you for two columns: three tasks you would hand to one of these systems, three you would not, with a reason beside each. Lesson 6 asked for it back, and lesson 11 asked you to write it again from memory.

Put all of those in front of you now, and write the current version.

Then, for every entry that moved between the first version and this one, write one line: what moved, and **which lesson moved it.** If an entry has not moved at all, say that too, and say whether the reason behind it has changed even though the entry has not. That happens more often than moving does, and it's the more interesting result.

Roughly 250 words.

## 2. The mechanism, in your own words

Close the course. Write one paragraph on what the system is doing when it answers you. Your own words, without looking at lesson 2.

Then write a second paragraph naming **one thing you believed at the start of this course that your first paragraph rules out.** Not a thing you believed in general. A specific belief you had, about what it was doing or what it could do, that the mechanism makes impossible.

Then, and only then, go back and read lesson 2's account. If your paragraph is missing something load-bearing, add a third paragraph saying what, and why you think you left it out.

Roughly 300 words.

## 3. Six trials

This is the long section and it is the heart of the project.

Pick six tasks from your own work. Not exercises, not toy questions. Six things you would have had to do anyway.

They must include **at least two you expect to be reliable, at least two you expect not to be, and at least one you genuinely cannot call.** If you can call all six, you've picked six tasks that are too much like each other, and the honest thing is to go and find a harder sixth.

For each trial, the file gets four things, in this order.

1. **The request you sent**, pasted, in full. Lesson 4's three things are worth checking against it: context, constraints, form. If you left one out, say so; that's a finding too.
2. **Your prediction, written before you sent it.** Reliable or not, and one sentence on why. The why must be a reason from the mechanism, not a memory of last time.
3. **What came back.** Pasted, or a fair summary with the relevant part pasted.
4. **Which side of the frontier it turned out to be on, and why.** This is lesson 6's sorting, applied by you, with the reason stated in terms of what the answer had to match.

Somewhere in these six, do lesson 3's thing at least once: **run the same request twice in fresh conversations and paste both.** Say what the variation told you and what it did not.

Roughly 700 words, and it will want to be longer. Cut the prose rather than the transcripts.

## 4. One job, split

Take one real piece of work, bigger than a single request, and divide it.

Which parts of it sit on the side of the frontier where these systems are reliable, and which parts do not? Say why for each, in the terms lesson 6 gave you rather than in terms of how hard the parts feel.

Then the part people skip: **what would you do about the join?** Where the reliable part hands over to the unreliable part is where the errors live, because that's where something produced by one process gets used by another without anybody checking the handover.

If you did lesson 6's second exercise properly, this section is most of the way written.

Roughly 300 words.

## 5. One answer, checked four ways

Take an answer you could not have produced yourself, on something that matters enough to check.

**Write the checking budget first**, before you start, as lesson 8 asks: what the work is for, what being wrong would cost, how much checking that has earned. One sentence.

Then run all four steps, and for each one record two things: **what it caught, and what it cost you in minutes.**

Finish with three lines. Did you keep to the budget? Which step earned its time and which did not? And did step four find something none of the first three could have found, which is the question the whole procedure is built around?

Roughly 350 words.

## 6. One task you have stopped, and one you have redesigned

Two parts, and the second matters as much as the first.

**The task you have stopped.** One task you have decided not to hand over. What it is, why the doing of it is the point, what you will do instead, and **what you expect that to cost you.** The cost is not optional. A decision with no cost on the other side isn't a decision, and lesson 9 is explicit that declining is a trade rather than a virtue.

**The task you have kept and redesigned.** One task you still use a system for, changed so that you still do the part that builds the judgement. Say what the old shape was, what the new shape is, and what you are now doing yourself that you were not doing before.

Roughly 300 words.

## The rubric

This is what a marker looks for, and it's worth reading before you start rather than after.

| Criterion | What full marks look like |
|---|---|
| Predictions are written first | Every trial has a prediction recorded before the outcome, and at least one of them is wrong |
| The reason comes from the mechanism | Each verdict is explained by what the system is doing, and not by what happened the last time |
| The transcripts are real | Requests and answers are pasted from your own use, including the ones that went badly |
| The split is honest | Section 4 finds a genuine join rather than assigning the whole job to one side |
| Checking is budgeted | Section 5 sets the budget from what the work is for, and reports the cost as well as the catches |
| Something was given up | Section 6 names a real task that has stopped, and not a hypothetical one |
| Dated and named | Every observation about what a system did carries the system and the date it was tried |
| Honest about the gap | Where a prediction was wrong, the file says so and says what the mistake was |

**Read the first row and the last row together.** A project whose predictions were all correct is a project whose predictions were written afterwards. The rubric says that out loud because it's the only failure mode this project has that looks like success, and because a course that spent eleven lessons on the difference between an impression and a measurement would be embarrassing itself to mark the work any other way.

## What you are left holding

Not a list of what these systems can do. That'd be out of date before you finished the project, which is why no section asked you to produce one.

What you are left holding is a map of one frontier, dated, with your own work on it, and a record of the six places your predictions were wrong. The map goes stale. **The habit of drawing it does not**, and the next time the models move you will know exactly which six trials to run again.
