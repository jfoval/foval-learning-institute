Everything in this course has been arithmetic done on other people. Priya, Elias, Dana, Ravi. This is the same arithmetic done on you, and it fits on one page.

That is the whole brief: **one page, your own numbers, six sections.** Not a spreadsheet, not a system, not something you maintain forever. A single sheet you could hand to somebody who needed to understand your finances in two minutes, except that nobody is going to see it.

**Two things before you start.**

**This is yours and it stays yours.** Do not submit it anywhere, do not post it, and do not send it to us. The institute does not collect it and there is nothing to upload. A financial plan is a list of everything somebody would need to take your money, and the only safe place for it is with you. If it lives on a computer, treat it like the documents you would not want copied.

**This is education, not personalised advice.** The framework is general; the judgements are yours. Where a section asks for a decision, the point is that you can state the reason for it, not that the reason matches ours.

## What the page has on it

Six sections. Each one is the exercise from the lesson that taught it, so if you did the exercises as you went, most of this is assembly rather than new work. If you didn't, each section names its lesson and you can go back.

**1. Where the money went last month.** One real month, categorised, from your own statements. Fixed and variable separated. Every irregular bill annualised and divided by twelve, so the insurance that lands twice a year appears as a monthly number rather than as a surprise. *(Lesson 1.)*

**2. Next month's budget, with every dollar assigned.** Built from section 1 rather than from a template, and built to survive a bad month rather than an average one if your income moves. The savings line goes in as a line, not as whatever is left. *(Lesson 2.)*

**3. The cushion: a number and a date.** How much, in dollars, and the date you will have it. "Three to six months" is not a number and "soon" is not a date. If the number is far away, write the first milestone instead and the date for that. *(Lesson 2, and the sizing evidence in it.)*

**4. The debts, in the order you will pay them, with the reason.** Every debt, its balance, its rate, and the order. Then one sentence saying why that order: either the arithmetic, or the evidence on finishing, or something true about you. Any of those is a reason. "It's what people say" is not. *(Lesson 3.)*

**5. The retirement line.** What percentage of pay you contribute, what your employer's match formula actually is, whether you are capturing all of it, and what the money is invested in, including the expense ratio. If you are not capturing the whole match, either the plan says when you will be, or it says in one sentence why not. Declining a match on purpose is a defensible decision. Not knowing there was one is not a decision. *(Lesson 7, and lesson 5 for what is inside the wrapper.)*

**6. The one large decision on the horizon, with its arithmetic.** A house, a car, a move, a course, a child, leaving a job. Whichever is actually coming. Show the numbers, including at least one cost that does not appear on any statement: the forgone return on money tied up, the transaction cost on the way out, the insurance nobody quoted you. *(Lessons 8 and 9 for the method.)*

## How to tell whether it is finished

Seven checks. Go through them honestly, because nobody else will.

1. **The numbers are yours.** Not illustrative, not rounded from memory, not from an example. This one is worth more than the other six put together, because a plan built from plausible numbers is a piece of creative writing.
2. **Irregular costs are annualised**, not omitted. Look specifically for anything that arrives once or twice a year.
3. **The payoff order has a stated reason**, and the reason matches something true about you rather than about a hypothetical person.
4. **The employer match is captured, or declined in writing with a reason.**
5. **The cushion has a number and a date.**
6. **The large decision shows its arithmetic**, including at least one cost that never appears on a statement.
7. **Nothing on the page contradicts anything else on it.** The savings line, the debt payments and the budget total add up to take-home pay. This is the check that fails most often, and finding the contradiction is the most valuable ten minutes in the project.

## What to expect while doing it

Three things happen to almost everybody, and they are the project working rather than going wrong.

**Section 1 takes longer than you think and is the one that changes your mind.** Categorising a real month from real statements is dull for forty minutes and then produces one number you did not expect. That number is the reason this section is first.

**Sections 2 and 7 will contradict each other on the first pass.** The budget assigns money you have already assigned to a debt, or the savings line and the payoff schedule both claim the same $200. That contradiction was in your finances before you wrote it down; the page is just where it becomes visible.

**Section 5 usually contains a fact you did not know**, because most people have never read their own plan documents. Sometimes it is a match you were not capturing. Sometimes it is money that has been sitting in cash for years.

## Afterwards

Put a date six months out in your calendar with the words "re-read the page". Most of it will still be right, one section will be badly out of date, and the section that is out of date will be the one that tells you what changed in your life.

If you do nothing else after this course, do sections 3 and 5. The cushion and the match are the two places where a single afternoon's work has a measurable effect on a decade.
