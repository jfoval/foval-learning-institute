  # where the money goes
  - q: >-
      Dana's take-home pay is $3,400 a month and she cannot work out where it goes. Her fixed
      costs come to $2,050. She pays car insurance of $720 twice a year and a professional
      membership of $480 annually, both from whatever is in the account when they land. What is
      the first thing wrong with her picture?
    options:
      - >-
          Her fixed costs are too high a share of take-home pay at 60%
      - >-
          She has no savings line, which should come before any of this
      - >-
          She treats $1,920 of annual bills as if they were surprises
      - >-
          Her variable spending is unmeasured, so no budget can be built
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Two insurance payments and a membership come to $1,920 a year, or $160 a month, and they
      are as certain as rent. Left out of the monthly picture they arrive three times a year as
      shocks and get paid from whatever is lying around, which is exactly how a month that
      balanced on paper ends in the red. Annualise and divide. Option D is also true and comes
      second: you cannot budget what you have not measured, but the irregular bills are the
      specific defect here and they are fixable this afternoon. Option A judges a ratio against
      a rule of thumb that lesson 1 spent its time warning against. Option B reverses the order;
      a savings line built on top of an inaccurate picture is a wish.
  # reading a money statistic
  - q: >-
      A headline says 40% of Americans could not cover a $400 emergency expense. The underlying
      survey asked how people would pay for it, and counted anyone who said they would use a
      credit card and carry the balance. Transaction data from bank accounts puts the share who
      genuinely could not cover it far lower. What is the right use of this?
    options:
      - >-
          Say what each measured: one asked how, the other measured whether
      - >-
          Repeat the 40%, since a survey of households is the better evidence
      - >-
          Discard both, since the two sources disagree and neither can be trusted
      - >-
          Report that most people can cover it, so the concern is overstated
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The survey and the transaction data are not two attempts at the same measurement. One
      asked how people would choose to pay, and someone who would reach for a card is not the
      same as someone who cannot cover it; the other looked at what was in the accounts. Both
      are informative, they answer different questions, and the famous figure has been repeated
      for years as though it answered the second. Option B takes the more alarming number
      because it is more familiar. Option C is the shrug that leaves you with nothing. Option D
      swings to the other extreme and drops the fact that for some households the answer really
      is no.
  # irregular income
  - q: >-
      Ben's income swings between $2,200 and $4,600 a month because he is paid by the job. He
      has tried three budgets and abandoned all of them in a low month. What should the budget
      be built on?
    options:
      - >-
          His average month, since over a year the highs and lows cancel out
      - >-
          A high month, so that the plan is never a constraint on his spending
      - >-
          A low month, with the surplus in good months assigned deliberately
      - >-
          Nothing fixed, since irregular income makes budgeting impractical
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      A budget built on the average fails every month below the average, which for Ben is
      roughly half of them, and a plan that breaks half the time gets abandoned. Building on a
      low month means the plan holds in the worst case, and the surplus in a good month is then
      a decision rather than a windfall: it goes to the cushion, or to a debt, or into a buffer
      that funds the next thin month. Option A is the most common mistake with irregular income
      and the reason his three previous attempts died. Option B inverts the problem. Option D
      gives up on the tool that helps most where income is least predictable.
  # automating a plan
  - q: >-
      A behavioural study found that automatic escalation of retirement contributions raised
      average saving rates from 3.5% to 13.6% over about four years, while asking people to save
      more did much less. What does that suggest about how to set up a savings plan?
    options:
      - >-
          Set a target you find motivating and review it against progress each month
      - >-
          Save whatever is left at the end of the month, once spending is known
      - >-
          Educate yourself thoroughly first, since knowledge is what changes behaviour
      - >-
          Move the decision once, into a default, rather than repeating it monthly
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The finding is about mechanism rather than motivation: the saving happened because the
      default did it, not because anyone decided harder each month. That points at making the
      decision once and building it into a transfer that runs whether or not you feel like it.
      Option B is the arrangement that reliably saves the least, because what is left at the end
      of the month is usually nothing. Option A is not wrong and is weaker than a default, since
      it relies on the same monthly decision the evidence says fails. Option C is contradicted
      twice in this course: telling people they are losing an employer match raised
      contributions by an amount that could not be told from zero.
  # secured and unsecured
  - q: >-
      What is the difference between secured and unsecured debt, and why does it matter to
      somebody deciding what to pay first?
    options:
      - >-
          Secured debt is backed by an asset the lender can take if you stop paying
      - >-
          Secured debt has a fixed rate, so its cost is predictable and lower
      - >-
          Secured debt is issued by a bank rather than by a retailer or card company
      - >-
          Secured debt is reported to the credit bureaus and unsecured debt is not
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      A secured debt is attached to something: the mortgage to the house, the car loan to the
      car. That collateral is why the rate is usually lower, and it is also why the consequence
      of not paying is losing the thing rather than a collections process. It changes the
      ordering because the worst outcome differs, not only the rate. Option B names a
      consequence and calls it the definition. Options C and D describe things that are
      sometimes true and are not what the distinction is.
  # payoff order
  - q: >-
      Ravi has three debts: $2,000 at 22%, $6,000 at 18% and $900 at 24%. He has $400 a month
      spare beyond the minimums. He has abandoned two previous payoff attempts. What should he
      weigh?
    options:
      - >-
          The avalanche is the only defensible order, since the arithmetic is not in doubt
      - >-
          The snowball is the better order because motivation is what actually matters
      - >-
          The order barely matters at these rates, so he should pick either and start
      - >-
          The avalanche saves the most interest; the snowball completes more often
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Both facts are established and they point different ways for him. Highest rate first
      minimises interest paid and nobody disputes the arithmetic. Smallest balance first is
      associated with people finishing, and the evidence for that is real too, which matters a
      great deal to somebody who has abandoned two attempts. The honest answer names both and
      lets him choose on what he knows about himself, and here his own history is the strongest
      piece of evidence available. Option A treats a behavioural question as settled by
      arithmetic. Option B treats it as settled the other way. Option C is closer to true than
      it looks at these rates and still ducks the question he is actually asking.
  # the balance myth
  - q: >-
      A friend tells you he carries a small balance on his credit card every month to build his
      credit score. What is wrong, and what does it cost him?
    options:
      - >-
          Nothing is wrong, though the interest makes it an expensive way to do it
      - >-
          The score responds to utilisation on the statement, not to interest paid
      - >-
          Carrying a balance is reported as a missed payment, which lowers the score
      - >-
          Nothing is wrong, but the effect is small compared with payment history
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      What is reported is the balance on the statement and the payment history, and paying in
      full after the statement date does not remove the reported utilisation. Interest paid is
      reported nowhere and buys him nothing at all. This is the most repeated myth in consumer
      credit and the one that costs the most money for the least benefit. Options A and D both
      accept the premise and haggle over the price, which is the trap: the practice is not
      merely inefficient, it is entirely ineffective at the thing he thinks it does. Option C
      invents a mechanism; carrying a balance is not a missed payment.
  # compound interest (Big Three)
  - q: >-
      Suppose you had $100 in a savings account and the interest rate was 2% a year. After five
      years, how much do you think you would have in the account if you left the money to grow?
    options:
      - >-
          Exactly $102, since the interest is paid once at the end of the term
      - >-
          Exactly $110, since 2% of $100 is $2 in each of the five years
      - >-
          Less than $102, once account fees are taken from the balance
      - >-
          More than $110, because each year's interest earns interest too
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      This is one of the three questions Lusardi and Mitchell use to measure financial literacy
      worldwide, worded as they word it, and only about a third of adults answer all three
      correctly. Five years at 2% on $100 gives $110.41. Simple interest alone would give $110,
      and the extra 41 cents is the interest earning interest, which is small here and is the
      whole mechanism that makes forty years look the way lesson 4 showed. Option B is the
      linear answer and the most common wrong one, and it is close enough to be worth noticing
      that the error is in the method rather than in the size. Option A treats the account as
      paying once. Option C imports a fee the question does not mention.
  # inflation (Big Three)
  - q: >-
      Imagine the interest rate on your savings account is 1% a year and inflation is 2% a year.
      After one year, how much would you be able to buy with the money in this account?
    options:
      - >-
          More than today, because the balance is larger than it was
      - >-
          Exactly the same, since the interest and inflation broadly cancel
      - >-
          Less than today, because prices rose faster than the balance
      - >-
          It depends entirely on what you choose to buy with it
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The second of the Big Three, and the one that separates people who think in nominal terms
      from people who think in real ones. The balance grows 1% and prices grow 2%, so what the
      money buys falls by about 1%. Option A is the nominal illusion in its purest form: the
      number on the statement is larger and buys less. Option B has the arithmetic wrong by a
      factor. Option D sounds careful and is a way of not answering; the general price level is
      what inflation measures.
  # starting early
  - q: >-
      Ana can save $250 a month. She is 27 and wondering whether to start now or wait until 32,
      when she expects to earn more. Assuming the same return either way, what is the honest
      description of the difference?
    options:
      - >-
          Large: the earliest contributions have the most time to compound
      - >-
          Small, because five years is short next to the decades that follow
      - >-
          Zero, provided she increases the amount to make up the missed years
      - >-
          Unknowable, since nobody can say what returns will be over that period
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Every dollar's contribution to the final balance is set by how long it compounds, so the
      first years' contributions do the heaviest lifting and they are the ones a delay removes.
      Waiting five years does not cost five years of contributions; it costs the five most
      productive ones. Option C is the reasonable-sounding fix that does not work, because the
      later dollars have less time and cannot be made equivalent by being larger without being
      much larger. Option D is true of the exact number and is used here to avoid the point,
      which holds whatever the return is.
  # diversification (Big Three)
  - q: >-
      True or false: buying a single company stock usually provides a safer return than a stock
      mutual fund.
    options:
      - >-
          True, because you can research one company far more thoroughly
      - >-
          False, because a fund spreads the same money across many companies
      - >-
          True, because a fund's fees reduce the return you actually receive
      - >-
          It depends on the company, since a large stable one is safer than a fund
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The third of the Big Three, and the one most adults get wrong. Safety here means
      variability of outcome, and one company can fail entirely while a fund holding hundreds
      cannot. Research does not remove that: it may improve your estimate and it cannot
      diversify a single holding. Option C names a real cost and answers a different question,
      since fees affect expected return rather than the spread of outcomes. Option D is the
      interesting distractor, because a large stable company is genuinely less volatile than a
      small one and is still one company, which is the point.
  # what costs do
  - q: >-
      Two funds hold nearly identical baskets of shares. One charges 0.05% a year and the other
      1.05%. Over forty years, on the same gross returns, what does the difference do?
    options:
      - >-
          Costs about 1% of the final balance, which is the annual difference
      - >-
          Costs about 40% of the final balance, being 1% for each of forty years
      - >-
          Costs roughly a quarter of the balance, because the fee compounds too
      - >-
          Costs little, since a good manager earns the higher fee back in performance
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      A fee is not a slice off the end; it is taken every year, and each dollar taken also stops
      compounding for all the years that remain. On a 7% gross return over forty years, a one
      point difference removes roughly a quarter of the final balance. This is the single most
      useful piece of arithmetic in the investing lesson because it is the one variable an
      investor controls completely. Option A treats the fee as a one-off. Option B adds the
      percentages linearly and lands close to the right size by the wrong route, which is worth
      noticing. Option D is the claim the evidence on active management is least kind to.
  # persistence
  - q: >-
      A colleague shows you a fund that beat its benchmark in each of the last three years and
      says the record speaks for itself. What does the evidence on fund performance actually say
      about that record?
    options:
      - >-
          It is meaningful, since three consecutive years is unlikely by chance
      - >-
          It says little, because past winners have not reliably repeated
      - >-
          It is meaningless, because no active manager has ever beaten a benchmark
      - >-
          It is meaningful only if the fund also has low fees and broad holdings
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The persistence question has been measured directly and repeatedly: funds that outperform
      in one period do not reliably outperform in the next, which is exactly what you would
      expect if a good deal of short-run outperformance is variation rather than skill. Three
      years of winning is also less unlikely than it feels when thousands of funds are trying.
      Option C overstates the case into something false, and the honest version of the argument
      never says no manager outperforms; it says the average investor after costs does not, and
      you cannot identify the exceptions in advance. Option A treats a small sample as evidence.
      Option D adds sensible criteria that do not repair the inference.
  # two rates
  - q: >-
      What is the difference between a marginal tax rate and an effective tax rate, and which
      one do you use to work out what a raise is worth?
    options:
      - >-
          Marginal is the rate before deductions; effective is the rate after them
      - >-
          Marginal is the federal rate; effective adds state and payroll taxes to it
      - >-
          They are two names for the same figure, calculated from different sides
      - >-
          Marginal is the rate on your next dollar; effective is the whole share
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Both are real and they answer different questions. The marginal rate is what the next
      dollar is taxed at, which is the one you need for a raise, an overtime shift or a
      retirement contribution. The effective rate is total tax divided by total income, which is
      what you have paid overall and is always lower than the marginal rate once you have
      crossed a bracket. Option A describes deductions rather than the distinction. Option B
      describes a broader tax picture, which is a third thing. Option C is the confusion the
      question exists to remove.
  # brackets
  - q: >-
      Nina earns $58,000 and is offered a promotion to $70,000. She has heard that the extra
      money will push her into a higher bracket and that she could end up worse off. What is the
      accurate reply?
    options:
      - >-
          Brackets tax slices, so only the part above the line pays the higher rate
      - >-
          She is right to check, since crossing a bracket can reduce take-home pay
      - >-
          She is wrong, and no arrangement of any kind can make extra income cost her
      - >-
          She should ask for the raise as a bonus instead, which is taxed differently
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      A bracket applies to the slice of income inside it, not to the whole. Only the portion
      above the threshold is taxed at the higher rate, so a raise can never leave her with less
      after income tax. Option C states the right conclusion about brackets and then overreaches
      into something false, because benefit cliffs are real and a threshold that switches a
      benefit off can genuinely leave someone worse off; that is a different mechanism and it is
      worth knowing. Option B concedes the myth. Option D invents a difference; a bonus is taxed
      as income, though it may be withheld at a different rate, which is not the same thing.
  # deduction against credit
  - q: >-
      Two people each qualify for something worth $2,000. For one it is a deduction and for the
      other a credit. Both have taxable income and owe more than $2,000 in tax. Who does better
      and why?
    options:
      - >-
          The one with the deduction, since it reduces income before the tax is worked out
      - >-
          The one with the credit, since it reduces the tax bill by the full amount
      - >-
          Neither, since both reduce what is owed by the same $2,000 in the end
      - >-
          It depends on their marginal rates, which decide the value of both
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      A deduction comes off income, so it is worth your marginal rate: at 22%, a $2,000
      deduction saves $440. A credit comes off the tax itself, so a $2,000 credit saves $2,000,
      provided you owe at least that much. The credit wins for anybody, and it wins by more the
      lower your rate. Option D is half right in a way worth naming: marginal rates decide the
      value of the deduction and have nothing to do with the value of the credit. Option A has
      the two mechanisms swapped. Option C is the assumption the news relies on when it reports
      both as tax breaks.
  # what a wrapper is
  - q: >-
      What does a 401(k) or an IRA actually do to the money inside it?
    options:
      - >-
          It invests the money automatically in a diversified portfolio of assets
      - >-
          It guarantees the balance against loss up to a federally insured limit
      - >-
          It changes when the money is taxed; what it holds is a separate choice
      - >-
          It locks the money until retirement age with no exceptions of any kind
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      An account of this kind is a wrapper: a set of tax rules that applies to whatever is
      inside it. What is inside is chosen separately, and it can be a fund, several funds, or
      cash sitting there for a decade. Option A is the error that costs the most money in
      practice, because someone who believes it may never look at what their contributions
      actually bought. Option B confuses a retirement account with a deposit account. Option D
      is nearly the opposite error, and exceptions do exist, though they should not be planned
      around.
  # traditional against Roth
  - q: >-
      Nadia's marginal rate is 22% now. She puts $1,000 into a traditional account, the money
      quadruples, and she withdraws it at a marginal rate of 22%. Her colleague puts the
      equivalent into a Roth on the same numbers. What happens?
    options:
      - >-
          The traditional wins, since the untaxed amount compounds on a larger base
      - >-
          The Roth wins, since money that comes out untaxed is worth more at the end
      - >-
          The answer depends on the growth rate, which is unknown in this example
      - >-
          They come out identical: growth and tax multiply in either order
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Traditional: $1,000 grows to $4,000 and is taxed at 22%, leaving $3,120. Roth: the tax is
      paid first, so $780 goes in, grows to $3,120 and comes out whole. The two are identical,
      because multiplying by four and by 0.78 gives the same product in either order. Option A
      is the intuition that more money is compounding, which is true and does not matter, since
      the extra money compounding is the tax's share and it grows too. Option C looks careful
      and is not: the growth rate cancels. What breaks the tie in real life is the difference
      between the two rates, which this question deliberately held equal.
  # the ordering question
  - q: >-
      Elias earns $50,000. His employer matches 50 cents per dollar on the first 6% of pay. He
      contributes nothing, carries $6,000 on a card at 24% APR, and has found $3,000 a year he
      can redirect. He also has $300 in savings. What ordering does this course defend, and on
      what grounds?
    options:
      - >-
          Card first, then the match, since 24% is the highest rate he faces
      - >-
          Match first, then the card, since the match expires and the debt waits
      - >-
          Split the money evenly, so that both problems shrink at the same time
      - >-
          Cushion to $2,000 first, then the card, and retirement once he is debt free
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      This is the cross-lesson question the course builds to, and the reasoning matters more
      than the order. A small cushion comes first, and then the match, because $3,000
      contributed captures $1,500 that was not his before, while $3,000 against the card saves
      about $720 of interest in a year. The decisive point is not the size but the asymmetry: he
      can pay the card next year and it costs him more interest, and nobody lets him make last
      year's contribution. Option A applies lesson 3's rule for ranking debts to something that
      is not a debt. Option D is the standard ordering and it is the expensive part of the
      mistake, since it leaves $1,500 a year on the table for as long as the payoff takes.
      Option C avoids the question. Note that the ordering is a judgement about failure modes
      rather than a theorem, and for someone facing possible default it changes.
  # the number to look at first
  - q: >-
      Two health plans. Plan A costs $250 a month with a $1,000 deductible and a $4,000 out-of-
      pocket maximum. Plan B costs $150 a month with a $3,500 deductible and a $7,000 maximum.
      Both pay 20% coinsurance after the deductible. Which number should you look at first, and
      why?
    options:
      - >-
          The out-of-pocket maximum, since it bounds how bad the year can be
      - >-
          The premium, since it is the only cost you are certain to pay
      - >-
          The deductible, since it is what you pay before the plan pays anything
      - >-
          The coinsurance rate, since it applies to the largest share of the costs
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Insurance is bought for the bad year, and the out-of-pocket maximum is the number that
      says how bad the bad year can get: $7,000 plus premiums under B, $4,000 plus premiums
      under A. It is the figure almost nobody quotes when comparing plans and the one that
      decides what the policy is actually for. The premium and the deductible matter for the
      ordinary year and they do not bound the disaster. Option D names a rate that stops
      applying once the maximum is reached, which is precisely why the maximum is the number to
      find.
  # the deductible and the cushion
  - q: >-
      Someone with $300 in savings chooses the plan with the lower premium and the $3,500
      deductible, reasoning that it costs less. When is that reasoning wrong, and why?
    options:
      - >-
          Always, since a high deductible is worse coverage at any level of savings
      - >-
          Never, since the lower premium is a certain saving and the deductible is not
      - >-
          When they cannot produce the deductible, which turns it into card debt
      - >-
          When they are in poor health, since the plan is priced for healthy people
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The comparison between two plans assumes the person can pay whatever the plan asks at the
      moment it asks. With $300 in savings they cannot, so the deductible does not come out of
      savings; it goes on a card at credit-card rates, and the premium saving is eaten inside
      two years. The right deductible is a function of the cushion rather than of the plan.
      Option B states the argument for the cheaper plan, which is real and incomplete, since the
      possibility is the entire reason for holding a policy. Option A overstates: for somebody
      with a funded cushion the high deductible is a reasonable bet with a known worst case.
      Option D names a genuine consideration that is not the one that decides this.
  # the right comparison
  - q: >-
      A house costs $400,000 and the mortgage payment would be $1,919 a month. An equivalent
      rental is $2,200. Which comparison is the right one, and roughly what does it show?
    options:
      - >-
          Payment against rent, which shows buying cheaper by $281 a month
      - >-
          Payment plus maintenance against rent, which is roughly a wash
      - >-
          Total cost of ownership against rent, which usually favours buying sooner
      - >-
          Unrecoverable costs against rent, which can reverse the answer entirely
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The payment is what the bank charges, not what owning costs, and the part of it that is
      principal comes back to you. The comparison that means something is rent against
      everything you spend and never get back: interest, property tax, insurance, maintenance,
      and the return forgone on the money tied up in the house. In the course's worked example
      that turns a payment $281 a month cheaper than rent into a cost $734 a month higher.
      Option B adds one of the missing lines and stops. Option A is the comparison almost
      everyone makes. Option C uses the right phrase and draws the wrong conclusion from it.
  # the assumption that decides it
  - q: >-
      You run a rent-versus-buy calculation and get a break-even of seven years. Which single
      assumption is most likely to move that answer by ten years or more, and what follows?
    options:
      - >-
          The appreciation rate, so run the sum at several guesses
      - >-
          The mortgage rate, so shop several lenders before you commit to one
      - >-
          The property tax rate, so look up your own county's actual figure
      - >-
          The maintenance budget, so use a figure from the last three years
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      All four are real inputs and three of them are worth checking. Only one moves the answer
      by more than a decade: in the course's worked case, appreciation at 1% instead of 3%
      pushed the break-even from about seven years to nineteen, and 5% pulled it down to three.
      It is also the only one of the four you cannot look up, negotiate or shop for, because it
      has not happened yet. So the instruction attached to it is different in kind: run the
      calculation pessimistic, middling and optimistic, and see whether your intended stay
      clears the break-even in all three. If it only clears in the optimistic one, you are
      making a forecast rather than a calculation.
  # the request that settles it
  - q: >-
      A caller who knows your address and the last four digits of your card, and whose number
      displays as your bank's, says there is fraud on your account and you should move your
      balance to a safe account today without discussing it with branch staff. Which feature
      settles it, and why?
    options:
      - >-
          The knowledge of your details, since a real bank would verify differently
      - >-
          The instruction to move money and to tell nobody, which no bank gives
      - >-
          The displayed number, since caller ID can be set to show any number
      - >-
          The same-day deadline, since a manufactured deadline signals a scam
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Three of the four are real signals that require a judgement call. Details are cheap now,
      caller ID is display text, and deadlines are sometimes genuine, so each of those leaves
      you weighing probabilities on a phone call while somebody applies pressure. The request
      settles it without any weighing: a bank that suspects fraud blocks the card, no
      institution asks you to move your money to a safe account, and no legitimate caller asks
      you to conceal the conversation from the institution's own staff. That combination has no
      honest version, which is what makes it a fact rather than a judgement.

Twenty-four questions across all ten lessons, deliberately not grouped by lesson. Most of them describe a situation and leave you to work out which idea it calls for, because that is the thing the course was for. The pass mark is 70%.

Three of the questions are not ours. They are the questions Lusardi and Mitchell have used for two decades to measure financial literacy across dozens of countries, in their own wording, on compound interest, inflation and diversification.
