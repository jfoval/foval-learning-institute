## Lesson 10, "Defending it" — Stage 4, Tier A, 2026-09-09

Drafted from the scams research added to `SOURCES.md` the same day. **Tier A**, per the outline's note
that this is low sensitivity even though crime is a 3.4 domain, with the reviewer given two specific
instructions: check the age framing against both halves of the FTC data, and check for anything that
would make a reader who has been scammed feel judged.

The draft came back stronger than the run's average on the two things that usually fail. The quiz was
unshapeable on the first attempt (pick-the-longest scored 0 of 6, all four indices used, no adjacent
repeat, every option-letter reference correct), and `minutes` was honest for the first time in this
course, which is the exercise-cue-word fix from lesson 9 already paying off.

### What sent it back

**10.1 A chart built from numbers that do not exist.** The reversibility ladder was drawn as six bars
of 320, 250, 150, 70, 45 and 20 pixels against a labelled axis, which invites a reader to conclude
that a debit card is 78% as recoverable as a credit card. Nothing supports those magnitudes; the FTC
publishes a qualitative ladder. Worse, it gave the payment app a fixed middling value while the prose
two paragraphs later says it "depends entirely on that app's own policy", so **the chart contradicted
its own lesson**, which is defect 3. **Replaced with a ranked table carrying the FTC's own words per
row**, and a line saying in terms that it is a ranking rather than a measurement.

**And the swap that fixes both halves of the problem.** The lesson had four real, sourced,
chart-shaped numbers it was not drawing (median loss by age band) and one invented chart it was.
Those are now the other way round: the age bands are the chart, which is the right place for one,
because that section is the lesson's neutrality core and the two-halves point lands instantly in a
picture.

**10.2 The one fact that most directly defends older adults was gathered at Stage 1 and dropped.**
SOURCES.md carried it: older adults are much more likely than people aged 18 to 59 to report a scam
they spotted and avoided losing anything to. The lesson kept the two loss figures and left out the
one about competence, in a section whose stated purpose is killing the myth that older people are
gullible. **That is motivated selection even when every retained sentence is true**, and it is the
same defect as lesson 8's finding 8.5 in a single paragraph rather than across a section. Restored,
and the summary sentence now carries all three.

**10.3 Two-year-old statistics presented undated inside a paragraph of current ones.** The 34% figure
and the four median-loss bands are 2021 data; the lesson opened with 2024 figures and then ran
straight into them with no year. Dated, and the newer 2025 figure for people over 80 added.

### The two traps, both present and both small

- **A quotation trimmed of its qualifier.** The lesson quoted a credit freeze as meaning "nobody can
  open a new credit account in your name". The FTC's sentence ends ", including you", which is the
  operationally useful half. Restored inside the quotation marks.
- **A position stated in its opponents' words.** "Standard advice says: if they know your details,
  they're probably genuine" is advice nobody publishes in that form; it was a straw built by the
  person knocking it down, under a heading that advertised the win. It is now attributed where it
  actually lives, which is folk intuition, and the heading is descriptive.

A third: a paraphrase from the research file had been printed inside quotation marks as though it
were the FTC's words. Unquoted.

### Also applied

- **The debit-card mechanism was the wrong one.** The lesson said the money leaves your account first,
  which is true and secondary. The mechanism is the liability clock: $50 if you report within two
  business days, $500 within 60, and everything after that if you go past 60 days. Added, with a CFPB
  citation, and it was a research gap as much as a drafting one.
- **A live policy question stated as settled.** "Most consumer protection stops" at authorised
  payments is true of US practice today and is contested policy, and the UK required reimbursement for
  most authorised push payment fraud from October 2024. The lesson now says so and takes no side.
- Five flat assertions given sources or softened, including the load-bearing one in the worked call
  ("banks do not move your money to a safe account").
- A fourth objective for the age material, which had a section and a quiz item and no objective, and
  the quiz item rebuilt from a restatement of the body's own sentence into a case about a relative who
  has one of the two figures right.
- Pulling a credit report added to the practice, since the objective promised it and only the freeze
  was practised.
- Three superlatives about the lesson's own value, two "Here's" openers, and four one-line morals cut.
- `course.yaml` updated in the same pass: `estimated_hours` from 6.5 to 11.8, which is what the ten
  lessons actually sum to, and outcomes added for insurance, housing and fraud, which stopped at
  lesson 7.
