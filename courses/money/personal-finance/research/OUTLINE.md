# Outline: Personal Finance Fundamentals

*Stage 2, written 2026-09-06, after Stage 1 research and after all five live lessons had been
through Stage 4 and been rewritten from `research/SOURCES.md`. This is a re-outline, not a first
outline: lessons 1 to 5 exist and are at standard, so this document records what they cover, in
one line each, and then plans the five lessons that close the scope gaps SOURCES.md logged
(taxes, retirement-account machinery, insurance, housing, scams and fraud) plus the assessments
that standard 4.4 requires once a course passes six lessons.*

## The shape of the course, and why

The existing five lessons form one arc: **money in your hands**. You measure it (1), plan it (2),
stop it leaking to interest (3), understand it growing (4), and put it somewhere sensible (5).
That arc is complete and coherent, and the reviews rewrote it to standard. Nothing in it is
renumbered or resequenced by this outline.

The five new lessons form a second arc: **the systems around the money**. Between your gross pay
and your spending sits a tax system (6). Between your savings and your retirement sits a set of
accounts with rules (7). Between your plan and a bad year sits insurance (8). The largest single
decision most people make is housing (9). And everything built across nine lessons can be taken
in an afternoon by someone who calls you on the phone (10).

The order inside the second arc is forced in two places and free elsewhere. Taxes must come before
accounts, because the Roth-versus-traditional choice is a bet about your own marginal rate now
versus later, and that sentence is meaningless to someone who does not know what a marginal rate
is. Scams goes last because it defends everything the other nine lessons built, and because it is
the lesson people are most likely to send to a relative on its own; ending there leaves the course
with a piece that travels.

Insurance before housing is a choice rather than a constraint: the rent-versus-buy arithmetic has
to price homeowner's insurance, property tax and maintenance, and a reader who has just spent a
lesson on how risk transfer is priced will not treat those as small print.

**US specificity.** Lessons 6, 7 and parts of 8 and 10 describe machinery that is US-specific.
Each of them says so in its first hundred words, names the equivalent question a reader elsewhere
should ask their own system, and keeps the transferable principle in the foreground. This follows
the note in SOURCES.md under "Open concerns about scope" and applies the same treatment lesson 3
already gives credit scores and lesson 5 gives account types.

**No figures with expiry dates.** Contribution limits, bracket thresholds, standard deduction
amounts and phase-outs change every year, and a stale number is worse than no number. Lessons 6
and 7 teach the mechanism with clearly invented round numbers, label them as illustrative, and
link IRS.gov for the current year's figures. Where a real current figure is unavoidable, the
sentence carries its year.

## Lesson sequence

### Lessons 1 to 5 (live, at standard, unchanged by this outline)

1. **Where your money actually goes** (60 min). The audit; fixed versus variable; reading a money
   statistic for what it measures ($400 factoid, 50/30/20).
2. **A budget that survives real life** (50 min). Building the budget from the audit; four methods
   matched to four people; irregular income; the savings line.
3. **How debt really works** (55 min). What a carried balance costs; the minimum-payment
   arithmetic; secured versus unsecured; avalanche versus snowball with both evidence bases;
   credit scores and the carrying-a-balance myth.
4. **Compounding: interest working for you** (40 min). Growth proportional to balance; rule of 72;
   real versus nominal; matching money to timescales; costs compound too.
5. **Investing without the noise** (45 min). Shares, bonds, funds; diversification as the free
   lunch; SPIVA and persistence; Sharpe's arithmetic; what an expense ratio costs over decades.

### 6. What you actually keep: how income tax works (~50 min)

- **Objectives:**
  - Calculate the tax on a given income using marginal brackets, and explain why a raise into a
    higher bracket never reduces take-home pay
  - Distinguish marginal rate, effective rate, and withholding, and identify which one answers
    which practical question
  - Explain what a refund is, and compare the cost of over-withholding against the cost of
    under-withholding
- **Core ideas:** Gross pay is not your money. A bracket taxes a slice of income, not the whole of
  it. Your effective rate is always lower than your marginal rate. Withholding is an estimate made
  in advance by a formula on a form you filled in, so a refund is a return of your own money and a
  bill is not a penalty. Deductions reduce taxable income (worth your marginal rate); credits
  reduce tax owed (worth their face value); the difference is the single most useful piece of tax
  literacy a general reader can carry. Payroll taxes are flat, come off the top, and are invisible
  to bracket arithmetic, which is why a low earner's total tax burden is not what the bracket table
  suggests.
- **Mechanism to explain:** the stacked-slice model. Income is poured into brackets from the
  bottom; each slice is taxed at its own rate; only the top slice moves when you get a raise. This
  is drawn, not just stated: a stacked bar of one income with each slice labelled with its rate and
  its dollars, with the effective rate marked as a line across it.
- **Worked examples:**
  (a) *Simple:* a single filer on an illustrative $60,000 walked slice by slice, ending with the
  effective rate, then given a $5,000 raise so the reader can see exactly which dollars are taxed
  at the higher rate and how much of the raise survives.
  (b) *With a wrinkle:* the same person with a $1,000 deduction versus a $1,000 credit, side by
  side, showing why they are not worth the same; then the case that actually can make a raise cost
  money, which is not a bracket at all but a benefit cliff or a phase-out, named honestly as the
  real version of the myth.
- **Misconceptions to address:** "a raise pushed me into a higher bracket so I take home less"
  (false as stated about brackets; true only via cliffs and phase-outs, which is why the myth
  survives); "a big refund means I did well" (it means an interest-free loan to the government);
  "deductions and credits are the same thing"; "my tax rate is 22%" (conflating marginal with
  effective).
- **Practice:** compute your own effective rate from last year's return or a paystub; take the
  bracket table and calculate tax on three incomes without a calculator app; find the withholding
  line on your own paystub and multiply it out to a year.
- **Sources:** IRS.gov (brackets, withholding estimator, the deduction/credit distinction);
  Kapoor part 1 for the field's own treatment; Khan Academy's Taxes & Tax Forms unit as the linked
  go-deeper for filing mechanics this lesson deliberately does not cover.
- **Connects:** builds on lesson 1, which used "take-home pay" as a given and now says where the
  gap came from; leads to lesson 7, where the marginal rate becomes the input to the Roth decision.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's fixed-versus-variable distinction (payroll tax as the most fixed
  cost there is); lesson 4's real-versus-nominal habit (bracket thresholds are inflation-indexed,
  which is why they move every year).
- **Neutrality note:** taxation is a 3.4 domain. This lesson teaches the machinery and reaches no
  verdict on rates, fairness, or size of government. Where the machinery embodies a policy
  argument (progressivity, the payroll cap, the mortgage interest deduction), the lesson names
  that the argument exists, states it in the terms each side uses, and moves on. Mandatory
  neutrality audit.

### 7. Where the money lives: accounts, employers, and the tax-timing choice (~50 min)

- **Objectives:**
  - Compare a taxable account, a tax-deferred account, and a Roth account by what each one taxes
    and when
  - Calculate the return on an employer match and explain why it outranks nearly every other use
    of a dollar
  - Apply a rule for choosing among cushion, match, high-rate debt, and retirement contributions,
    and defend the order chosen
- **Core ideas:** An account is a wrapper, not an investment; the same index fund behaves
  differently in three wrappers because of when it is taxed. Traditional defers tax to withdrawal
  (bet: my rate will be lower then); Roth pays tax now (bet: my rate will be higher, or I value the
  certainty); nobody knows their future rate, which is an argument for holding some of each rather
  than for agonising. An employer match is an immediate return of 50% or 100% on the matched
  dollars, which no investment offers, subject to vesting. Liquidity is the axis people forget:
  retirement wrappers trade access for tax treatment, which is exactly why lesson 2's cushion sits
  outside them.
- **Mechanism to explain:** the three-wrapper diagram. One dollar earned, followed through each
  wrapper to withdrawal, with the tax event marked in each path. Then the match: a bar showing a
  contributed dollar next to the matched dollar next to what a year of market return looks like on
  the same scale, so the reader sees the match dwarf the return.
- **Worked examples:**
  (a) *Simple:* someone earning an illustrative salary contributing to a 401(k) up to a 50% match,
  showing what leaving the match on the table costs over ten years.
  (b) *With a wrinkle:* the same person with 24% APR card debt and no cushion, working the real
  ordering question: cushion to a floor, then match, then the card, then back to retirement, with
  the reasoning shown and the honest note that the ordering is a judgement about failure modes,
  not a theorem. Includes the vesting case where the match is not yet the reader's money.
- **Misconceptions to address:** "a 401(k) is an investment" (it is a wrapper; what is inside it is
  the investment, and it is possible to have a 401(k) sitting in cash for a decade); "Roth is
  better" / "traditional is better" as universal claims; "I'll do retirement later, the cushion
  first, always" (the match is the exception and the arithmetic says so); "I can't touch it until
  I'm 59 and a half, so it's locked forever" (name that exceptions exist, do not teach them as
  strategy, and never suggest tapping retirement money for unsecured debt, per SOURCES.md's
  safety note).
- **Practice:** find your own employer's match terms and vesting schedule and write them in one
  sentence; compute what your current contribution captures and what it leaves; look up what your
  retirement money is actually invested in, including its expense ratio, using lesson 5's method.
- **Sources:** IRS.gov for account rules and current-year limits (linked, not quoted); Kapoor
  ch. 18; Investor.gov; FINRA's fund-fee analyzer; Bogleheads wiki on account placement as the
  practitioner go-deeper.
- **Connects:** builds on lesson 6 (marginal rate) and lesson 5 (what goes inside the wrapper) and
  answers the question lesson 3 explicitly deferred; leads to lesson 8, which is the other half of
  protecting a plan.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 5's expense ratios (applied now inside a 401(k) menu, where the
  reader has less choice); lesson 3's ordering question, now answered with a fourth option on the
  table; lesson 4's compounding, applied to matched dollars.
- **Neutrality note:** retirement policy is 3.4-adjacent. Teach the machinery as it is; where the
  design is contested (the shift from defined benefit to defined contribution, whether the system
  serves low earners well), name the disagreement and its main positions without resolving it.

### 8. Insurance: paying a little to hand off a lot (~45 min)

- **Objectives:**
  - Explain risk pooling and why an insurer's expected profit does not make buying insurance
    irrational for the insured
  - Identify which risks a given person should insure and which they should carry, using the
    can-I-absorb-this test
  - Compare two policies by premium, deductible, and out-of-pocket maximum, and calculate the
    total cost of each in a good year and a bad year
- **Core ideas:** Insurance is not an investment and is not supposed to pay off on average; you
  buy it to convert an unbearable variance into a bearable fixed cost. That is why the rule is
  *insure the catastrophe, not the inconvenience*, and why extended warranties on a $400 phone are
  the clearest teaching case of getting it backwards. The vocabulary is small and does almost all
  the work: premium, deductible, out-of-pocket maximum, coverage limit, exclusion. Term life
  insures a specific obligation for a specific period; the reason to know the difference between
  term and whole is that one is priced as insurance and the other bundles insurance with a savings
  product, which the reader can now evaluate with lesson 5's cost lens.
- **Mechanism to explain:** pooling. Ten thousand people each face a small chance of a large loss;
  the pool converts a lottery into an average plus a margin. Drawn as two distributions side by
  side: the individual's loss distribution (mostly zero, rare catastrophe) and the same person's
  distribution after insuring (a flat premium, small tail).
- **Worked examples:**
  (a) *Simple:* two health plans, one high-premium/low-deductible and one the reverse, costed out
  in a year with no claims and a year with a hospital stay, ending at the out-of-pocket maximum in
  both, so the reader sees where each plan wins.
  (b) *With a wrinkle:* the same reader deciding on the extended warranty and on a low deductible
  for a car they could replace from savings; then the case where the answer flips, which is a
  reader whose cushion is $300 and for whom a $1,000 deductible is not a cheaper policy but an
  uninsured risk. The wrinkle is that the right deductible depends on lesson 2's cushion.
- **Misconceptions to address:** "insurance is a rip-off because they profit"; "I've paid in for
  years and never claimed, so it was wasted"; "a low deductible is better coverage"; "life
  insurance is for everyone" (it is for people whose income someone else depends on, which is a
  real answer rather than a sales answer); "health insurance covers what I think it covers"
  (exclusions and networks are where the money is).
- **Practice:** list every risk you currently carry uninsured and mark which ones would end you;
  find your own deductible and out-of-pocket maximum without calling anyone; price the same car
  with two deductibles and calculate the break-even number of claim-free years.
- **Sources:** Kapoor part 4 (insuring); Garman & Forgue's checklists; CFPB's insurance pages;
  the state insurance regulator pages for the reader's own jurisdiction, linked as the thing to
  actually check.
- **Connects:** builds on lesson 2's cushion (which is self-insurance for small risks, and the
  reason the deductible question has a personal answer) and lesson 4's habit of putting a number
  on things; leads to lesson 9, where insurance is a line item in the cost of owning.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 2's cushion sizing; lesson 1's fixed-versus-variable (premiums are
  fixed, claims are not); lesson 4's expected-value reasoning.
- **Neutrality note:** health insurance touches health policy, a 3.4 domain. The lesson teaches how
  a policy works and how to read one; it does not argue about health systems. Where a system-level
  claim is unavoidable, attribute it and give the main positions. Mandatory neutrality audit.

### 9. Renting versus buying, and other large decisions (~50 min)

- **Objectives:**
  - Build the full cost of owning for a specific home, including the costs that do not appear on a
    mortgage statement
  - Compare that against rent for an equivalent home and calculate the break-even holding period
  - Identify the non-financial factors that override the arithmetic, and explain why the answer is
    situational rather than universal
- **Core ideas:** "Renting is throwing money away" compares a rent cheque to a mortgage payment,
  which is the wrong comparison; the right one is rent against the *consumption* part of owning
  (interest, property tax, insurance, maintenance, transaction costs, and the forgone return on the
  down payment), with only principal counting as saving. Transaction costs are large and are paid
  twice, which is what creates a break-even horizon; mobility risk is real and is not a soft
  factor. Price-to-rent is the market-level signal, with the honest caveat that economists dispute
  how much information it carries. This is the course's set-piece on frameworks: the lesson does
  not answer the question, it hands the reader the arithmetic that answers it for their case.
- **Mechanism to explain:** the two-column cost sheet, then the break-even curve. A chart of
  cumulative cost of owning versus cumulative cost of renting over years, crossing somewhere, with
  the crossing point moved by three sliders described in the text: transaction costs, the
  price-to-rent ratio, and the return on the down payment if invested instead.
- **Worked examples:**
  (a) *Simple:* one home and one equivalent rental, both fully costed for one year, showing the
  gap between "the mortgage is cheaper than rent" and the real comparison.
  (b) *With a wrinkle:* the same numbers held for three years versus ten, so the reader sees the
  answer flip on holding period alone; plus the case of a reader with an unstable job in a
  high-price-to-rent city, where the arithmetic and the mobility risk point the same way for
  different reasons.
- **Misconceptions to address:** "renting is throwing money away"; "buying is always an
  investment" (a home is consumption plus a leveraged, undiversified, illiquid asset, which is a
  fair description rather than a criticism); "the mortgage payment is the cost of owning"; "you
  need 20% down" (name what the actual consequence of less is, which is insurance and rate, not
  refusal); "prices always go up".
- **Practice:** cost a specific home you could actually buy against a specific rental you could
  actually take, using the lesson's sheet; compute the price-to-rent ratio where you live from two
  real listings; write the one non-financial factor that would override your own arithmetic.
- **Sources:** Kapoor ch. 9 (housing); NBER w31013 and the Erdmann critique for the price-to-rent
  caveat; CFPB's Buying a House tools; the SOURCES.md contested-questions entry, which this lesson
  implements almost directly.
- **Connects:** builds on lesson 3 (secured debt, amortisation, what interest does early in a
  loan), lesson 4 (forgone return on the down payment is an opportunity cost with a compounding
  size) and lesson 8 (homeowner's insurance as a real line); leads to lesson 10 only loosely, and
  to the project, where this framework is the largest single component.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 3's secured-versus-unsecured distinction; lesson 4's rule of 72
  applied to the down payment; lesson 1's discipline about what a statistic measures, applied to
  house price indices.
- **Neutrality note:** housing policy is a 3.4 domain and housing affordability is a live political
  argument. The lesson stays on the household decision, states the contested points as contested
  (does price-to-rent carry information; do house prices reliably outrun inflation), and takes no
  position on zoning, subsidy, or the mortgage interest deduction beyond naming that the argument
  exists. Mandatory neutrality audit.

### 10. Defending it: scams, fraud, and the accounts in your name (~45 min)

- **Objectives:**
  - Identify the three features nearly every scam shares, and apply them to a message the reader
    has actually received
  - Explain why the payment method a stranger asks for is the strongest single signal, and rank
    common payment rails by reversibility
  - Apply the concrete defences: freezing credit, checking reports at the one free federal source,
    and what to do in the first hour after a compromise
- **Core ideas:** Scams are not a test of intelligence; they are engineered to defeat ordinary
  judgement by manufacturing urgency, isolating the target from anyone who would say "wait", and
  routing the money down a rail that cannot be reversed. Learn the shape and you do not need to
  learn every variant, which is fortunate because the variants change monthly. Irreversibility is
  the tell: gift cards, wire transfers, crypto, and peer-to-peer payment apps are chosen by
  criminals for the same reason banks warn about them. A credit freeze is free, reversible, and
  the single highest-value defensive action most people have never taken. AnnualCreditReport.com is
  the one federally mandated free source and the imitators are named as imitators.
- **Mechanism to explain:** the con's structure, drawn as three gates the scammer must pass
  (contact, pressure, payment) with the reader's counter-move at each gate. Plus a
  reversibility ladder: a ranked chart of payment methods from "reversible within months" to "gone
  the moment you press send".
- **Worked examples:**
  (a) *Simple:* a real-shaped tech-support or bank-impersonation call taken apart line by line,
  with the urgency and isolation moves marked as they happen.
  (b) *With a wrinkle:* a scam that passes the usual filters because the caller has real
  information about the target (from a breach) and the number is spoofed, which is where "would a
  real bank do this?" beats "do they know my details?"; plus the romance or investment scam that
  runs for months and so has no urgency at all until the end, so the reader learns the shape has
  variants rather than a checklist.
- **Misconceptions to address:** "smart people don't fall for scams"; "if it's my bank's real
  number on the screen it's my bank" (spoofing); "the money is insured so I can get it back" (rail
  dependent, and often not); "checking my credit report hurts my score" (carried over from lesson
  3 and finished here); "I'd know if my identity was stolen".
- **Practice:** freeze your credit at all three bureaus and time how long it takes; pull one free
  report and read it for accounts you do not recognise; write the sentence you will use to end a
  pressured call, and give one relative the same sentence.
- **Sources:** FTC consumer advice and IdentityTheft.gov; CFPB fraud pages; AnnualCreditReport.com;
  FTC Consumer Sentinel data for what is actually reported, so the lesson's picture of the threat
  comes from counts rather than anecdote; Khan Academy's Scams & Fraud unit as go-deeper.
- **Connects:** builds on lesson 3 (credit reports and scores, now used defensively) and on
  everything else, since this is what protects it; closes the course.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 3's credit-report machinery; lesson 1's calibrated reading of a
  statistic, applied to scam-loss headlines; lesson 5's "if it sounds like a guaranteed return,
  price it against what the market actually returns".
- **Neutrality note:** low sensitivity, but crime is a 3.4 domain. Report who is targeted using
  the FTC's own reported-loss data rather than characterising groups; avoid any framing that reads
  as blaming victims, which is also the empirically accurate stance.

## Assessments

Ten lessons, so 4.4 requires a course-end test and at least one project. Both live in
`courses/money/personal-finance/assessments/`.

### Course-end test blueprint

Twenty-four questions, drawing on every lesson, weighted toward the objectives that carry the most
practical consequence rather than evenly across lessons. Pass mark 70% per 4.3. Interleaved
deliberately: questions are not grouped by lesson, so the learner has to identify which idea a
situation calls for, which is the transfer skill the course is actually for.

| Source | Items | Recall | Application |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1 where money goes | 2 | 0 | 2 |
| L2 budget | 2 | 0 | 2 |
| L3 debt | 3 | 1 | 2 |
| L4 compounding | 3 | 1 | 2 |
| L5 investing | 3 | 1 | 2 |
| L6 taxes | 3 | 1 | 2 |
| L7 accounts and match | 3 | 1 | 2 |
| L8 insurance | 2 | 0 | 2 |
| L9 rent versus buy | 2 | 0 | 2 |
| L10 scams | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| **Total** | **24** | **5** | **19** |

Four of the twenty-four are **cross-lesson items** that cannot be answered from one lesson: the
ordering question (cushion / match / card / contribute), the deductible question that depends on
cushion size, the down payment that depends on opportunity cost, and one statistic-reading item
that uses a claim from a lesson the question does not name.

Three items are drawn from the **Lusardi and Mitchell "Big Three"** (compound interest, inflation,
diversification), worded as the researchers word them. They appear in the test unchanged so the
learner can compare their own answers to the roughly 30% of adults who get all three right, which
is both a real diagnostic and the most honest possible answer to "did this course work".

### Project: your own one-page financial plan

- **Deliverable:** a single page, written by the learner about their own situation, with six
  sections: where the money went last month; the budget for next month with every dollar assigned;
  the cushion target and the date it is reached; the debt list with a chosen payoff order and the
  reason for the choice; the retirement line, including whether the employer match is fully
  captured; and the one large decision on the horizon, with its arithmetic.
- **Steps:** each section is the practice exercise from the lesson that taught it, so a learner who
  did the exercises has already written most of the page. The project's work is assembling them
  into something coherent and noticing where they contradict each other, which they usually do.
- **Rubric** (each item present / partial / absent, with what "present" means):
  1. *Numbers are the learner's own*, not illustrative. A plan built from example numbers scores
     zero on this row and the row is worth more than any other.
  2. *Irregular costs are annualised* rather than omitted (lesson 1's wrinkle).
  3. *The payoff order has a stated reason* that names either the arithmetic or the behavioural
     evidence, and that reason matches something true about the learner.
  4. *The employer match is either captured or explicitly declined with a reason.*
  5. *The cushion has a number and a date*, not an aspiration.
  6. *The large decision shows its arithmetic*, including at least one cost that does not appear on
     a statement.
  7. *No line contradicts another*: the savings line, the debt payments and the budget total add up
     to take-home pay.
- **Privacy note in the brief:** the learner is told, in the assessment text, not to submit or post
  this anywhere, and that it is theirs. The institute does not collect it. This matters more here
  than in any other course's project.
- **A note on advice:** the project brief repeats the 2.5 line. The plan is the learner's own work
  and the course is education, not personalised advice.

## Neutrality notes for the course

Personal finance sits inside economics, which 3.4 names, and lessons 6, 8 and 9 touch tax policy,
health policy and housing policy respectively. Each of those three gets a mandatory neutrality
audit before it publishes. The recurring risks, and the standing decisions:

- **Guru material.** Ramsey's programme is the most-followed plan in America and is treated per
  SOURCES.md: presented fairly, in its own terms, with its criticisms in theirs. Lesson 3 already
  does this for the snowball; lesson 7 does it again for the "debt first, always" ordering, where
  the employer match is the strongest counter-argument and gets stated as one rather than as a
  refutation of a person.
- **Active versus passive** is the one place standard 3.1 says not to false-balance, and lesson 5
  already handles it with the honest scope statement. Lesson 7 does not reopen it.
- **Tax and housing policy arguments** are named as existing, stated in each side's own terms in a
  sentence, and left unresolved. The course teaches the household's decision, not the polity's.
- **Class framing.** The largest bias risk in a personal finance course is writing to a reader who
  has slack. Lesson 1 already refuses to treat a structural shortfall as a discipline problem;
  lessons 7 and 8 carry the same discipline, since "just capture the match" and "raise your
  deductible" both assume money that some readers do not have. Where advice presupposes slack, the
  lesson says so.

## How `course.yaml` tracks this outline

`course.yaml` describes the course a learner can take **today**, not the course this outline
plans. So its `outcomes` list and `estimated_hours` were corrected in the same commit as this
file to match the five live lessons (5 outcomes, 4.2 hours, and the renting-versus-buying promise
removed until lesson 9 actually delivers it, which is what SOURCES.md's scope note demanded).
Each new lesson adds its own outcome and its minutes to the estimate in the commit that publishes
it. At ten lessons the file should read roughly nine outcomes and about 8 hours plus assessments.

## Risks and things that will be hard to do well

- **Lesson 6 without dated numbers.** Teaching brackets while refusing to print this year's
  bracket table is the hardest constraint in this outline. The plan is illustrative round numbers,
  clearly labelled, plus a live link. If the drafting session finds the lesson goes vague, the
  fallback is one real table with its year stamped in the caption and in the text, which is
  honest, but it dates the lesson and commits someone to updating it.
- **Lesson 9's chart** needs real inputs to be worth drawing, and house prices, rents and rates
  vary enormously. The chart should be explicitly a worked case with its assumptions printed on
  it, not a general claim about renting versus buying.
- **Lesson 10 ages fastest.** Scam variants change monthly. Writing it around the three-gate
  structure rather than a catalogue of current scams is the mitigation, and the lesson should say
  outright that the catalogue changes and the shape does not.
- **Course length.** Ten lessons at these durations is about 8 hours of lesson time plus the test
  and project. That is a real course, and it is roughly four times what `course.yaml` currently
  claims. The estimate is being corrected in the same commit as this outline.
- **The second arc is US-heavy.** Lessons 6 and 7 are almost entirely US machinery. That is
  unavoidable for the topic and is handled by labelling, but it means a reader in another country
  gets less from lessons 6 to 7 than from 1 to 5. Say so at the top of each, rather than hiding it.
