# Sources: Personal Finance Fundamentals

*Stage 1 research, written 2026-09-06, after the course was already live with five placeholder lessons. This file is the working document the Stage 4 reviews and any redrafts run against. For each source: what it is good for and what it establishes. The course is US-centred where the mechanics are US-specific (credit scores, tax-advantaged accounts) and should say so in the text; the principles (compounding, spending less than you earn, diversification) are universal.*

## Canonical textbooks

- **Kapoor, Dlabay & Hughes, *Personal Finance* (McGraw Hill, 2025 release; 13th ed. 2019 widely assigned).** The most commonly assigned US college text for this course, used at Iowa State and many others. Best for: the field's own map of what a complete treatment covers. Its six-part structure is the reference skeleton: (1) Planning (financial statements, budgeting, time value of money, taxes), (2) Managing (banking, consumer credit, cost of credit), (3) Purchasing (consumer strategy, housing), (4) Insuring (property, health, life), (5) Investing (fundamentals, stocks, bonds, mutual funds), (6) Controlling your future (retirement, estate). Chapters we draw on: 1 (time value of money), 3 (financial statements and budgeting), 6–7 (consumer credit and its cost), 9 (housing decision), 13 and 16 (investing fundamentals and mutual funds), 18 (starting retirement early). Our five-lesson course covers roughly its chapters 1, 3, 6–7, 13 and 16; the gaps (insurance, taxes, housing) are recorded under "Open concerns about scope".
- **Garman & Forgue, *Personal Finance* (Cengage, 15th ed., 2023).** The other standard assigned text (Ohio State's Jonathan Fox praises its activities and exercises). Best for: worked exercises and the practitioner's checklists; a second witness on what the field considers standard.
- **OpenStax, *Principles of Finance* (Rice University, free, CC BY-NC-SA).** Openly licensed and free to link, unlike the two above. Best for: time value of money chapters with real worked math the lessons can point to for readers who want the algebra. Not a personal-finance text (it is corporate-finance flavoured), so use it for the math, not the life advice.

## University syllabi and curricula consulted

- **Khan Academy, Financial Literacy course** (khanacademy.org/college-careers-more/financial-literacy). Free, self-paced, mastery-based. Units: Budgeting & Saving; Consumer Credit; Financial Goals; Loans & Debt; Insurance; Investments & Retirement; Scams & Fraud; Careers & Education; Taxes & Tax Forms. Notable choices: leads with budgeting, treats scams and fraud as a first-class unit (our course currently has nothing on fraud; see scope concerns), and keeps investing near the end. The best free full-course alternative online; we link to it rather than compete on breadth.
- **Foothill College BUSI 45, Fundamentals of Personal Finance** (course outline PDF, catalog.foothill.edu). A typical community-college treatment: goal setting, budgets, credit, taxes, insurance, investing, retirement. Confirms the Kapoor sequence is the consensus sequence.
- **CRR Boston College, "Personal Finance Instruction on U.S. College Campuses"** (crr.bc.edu). Survey of how the course is actually taught; notable finding that instructors are broadly dissatisfied with available textbooks, which supports building lessons from primary evidence rather than paraphrasing a text.

## Primary sources and key research

- **Lusardi & Mitchell, the "Big Three" financial literacy questions** (Lusardi 2019, *Swiss Journal of Economics and Statistics*, open access; NBER w31145 for the 2023 update in *JEP* 37(4)). Three questions on compound interest, inflation, and diversification; only about 30% of adults worldwide answer all three correctly, and about a third of young adults. Performance predicts retirement planning and saving. Establishes: what "the basics" measurably are, and that the three ideas our course centres on (compounding, real versus nominal, diversification) are the empirically identified core. The Big Three make an excellent diagnostic self-test for lesson 1 or the course project.
- **Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff & Urban, "Financial Education Affects Financial Knowledge and Downstream Behaviors"** (2022, *Journal of Financial Economics*; NBER w27057 free). Meta-analysis of 76 RCTs, 160,000+ participants: financial education has positive causal effects on knowledge and behaviour, effect sizes comparable to education interventions in other domains, robust to publication-bias correction, strongest for budgeting, saving and credit. Establishes: the course's own premise (teaching this works) is evidence-backed, and honestly, that behaviour effects are smaller than knowledge effects.
- **SPIVA Scorecards, S&P Dow Jones Indices** (spglobal.com/spdji/en/spiva, updated annually, free). The standard evidence on active-fund underperformance: 79% of active US large-cap funds underperformed the S&P 500 in 2025; over 15 years no category has a majority of active managers ahead; over 2005–2024, 94% of domestic funds underperformed the S&P 1500. The persistence scorecard shows winners don't repeat. Establishes: the empirical case behind the index-fund lesson, from the index provider's own methodology. Cite the scorecard, not summaries of it.
- **Bogle, *The Little Book of Common Sense Investing* (2007; 10th-anniversary ed. 2017).** The canonical popular statement of the cost-matters hypothesis: investors as a group earn the market return minus costs, so minimising costs is the only sure lever. Best for: the arithmetic-of-costs argument (Sharpe's "The Arithmetic of Active Management" is the two-page academic version, free on Stanford's site, and worth citing directly).
- **Kettle, Trudel, Blanchard & Häubl (2016), "Through the Eyes of the Debtor"** (often reported as the Kellogg/HBR account-closure research; see also Gal & McShane 2012, *Journal of Marketing Research*, on nearly 6,000 debt-settlement clients). Finding: people who concentrate payments and close individual accounts entirely are more likely to eliminate their whole debt; the sense of progress from killing a balance outright drives persistence. Establishes: the evidence base for the snowball-versus-avalanche treatment in the debt lesson (see contested questions).
- **JPMorgan Chase Institute, "Weathering Volatility" and the 2024 $400-shock report** (jpmorganchase.com/institute, free). Real bank-account data: income volatility is large for typical households; measured by actual transactions, 92% of households could cover a $400 shock (including 77% of the lowest income quartile), a very different picture from the survey soundbite. Establishes: the emergency-fund lesson can be built on measured behaviour, and the famous "40% can't cover $400" claim needs careful handling (it comes from a survey question about *how* people would pay, not whether they could).
- **Vanguard research notes on emergency savings** (corporate.vanguard.com/research, free PDFs: "In case of emergency, break glass" 2023; "Emergency savings protect retirement savings" 2025). Having even $2,000 set aside is associated with a 21-point difference in financial well-being; 3–6 months of expenses with a further 13%; those without liquid savings tap retirement accounts. Establishes: the case for the cushion, with honest correlational framing.
- **Thaler & Benartzi, "Save More Tomorrow"** (2004, *Journal of Political Economy* 112(S1); journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/380085). Field study of automatic saving escalation: average contribution rates rose from 3.5% to 13.6% over about four years. Establishes: the trial-grade evidence behind "automate the transfer" in the budgeting lesson; the honest mechanism (defaults and pre-commitment, not willpower).
- **Prelec & Simester, "Always Leave Home Without It"** (2001, *Marketing Letters* 12(1); link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1008196717017). Willingness to pay for identical goods was substantially higher, up to roughly double, with credit card versus cash. Establishes: the pain-of-paying mechanism behind envelope/cash budgeting, cited in the budgeting lesson.
- **CFPB, "Financial well-being: The goal of financial education"** (2015, files.consumerfinance.gov, free) **and the CFPB Financial Well-Being Scale** (2017 technical report). Defines the outcome the whole course aims at: control over day-to-day finances, capacity to absorb a shock, being on track for goals, and freedom to enjoy life. Establishes: a non-arbitrary definition of "winning" at personal finance that isn't a dollar figure.

## What the field considers essential

In the order the ideas build, drawing on Kapoor's structure, Khan Academy's units, and the Lusardi/Mitchell core:

1. **Cash flow awareness before everything**: you cannot manage what you don't measure; financial statements for a person (what comes in, what goes out, what you own, what you owe).
2. **A budget as a plan you can keep**, not a diet; the evidence-backed point is that tracking and planning behaviours respond to education (Kaiser et al.).
3. **The cushion**: liquid savings against income volatility, sized honestly (the $2,000 threshold effect and the 3–6 month convention, presented as a range with the evidence for each).
4. **Interest working against you**: APR, compounding of debt, why revolving credit-card debt is an emergency, the real cost of minimum payments; US credit scores and reports as machinery (utilisation, payment history, the carrying-a-balance myth).
5. **Interest working for you**: compound growth, the time-in-market arithmetic, real versus nominal (inflation is one of the Big Three).
6. **Diversification and costs**: single stocks versus funds (the third Big Three question), index funds and the SPIVA/Bogle/Sharpe evidence, expense ratios.
7. **Big decisions with frameworks**: rent versus buy (price-to-rent plus the non-financial factors), when to use which account type.
8. **Defence**: insurance as buying certainty, and scams/fraud recognition. (Both currently missing from the course.)

## Common misconceptions

- **"Carrying a credit-card balance builds your credit score."** False and expensive; utilisation reported on the statement matters, interest paid does not (CFPB, Experian). The single most-cited myth by credit educators.
- **"Checking my own credit hurts my score."** Soft pulls don't; the confusion is with hard inquiries.
- **"Income is part of your credit score."** It isn't in the FICO model; lenders consider it separately.
- **"Minimum payments keep me in good shape."** They avoid delinquency while maximising interest paid; the lesson should show the arithmetic of a real minimum-payment schedule.
- **"You need to pick stocks (or a hot fund) to invest."** The diversification question is the one most adults get wrong in the Big Three; SPIVA shows professionals mostly fail at picking too.
- **"Past fund performance tells you which fund will win next."** The SPIVA persistence scorecard is the direct evidence against.
- **"A budget means cutting everything fun."** The field's own framing (CFPB well-being definition) makes room-to-enjoy part of the goal; a budget that ignores it fails behaviourally.
- **"The '40% of Americans can't cover $400' factoid."** Mangles a survey about payment methods; JPMC Institute's transaction data put the true can't-cover share far lower. A good worked example in calibrated reading of statistics.
- **"Renting is throwing money away."** Ignores the full cost of owning (interest, tax, insurance, maintenance, transaction costs, mobility risk); see contested questions.

## Contested questions and the positions on each

- **Debt payoff order: avalanche versus snowball.** Position A (mathematical): pay highest APR first; strictly minimises total interest; any finance textbook. Position B (behavioural): pay smallest balance first; account closures sustain motivation and predict completing the payoff (Gal & McShane 2012; Kettle et al. 2016; HBR coverage). What would settle it: an RCT measuring completion rates and total interest under each assignment; the observational evidence favours "snowball completes more often, avalanche costs less when completed". Classification: contested empirical, present both with their evidence and let the learner choose by self-knowledge. Neither side disputes the arithmetic; the dispute is about which failure mode (paying more interest versus giving up) is more likely for a given person.
- **How big should the emergency fund be, and does the $1,000-starter approach (Ramsey) suffice?** Positions: Ramsey's Baby Steps ($1,000 starter, then debt, then 3–6 months) prioritise momentum; critics note $1,000 no longer covers common emergencies and that the 12% return assumption used in Ramsey projections is far above consensus expectations; Vanguard's threshold research supports "even $2,000 changes outcomes"; the 3–6 month convention has expert-consensus status but thin direct evidence for the specific range. Classification: contested practical guidance; teach the range, the evidence for the low threshold, and the reasoning for scaling with income volatility and dependants. Present Ramsey's programme fairly (it is the most-followed plan in America and its adherents' results are real) alongside the criticisms in their own terms.
- **Rent versus buy.** Not a settled answer but a settled framework: compare full cost of owning (mortgage interest, taxes, insurance, maintenance, transaction costs, forgone returns on the down payment) against rent for an equivalent home, adjusted for how long you'll stay and mobility risk; price-to-rent ratio as the market-level signal, with the caveat that economists dispute how much information the ratio carries (NBER w31013; Erdmann's critique). Classification: the framework is established; the answer is situational. Never teach "buying always wins".
- **Active versus passive investing.** Closer to settled than most: the SPIVA record and the cost arithmetic (Sharpe 1991) make low-cost indexing the evidence-backed default for long-horizon savers, and this is the rare case where standard 3.1 says don't false-balance. But state the honest scope: the claim is about the average investor after costs, not that no active manager ever outperforms, and factor/dimensional views (and the market-can't-be-100%-indexed argument) deserve their sentence.
- **Does financial education work?** Older reviews (Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer 2014) found tiny effects; Kaiser et al. 2022's RCT meta-analysis found meaningful positive effects, roughly three times earlier estimates. Present as: the evidence moved, here's why (better studies, more of them), and behaviour effects remain smaller than knowledge effects. A course teaching personal finance owes the reader honesty about its own limits.

## Safety-critical guidance (practical subjects)

- The standards' Part 2.5 note is mandatory: this is education, not personalised financial advice; the course already carries it in course.yaml's description and each lesson touching investment decisions should carry the once-per-course note.
- Never name specific funds, tickers, or providers as recommendations; teach the category (broad-market index fund, expense ratio under some threshold) and point to regulators' tools.
- Anything with tax or contribution limits must state its year or omit the number; stale limits are worse than none.
- Scams: the course currently teaches nothing about fraud, which is the highest-severity practical omission (older relatives and new earners are primary targets; Khan Academy gives it a whole unit). At minimum the debt or banking lesson should link IdentityTheft.gov and the FTC.
- Debt: always distinguish secured from unsecured, and never suggest tapping retirement accounts or home equity to pay unsecured debt without naming the risks (loss of protected status, foreclosure exposure).

## Taxes: research added 2026-09-06 for lesson 6

*This section was written after the Stage 4 neutrality audit of lesson 6 pointed out, correctly,
that the lesson was drafted for a 3.4-sensitive domain with no researched source base covering it.
Everything below was verified live on 2026-09-06.*

### Primary sources for the mechanics

- **IRS, "Federal income tax rates and brackets"** (irs.gov/filing/federal-income-tax-rates-and-brackets).
  The bracket table, and the IRS's own statement of the slice model: "when your income jumps to a
  higher tax bracket, you don't pay the higher rate on your entire income." Tax year 2025, single:
  10% to $11,925, 12% to $48,475, 22% to $103,350, 24% to $197,300, 32% to $250,525, 35% to
  $626,350, 37% above. Check this page every year rather than trusting any printed figure.
- **IRS Publication 501** (irs.gov/publications/p501). Standard deduction by filing status. Tax
  year 2025 single under 65 is **$15,750**, which is not the $15,000 originally announced in the
  autumn 2024 inflation adjustment: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed 4 July 2025, raised it
  mid-year. A lesson quoting the older figure would be wrong, which is the concrete case for the
  rule about never trusting a remembered tax number.
- **SSA, "Contribution and benefit base"** (ssa.gov/oact/cola/cbb.html). The single best citation
  for payroll tax: it gives the OASDI rate (6.2% employee and employer each, 12.4% for the
  self-employed), the Medicare rate (1.45% each, 2.9% self-employed, no cap since 1993), and the
  full table of wage bases from 1937. **2025: $176,100. 2026: $184,500.** Prefer this to IRS Topic
  751, which now shows only the current year and so silently stops supporting a citation for an
  earlier one.
- **IRS Topic 751** for the rates and the Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% on wages above $200,000
  (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly), which makes the Medicare component progressive at
  the top and is the standard omission in "payroll tax is regressive" summaries.
- **IRS Topic 306, "Penalty for underpayment of estimated tax"** (irs.gov/taxtopics/tc306), and the
  safe harbours: broadly, pay 90% of the current year's tax or 100% of the prior year's (110% at
  higher incomes) and no penalty applies. Any lesson that tells a reader to cut their withholding
  owes them this.
- **IRS, "Self-employment tax"** (irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employment-tax-social-security-and-medicare-taxes)
  for the 15.3% combined rate and the absence of withholding, which the employee-shaped account of
  payroll tax leaves out.
- **IRS, "Credits and deductions for individuals"**, plus the EITC and Child Tax Credit pages, for
  what refundable actually means. This is the distinction that decides whether a credit is worth
  anything to a household with little or no income tax liability.
- **HHS ASPE, poverty guidelines** (aspe.hhs.gov/topics/poverty-economic-mobility/poverty-guidelines).
  Needed for any statement about the 400% threshold. 2025 guideline for a household of one in the
  48 contiguous states is $15,650, so 400% is exactly $62,600, and marketplace eligibility for a
  coverage year uses the previous year's guidelines. Attribute the arithmetic here, not to KFF.

### Benefit cliffs and effective marginal rates, which is where the myth is true

- **CBO, "Effective Marginal Tax Rates for Low- and Moderate-Income Workers in 2016"**
  (cbo.gov/publication/50923, November 2015, free). Headline: **31% on average**, with federal
  income and payroll taxes the main contributors and benefit phase-outs (SNAP, ACA cost-sharing)
  adding the rest. Individual cases run far higher. This is the neutral, quantified statement of
  the thing people are describing when they say a raise cost them money, and it concerns low and
  moderate earners rather than the households near the ACA subsidy cliff. **A lesson that teaches
  cliffs using only the ACA example has picked the politically live case over the common one**;
  lead with this.
- **KFF, "What we know so far about 2026 ACA marketplace enrollment, premiums, and deductibles"**
  (kff.org). Supports: the ARPA 2021 enhancement (benchmark premium capped at 8.5% of income, no
  upper income limit), its extension through 2025, its expiry on 1 January 2026, the return of the
  pre-2021 400% FPL cutoff, and the size of the premium increases. It does **not** state the
  $62,600 figure; cite ASPE for that.

### The contested questions, and the positions on each

- **What do the highest earners actually pay?** Position A (Saez and Zucman, *JEP* 34(4) 2020 and
  *The Triumph of Injustice*): once unrealised gains and corporate tax are assigned to their
  owners, the US system is close to a flat tax and regressive at the very top, with top-end
  effective rates in the single digits. Position B (Auten and Splinter, *JPE* 2024,
  davidsplinter.com): the same tax data, distributed differently, gives average rates rising from
  about 12% for the bottom half to about 50% for the top 0.01%. Position C, the one both sides
  agree on because it measures something narrower: **IRS Statistics of Income**, summarised each
  year by the Tax Foundation, where the top 1% paid an average effective *federal income tax* rate
  of 26.3% in 2023, on 20.6% of the income, supplying 38.4% of all federal income tax. What would
  settle it: agreement on whether unrealised gains are income and how to allocate corporate tax and
  unreported income, which is a definitional question as much as an empirical one. Classification:
  **contested empirical, with a definitional core**. Never state any of the three answers in the
  author's voice.
- **Who bears the employer's half of payroll tax?** Mainstream economics and CBO's distributional
  methodology assume it falls on workers' wages in the long run. Attribute it; do not assert it
  flatly and do not hedge it into meaninglessness either.
- **Is a deduction that is worth more to a higher earner a subsidy?** Critics call it an
  upside-down subsidy: the higher your rate, the more the government pays toward the deducted
  expense. Defenders reply that a deduction defines what counts as income in the first place, so
  the larger saving simply mirrors the higher rate that dollar would otherwise have faced, and the
  progressivity lives in the rate schedule rather than in the deduction. Both describe the same
  arithmetic. Classification: **value question about a shared fact.** Also relevant to the fairness
  of the argument: since the 2017 act roughly 90% of filers take the standard deduction (about 91%
  in tax year 2022, against about 70% in 2017), so the distributional point applies mostly to
  itemised deductions, and many deductions and credits phase out at higher incomes, which cuts the
  other way.
- **Should the ACA subsidy cliff exist?** Live political argument in 2026. The course takes no
  position. What the lesson owes the reader is the mechanism and the date.

### For "Go deeper" rather than for claims

- **Tax Policy Center Briefing Book** (taxpolicycenter.org/briefing-book), "How do federal income

- **Khan Academy Financial Literacy**, taxes and tax forms unit, for filing mechanics, W-2s and

### Notes for whoever writes lesson 7

The Roth versus traditional decision needs the marginal rate, so lesson 6 has to hand it over
cleanly. Two things lesson 6 establishes that lesson 7 should reuse rather than re-teach: that a
deduction is worth your marginal rate (which is exactly what a traditional contribution is, and it
is an above-the-line deduction, so it works whether or not you itemise), and that the employee
payroll tax is charged on wages before that deduction, so a traditional 401(k) contribution saves
income tax and not payroll tax.

## Retirement accounts: research added 2026-09-09 for lesson 7

### Primary sources for the mechanics

- **IRS, "401(k) and profit-sharing plan contribution limits"**
  (irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-401k-and-profit-sharing-plan-contribution-limits).
  Tax year 2026: elective deferral **$24,500**, catch-up **$8,000** at 50 and over, **$11,250** for
  ages 60 to 63, overall annual additions limit **$72,000** ($80,000 with catch-up), compensation
  limit **$360,000**. Every one of these moves annually. The lesson teaches the mechanism with round
  invented numbers and links this page.
- **IRS, "IRA contribution limits"**
  (irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-ira-contribution-limits).
  Tax year 2026: **$7,500**, or **$8,600** at 50 and over. The load-bearing fact is not the number:
  it is that **the limit is shared across traditional and Roth IRAs**, so they are one bucket with
  two taps, which is what makes the choice a choice.
- **IRS, "Retirement topics: vesting"**
  (irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-vesting). The employee's own
  contributions are "always 100% vested"; employer contributions may sit behind a cliff schedule
  (typically 100% at three years) or a graded one (typically full at six). SEP and SIMPLE IRA
  contributions vest immediately. This is the source for the claim that a match is not yet the
  reader's money.
- **IRS, Topic 558, "Additional tax on early distributions"** (irs.gov/taxtopics/tc558). "The
  additional tax is equal to 10% of the portion of the distribution that's includible in gross
  income", applying before age 59 and a half, with a listed set of exceptions: substantially equal
  periodic payments, total and permanent disability, separation from service after attaining age 55,
  deductible medical expenses above 7.5% of AGI, IRS levy, qualified birth or adoption up to $5,000,
  and (after 31 December 2023) disaster, domestic abuse, and personal or family emergency
  distributions. **Name that exceptions exist; do not teach them as strategy**, per the safety note
  above, and never as a route to paying unsecured debt.
- **IRS, "Saver's Credit"**
  (irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-savings-contributions-savers-credit).
  A credit of 10% to 50% of up to $2,000 of contributions ($4,000 filing jointly), phasing out by
  AGI, for filers 18 or over who are not students and not claimed as dependents. Worth naming
  because it is the one part of this machinery aimed at low earners and it is widely unclaimed.
  Link the page rather than quoting a year's thresholds.

### How employer matches actually look, from the largest recordkeeper dataset

- **Vanguard, *How America Saves 2025*** (corporate.vanguard.com, free PDF, 2024 plan-year data
  across roughly five million participants). This is the single most useful file for the lesson,
  because it replaces plausible generalities with distributions.
  - **Match formulas.** Among plans with a match, **68%** (60% of participants) use a single-tier
    formula such as $0.50 per dollar on the first 6% of pay; **25%** use a multitier formula such as
    $1.00 on the first 3% and $0.50 on the next 2%; 6% impose a dollar cap. The single most common
    formula is **50% on the first 6% of pay, at 13% of plans**, followed by 100% on the first 3% and
    50% on the next 2% (10%), 100% on 6% (9%), 100% on 5% (7%), 100% on 4% (6%). **The teaching
    point is that "the most common formula" covers one plan in eight**, so a reader who assumes
    theirs and does not look it up is guessing.
  - **Value.** Average promised match **4.6% of pay**, median **4.0%**. The deferral needed to
    capture the maximum averaged **6.5% of pay**, median **6.0%**, with 8 in 10 plans requiring
    between 4.0% and 6.99%.
  - **Vesting.** Nearly half of plans vest matching contributions immediately; 1 in 4 plans with a
    match uses a 5- or 6-year graded schedule.
  - **Employer contributions overall.** 50% of plans match only, 36% match and also make a
    nonmatching contribution, 10% nonmatching only, **4% make no employer contribution at all**.
  - **Balances.** Average **$148,153**, median **$38,176** in 2024, with nearly 3 in 10 participants
    under $10,000 and 16% at $250,000 or more. A ready-made second visit to lesson 1's habit of
    asking what a statistic measures: the mean here is nearly four times the median.
  - **Participation and savings rates** (2025 data, from the June 2026 release of the 25th edition):
    participation **86%** of eligible employees, average total savings rate **12.1%**, average
    promised match **4.7%**, plan auto-enrolment adoption up from 10% in 2006 to 61% in 2024.
  - Honest scope: this is Vanguard's book of business, which skews to larger employers with better
    plans. It describes plans that exist, not the workforce. Pair it with the BLS access data below.

### Who is even offered one, which is the fact both sides of the policy argument accept

- **BLS, *Employee Benefits in the United States, March 2025*, Table 1**
  (bls.gov/news.release/ebs2.t01.htm). Private industry workers: retirement benefits **access 72%,
  participation 53%, take-up 73%**. Lowest 25% wage category: **access 49%, participation 23%**.
  Highest 10%: **access 93%, participation 83%**. Access by employer size runs 59% under 100
  workers to 90% at 500 or more. 70% have access to a defined contribution plan, 14% to a defined
  benefit plan.

### The evidence that people leave the match on the table, and that telling them does not fix it

- **Choi, Laibson & Madrian, "$100 Bills on the Sidewalk: Suboptimal Investment in 401(k) Plans"**
  (NBER w11554, 2005; *Review of Economics and Statistics* 93(3), 2011, free PDF at nber.org). The
  clean identification: employees over 59 and a half, already vested, who can withdraw without
  penalty for any reason, so contributing below the match threshold is **dominated whatever they
  think about retirement, liquidity or taxes**. Across seven companies, **between 20% and 60%
  contributed below the threshold**, and at the average firm **36% of match-eligible employees over
  59 and a half forwent about 1.6% of pay, averaging $507 a year**. And the finding that keeps the
  lesson honest: a survey that explained the free lunch raised contributions by a statistically
  insignificant 0.67% of income. **Information is not the whole problem, which is an argument for
  changing the default rather than for lecturing.** Pairs with Thaler and Benartzi, already in this
  file.

### The contested question, and the positions on it

**Was the shift from defined benefit pensions to 401(k)s good for workers, and does the current
system serve low earners?** Retirement policy is 3.4-adjacent and the lesson takes no side.

- **Position A: the shift was the problem.** Defined contribution plans move investment risk,
  longevity risk and the decision burden onto individuals who did not ask for them, and the results
  are unequal: the Economic Policy Institute's "The State of American Retirement" is the standard
  statement (epi.org; the page returned 403 to automated fetching on 2026-09-09, so cite it as a
  named position rather than quoting figures nobody opened).
- **Position B: coverage, not plan type, is the problem.** Center for Retirement Research at Boston
  College (crr.bc.edu, Munnell and colleagues): about half of private sector workers are not
  participating in any workplace plan at a given moment, the top third who are continuously covered
  do well and the bottom third who are never covered rely on Social Security, and traditional DB
  plans were themselves back-loaded, so they paid off only for long-tenure workers. Among workers
  aged 45 to 49, only about a third had been with an employer more than ten years, and frozen
  benefits at job change plus unindexed benefits in retirement (purchasing power down more than 20%
  since 2021) cut against the nostalgia.
- **The fact both accept, and the one the lesson should lead with:** CRR's "401(k) saving is harder
  at lower incomes" (crr.bc.edu) reports high earners contributing about **6.11% of pay against 2.8%
  for the bottom half**, with earnings volatility and job loss hitting the bottom half repeatedly
  and barely touching the top 10%, and lower earners' balances depending more on the employer's
  contribution and so on the employer's choices. **What would settle it** is not more data on plan
  design but agreement on what the system is for, which makes this a value question resting on
  shared facts.

### Added after the lesson 7 review, 2026-09-09

- **IRS Publication 590-B** (irs.gov/publications/p590b). Two claims in lesson 7 rest on it and it was
