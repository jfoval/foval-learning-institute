S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Personal Finance, lesson nine. Renting against buying, and how to tell.

S1: Most people meet this question as a slogan, and the slogans come from both directions. Renting is throwing money away. You're paying your landlord's mortgage. Get on the ladder before it's too late.

S2: And from the other side?

S1: A house is a liability, not an asset, and you should wait for the crash.

S2: So which is it?

S1: There's a real question underneath, and it has an answer, but the answer is different for different people in a way slogans can't carry. So the lesson doesn't tell you whether to buy. It hands you the arithmetic that decides it, and then shows you which number in that arithmetic matters most. Which turns out to be the one nobody can know in advance.

S2: Scope.

S1: The machinery is US. Thirty year fixed mortgages, private mortgage insurance, local property taxes, and agents paid by percentage commission. The thirty year fixed barely exists outside the United States, and in much of the world a mortgage rate resets every few years, which changes the risk of owning considerably.

S2: And what transfers?

S1: The method. Total everything owning costs you that you never get back, compare it against rent for the same place, and find how long you'd have to stay for the transaction costs to be worth paying. And this is education, not personalised advice, which the lesson says matters most here, because a house is the largest transaction most people ever make.

S2: Work the case.

S1: Priya is looking at a house at four hundred thousand dollars and a rental down the street at two thousand two hundred a month. She's got eighty eight thousand saved, enough for twenty percent down and closing costs.

S2: And she does the sum everybody does.

S1: With twenty percent down, a three hundred and twenty thousand dollar mortgage at six percent over thirty years is one thousand nine hundred and nineteen dollars a month. Rent is two thousand two hundred. The mortgage is two hundred and eighty one dollars a month cheaper, and at the end of thirty years she owns a house instead of a pile of receipts.

S2: It looks decided.

S1: It isn't, because one thousand nine hundred and nineteen isn't what owning costs. It's what the bank charges. The costs of owning that don't appear anywhere on that statement are larger than the statement.

S2: Name some.

S1: Property tax. Homeowner's insurance. Maintenance and repair. The money you could have earned on the down payment if it were invested instead. The cost of buying, closing costs, inspection, title, and the much larger cost of selling, agent commissions and transfer taxes, which you pay on the way out. Private mortgage insurance if you put down less than twenty percent.

S2: And the one people forget entirely?

S1: Of that monthly payment itself, most of the early years is interest, which is a cost. Only the principal part is yours.

S2: So what's the right sum?

S1: One distinction does all the work. Some of what you pay while owning comes back to you, and some of it doesn't. The principal portion comes back: it's yours, sitting in the house, and you get it when you sell. Everything else is gone the way rent is gone.

S2: List the gone.

S1: Interest is gone. Property tax is gone. Insurance is gone. A new roof is gone. And the return your down payment would have earned somewhere else is gone too, which is a real cost even though nobody sends you a bill for it.

S2: So the honest comparison is.

S1: Not rent against the mortgage payment. Rent against the unrecoverable costs of owning.

S2: Do Priya's first year.

S1: Mortgage interest, nineteen thousand and ninety three dollars. Property tax at one point two percent of four hundred thousand, four thousand eight hundred. Homeowner's insurance, one thousand eight hundred. Maintenance and repair, six thousand. And the return forgone on the eighty eight thousand tied up, at four percent, three thousand five hundred and twenty.

S2: Total?

S1: Thirty five thousand two hundred and thirteen dollars. Which is two thousand nine hundred and thirty four a month. Rent on the equivalent house is two thousand two hundred a month, or twenty six thousand four hundred for the year.

S2: So the comparison flips completely.

S1: The mortgage payment was two hundred and eighty one dollars a month cheaper than rent. The actual cost of owning is seven hundred and thirty four dollars a month more expensive. Before anything has gone wrong, and before she's paid a penny of transaction costs.

S2: Now, every line in that table is a choice, isn't it.

S1: And the lesson says so, because you should be able to check every one. The forgone return is four percent a year on the money actually tied up in the house, which starts at the eighty eight thousand and grows as she repays principal. There's no HOA or condo fee in this example, because Priya is buying a house. If you're buying a flat, that fee is a large unrecoverable line, it rises, and it belongs in the table next to property tax.

S2: What about tax deductions?

S1: The interest, tax, insurance and maintenance lines are charged at full cost, with no deduction. Mortgage interest and property tax can be deductible if you itemise, which would lower the true cost of owning.

S2: So why not model it?

S1: Because since twenty seventeen roughly nine filers in ten take the standard deduction and so get nothing from it, and lesson six taught you how to check which of the two you are. If you itemise, subtract your marginal rate from those two lines and the answer moves in favour of buying.

S2: And the property tax rate?

S1: One point two percent is a little above the national average, which tilts against buying by a few hundred dollars a year. Use your own county's figure.

S2: Anything else the model assumes?

S1: It runs prices and rents at the same three percent, which holds the price to rent ratio fixed for fifteen years. And that's an assumption, not a neutral default. Rents rising faster than prices shortens the break even, and the reverse lengthens it.

S2: Does any of it cut the other way?

S1: One does. The model charges Priya four percent on the lump sum tied up in the house but doesn't credit the renter with a return on the monthly difference. Both sides are plain cumulative cash. Investing that difference would push the break even out somewhat, so the lesson's break even of about seven years is, on this point, generous to buying.

S2: And in the renter's disfavour?

S1: Moving costs. A renter who moves four times in fifteen years pays deposits, movers and overlapping rent, and none of that is in the rent line.

S2: Two of those lines would start an argument. Defend the maintenance one.

S1: Six thousand dollars is not pessimistic. The Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies found that US homeowners spent an average of seven thousand one hundred dollars on improvements and repairs in twenty twenty three, ranging from three thousand one hundred in the lowest income fifth to ten thousand nine hundred in the highest.

S2: Those mix repairs with a new kitchen, though.

S1: They do, so they aren't a pure maintenance number, and the lesson says so. And the popular budget one percent of the value each year rule has no evidence behind it that the drafter could find.

S2: So what's certain?

S1: That the number isn't zero, and a reader who leaves the line out has decided it is.

S2: The full lesson has the break even calculation, a sensitivity table for the assumptions, the transaction costs on the way in and the way out, and the number in all of this that nobody can know in advance. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Compare rent with what you never get back.
