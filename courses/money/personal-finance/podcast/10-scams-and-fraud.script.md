S1: Because all of that can be taken in an afternoon by somebody who calls you on the phone.

S2: And the lesson opens with a claim.

S1: The one that matters more than anything else in it. Being scammed is not a failure of intelligence. It's tempting to believe otherwise, because believing otherwise is comforting. If only careless people get caught, and I'm not careless, I'm safe.

S2: And the evidence?

S1: In twenty twenty four people in the US reported losing more than twelve and a half billion dollars to fraud, up twenty five percent in a year. And read that the way lesson one taught you to. The number of reports barely moved, at two point six million. What changed was the share of reports involving an actual loss, which went from twenty seven percent to thirty eight.

S2: So the rise is.

S1: Mostly people getting caught more often, rather than more attempts being made. And all of it is a floor rather than a total, because most fraud is never reported at all.

S2: Scope note?

S1: The credit bureaus, the freeze rules, the recovery sites and the payment protections in this lesson are US ones. The structure of a con is not, and neither is the rule about payment methods. The names of the rails change and their reversibility does not.

S2: Now. Who actually gets scammed? Because I'd say older people.

S1: Most people would, and the FTC keeps the national count. Its own summary is blunt. Many people think scams mostly affect older adults, but reports to the FTC's Consumer Sentinel tell a different story. Anyone can be scammed.

S2: Give me the numbers.

S1: Two of them, and you need both or you'll believe something false. First, younger adults report losing money more often. In the FTC's analysis of this question, using twenty twenty one reports, adults aged eighteen to fifty nine were thirty four percent more likely than adults sixty and over to report losing money to a scam.

S2: And the second?

S1: Older adults lose much more when it happens. Median individual losses in the same data run five hundred dollars for ages eighteen to fifty nine, five hundred and twenty for sixty to sixty nine, eight hundred for seventy to seventy nine, and fifteen hundred for eighty and over. And by the FTC's twenty twenty five figures the median for people eighty and over had passed sixteen hundred.

S2: And at the top end?

S1: Sharper still. Losses of ten thousand dollars or more to business and government imposter scams were more than twice as likely to be reported by older adults, and losses over a hundred thousand three times as likely.

S2: So are older people worse at spotting them?

S1: And here's the third figure, which belongs with the other two. Older adults were also much more likely than people aged eighteen to fifty nine to report a scam they had spotted and avoided losing anything to. Whatever is going on in the loss figures, it isn't that older people are worse at recognising a con.

S2: So what happens if I take only one of the figures?

S1: Take only the first and you'll conclude age doesn't matter, and stop worrying about your parents. Take only the second and you'll reproduce the myth, and stop worrying about yourself. The true picture is that everybody is a target, the young lose more often, the old lose more when it happens, and the old spot more of them coming.

S2: Where do scams reach people from?

S1: The most common first contact is email, then phone calls, then text messages. And by number of reports the ranking is imposter scams, then online shopping, then business and job opportunities.

S2: And the money?

S1: Goes somewhere else. Investment scams took five point seven billion dollars in twenty twenty four, against two point nine five billion for imposter scams, which are reported far more often.

S2: What's growing fastest?

S1: Job scams. Their reports tripled between twenty twenty and twenty twenty four while the losses went from ninety million dollars to five hundred and one million.

S2: So most contacts fail.

S1: Most contacts are cheap and fail. The ones that work run for weeks and take everything.

S2: Then how do I defend against a thing that changes every month?

S1: You don't memorise a list of current scams, because that's a losing game. The variants change and the structure doesn't, because it can't. To take your money, a stranger has to get through three gates, and if any one of them fails, they get nothing.

S2: Gate one.

S1: Contact. They have to reach you and give you a reason to stay on the line. A suspicious charge, a package, a warrant, a job, a match, a tip about a coin. And the reason is chosen to make you feel something. Usually alarm or hope.

S2: Gate two.

S1: Pressure, and it has two halves that always travel together. Urgency, so you act before you think. And isolation, so nobody else can look at it.

S2: Which of those do I watch for?

S1: The second, because it's the more distinctive. Real institutions do not tell you to keep a call secret from your family, and they don't stay on the line while you drive to the bank. The instruction to tell nobody is the single most reliable sign in the entire lesson.

S2: And gate three.

S1: Payment, and it's the one the lesson spends the most time on. Because it's where the whole thing becomes irreversible, and because it's the gate you can close with a rule rather than a judgement.

S2: Try me on a case. A caller says they're from my bank's fraud team, there's an unauthorised charge, and to protect my money I should move it to a safe account they'll set up. And not discuss it with branch staff, because the fraud may involve an employee.

S1: All three gates are open. Contact is the call itself, and the reason is alarm about your own money. Payment is the transfer to the safe account, which is a bank transfer and near irreversible.

S2: And the tell?

S1: The isolation clause. Not to discuss it with branch staff. Which exists for exactly one reason: any bank employee who heard the request would stop it. No real fraud team asks you to move money anywhere, and none of them asks you to keep the conversation from the bank itself.

S2: You said gate three can be closed with a rule.

S1: Some ways of paying can be reversed. Others are gone the second you press send. Criminals know exactly which is which, and they choose accordingly. In twenty twenty four, US consumers reported losing more money to bank transfers and cryptocurrency combined than to every other payment method put together. Which isn't a coincidence about what people happen to use. It's what criminals ask for.

S2: So the question is.

S1: What are they asking me to pay with. And that tells you more than, does this story sound plausible, because a good story is cheap to write and an irreversible rail is the whole business model.
