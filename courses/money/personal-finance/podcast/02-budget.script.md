S1: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast. I'm John.

S2: And I'm Haley. Personal Finance Fundamentals, lesson two. A budget that survives real life.

S1: And the framing I like is right at the top. A budget is just the audit from lesson one, run forwards, on purpose. Where the money went, turned into where you want it to go.

S2: Before we build one, the lesson spends time on why they fail. Which is unusual and I think correct.

S1: Four killers, and they're predictable enough to design against. The first is building on guesses. Lesson one had people guessing eighty six dollars a month on subscriptions and actually paying two hundred and nineteen. A budget built on guessed numbers springs leaks that look like discipline failures and are really measurement failures.

S2: The second one is the one I'd have got wrong.

S1: Deleting all the fun. The definition of financial well-being the course uses has four parts, and the fourth is the freedom to make the choices that let you enjoy your life. That isn't a reward for finishing the other three. It's a quarter of the goal. A budget with no restaurant line and no hobby line is failing at the definition, and in practice it collapses the first weekend something fun comes up.

S2: Third?

S1: Months that lie. Annual insurance, car registration, gifts, the dentist. Irregular but inevitable, and a budget with no line for them breaks the month they arrive.

S2: And the fourth.

S1: Zero slack. A budget where every dollar is assigned to the cent, with nothing miscellaneous, is a machine with no tolerances. The first forty dollar surprise makes it wrong, and a plan that's already wrong is easy to abandon.

S2: Okay. Maya. She's the worked example from lesson one.

S1: Three thousand four hundred take home. Last month she spent one thousand two hundred and forty five on housing, eight hundred on food, five hundred and forty five on transport, a hundred and eighty on her student loan, eighty seven on subscriptions and two hundred and sixty on everything else. Two hundred and eighty three stayed put, by accident.

S2: And the plan?

S1: Housing, transport and the loan don't move. The lease is signed and the car loan is fixed. Subscriptions go from eighty seven to sixty, because she cancelled a meditation app she'd forgotten and some cloud storage. Groceries go up, from four fifteen to four thirty.

S2: Up?

S1: On purpose, so the number is honest. Eating out and delivery goes from three eighty five to two forty. Cut, not killed. Roughly one delivery a week plus dinners out. Everything else goes from two sixty to two hundred. There's a new hundred dollar line we'll come to. And savings become four hundred, transferred automatically on payday.

S2: So her deliberate saving goes from a drifting two eighty three to five hundred.

S1: Four hundred transferred plus a hundred set aside for irregulars. And she found it in three places. Two cancellations she didn't miss, a real but survivable cut to delivery, and a trim to miscellaneous.

S2: And eating out kept two hundred and forty dollars.

S1: On purpose. That's not weakness. That's the fourth element of well-being with a number on it.

S2: The lesson asks you to predict what happens if she tries harder. Eating out at zero, everything else at a hundred, savings at six eighty three.

S1: Abandoned by week three, most likely. It deletes the enjoyment element and leaves no slack, so the first birthday dinner makes the plan wrong. A slightly worse plan you keep beats a perfect plan you quit.

S2: Then four methods. Give me the short version of each.

S1: Fifty thirty twenty. About half of take home to needs, thirty percent to wants, twenty percent to saving and extra debt payments. It's a diagnostic sketch rather than a ledger, and it breaks at low incomes and in expensive cities.

S2: Zero based.

S1: Maya's method. Every dollar assigned before the month starts, income minus assignments equals zero. Most control, most information, and the price is upkeep.

S2: Pay yourself first.

S1: Flip the order. Automate the savings transfer on payday, cover the fixed costs, spend the rest however you like, no tracking. And this is the one with the strongest evidence behind it. When Thaler and Benartzi got employees to pre-commit to automatic future increases, average contribution rates went from three and a half percent to thirteen point six percent in about four years.

S2: That's a big number.

S1: And the reason it works isn't willpower. Waiting to save whatever is left fails because it puts saving last in line behind every temptation of the month. Whatever method you pick, steal that piece.

S2: And envelopes.

S1: Each category gets an envelope of money, physical or virtual, and when it's empty that category is done for the month. The engine is the pain of paying. In one experiment people were willing to pay roughly twice as much for the same tickets when paying by card rather than cash.

S2: Twice.

S1: So envelopes suit people whose problem is one or two runaway categories. And you don't have to adopt the whole system. One envelope for your one leaky category, alongside any other method, is a legitimate combination.

S2: Then the two fixes for months that lie. First, lumpy costs.

S1: A sinking fund, which is an old accounting term for saving into a known future cost. List every irregular but inevitable cost for the year, total it, divide by twelve, give it a monthly line. Maya's car registration is a hundred and forty, gifts about seven hundred a year, a dental visit three hundred and sixty. About twelve hundred, so a hundred a month.

S2: And then December isn't an emergency.

S1: Because it was never a surprise. It happens every year.

S2: And irregular income.

S1: Dan, the freelance designer. His last three months were two thousand one hundred, four thousand nine hundred, and two thousand six hundred. A monthly budget built on any one of those is wrong two months out of three.

S2: So what does he do?

S1: Separates earning from spending. Client payments land in one account and stay there. On the first of the month he pays himself a flat salary into his spending account, and the budget runs on the salary. He sets it low, near essentials plus a modest life. Two thousand eight hundred against a three month average of three thousand two hundred.

S2: And when a four thousand nine hundred month lands, what happens to the extra two thousand one hundred?

S1: Nothing. It stays in the holding account as buffer, and it absorbs the next lean month. The temptation is to treat a good month as a raise, and the system only works if the salary changes rarely and deliberately, after the average has genuinely moved.

S2: One thing to say before we finish, which the course says throughout.

S1: This is education, not personalised advice. The course teaches how money works in general and doesn't know your situation.

S2: If someone does one thing after this episode?

S1: Automate one transfer on payday. It's the single component with trial grade evidence behind it, and it's the one most people postpone.
