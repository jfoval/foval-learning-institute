S1: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast. I'm John.

S2: And I'm Haley. Personal Finance Fundamentals, lesson three. How debt really works.

S1: And the lesson opens with a fact I'd never thought about. Since twenty ten, every US credit card statement has been required to carry a small box showing how long the balance would take to clear at the minimum payment, and what it would cost.

S2: And card companies fought that requirement.

S1: They did. And once you've worked the arithmetic you can see why.

S2: So let's do the arithmetic. Start with what a balance costs to just sit there.

S1: APR is the annual percentage rate, the yearly price of borrowing as a percentage of what you owe. For a quick monthly figure, divide by twelve. So a card at twenty four percent costs about two percent a month, and a two thousand dollar balance costs about forty dollars a month just to hold. Before a cent of it goes away.

S2: And the lesson says the real mechanics are slightly worse.

S1: Card interest is charged daily, not monthly. The issuer divides the APR by three hundred and sixty five and applies that to your average daily balance, so interest starts accruing on interest within the month.

S2: And there's the grace period thing, which I think is the practical part.

S1: Most cards charge no interest on purchases if you pay the full statement balance by the due date. But carrying any balance usually forfeits that, so new purchases start accruing immediately. Pay in full and the card is an interest free convenience. Carry a balance and everything you buy costs extra from day one. There's no middle setting.

S2: Alright, the minimum payment. This is the centre of the lesson.

S1: Five thousand dollars on a card at twenty two percent, which is close to the current US average for accounts paying interest. A common minimum formula is that month's interest plus one percent of the balance, with a floor of twenty five dollars.

S2: Work the first month.

S1: Interest is five thousand times twenty two percent divided by twelve, so about ninety two dollars. One percent of the balance is fifty. So the minimum is about a hundred and forty two dollars, and of that, only fifty touches the debt. You paid a hundred and forty two and you owe four thousand nine hundred and fifty.

S2: And it gets worse from there, because the minimum shrinks.

S1: That's the trap. The minimum is a percentage of the balance, so as the balance falls the payment falls, the debt therefore falls slowly, and the schedule stretches.

S2: Listeners, guess before he says it. Paying exactly the minimum, never missing, never charging another dollar. How long, and how much interest?

S1: Nineteen years and two months. And about eight thousand one hundred dollars in interest, which is more than the original debt. Two hundred and thirty payments, thirteen thousand one hundred dollars handed over to settle five thousand.

S2: With perfect behaviour.

S1: No missed payments, no new purchases. And it isn't one card's odd terms. A percentage based minimum produces that shape wherever it's used, and some older formulas, a flat two percent of balance, stretch the same debt past eighty years.

S2: Okay, so give me the good news, because there is some.

S1: It's the same arithmetic pointed the other way. Every dollar above the minimum goes entirely to the balance, and a smaller balance means less interest next month, which frees more of the next payment for the balance. Fifty extra dollars a month turns nineteen years into five and a half, and cuts the interest from eight thousand one hundred to two thousand eight hundred and thirty.

S2: Fifty dollars.

S1: Which is why the lesson says revolving card debt is an emergency in a way other debts aren't. Not moral failure. Arithmetic. At twenty two percent the debt grows faster than almost anything you could plausibly earn on savings or investments, so while it exists it silently outruns everything else you do with money.

S2: Then there's a distinction that organises the whole rest of the lesson.

S1: Secured and unsecured. A secured debt is pinned to a thing the lender can take. A mortgage to the house, a car loan to the car. Because the lender has that safety net, the rate is lower. An unsecured debt, credit cards, most personal loans, medical bills, payday loans, has no collateral, so the lender prices in the risk and the rate is higher.

S2: And the lesson has a table of what borrowing actually costs.

S1: These are late twenty twenty six figures and rates move, so the point is the pattern rather than the digits. Payday loans around four hundred percent. Credit cards averaging twenty two percent. Car loans about six point four percent new and eleven point four used. Federal student loans between six point five and nine point one percent. Mortgages about six point seven percent for a thirty year fixed.

S2: And the secured column matters twice, the lesson says.

S1: First for priority when things go wrong. If you ever can't pay everything, the debts attached to your housing and your transport are the ones whose consequences arrive fastest and hardest. And second for a trap that arrives dressed as help.

S2: Which is?

S1: Consolidating card debt into a home equity loan, or pulling money out of a retirement account to clear cards. Both convert debt with survivable worst cases into losses that are much harder to recover from. Home equity consolidation turns unsecured debt into debt your house now secures, so if your income fails the failure mode changes from collections calls to foreclosure.

S2: And the retirement version?

S1: It typically triggers taxes and penalties, and money in US retirement accounts is generally protected from creditors while money you pull out isn't. Neither move is always wrong. Both are sold on the rate and priced in the tail risk. Run the worst case, not just the interest saved.

S2: Last thing. Several debts, some spare money. Which one first?

S1: Everyone agrees on the frame. Pay every minimum, then concentrate everything extra on one target until it's gone. The argument is which target. Avalanche says highest interest rate first, and the case is pure arithmetic. Nobody disputes it minimises total interest.

S2: And snowball.

S1: Smallest balance first, whatever the rate. That's the order Dave Ramsey's programme popularised, and the case for it is behavioural, and it has data behind it. Gal and McShane studied thousands of clients of a debt settlement firm and found the ones who closed individual accounts entirely, rather than spreading payments, were more likely to eliminate their whole debt. Kettle, Trudel, Blanchard and Häubl found the same thing experimentally.

S2: So which does the course tell you to do?

S1: Neither, and honestly. Avalanche costs less if you finish. Snowball makes finishing more likely. What would settle it is a trial randomly assigning real debtors to each plan and measuring both completion and interest, and we don't have one. What we have is observational work and experimental proxies. So it's a judgement about you rather than about the maths.

S2: And that's the pattern in this whole course, isn't it.

S1: Where the evidence settles it, we say so. Where it doesn't, you get both sides and the reasoning, and the decision is yours.

S2: This is education, not personalised advice. The full lesson, with the payoff chart and the credit score section, is at Foval Learning Institute dot org. Thanks for listening.
