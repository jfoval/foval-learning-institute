S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Personal Finance, lesson five. Investing without the noise.

S2: Lesson four left a number worth repeating: a one percent annual fee, compounding over a forty year saving career, cost Ava about a hundred and twenty seven thousand dollars. And it left a question open.

S1: Your savings line is growing at lesson four's seven percent. But growing in what? For money you won't need for decades, the evidence points to owning a slice of the whole economy, cheaply, and leaving it alone.

S2: Surely investing means finding winners.

S2: The best data on that come from an unlikely witness. But first, what am I buying? Start with a share.

S1: A share is a piece of ownership in a company: a claim on its future profits and, if it's ever wound up, its assets.

S2: A bond?

S1: A loan. You lend a government or a company money, and it promises fixed interest on fixed dates and your money back at the end. That promise is a contract, and bondholders get paid before shareholders see a cent, which is why bonds are steadier and pay less. The steadiness is the product. And nobody promises a shareholder anything: you're last in line, paid in whatever is left.

S2: And a fund?

S1: Not a third kind of investment. A container: people pool their money and the pool buys shares or bonds. Buy one unit of a broad fund and you own a sliver of everything in it. Hundreds of companies in a single purchase.

S2: Which brings us to the question most adults get wrong.

S1: Lesson one's three question check ended with it. Is a single company's stock usually a safer return than a stock fund? The Big Three question most people miss, worldwide.

S2: Take it apart.

S1: Any company can be destroyed by things that have nothing to do with the economy. A fraud, a product that kills someone, a lawsuit. Call that single company risk. The whole market can also fall together, in a recession or a panic. Call that market risk. Single company risk disappears when you hold many companies, because one firm's disaster is noise in a portfolio of hundreds. Market risk doesn't, because it hits everything at once.

S2: And that's where the free lunch is.

S1: The market pays a higher expected return for bearing risk. That's what the seven percent real average from lesson four is: payment for enduring the minus thirty seven percent years. But it only pays for the risk you can't diversify away. Single company risk earns you nothing extra, because any investor can shed it at no cost just by spreading out.

S2: So holding one stock is.

S1: Lottery ticket risk without lottery ticket winnings. Hold the market through a broad fund and you keep the whole expected return while carrying only the risk that's compensated. Spreading out costs nothing in expectation and removes an entire category of catastrophe.

S2: What about the cousin with twenty thousand in the stock of the solid company she works for?

S1: The company being good isn't the point. Good companies fail too, and nothing pays her extra for betting on one. And her salary already depends on that company, so its bad year could take her income and her savings together.

S2: All right. So a broad fund is the container. Who decides what's in it?

S1: An actively managed fund employs professionals to pick investments they expect to beat the market, and charges for it. Fees near one percent a year are common, and you know from lesson four what that compounds into.

S2: And the other kind?

S1: An index fund doesn't pick at all. It buys every company in a published index, such as the S and P five hundred, in proportion to size, and then does almost nothing. No analysts, no forecasts, no trading desk to pay for, so the fees are close to zero. US index equity mutual funds charged an average of about nought point nought five percent a year in twenty twenty five.

S2: Which is twenty five dollars a year on a fifty thousand dollar balance, against five hundred at one percent. But an index fund guarantees you'll never beat the market.

S1: It delivers the market's return, minus almost nothing. Whether that's settling depends on how often the professionals win, which is a factual question with two decades of good data.

S1: From S and P Dow Jones Indices, which publishes the S and P five hundred itself. Twice a year since two thousand and two its SPIVA scorecard has counted what fraction of active funds beat their benchmark, correcting for failed funds that vanish from other records.

S2: Give me the number. Twenty years to June twenty twenty five, US large company stock funds against the plain index.

S1: About nine percent beat it. The scorecard puts it the other way round: ninety one percent of active large cap funds underperformed the index over those twenty years. Against the broad S and P fifteen hundred, ninety four percent of all US stock funds did.

S2: Most people would guess about half. And in any single year some managers do win, presumably.

S1: Calendar twenty twenty five was a bad one, with seventy nine percent of large cap funds losing to the index, and a lucky year is better. But stretch the horizon and the winners thin out relentlessly, because a fee is charged every year while outperformance comes and goes. Over a saving career roughly nine in ten lose to the thing they're paid to beat.

S2: How far does that go?

S1: Over fifteen years, not one category of US active stock fund, large, small, growth or value, has a majority of its funds ahead of its index.

S2: Then I'll just buy one of the winners.

S1: Here the evidence gets less kind. Take the US large cap funds in the top quarter of five year performers as of twenty twenty, the ones an advertisement would feature. Four years later, how many were still in the top quarter?

S1: Zero.

S2: And the top half?

S1: About two percent stayed there over five years, when chance alone would keep several times that many.

S2: A third, maybe?

S1: Nought point nought percent, in S and P's persistence scorecard.

S2: So a great record is.

S1: What luck looks like afterwards. Every fund document in America carries the sentence, past performance does not guarantee future results, as a legal formality. The persistence data show it isn't a formality. It's the finding.

S2: Why does this keep happening? Are the managers not good at their jobs?

S1: Nothing here requires managers to be fools. The arithmetic works even if every one is brilliant, and the cleanest statement of it is two pages long. In nineteen ninety one the economist William Sharpe, who later won a Nobel prize, published a piece called The Arithmetic of Active Management.

S2: Give me the argument.

S1: Every share is held by someone, so all investors together hold the market and earn exactly the market's return. Index investors, holding the market in miniature, earn it too. Subtract them and what's left, active investors as a group, must also be holding the market and earning its return.

S2: So before costs the average active dollar earns what the average indexed dollar earns.

S1: After costs the active dollar earns less, because its costs are ten to twenty times higher. No forecast, no ideology. Just subtraction.

S2: Last thing, and I want the honest version. Is the claim that nobody beats the market?

S1: It is not, and the lesson is careful here. The claim the evidence supports is that the average investor in active funds trails the index after costs, and that no reliable method exists for picking the exceptions ahead of time. Some investors have beaten it, some for long periods.

S2: And is active management useless?

S1: Active managers point out, correctly, that markets need active traders to set prices. If everyone indexed, nobody would do the research that makes prices mean anything. So it isn't useless to the world. Just unrewarding for its average customer.
