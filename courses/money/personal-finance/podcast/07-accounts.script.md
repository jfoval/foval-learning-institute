S1: Lesson five ended with a recommendation you could act on: for long term money, a broad, cheap, diversified fund beats picking. Then it stopped, because a question underneath couldn't be answered yet. You've chosen the fund. Where does it go?

S1: Not which company holds it. Which kind of account. The same fund, the same shares, the same return, will leave you with meaningfully different amounts depending on which of three containers you put it in. The difference comes entirely from when the tax is charged.

S2: Where does this apply?

S1: The specific accounts are US ones: the four oh one k and the traditional and Roth IRA. Elsewhere the machinery has a different name and a similar shape. The UK has ISAs and workplace pensions, Canada has RRSPs and TFSAs, Australia has superannuation.

S2: And the question to take to my own system?

S1: The same one the lesson answers. Which accounts change when my money is taxed, does my employer contribute, and what must I do to get all of it. The arithmetic doesn't care what they're called.

S2: Start with the confusion you say costs the most.

S1: A four oh one k is not an investment. Neither is an IRA. They're wrappers. A wrapper is a set of tax rules that applies to whatever you put inside it, and has no return of its own. Inside it you still choose something to hold.

S2: So the wrapper decides one thing?

S1: How the growth is taxed. The thing inside decides whether there's growth to tax.

S2: And that's not abstract. It's common to open a retirement account, move money in, feel you've done the responsible thing, and leave it in the default cash option for a decade.

S2: So if someone says, I have a four oh one k, so I'm covered for retirement?

S1: Three separate questions are left unanswered. What's inside it, cash or investments, and at what cost. How much is going in, and does that capture the whole employer match. And if there's more than one account from more than one job, is anyone looking at all of them together.

S2: So the first thing to do.

S1: Open it and read what it's holding and what that holding charges. Some plans default new money into a target date fund, which invests it. Others leave it in cash until you choose, and the only way to know is to look. And in a workplace plan you're choosing from a menu somebody else wrote, which may be short and may be expensive.

S2: Now the three routes.

S1: A dollar you earn can travel three ways, and what matters is where the tax happens on each. Take a thousand dollars and suppose it quadruples.

S1: Take route one, the taxable account, taxed at twenty percent on the gain. It loses, and it loses to the tax on growth rather than the tax on income. All three routes pay income tax once. The taxable route pays a second tax the other two don't: tax on the growth. In the lesson's example that's three hundred and sixty dollars on two thousand four hundred of gain.

S2: And the real figure is worse than that.

S1: Because a fund throws off dividends most years and those are taxed as they arrive, so the compounding happens on a slightly smaller number every year. Which is the honest case for using a wrapper at all.

S2: And the other two routes?

S1: Traditional and Roth came out exactly the same. Not roughly. Exactly, to the dollar.

S2: That can't be right.

S1: It surprises nearly everyone, so watch why. The traditional route multiplies by four, for the growth, and then by nought point eight, for the tax. The Roth route multiplies by nought point eight and then by four.

S2: Multiplication doesn't care about the order.

S1: So if your tax rate is the same when the money goes in and when it comes out, the two accounts are the same account wearing different clothes.

S2: Then what is the traditional against Roth argument actually about?

S1: One thing. Whether your rate when the money comes out will be lower or higher than your rate today. Everything else is either a consequence of that or a tie breaker.

S2: Do the case where the rates differ.

S1: Nadia's rate today is twenty two percent and she's confident it'll be twelve when she retires, because she'll have no salary and will be living on a smaller drawdown. Traditional: a thousand dollars grows to four thousand and is taxed at twelve percent, leaving three thousand five hundred and twenty. Roth: two hundred and twenty of tax first, so seven hundred and eighty grows to three thousand one hundred and twenty, kept whole.

S2: So traditional wins.

S1: By four hundred dollars on a thousand dollar contribution, which is a thirteen percent difference in the final amount, produced by nothing but the rate gap. Reverse the assumption, twelve percent now and twenty two later, and the Roth wins by the mirror image amount.

S2: But I'm being asked to forecast my own income decades ahead.

S1: And the tax law with it. Nobody can, which is the first tie breaker. Tax rates change by law, not just by your salary. Holding some money in each wrapper means that whatever happens, half your money is in the right one, and in retirement you can choose which account to draw from in a given year, which is itself a way of managing your rate.

S2: Is that just indecision?

S1: It's diversification, from lesson five. When the future is genuinely unknown, spreading is a decision rather than an absence of one.

S2: What else is on the table?

S1: The contribution limit is stated in nominal dollars, which quietly favours Roth. Both wrappers cap the year at the same number. But a dollar in a Roth is a dollar that will never be taxed again, and a dollar in a traditional account is a dollar that still owes tax. If you're contributing the maximum and would otherwise put the leftover cash in a taxable account, the Roth shelters more real money behind the same nominal cap.

S2: And if I'm nowhere near the cap?

S1: Then that argument doesn't apply to you at all. And one more fact makes this a choice at all. The annual limit is shared between your traditional and your Roth IRA. One bucket with two taps. You're not deciding which account to open, but how to split one allowance.

S2: What about getting at the money?

S1: Money in a Roth IRA comes out in a fixed order, your own contributions first, and those were taxed before they went in, so they come back without tax or penalty at any age. Earnings have their own rules. Which makes a Roth IRA the least locked of the retirement wrappers, and that matters if your cushion is thin.

S1: It is not.
