S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Personal Finance, lesson eight. Handing off what you cannot absorb.

S1: Everything in this course so far has been about making a plan work. Measuring, budgeting, clearing debt, growing what's left, and putting it in the right container. All of it assumes the ordinary case. This lesson is about the other case.

S2: Which is?

S1: A car is written off. A pipe bursts. You're admitted to hospital. And the thing those have in common isn't that they're unlikely, because over a long enough life they're close to certain. It's that they're large and badly timed, and no budget survives them by being tidier.

S2: And people get insurance wrong in both directions, I think.

S1: Some think it's a rip off because the company expects to profit. Others think it's a good investment because they got more back than they put in. Both have the same misunderstanding underneath, and by the end of the lesson you should be able to say what it is.

S2: Scope first.

S1: The vocabulary is US health, car and home insurance, and the dollar figures are US ones. Pooling is the constant. Who does the pooling is not, and in a tax financed system the pool is the tax base rather than a book of customers, with the same arithmetic underneath.

S2: And the standing note.

S1: Which the lesson says matters more here than anywhere else in the course, because this lesson gives more direct answers than any other. This is education, not personalised advice. What follows is the arithmetic and the vocabulary, and the weighting depends on facts about your life no course can know.

S2: All right. What is insurance for?

S1: It isn't an investment, and it isn't supposed to pay off on average. If it did, nobody could sell it. What you're buying is a change in the shape of your risk.

S2: Say that more slowly.

S1: Without insurance, most years cost you nothing and a rare year costs you everything. With insurance, every year costs you a known premium and no year costs you everything. You've traded a small certain loss for the removal of a large uncertain one.

S2: And when is that trade worth it?

S1: When the large uncertain loss would break something. It isn't worth making when it would only annoy you.

S2: Before we go on, you flagged an exception.

S1: And the lesson states it early so it doesn't quietly break the arithmetic later. All of this assumes you're paying the full price of the policy. Often you are not. In the US in twenty twenty five the average employer health plan cost nine thousand three hundred and twenty five dollars a year for single cover, and the worker paid one thousand four hundred and forty of it.

S2: So the employer paid about six sevenths.

S1: And that share isn't taxed as your income. A policy somebody else largely pays for isn't a negative expected value trade at all. It's a transfer, and turning it down to save your share is usually a mistake on any reading. The reasoning in this lesson is about which policies to buy at their full price.

S2: Good. Now, a friend says, I've paid car insurance for twelve years and never claimed, that's thousands of dollars wasted.

S1: It treats the payout as the product. The product was the twelve years in which a crash would not have ended them, and they consumed all of it. Compare it with a fire alarm that never went off. Nobody says the alarm was wasted.

S2: Is the sentence ever right?

S1: Only if the loss it protected against was one they could comfortably have absorbed. In which case they were insuring the wrong thing, which is a real mistake and a different one.

S2: Explain the pooling, then.

S1: Ten thousand people each face a one percent chance of a fifty thousand dollar loss in a year. For any one of them the year is wild: almost certainly nothing, occasionally ruin. Across ten thousand of them the total is dull and predictable. About a hundred losses, about five million dollars, year after year, within a narrow range.

S2: So the trick is?

S1: Risk that is unbearable one at a time becomes forecastable in bulk, and an insurer is a machine for standing in the bulk position.

S2: And the price?

S1: The company can charge each person the five hundred dollars the average loss costs, plus something for running the business and something for profit. Say six hundred and fifty. Every customer now pays six hundred and fifty with certainty instead of facing a one percent chance of losing fifty thousand.

S2: But six hundred and fifty is more than five hundred.

S1: Do the expected value from the customer's side and it looks like a bad deal. It is a bad deal, in expected value terms, and it's meant to be.

S2: Then why buy it?

S1: Because you aren't an insurance company. You can't run the same year ten thousand times and collect the average. You run it once, and the version of you who has the fifty thousand dollar loss and no policy has lost the house.

S2: So, they make a profit, therefore it's a rip off.

S1: Doesn't follow. The insurer's profit is the price of moving a risk from someone who cannot carry it to something built to carry it.

S2: And what does follow?

S1: The sharp end of the same reasoning. You should not pay that margin on losses you could carry yourself.

S2: Which gives you the rule.

S1: Insure the catastrophe, not the inconvenience. And the test that turns it into a decision is one question, asked about a specific loss. If this happened tomorrow and nobody paid me a penny, what would I do?

S2: And the two answers.

S1: If the answer is, pay it from savings and be irritated, carry the risk yourself. Paying an insurer's margin to be spared an irritation is a poor trade, and over a lifetime of small policies it adds up to real money.

S2: And the other?

S1: If the answer is, borrow at twenty four percent, or move, or not get the treatment, or I don't know, hand it off. That's what insurance is for, and the premium is worth paying even though the expected value is against you.

S2: Notice what that test doesn't depend on.

S1: Not on how likely the loss is, and not on how big it is in the abstract. On how big it is compared with what you have. The same two thousand dollar risk is an inconvenience to one household and a catastrophe to another, so the same policy is a waste for one and essential for the other.

S2: Which is lesson two's cushion again.

S1: Arriving in a new place. The cushion is self insurance for small risks, and the more of it you have, the more risk you can sensibly carry yourself.

S2: Sort four things for me. Someone with fifteen hundred dollars in savings and no dependants. A four hundred dollar phone, a car worth nine thousand that they need to get to work, the chance of a long illness that stops them working, and a hospital stay.

S1: Phone, carry it. Losing four hundred hurts, but it doesn't break anything, and the extended warranty is the classic case of paying a margin on an inconvenience.

S2: The car?

S1: Insure, and note that liability cover for damage done to other people is both legally required in most places and the genuinely unlimited risk here. The nine thousand is the small part.

S2: The long illness?

S1: This is the one people skip, and for someone with fifteen hundred dollars and no fallback it's the largest uninsured risk on the list. Which is what disability cover exists for. And insure the hospital stay, because US bills routinely run past everything else on that list combined.

S2: Does any of it change at forty thousand in savings?

S1: The car's own value moves into, could absorb, so a higher deductible becomes sensible. The liability and health answers don't move at all, because forty thousand doesn't touch what those can cost.

S1: And the standing note again: this is education, not personalised advice. Thanks for listening. Insure the catastrophe, not the inconvenience.
