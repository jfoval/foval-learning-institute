S1: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm John.

S2: And I'm Haley. Today we're starting the Personal Finance Fundamentals course, on the first lesson. Where your money actually goes.

S1: And I want to open with a number, because it's the number the whole lesson hangs on. In twenty twenty two, a market research firm asked a thousand Americans to guess how much they spend on subscriptions every month. The average guess was eighty six dollars.

S2: Okay. And the real figure?

S1: Two hundred and nineteen. About two and a half times the guess. And forty two percent of people were paying for at least one subscription they had completely forgotten about.

S2: That's the bit that gets me. Not that they were wrong, but that they were wrong in the same direction.

S1: That's exactly it. The gap between what you believe you spend and what you actually spend is not random. It runs one way. And closing that gap is the entire job of this lesson, and the tool is about as unglamorous as it gets. One hour with last month's bank and card statements.

S2: So before we ask people to spend an hour on that, is there any evidence it does anything?

S1: There is. A twenty twenty two meta analysis of seventy six randomised trials, over a hundred and sixty thousand people, found that financial education causes real improvements in knowledge and in behaviour, strongest in budgeting, saving and credit. And the same study is honest about the limit: the effects on behaviour are smaller than on knowledge. Knowing where your money goes doesn't change anything by itself. It makes the changes possible.

S2: There's a three question test near the top of the lesson, from two economists, Annamaria Lusardi and Olivia Mitchell, who spent two decades testing what people know about money. Listeners, answer along.

S1: One. A hundred dollars in a savings account earning two percent a year. After five years, is the balance more than a hundred and two, exactly a hundred and two, or less? Two. Your account pays one percent and inflation is two percent. After a year, can you buy more than today, the same, or less? Three. True or false. Buying a single company's stock usually gives you a safer return than a stock mutual fund.

S2: Answers. More than a hundred and two, because the interest earns interest. Less than today, because your balance grew one percent and prices grew two. And false, because one company can fail and a fund spreads that across many.

S1: And how many people get all three?

S2: About thirty percent of adults worldwide. And it isn't a trivia score. People who get them right are markedly more likely to plan for retirement and to save.

S1: Before the counting, the lesson defines what winning looks like, and not as a dollar figure. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau landed on four parts, and the fourth is having the freedom to make the choices that let you enjoy your life.

S2: Which is a quarter of the definition. Enjoying your life is not the thing you sacrifice for financial health.

S1: So let's count. What's the method?

S2: Every transaction from your last full month, into one of six buckets. Housing. Food, with groceries and eating out kept separate. Transport. Debt payments. Subscriptions. And everything else. Total each, and compare to your take home pay.

S1: And the lesson works one all the way through.

S2: Maya takes home three thousand four hundred a month. Housing, one thousand two hundred and forty five. Food, eight hundred. Transport, five hundred and forty five. Debt, a hundred and eighty. Subscriptions, eighty seven. Everything else, two hundred and sixty. Total, three thousand one hundred and seventeen, so two hundred and eighty three stayed put. About eight percent.

S1: And what had she believed?

S2: That she saves a few hundred, maybe more, and doesn't spend much on delivery. The delivery line on its own was three hundred and eighty five dollars, out of an eight hundred dollar food bucket.

S1: And nothing about that month was reckless.

S2: It just wasn't what she believed. That's the finding.

S1: Then the lesson sorts the same spending a second way, and this is the part I'd want people to hear if they hear nothing else.

S2: Fixed against variable. Fixed costs arrive the same size every month and take real effort to change. Rent, insurance, the car payment, the loan minimum, subscriptions. Variable costs move with your choices week to week. Groceries, fuel, eating out.

S1: And why does the cut matter?

S2: Because it tells you which kind of problem you have. Variable costs respond to daily decisions, so willpower works on them. Fixed costs only respond to structural moves, and each of those is done once rather than resisted daily.

S1: Where does Maya land?

S2: About one thousand nine hundred and seventeen. Fifty six percent of her take home, set months ago by a lease and a car loan. No amount of discipline this week touches it.

S1: And there's a warning line in the lesson.

S2: From Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi. When your must haves go past fifty percent of your after tax income, money will feel tight no matter how carefully you live. Must haves is wider than fixed costs, because it includes basic groceries and the fuel to get to work. Count Maya that way and she's at about seventy percent.

S1: So the diagnosis writes itself.

S2: Her budget will feel tight, the tightness is structural, and if she wants real room the lever is the car or the rent or her income. Not her coffee.

S1: And treating a structural problem as a willpower problem gets you what?

S2: Months of self blame and no progress. That's the sentence I'd underline.

S1: There's one more thing I want to flag, because it's a habit rather than a fact.

S2: The forty percent number.

S1: Right. The headline that forty percent of Americans can't cover a four hundred dollar emergency.

S2: The number is real, and it measures something different from what the headline says. The Federal Reserve's survey asks how you would handle a four hundred dollar expense, and answering credit card, paid off over time, counts against paying with cash. Even if you could have paid cash.

S1: And when someone looked at actual accounts?

S2: The JPMorgan Chase Institute looked at real balances, and ninety two percent of households could cover a four hundred dollar shock. Including seventy seven percent of the lowest income quartile.

S1: So the number isn't invented. It's answering a different question.

S2: And that's the habit the course wants. Ask what a statistic measured before you let it tell you something about yourself.

S1: If somebody does one thing after this episode?

S2: One hour, last month's statements, six buckets. Everything in this course is built on the numbers you produce in that hour, and almost nobody has them.
