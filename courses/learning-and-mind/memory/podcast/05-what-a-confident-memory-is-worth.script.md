S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Memory, lesson five. What a confident memory is worth.

S2: You told me this is the best-evidenced thing in the course.

S1: And the hardest lesson to write, because the honest answer has a condition attached to every part of it. I'll be slower than usual here, and the slowness is the point.

S2: Start with what everybody knows.

S1: A confident witness is no more likely to be right than an unconfident one. That's the position the lesson starts from, and the synthesis describes it as one the legal system increasingly accepts. This course hasn't read a textbook or a judicial direction stating it, so where exactly it's written down is something the lesson doesn't claim.

S2: And the synthesis disagrees with it.

S1: It does, and how it disagrees is worth as much as the finding. Here's its opening, in the authors' words. The U.S. legal system increasingly accepts the idea that the confidence expressed by an eyewitness who identified a suspect from a lineup provides little information as to the accuracy of that identification. There was a time when this pessimistic assessment was entirely reasonable because of the questionable eyewitness-identification procedures that police commonly employed.

S2: They start by agreeing.

S1: They start by saying the old view was correct about the evidence that produced it, and that the evidence changed. Not everyone was wrong. That's a model of how to disagree with somebody and I'd keep it whatever you make of the rest.

S2: Whose paper is this and how much of it did the course read?

S1: Wixted and Wells, twenty seventeen. This course read its Summary, its Box three, the section introducing the five recommendations, and one field-study passage. The fifty pages of evidence review weren't opened, so what follows is the paper's own account of its evidence rather than this course weighing it.

S2: Then their question.

S1: In their words. After thirty years of research, the time seems ripe to ask how eyewitness confidence informs accuracy under more pristine testing conditions. Initial, uncontaminated memory tests using fair lineups, with no lineup administrator influence, and with an immediate confidence statement.

S2: And their answer.

S1: Under those conditions, mock-crime studies and police department field studies have consistently shown that, for adults, confidence and accuracy are strongly related, and high-confidence suspect identifications are remarkably accurate.

S1: And here's the other half of the same sentence, which has to travel with it. However, when certain non-pristine testing conditions prevail, for example when unfair lineups are used, the accuracy of even a high-confidence suspect identification is seriously compromised.

S2: So what are the conditions?

S1: Box three of the paper, verbatim and complete. Five of them. One, include only one suspect per lineup. Two, the suspect should not stand out in the lineup. Three, caution that the offender might not be in the lineup. Four, use double-blind testing. Five, collect a confidence statement at the time of the identification.

S2: Say what each is protecting against, because some of those sound obvious.

S1: I'll say it, and I'll say first that these five sentences are the course's own reasoning from the conditions, not the paper's account of them. One suspect: a lineup where everybody is a suspect is a test with no wrong answer available. Not standing out: if the suspect is the only one matching the description, the lineup asks which of these looks most like your description, rather than is your man here. The caution: without it a witness reasonably assumes the offender is present and picks whoever is closest. Double-blind: an administrator who knows the answer can signal it without meaning to. And confidence at the time: everything afterwards can change how confident somebody feels, and lesson four is one mechanism by which.

S2: Give me the numbers. What is a high-confidence identification actually worth?

S1: In one police-department field study the synthesis analyses, of seventy-two suspect identifications made with high confidence at the identification itself, ninety-six percent were correct. A signal-detection model fitted to those data estimated high-confidence accuracy at approximately ninety-seven percent correct.

S2: Higher than I'd have guessed.

S1: Almost everybody guesses too low there, and too high at the other end. The same model estimated low-confidence accuracy at closer to fifty percent correct.

S2: Fifty.

S1: A witness who says they aren't sure is, on that estimate, about as likely to be wrong as right. And that's the number that matters more, because it's the one nobody expects. The traditional position was that confidence tells you nothing. This says it tells you a great deal, and most of what it tells you is on the low-confidence side, where it's a warning.

S2: Before I carry any of that away, what's the scope?

S1: Good, because the lesson gives it every time. One field study, analysed inside a synthesis this course read four sections of, on adults, on initial confidence statements. And what this course read doesn't say which of the five conditions that police department met, which the figures are worth little without.

S2: You keep saying initial.

S1: It's the word the whole thing rests on, and it's the first word of the list of pristine conditions. The claim is about confidence expressed at the moment of the identification. Not confidence in court. Not confidence after months of being treated as the witness whose identification mattered. A lesson that dropped it would be teaching the opposite of the paper.

S2: Then let me try the conclusion somebody will reach. Eyewitness identification is reliable after all, and the reform campaigns were overblown.

S1: Almost everything is wrong with that. The conclusion is conditional and the conditions are the reforms. And the authors say so themselves, in the sentence most likely to be dropped. Unfortunately, some jurisdictions have not yet made reforms that would create pristine testing conditions and, hence, our conclusions about the reliability of high-confidence identifications cannot yet be applied to those jurisdictions.

S2: That's present tense.

S1: It is. And there's a second error in that conclusion, and it's subtler. The finding isn't that eyewitness identification is reliable. It's that confidence is informative, which cuts both ways. Initial high confidence is very accurate and initial low confidence is about a coin toss. A system that took this seriously would convict on some identifications and throw out others, which is a different thing from trusting witnesses more.

S2: Give me the honest sentence.

S1: It's a mouthful. Under five stated procedural conditions, for adults, an initial confidence statement carries a great deal of information about whether an identification is correct, in both directions, and those conditions aren't met everywhere.

S2: What's in the lesson we haven't done?

S1: An exercise that has you take a real identification procedure from a news report or a case write-up and check it against Box three one condition at a time, where the commonest answer is that the account doesn't say. A second exercise on writing the strongest case for the traditional position and then against it, same length, using the synthesis's own words for both. And three things people get wrong about this, including the one about a confident witness in court.
