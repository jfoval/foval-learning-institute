S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Memory, lesson two. What forgetting looks like when somebody plots it.

S2: This is the curve on every poster about studying.

S1: It is, and the lesson is about where it came from, what it measures, and the two things that fall off it before it reaches you.

S2: Start with what it measures, because I've only ever seen the picture.

S1: Then you're already ahead of most people, because the picture usually has nothing on the vertical axis. The measure is savings. You learn a list until you can produce it. Some time later you learn it again, and the question is how much of the original effort you're spared. If learning took twenty minutes and relearning takes ten, you saved half.

S2: That isn't the same as how much I can remember.

S1: It isn't, and the difference isn't a technicality. Somebody can be unable to produce a single item and still show substantial savings, because relearning is easier than learning even when nothing comes back on demand. That follows from the definitions rather than from any comparison this course read, and the lesson says so where it says it. On a savings measure that material is partly there. On a recall measure it's entirely gone.

S2: So which one is right?

S1: Both. They're answering different questions. Focus and Deep Work lesson one called this the instrument question, and this is the same question in a subject a century older. Before asking whether a claim is true, ask what would have had to be measured.

S2: And here it bites, I take it.

S1: Hard. Because the figure that travels from this curve gets reported as though it were recall. You forget half of what you learn in an hour is a sentence about production. The curve behind it is about relearning.

S2: All right. The study.

S1: Murre and Dros published a replication in twenty fifteen. This course read its abstract and the summary statements of method and result, and not the curve fitting. And it didn't open the eighteen eighty-five monograph the replication replicates, so everything the lesson says about the original reaches you at one remove, through the twenty fifteen paper.

S2: Noted. What did they do?

S1: Here's the design in their own words. One subject spent seventy hours learning lists and relearning them after twenty minutes, one hour, nine hours, one day, two days, or thirty-one days.

S2: One subject.

S1: One. Not a small sample. One person, doing seventy hours of it, on nonsense syllables, across six relearning intervals from twenty minutes to a month.

S2: Then I'll ask the obvious question. Is a study with one participant worthless?

S1: Most people say yes, some say it depends, and the useful answer is narrower than either. What it can't do is tell you about people. One person isn't a sample of a population, and no amount of data from that person makes it one. Any sentence beginning "people forget" is out of reach.

S2: And what it can do?

S1: Test whether an older result reproduces, which is exactly what it was built for. The study it replicates was also a study of one subject, from the eighteen eighties. Replicating a one-subject study with one subject is the right shape of test for the question does this hold up. It's the wrong shape for is this true of everybody.

S2: So the honest sentence is what?

S1: In one modern participant, the savings curve reproduced the shape reported in the eighteen eighties. That's narrow, it's real, and it's very much more than nothing given how much of this subject rests on the original.

S2: What did they conclude?

S1: Verbatim. We conclude that the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve has indeed been replicated and that it is not completely smooth but most probably shows a jump upwards starting at the twenty-four hour data point.

S2: Two things in one sentence.

S1: And they pull in different directions. The curve replicated. And the shape isn't the one everybody draws.

S2: What's a jump upwards in savings?

S1: More of the original learning surviving at that point than a smooth decay would predict. Not that forgetting stops. The measured value at a day sits higher than the smooth curve through the other points would put it. And this course can't tell you why, because the analysis that would bear on it is in the parts of the paper nobody here opened.

S2: Then what does it establish?

S1: Something smaller and more useful. The curve on the poster is smoother than the curve in the data. A curve drawn from an equation is a model of the measurements, and the measurements didn't lie on it.

S2: Give me the two instruments with numbers, because I want to feel the size of the gap.

S1: The lesson builds a case for exactly that, and says it's constructed, because the paper reports one person's curve rather than a comparison of instruments. Priya learns twenty Welsh words on a Monday. She can produce all twenty at the end of the session, and it took her eighteen minutes.

S2: Fine.

S1: On Friday somebody asks her to write down as many as she can. She gets four. On a recall measure she's retained twenty percent, and if somebody put that on a slide, that's the figure that would travel.

S2: And savings?

S1: Same Friday, she relearns the list to the same standard. Seven minutes against the original eighteen. On a savings measure she's retained about sixty percent. And notice which way the gap runs in her case: savings is the larger of the two. Whether that holds generally isn't something this course can tell you, because no source it read compares the two measures on the same material.

S2: Same person, same material, same day.

S1: And two answers that differ by a factor of three. Neither is wrong. They're answers to different questions. What can you produce right now, and how much of the work is still done.

S2: So is the curve useless?

S1: No, and the reason is worth more than the curve. What it establishes is the shape of the problem, and the shape is what you act on. Forgetting is fast at first and slows down. That isn't in doubt, it's what the replication reproduced, and it's why How to Learn Anything lesson four's spacing advice is built the way it is. The first review matters more than the fourth.

S2: And what it doesn't establish.

S1: Any number you should plan with. The interval schedule that lesson gives you comes from studies designed for the purpose, not from this curve, and nothing here undermines it.

S2: What's left in the lesson?

S1: An exercise that has you measure savings on yourself over a week, in three short sittings, so the two numbers are yours rather than Priya's. A second exercise on finding the curve in the wild and checking four things about how it's presented. And the sourcing note, which sets out every claim in the lesson that is the course's own reasoning rather than the paper's.
