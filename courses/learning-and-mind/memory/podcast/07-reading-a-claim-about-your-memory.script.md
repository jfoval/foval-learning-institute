S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Memory, lesson seven, which is the last one. Reading a claim about your memory.

S2: Every figure in the other six will date, I take it.

S1: All of them. This lesson is about what's left when they have. And the subject has an unusually confident popular literature sitting on top of unusually careful research, so the gap between the two is where you'll meet almost everything anybody tells you about memory.

S2: Start with the sorting, because I know the institute has a standard one.

S1: Three categories, and five courses before this one use it. Measurable: a study could check it. Unmeasurable as stated: it uses a term nobody has defined. False: it's been checked and it didn't hold. The lesson works four claims, and they're specimens written for the lesson rather than quotations from anywhere.

S2: Work one.

S1: Most people forget half of what they learn within an hour. Measurable, and something close to it has been measured. Lesson two's replication plotted savings on relearning for one subject across six delays. So the sort's easy and the sentence still misleads, for a reason we'll get to.

S2: Work two.

S1: Memory is reconstructive, so your memories are not recordings. Measurable in the second half and almost content-free in the first. Nothing in the sentence says what a reconstruction would have to do for the claim to fail, and a claim that nothing could falsify isn't a finding. As it stands it's a framing, and framings are useful without being results.

S2: Which is awkward, since that's the course's own opening idea.

S1: It is, and the lesson says so rather than hiding it.

S2: Work three.

S1: Eyewitness testimony is notoriously unreliable. Measurable, and the answer turns out to be conditional, which is the most interesting sort in the course. Lesson five's synthesis opens by saying the pessimistic view was entirely reasonable for the procedures that produced it, and then reports that under five named conditions an initial high-confidence identification by an adult is remarkably accurate.

S2: So it's false.

S1: False of a properly run lineup, and still true where the five conditions haven't been adopted, which the paper says is many jurisdictions and says in the present tense. A sorter who stopped at false would have dropped the sentence the synthesis is most often quoted without.

S2: You said there's a second habit that's this course's own.

S1: It is, and it's here because of something the research turned up. The samples behind this course are the smallest you'll meet on the Core path, and the claims made about memory in general are among the largest. That comparison is the course's own rather than a finding, and it's a comparison with the courses on the path rather than with every study anybody has run.

S2: How small is small?

S1: Lesson two's founding measurement of the whole field is one subject. Lesson four's famous demonstration is a hundred and fifty undergraduates in nineteen seventy-four. Lesson six's training study is about fifty people. Set those against what gets said about memory in general, which is usually about everybody who has ever lived.

S2: Then does a finding from a sample that small tell you anything at all?

S1: Yes, and saying exactly what takes a sentence rather than a shrug. A small sample is bad at one specific job: telling you how big an effect is in a population. It's much better at a different one, which is showing that something happens at all. One subject relearning lists over thirty-one days can't tell you how fast you forget. It can show that savings on relearning falls steeply and then flattens.

S2: So the question isn't only whether somebody checked.

S1: It's how many people they checked and who those people were. And the trouble comes when a demonstration gets reported as an estimate. You forget half of what you learn in an hour takes a curve measured on one person relearning nonsense syllables and prints it as a fact about you learning anything. The study is sound and the sentence isn't its finding.

S2: The lesson has a table of all five sources. Give me the shape of it.

S1: Four columns. What the source is, what it rests on, what read level this course holds it at, and what it licenses, with that last column marked as the course's own reading. Three rows will do. The forgetting curve replication: one subject, seventy hours, six delays, read at abstract and summary statements, licensing the shape of forgetting on relearning and not a rate for you. The questions and memory study: a hundred and fifty participants in nineteen seventy-four, read through a teaching summary rather than the paper. And the method of loci training: about fifty people in three arms, read at the abstract and the participant counts.

S2: And the pattern?

S1: In every row the licence is narrower than the source's reputation, and in every row something real survives. One row points the other way from the claim you'll meet in the wild, which is the eyewitness one, and that's what makes it the interesting row.

S2: Anything else in the table worth saying out loud?

S1: One thing. The pooled review in lesson three is the only source here that turns many studies into a single estimate, and it supports the narrowest claim of the five. That isn't a coincidence. Pooling is what turns a demonstration into an estimate, and estimates are usually smaller than the demonstrations that made people interested.

S2: All right. The thing I actually want. Can this course tell me whether something I remember happened the way I remember it?

S1: No, and this is the refusal, and it's sharper than the ones ending the two courses before this. Not the argument, not the childhood scene, not what somebody said to you last year. Nothing in seven lessons is a test you can run on one recollection.

S2: Then what have I got?

S1: What memory does in general, under conditions somebody arranged and measured. The difference is the last thing the course teaches.

S2: And I assume the over-correction is out too.

S1: Both directions are the same mistake. Take lesson four and conclude that a person contradicting you has been led by a question, and you've gone past the evidence. Finish the course and decide to stop trusting anything you remember, and you've done the same thing in the other direction. That's a verdict on particular memories reached from general findings, and it costs you something real in exchange for a confidence that isn't more justified than the one it replaced.

S2: What's in the lesson we haven't done?

S1: A fourth sort, which is the commonest shape: two claims stuck together needing separate verdicts. The three very widely read books on memory, named and cited for nothing, with the reason. A checkpoint asking which parts of this course to hold most loosely and which most firmly, and the order isn't the obvious one. And the last exercise, which needs the account you wrote in lesson one and asks you to decide what to do with it, three endings and all three legitimate.

S2: Read it at Foval Learning Institute dot org. That's the course. Thanks for coming with us.
