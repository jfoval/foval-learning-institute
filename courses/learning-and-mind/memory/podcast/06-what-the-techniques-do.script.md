S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Memory, lesson six. What the techniques do.

S2: Five lessons of things going wrong, and now the one people actually want.

S1: And the honest verdict on it is narrower than the advertising and better than you might expect after everything that came before.

S2: The technique being the memory palace.

S1: The method of loci, which is the oldest technique in the subject. And here it is as it's generally described, which is not something the abstract this course read sets out. You take a route you know well, a walk through your house or a street you've lived on, and you place the things you want to remember at points along it. To recall them, you walk the route.

S2: Show me. I'd rather do it than be told about it.

S1: Then take a route, and I should say the route and the list are both invented. The front door. The hall table. The bottom stair. The bathroom sink. The bedroom window. Five points, in the order you'd walk them. And five things to buy. Bleach, a birthday card, batteries, bread, a bicycle pump. Bleach is poured over the front door, running down it and taking the paint off. The birthday card is on the hall table, propped open and singing. The batteries are stacked on the bottom stair, so you tread on them and they roll.

S2: And the last two are mine.

S1: The last two are yours. Bread and a bicycle pump, at the sink and the window. Do it now, before I say what the lesson had.

S2: A loaf jammed in the sink with the tap running into it. And the pump sticking out of the bedroom window like a cannon.

S1: Which is almost exactly what's written there, and yours would be better anyway, because you made it. Notice the two things you did. You put each item somewhere specific rather than thinking about it in general. And you made the picture strange.

S2: Why strange?

S1: Because an ordinary loaf on an ordinary worktop is not distinguishable from every other time you've seen a loaf on a worktop. The strangeness is part of the standard instruction, and nothing read for this course tests whether it helps.

S2: Now walk it back.

S1: Front door, hall table, bottom stair, sink, window. Bleach, card, batteries, bread, pump. That took two minutes to build and it's the whole of the method.

S2: All right, I'm convinced it does something. What's the evidence?

S1: Wagner and colleagues compared memory athletes with people who'd never used mnemonics, and then ran a training study. This course read the abstract and the participant counts, from the preprint. The published version wasn't located and the body wasn't read.

S2: And the design?

S1: Three arms. Memory training, working-memory training, or no intervention. Six weeks. About fifty people across the arms, with the athletes of the first study counted separately, and a behavioural retest at four months.

S2: Why would a study bother with the middle arm? The comparison everybody wants is training against nothing.

S1: That's Focus and Deep Work lesson six arriving in a new subject. A group that does nothing controls for almost nothing. The trained group turned up, did something for six weeks, expected to improve, got attention from researchers, and practised being tested. All of those move scores on their own.

S2: And a do-nothing group has none of them.

S1: None. The working-memory arm is an active control, which is what Focus lesson six called a treated control condition. Similar time, similar effort, similar attention, and it isn't the technique under test. What survives that comparison is attributable to the technique rather than to the fact of training.

S2: That's better than most of what we've talked about.

S1: It is, and it isn't a claim that the study settles anything. It's a claim that its design doesn't have the hole that emptied a neighbouring literature, where the whole far-transfer verdict turned on whether the control group had been given something to do.

S2: So how much better did the trained group get?

S1: The lesson doesn't tell you, and it says so twice. The abstract reports the design and the durability finding and gives no count of words recalled, before or after, by anybody. The numbers are in the body and the body wasn't opened.

S2: You could have found one somewhere.

S1: Very easily, and nothing would look wrong. That's the trouble. A figure whose provenance nobody states is the defect this course exists to teach, and printing one here would be worse than teaching without one. So the honest statement is: six weeks of training in the method of loci produced durable, longer-lasting memories, in a three-arm study of about fifty people, retested at four months. Which arm the effect was measured against is in the body, and the body wasn't opened.

S2: Then the question I actually care about. Will it help me remember what I read?

S1: That's the transfer question, which Focus lesson six taught and this lesson uses rather than re-teaching. Near transfer: you get better at the thing you practised. Intermediate: better at something different drawing on the same system. Far: better at something out in your life.

S2: And where does a memory palace sit?

S1: On ordered lists of arbitrary items, which is the research file's own description of what it's for rather than a list of materials anything read here tested. That isn't a small category and it's a specific one. Whether it reaches the material you actually want is the transfer question, and this course hasn't answered it. A memory palace for a shopping list and one for the argument of a book are different propositions.

S2: So is this the lesson where the course finally gives me something that works?

S1: Partly, and the qualifications are the useful part. Yes, in the sense that the technique was trained and something durable came of it. No, in the sense that the thing most people want isn't that. They want to remember what they read, and the names of people they met last month.

S2: And the cost?

S1: The lesson flags this as its own observation. Building a palace takes time and using one takes attention during encoding, and no study read here compares the technique against spending the same time rehearsing. A technique that works and costs more than the thing it saves is a bad deal.

S2: Give me the recommendation.

S1: If you've got ordered arbitrary material you must hold, this is the only method this course found a controlled training study for, and it's worth an afternoon to learn. If what you want is to remember what you read, this course can't tell you whether it helps, and somebody who tells you it does is going past the evidence.

S2: What's in the lesson we haven't done?

S1: An invented advertising claim, sorted sentence by sentence, where neither sentence is a lie and the selling happens in putting them next to each other. An exercise that has you build a palace properly and use it twice a week apart, to find out what it costs rather than whether it works. And a second exercise on sorting a memory product by the level it claims against the level its evidence reaches.

S2: Read it at Foval Learning Institute dot org. Lesson seven next, and it's the last one.
