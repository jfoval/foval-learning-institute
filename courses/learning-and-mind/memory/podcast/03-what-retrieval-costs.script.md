S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Memory, lesson three. What retrieving one thing does to its neighbours.

S2: How to Learn Anything told me testing myself is the best thing I can do. Is this the episode where you take it back?

S1: It isn't, and the lesson says so early and says it twice. But this is the lesson that talks to that course most directly, so let's do the finding first and the reconciliation after.

S2: Go on.

S1: Here it is in the meta-analysts' own words. Retrieving a subset of items can cause the forgetting of other items. It has a name. Retrieval-induced forgetting.

S2: Other items meaning what, exactly?

S1: The paradigm is worth having concretely, and I should say that the description is the course's own account of the design rather than a passage it quotes. You learn a set of items in categories. Then you practise retrieving some of the items from some of the categories. Then later you're tested on everything. Three kinds of item come out of that. The ones you practised. The ones you didn't practise from categories you did. And the ones from categories you never touched.

S2: And the finding is about the middle group.

S1: The middle group. They come out worse than the untouched ones. Not the ones you ignored. The ones that were sitting next to something you retrieved.

S2: That's unsettling. How big is it?

S1: Murayama, Miyatsu, Buchli and Storm published the meta-analysis in twenty fourteen. This course read its abstract, its overall effect sizes and its output-interference analysis, and not the theoretical sections. The headline effect size is a g of nought point three five, with a confidence interval from nought point three two to nought point three eight, across four hundred and seventy-two samples.

S2: I can't picture that.

S1: Nor can I, which is why the lesson is built on the raw numbers instead. In the authors' words, the average raw mean difference was eight point seven percent for the entire sample, ten point nine percent for samples that did not control output interference, and five point nought percent for samples that did.

S2: So it halves.

S1: It halves, on one choice about how the final test is run. And both intervals exclude zero, so something is there in both. A single reported figure here is a statement about the method as much as about memory.

S2: Then explain output interference, because that's doing all the work.

S1: And the explanation I'm about to give is the course's own reasoning from the analysis the paper reports, not a passage from it. At the end of the experiment you're tested on everything. If the test asks for the practised items first, then by the time it asks for the unpractised ones you've just spent several minutes producing their neighbours. That act of producing interferes with what comes next, quite apart from whatever the practice did earlier.

S2: So an uncontrolled study measures two things at once.

S1: The effect of the practice, and the effect of having just recalled a pile of related items. Control for it and the effect halves.

S2: Does that make the whole thing an artefact?

S1: No, and the lesson is careful here. The controlled figure is five percent, with an interval from three point seven to six point three, which excludes zero. What it shows is that about half of the number people quote is coming from the test rather than from the practice.

S2: Do they know why it happens at all?

S1: They set the dispute out themselves, and a course that reported one side would be misrepresenting a paper that doesn't. One camp says it's an inhibitory mechanism that reduces the accessibility of items competing with the one you're retrieving. The other says inhibition isn't necessary, and that competition or blocking explains it.

S2: And the verdict?

S1: Their own words. The results largely supported inhibition accounts, but also provided some challenging evidence, with the nature of the results often varying as a function of how retrieval-induced forgetting was assessed.

S2: Read that last part again.

S1: With the nature of the results often varying as a function of how it was assessed. The answer depends on how the effect was measured, which is the third time in three lessons that the instrument has turned out to be doing part of the work.

S2: All right. Now the reconciliation, because I'm still holding lesson three of the earlier course.

S1: Two findings about two different groups of items, and the sizes aren't comparable. Retrieval practice produces large benefits on the material you practised. This literature reports a cost of about five to eleven percentage points on related material you didn't. Those aren't the same quantity and the first is much larger.

S2: So what changes?

S1: Not whether to test. What to test. If you test the same third of a topic every time, you're strengthening that third and, slightly, making the rest harder to reach. The fix is coverage, and it costs nothing. Test across the topic rather than the part you enjoy.

S2: And the honest limit?

S1: These are laboratory studies with word lists and category exemplars, over minutes. Nothing this course read tests the effect on material somebody chose, cared about, and came back to over weeks, which is the situation you're actually in. The lesson names that gap rather than papering over it.

S2: Give me the sentence to carry.

S1: Retrieval practice works, it isn't free, and the cost is paid by whatever you keep skipping.

S2: What have we left?

S1: A worked case with two students revising the same twelve-topic syllabus two different ways, which is where you can see the trade rather than being told about it. An exercise that has you map your own coverage, and most people find they've actually retrieved a third of their material or less. And a second exercise on reading a method rather than a result, which is the habit the whole institute runs on.
