S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Memory, lesson four. What a question does to a memory.

S2: You told me at the end of the last one that this is the famous one.

S1: The most famous experiment in the subject, and the lesson is about two things. What it found, and how hard it turned out to be to check.

S2: Start with the finding.

S1: One thing first, because it shapes everything else. This course could not open the original paper. What it read is a published teaching summary, from an examination board's own resource pages, and every figure I'm about to give you is held at that level and not higher.

S2: Understood. Now the finding.

S1: Nineteen seventy-four. First experiment. Participants watched films of traffic accidents and were asked, about how fast were the cars going when they blank each other, with the blank filled by one of five verbs.

S2: And the verb changed the answer.

S1: Smashed, forty point eight miles an hour. Collided, thirty-nine point three. Bumped, thirty-eight point one. Hit, thirty-four. Contacted, thirty-one point eight.

S2: Nine miles an hour between the top and the bottom, for the same films.

S1: Differing only in one word. But the second experiment is the one that matters, and it's where the idea comes from. A hundred and fifty participants saw one clip of a multi-car crash. Fifty were asked how fast the cars were going when they smashed into each other. Fifty were asked the same with hit. Fifty weren't asked about speed at all.

S2: And then?

S1: A week later, all of them were asked a set of questions about the accident, including, did you see any broken glass.

S2: Was there any?

S1: None. Sixteen of the smashed group said yes. Seven of the hit group. Six of the control group.

S2: A week later, from one word. What do you make of that?

S1: The authors give two interpretations and the lesson keeps both, because the experiment doesn't settle between them. The first is that the question changed what people said. Nobody's certain either way, the word smashed implies a violent collision, and when somebody's asked a week later whether there was glass, the frame they were given nudges the guess. Nothing about the memory changed. The answer did.

S2: And the second?

S1: That the question changed what they had. The word entered the memory, the assembly now includes a more violent collision, and a violent collision is one with broken glass in it. On that reading the representation itself was altered by a word.

S2: Those are very different claims.

S1: Very. And they predict the same result in this experiment, which is why it can't tell them apart. What would separate them is something sensitive to what somebody has rather than to what they say. This course can't answer it from what it read, and the lesson says that's the shape of a test rather than a report of one.

S2: Let me ask about the size, because sixteen against six isn't many people.

S1: It isn't, and the lesson looks straight at it. The difference between the smashed condition and the control is ten people, out of a hundred that were asked. Between smashed and hit it's nine. Ten is the comparison the lesson rests on, because the control group is the one that was asked no speed question at all.

S2: And that's the foundation of an idea I've heard my whole life.

S1: Three things follow, and the third is the one this course insists on. One, the phenomenon is much better established than this study. Fifty years of work followed it, and this course has read almost none of it, so the lesson can teach the phenomenon and must not claim the evidence. Two, a lesson leaning on the size of the effect would be overreaching. Ten people is enough to notice something and not enough to say how big it is.

S2: And three?

S1: A famous study isn't automatically a strong study. Fame is a fact about citation.

S2: You said the lesson's real subject is the trace. What's the trace?

S1: This course tried to read the original and couldn't, and the lesson walks you through the trail step by step, because walking it is more useful than the result. Step one. The paper is Loftus and Palmer, nineteen seventy-four, in the Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, pages five eight five to five eight nine. Every citation this course saw gives those details and they agree.

S2: Step two.

S1: No copy this course could reach turned out to be the paper. Step three. What was reachable was a teaching summary, published by an examination board as a resource for students, giving both experiments, both tables and the authors' two interpretations. Step four. So the figures in this lesson are at one remove. They agree with the other summaries the course could reach, and agreement between summaries is not verification. It's what you'd see either way, because summaries copy each other.

S2: Somebody's going to say that's over-scrupulous. The figures are in twenty textbooks and they all agree.

S1: The weaker answer is that twenty textbooks agreeing is one source, twenty times. The stronger one is that the specific things that go missing are predictable. Not the headline figure, which survives. The range, the criterion, the sample and the instrument, which are what let you tell what the figure means. And in this case the read level is exactly the thing at risk. It would be very easy to write "Loftus and Palmer found" as though this course had read them, and nobody would ever know.

S2: Is there a cost to the fuss?

S1: There is, and the lesson owns it. This is a real, well-replicated phenomenon, and anybody who takes away "the broken glass study is dodgy" has learned the wrong thing. The study is fine as far as this course can tell. What's uncertain is this course's access to it, which is a statement about the course.

S2: What's in the lesson that we haven't done?

S1: An exercise that has you trace a famous study yourself in twenty minutes, with the five ways that can end and all of them counting as results. A second one on writing the same question two ways, neutrally and with a frame. And three things people get wrong about this study, including the commonest, which inflates sixteen out of fifty into everybody.
