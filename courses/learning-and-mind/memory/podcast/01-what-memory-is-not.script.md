S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're opening a course called Memory, with its first lesson. What memory is not.

S2: Not. That's a strange way to start a course.

S1: It is, and there's a reason for it. Ask most people how memory works and you get some version of a recording. Something happened, it went in, and remembering is playing it back. On that model a memory is either intact or it's degraded, and forgetting is loss.

S2: Which sounds right to me, honestly.

S1: It sounds right to nearly everybody. The account the research uses is different, and the difference is the whole course. Remembering is an act of assembly. What you've got stored is partial, and what arrives in your head is built from it each time, using what else you know, what you've been told since, and what the question you were asked suggests.

S2: Built each time. So the same memory gets built twice and the two builds needn't match.

S1: That's it. And I want to be careful here, because this isn't a new idea to anybody who's done the earlier course. How to Learn Anything already used reconstruction, to explain why testing yourself works: recall rebuilds a memory rather than reading it off a shelf, and the rebuilding is what strengthens it. That course used reconstruction to explain what builds a memory. This one's about what reconstruction does to one afterwards.

S2: All right, but how is that a claim and not just a nicer metaphor? What would be different if you were right?

S1: Good, because the lesson answers that, and it flags the answer as the course's own way of setting it out rather than something it read in one place. Three things come apart. One. On a recording model, how sure you feel about a memory ought to track how accurate it is, because the confidence would be reading off the state of the recording. On an assembly account it needn't, because the confidence gets generated along with the memory rather than before it.

S2: And has anybody measured that?

S1: Lesson five, and the answer there turns out to be conditional, which is the most interesting thing in the course. Two. On a recording model, asking somebody about an event doesn't change the recording. On an assembly account it can, because the question supplies material to the assembly. That's lesson four. Three. On a recording model, recalling one thing has no effect on the recordings next to it. On an assembly account it might, because retrieval is an act rather than a read. That's lesson three.

S2: So the course is three tests of one idea.

S1: More or less. And then a fourth lesson on the technique everybody asks about, and a last one on how to read the next claim you meet.

S2: Can I push on the evidence? Because I've noticed this subject gets talked about very confidently.

S1: Please, because the lesson does something unusual here. It prints a table of everything the course read properly, five sources, with a column for who each one was measured on. And then it tells you to read that column.

S2: Go on then.

S1: The forgetting curve replication, which is lesson two and the founding measurement of the whole field. One subject. One person, over seventy hours.

S2: One.

S1: One. The leading question experiments, which are the most famous thing in the subject. A hundred and fifty participants in the one that matters, in nineteen seventy-four. The mnemonic training study, which is lesson six. About fifty people. The eyewitness synthesis is a review of a literature rather than a single study, and it's about adults, on initial identifications, under lineup conditions many places haven't adopted. And the pooled review in lesson three covers four hundred and seventy-two samples from the published literature, and the lesson says plainly that this course didn't read who was in them.

S2: So the biggest claims in the popular version of this subject are sitting on the smallest studies in the course.

S1: That's the lesson's own line, near enough. And it says that isn't a failure of these particular papers. It's what this subject's best evidence looks like.

S2: Then why teach from them at all?

S1: Because small doesn't mean empty. It means narrow. Take the first row. What was measured is time saved on relearning, which is not the same as how much somebody could recall. Who was measured is one person. So a sentence beginning "people forget" is already wrong at the second word. But the sentence the row does support is, in one subject, relearning lists of nonsense syllables after gaps from twenty minutes to thirty-one days, the curve reproduced the shape reported in the eighteen eighties. That's duller, and it's the one lesson two can defend.

S2: Here's where I want to go, and I suspect you're going to stop me. If memory is assembled, and the evidence for all this is that thin, why would I trust anything I remember?

S1: You're right that I'm going to stop you, and the lesson thinks getting this right is worth more than any finding in it. Reconstruction is a claim about the mechanism. It's not a verdict on the output. A process that assembles can assemble accurately.

S2: Give me the case.

S1: Lesson five. In adults, on an initial identification, under five named lineup conditions, a confident identification turns out to be right the great majority of the time. And every one of those qualifications is carrying weight, especially the word initial. Those identifications are reconstructions like every other memory.

S2: So what does the assembly account actually buy me?

S1: A prediction about where the errors will be. Not scattered at random, but in the places assembly has to do work. Gaps filled in with whatever usually happens. Durations drifting. Details supplied by something said afterwards. That's much more useful than a general warning, because it tells you which parts of an account to check.

S2: And the cost of over-correcting?

S1: You lose the ability to tell a strong account from a weak one, which is the judgement this subject can actually help with. The lesson's closing sentence is narrower and better than either extreme. Memory is reconstructed, reconstruction fails in specific ways, and knowing the ways is what lets you tell those accounts apart.

S2: That's a good place to stop. What's in the lesson that we haven't done?

S1: A worked case the course built itself, one man's account of a family holiday written twice eight months apart, with four differences and what each one might mean. The full table with all five sources and what each was measured on. And the list of what this course deliberately leaves out, which includes memory and ageing, and why that's a decision rather than an oversight. There's also an exercise in there you should do before lesson two, because it's the first piece of the course project and it can't be done later.

S2: Read it at Foval Learning Institute dot org. We'll see you for lesson two.
