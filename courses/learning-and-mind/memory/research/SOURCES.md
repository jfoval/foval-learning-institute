## The first thing Stage 1 found, and it decides the course

**How to Learn Anything already teaches the constructive half of this subject**, and teaches it
well: storage strength against retrieval strength, working memory and chunking, retrieval practice,
spacing, and interleaving with its limits. `npm run taught memory` prints its objectives.

**So a second memory course has to be about something else or it is a restatement**, and the
something else is the half that course deliberately leaves: **memory as a thing that is
reconstructed every time it is used, and that is therefore wrong in particular, measurable ways.**

**That boundary is not a convenience; it is what the evidence supports.** The strongest literatures
this search found are all about failure and distortion rather than acquisition: what forgetting
looks like when somebody plots it, what retrieving one thing does to its neighbours, what a question
does to a memory, and what a confident recollection is actually worth. **Every one of those has a
recent synthesis and a popular version that has not caught up with it**, which is the shape this
institute's best courses have.

**One more thing the search establishes, and it should shape lesson 1.** This subject's popular
literature is unusually confident and its best sources are unusually careful. The eyewitness
synthesis below spends its first page saying that the pessimistic view everybody holds *was*
reasonable and is now out of date under particular conditions. **That is a more interesting shape
than "the science says you are wrong", and a course that flattened it would be doing the thing this
institute exists to teach against.**

## 1. What forgetting looks like when somebody plots it

**Murre and Dros, "Replication and Analysis of Ebbinghaus' Forgetting Curve", *PLOS ONE* 10(7),
2015, e0120644.** **Read in part on 2026-09-19**: the abstract verbatim and the summary statements
of method and result, from the open-access page. Open access, and the whole paper is available for a
later session that needs the curve fits.

- **The abstract, verbatim in full:** "We present a successful replication of Ebbinghaus' classic
  forgetting curve from 1880 based on the method of savings. One subject spent 70 hours learning
  lists and relearning them after 20 min, 1 hour, 9 hours, 1 day, 2 days, or 31 days. The results
  are similar to Ebbinghaus' original data. We analyze the effects of serial position on forgetting
  and investigate what mathematical equations present a good fit to the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve
  and its replications. We conclude that the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve has indeed been replicated
  and that it is not completely smooth but most probably shows a jump upwards starting at the 24
  hour data point."
- **What the method of savings is**, which a lesson has to explain before any curve makes sense: the
  measure is the relative amount of time saved on relearning something as a result of having learned
  it before. **It is not a percentage of items remembered**, and the difference matters, because a
  reader who meets the curve on a poster will assume it is.
- **The scope a lesson must carry, and it is the most striking thing here: one subject.** Seventy
  hours of one person's life, learning nonsense syllables, replicating a study of one person from
  the 1880s. **That is a real replication and it is a replication in the sense the word had in
  1885**, and a lesson that printed "the forgetting curve" without it would be doing exactly what
  this institute teaches against.
- **The jump at 24 hours** is the finding that is new and that nobody repeats. The curve is not the
  smooth exponential decay of the posters.

**Why this is the course's opening measurement.** It is the founding figure of the whole subject,
it is reported everywhere without its sample, and its replication is honest enough to say where the
original shape is wrong. **This is lesson 2's material and it is the direct counterpart of Habits
lesson 2 and Focus lesson 3.**

## 2. What retrieving one thing does to its neighbours

**Murayama, Miyatsu, Buchli and Storm, "Forgetting as a Consequence of Retrieval: A Meta-Analytic
Review of Retrieval-Induced Forgetting", *Psychological Bulletin* 140(5), 2014, pp. 1383 to 1409.**
**Read in part on 2026-09-19**, from the open copy in the University of Reading repository: the
abstract verbatim, the overall effect sizes, the output-interference analysis and the surrounding
discussion. The theoretical sections were not read.

- **The abstract's statement of the phenomenon, verbatim:** "Retrieving a subset of items can cause
  the forgetting of other items, a phenomenon referred to as retrieval-induced forgetting."
- **The contested question, in their words:** "According to some theorists, retrieval-induced
  forgetting is the consequence of an inhibitory mechanism that acts to reduce the accessibility of
  non-target items that interfere with the retrieval of target items. Other theorists argue that
  inhibition is unnecessary to account for retrieval-induced forgetting, contending instead that the
  phenomenon can be best explained by non-inhibitory mechanisms, such as strength-based competition
  or blocking."
- **What they conclude, verbatim, and note that it is not a clean win:** "The results largely
  supported inhibition accounts, but also provided some challenging evidence, with the nature of the
  results often varying as a function of how retrieval-induced forgetting was assessed."
- **The size of it.** Excluding studies where retrieval practice was replaced by something else, the
  average effect is **g = 0.35, 95% CI [0.32, 0.38]**, across 472 samples. In raw terms: "the
  average raw mean difference was 8.7% for the entire sample (k = 193, 95% CI = [7.5%, 9.8%]), 10.9%
  for samples that did not control output interference (k = 124; 95% CI = [9.4%, 12.4%]), and 5.0%
  for samples that did control output interference (k = 79; 95% CI = [3.7%, 6.3%])."
- **Read those three numbers together, because that is the lesson.** The effect is about nine
  percent overall, eleven percent when the method does not control for one particular artefact and
  **five percent when it does**. A single number here is a statement about a method as much as about
  memory, which is Focus lesson 4's point arriving in a different subject.
- **Heterogeneity was I² = 63.0 percent**, and the authors say the global analysis indicates a small
  to moderate effect that is nonetheless robust.

**Why this matters for the course's spine.** Retrieval practice is the single best-evidenced
technique in **How to Learn Anything**. This literature says the same act has a cost on the things
you did not retrieve. **Those two are not in conflict and a reader will think they are**, which
makes this the best bridge between the two courses.

## 3. What a question does to a memory

**Loftus and Palmer, "Reconstruction of automobile destruction: An example of the interaction
between language and memory", *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 13, 1974, pp. 585 to
589.** **The original was not opened. What was read is a published teaching summary of it**, from an
examination board's own resource pages, and the figures below come from that summary rather than
from the paper. **A lesson must say so where it uses them**, in the form Habits lesson 4 used for
Maltz.

At that level, established:

- **Experiment 1.** Participants watched films of traffic accidents and were asked "about how fast
  were the cars going when they ______ each other?", the blank filled by one of five verbs. Mean
  speed estimates: smashed 40.8 mph, collided 39.3, bumped 38.1, hit 34.0, contacted 31.8.
- **Experiment 2**, which is the one that matters. 150 participants saw one clip of a multi-car
  crash. Fifty were asked how fast the cars were going when they *smashed* into each other, fifty
  when they *hit*, and fifty were not asked about speed at all. **A week later** they were asked
  "Did you see any broken glass?" **There was no broken glass in the clip.** Sixteen of the smashed
  group said yes, seven of the hit group, and six of the control group.
- **The authors' own two interpretations**, which the summary reports and which a lesson should keep
  both of: a response-bias account, and a change in the memory representation itself.

**Three warnings for Stage 2, and the third is the important one.**

1. **This course could not verify the figures in the original**, and a lesson has to say that rather
   than presenting them as read.
2. **Sixteen against seven against six is a small study by any modern standard**, and the difference
   between conditions is a handful of people. A lesson that leans on the size of the effect rather
   than on its existence is overreaching.
3. **The claim this founds is enormous and the evidence for it is much broader than this one study**,
   which means a lesson can teach the phenomenon honestly while being careful about the study. **The
   trap is teaching the 1974 experiment as though it were the evidence.** It is the demonstration
   everybody knows; the evidence is fifty years of work this course has not read.

**A gap, recorded deliberately.** Search results repeatedly attribute a large meta-analysis of the
misinformation effect, with a figure around g = 0.739 from several hundred studies. **This course
could not find that paper, verify the figure, or establish who wrote it**, across several searches
and one full-text check of a paper that was supposed to cite it. **No lesson may print that
number**, and a later session that finds the source should record it here rather than in a lesson.

## 4. What a confident memory is worth

**Wixted and Wells, "The Relationship Between Eyewitness Confidence and Identification Accuracy: A
New Synthesis", *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 18(1), 2017, pp. 10 to 65.** **Read
in part on 2026-09-19**, from an openly posted copy: the Summary verbatim, Box 3 verbatim, the
section introducing the five recommendations, and one field-study passage. The fifty pages of
evidence review were not read.

- **The Summary, verbatim and in full**, because the shape of it is the lesson: "The U.S. legal
  system increasingly accepts the idea that the confidence expressed by an eyewitness who identified
  a suspect from a lineup provides little information as to the accuracy of that identification.
  There was a time when this pessimistic assessment was entirely reasonable because of the
  questionable eyewitness-identification procedures that police commonly employed. However, after
  more than 30 years of eyewitness-identification research, our understanding of how to properly
  conduct a lineup has evolved considerably, and the time seems ripe to ask how eyewitness
  confidence informs accuracy under more pristine testing conditions (e.g., initial, uncontaminated
  memory tests using fair lineups, with no lineup administrator influence, and with an immediate
  confidence statement). Under those conditions, mock-crime studies and police department field
  studies have consistently shown that, for adults, (a) confidence and accuracy are strongly related
  and (b) high-confidence suspect identifications are remarkably accurate. However, when certain
  non-pristine testing conditions prevail (e.g., when unfair lineups are used), the accuracy of even
  a high-confidence suspect ID is seriously compromised."
- **And the rest of that Summary, which is the part a lesson must not drop:** "Unfortunately, some
  jurisdictions have not yet made reforms that would create pristine testing conditions and, hence,
  our conclusions about the reliability of high-confidence identifications cannot yet be applied to
  those jurisdictions."
- **Box 3, "Pristine Lineup Conditions", verbatim and complete:** "1. Include only one suspect per
  lineup. 2. The suspect should not stand out in the lineup. 3. Caution that the offender might not
  be in the lineup. 4. Use double-blind testing. 5. Collect a confidence statement at the time of
  the identification."
- **What follows from the five, in their words:** "if the pristine conditions listed here
  (summarized in Box 3) are followed, then a low-confidence ID implies low accuracy, and a
  high-confidence ID implies high accuracy."
- **One field-study figure**, from a police-department study the paper analyses: of 72 suspect
  identifications made with high confidence, 96 percent were correct, and a signal-detection model
  fitted to those data "estimated high-confidence suspect-ID accuracy to be approximately 97%
  correct, whereas low-confidence suspect-ID accuracy was estimated to be closer to 50% correct."
- **The word doing the most work in the whole paper is "initial".** The claim is about confidence
  expressed at the moment of the identification, not confidence expressed in court months later.
  **A lesson that drops that word teaches the opposite of the paper.**

**Why this is the course's best lesson.** Everybody knows eyewitness confidence is worthless. That
belief was correct about the procedures that produced it, and the synthesis says so in its first
sentence. **The finding is not that everybody was wrong; it is that the answer depends on a list of
five conditions, and that the same evidence supports both exonerating the innocent and convicting
the guilty.** That is a harder and better lesson than a debunking.

**Scope a lesson must carry**: adults, under conditions many jurisdictions do not meet, on initial
identifications.

## 5. What the memory techniques actually do

**Isabella C. Wagner and colleagues, ["Durable memories and efficient neural coding through
mnemonic training using the method of
loci"](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.04.29.067561v1.full), preprint.** **Read in
part on 2026-09-19**: the abstract verbatim from the preprint page, and the participant counts.
**The published version was not located and the body was not read.** The first author's name and
the URL were added to this entry on 2026-09-19 from the preprint page itself, after a Stage 4
review found three lessons carrying them while this file did not.

- **The abstract, verbatim in part:** "Mnemonic techniques, such as the method of loci, can
  powerfully boost memory. Here, we compared memory athletes ranked among the world's top 50 in
  memory sports to mnemonics-naïve controls. In a second study, participants completed a six-weeks
  memory training, working memory training, or no intervention. Behaviorally, memory training
  enhanced durable, longer-lasting memories."
- **The design a lesson can use**: a six-week training study with **three arms**, memory training,
  working-memory training and no intervention, with a behavioural retest at four months. **The
  three arms and the six weeks are in the quoted abstract; the four-month retest and the
  participant counts are from the preprint page's own description of the design**, not from the
  sentences quoted above, and a lesson using the four months should say which it is leaning on.
- **Why the three arms matter**, and this is the link to Focus lesson 6: the working-memory training
  arm is an active control, and Focus lesson 6 is about a literature where the far-transfer verdict
  turned entirely on whether controls were treated. **A reader who has done that lesson can read
  this design and see why it is better than most.**
- **What this course cannot say**: how many words anybody recalled, before or after. The numbers
  are in the body and the body was not read. **Nor which arm the durability effect was measured
  against.** The abstract says memory training enhanced durable, longer-lasting memories and does
  not name the comparison, so a lesson must not write "against an active control": that is the
  result the three-arm design would be needed to show and the abstract declines to report it.
  **Nor what material the training was tested on**, which the abstract does not name either.

**And the honest frame for a lesson.** The method of loci is the oldest technique in the subject
and it works on the thing it works on: ordered lists of arbitrary items. **That last phrase is this
research file's own description of what the technique is for and not a finding**, and the abstract
read here describes the technique not at all: it names it in one clause and says mnemonic
techniques can powerfully boost memory. **Nothing read for this course describes walking a route,
placing items at points along it, or making the images strange**, and a lesson giving those has to
say they are the technique as generally described rather than as anything a source here sets out. **Whether it does anything for
the material a learner actually wants to remember is the transfer question**, which Focus lesson 6
answers for a different technique and which this course must not answer by analogy.

## What the field considers essential

In the order it builds, and this is the backbone Stage 2 should start from.

1. **What memory is not.** The recording metaphor, and why every finding in this course is a
   consequence of it being wrong. This is also where the boundary with How to Learn Anything is
   drawn.
2. **What forgetting looks like measured**, from source 1, including what the method of savings
   measures and the one-subject scope.
3. **Why interference explains more than time does**, from source 2, including what a single pooled
   number hides.
4. **What a question does to a memory**, from source 3, with its provenance handled honestly.
5. **What confidence is worth**, from source 4, which is the course's best lesson and its hardest.
6. **What the techniques do**, from source 5, and the transfer question.
7. **Reading a claim about memory**, which is this institute's closing move.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Memory is a recording."** Everything in this course is downstream of this being false.
- **"The forgetting curve shows you forget half of what you learn in an hour."** It shows savings on
  relearning, in one person, on nonsense syllables, and it is not smooth.
- **"You forget things because time passes."** Interference does more work than decay, and
  retrieving one thing can make its neighbours harder to reach.
- **"A confident witness is no more accurate than an unconfident one."** True of the procedures that
  produced the belief; not true of initial identifications under the five pristine conditions.
- **"Leading questions plant memories."** The effect is real and the 1974 study everybody cites is
  small; the evidence is much broader than that study and this course has read almost none of it.
- **"Memory athletes have better memories."** They have a technique, and the training studies are
  about what the technique does to ordered lists.

## Contested questions and the positions on each

**Is retrieval-induced forgetting caused by inhibition?** Standards 3.1 case 2, and the
meta-analysts state both positions themselves. **For inhibition**: the pattern of results across 470
samples largely supports it. **Against**: strength-based competition and blocking can account for
much of it, and the results vary by how the effect was assessed, which the authors say in their own
abstract. **What would settle it**: the authors discuss it and this course has not read that
section.

**Is eyewitness confidence informative?** Standards 3.1 case 2 shading towards case 1 under stated
conditions. **The traditional position** is that it is not, and the synthesis says that position was
entirely reasonable for the procedures that produced it. **The new synthesis** is that under five
named conditions, initial high confidence is highly accurate and initial low confidence signals a
high risk of error. **What would settle it further**: field studies in jurisdictions that have
adopted the reforms, which the paper calls for.

**Does mnemonic training transfer?** Standards 3.1 case 2 and this course has read too little.
Focus lesson 6's transfer distinction is the tool, and this course must apply it rather than
importing its answer.

## Open concerns for Stage 2

- **The boundary with How to Learn Anything must be drawn in lesson 1 and held.** Retrieval,
  spacing, interleaving, chunking and the two strengths are all taught there. **This course uses
  them and does not re-teach them**, and source 2 is the bridge.
- **Source 3's provenance is the course's own worked case.** The original was not opened, the
  figures come from a teaching summary, and a lesson must say so. **That is not a weakness to hide;
  it is lesson 4's teaching point**, the way Maltz was Habits lesson 4's.
- **The unverified meta-analysis figure must not appear in any lesson.** It is recorded above as a
  gap precisely so that a drafter meets the gap rather than the number.
- **This course touches a domain where being wrong has consequences for other people.** Eyewitness
  identification is about convictions. `sensitive_domain` is `false` and the call is worth stating:
  standards 3.4's list does not reach it, and lesson 5 must still be written with the care of a
  lesson that could be quoted at somebody.
- **Every sample here is small or specific.** One subject in source 1. A hundred and fifty
  undergraduates in source 3. Adults under conditions many jurisdictions do not meet in source 4.
  Fifty people in source 5. **A lesson naming that gap early is worth more than one caveat per
  figure**, which is what Focus lesson 1 did.
- **One thing this course will be asked and cannot answer**: whether memory declines with age in the
  way people fear. That literature was not searched and a course that improvised it would be doing
  harm. **Name it in lesson 1 as something the course is not doing.**
