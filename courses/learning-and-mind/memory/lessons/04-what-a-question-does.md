This is the most famous experiment in the subject, and this lesson is about two things: what it
found, and how hard it turned out to be to check.

## The claim

**A question isn't a neutral instrument.** Ask somebody about an event and the words you use supply
material, which the assembly in lesson 1 can take up. The 1974 experiments are the demonstration
everybody knows.

**This course could not open the original paper.**[1] What it read is a published teaching summary,
from an examination board's own resource pages, and **every figure below is held at that level and
not higher**. The second half of this lesson is about what that means.

## What the summary reports

**Experiment 1.** Participants watched films of traffic accidents and were asked "about how fast
were the cars going when they ______ each other?", with the blank filled by one of five verbs.[1]

| The verb | Mean speed estimate |
|---|---|
| Smashed | 40.8 mph |
| Collided | 39.3 mph |
| Bumped | 38.1 mph |
| Hit | 34.0 mph |
| Contacted | 31.8 mph |

**Nine miles an hour between the highest and the lowest**, for the same films, differing only in one
word.

**Experiment 2 is the one that matters**, and it is where the idea comes from.[1] A hundred and
fifty participants saw one clip of a multi-car crash. Fifty were asked how fast the cars were going
when they *smashed* into each other. Fifty were asked the same with *hit*. Fifty were not asked
about speed at all.

**A week later**, all of them were asked a set of questions about the accident, including: **"Did
you see any broken glass?"**

**There was no broken glass in the clip.**

| Condition | Said yes | Said no |
|---|---|---|
| Smashed | 16 | 34 |
| Hit | 7 | 43 |
| Control | 6 | 44 |

:::predict Before the interpretation. A week after a single word was changed, sixteen people in one group said they saw glass that was not there, against seven and six. Write down two different explanations for that, before reading the authors'.
Most people find the first and the second is the one this lesson is for.

**The first is that the question changed what they said.** People are not certain either way, the
word "smashed" implies a violent collision, and when somebody is asked a week later whether there
was glass, the frame they were given nudges the guess. **Nothing about the memory changed; the
answer did.**

**The second is that the question changed what they had.** The word entered the memory, the assembly
now includes a more violent collision, and a violent collision is one with broken glass in it. **On
this reading the representation itself was altered by a word.**

**Those are the authors' own two interpretations**, which the summary reports.[1] And notice that
they predict the same result in this experiment, which is why the experiment doesn't settle it.

**What would separate them** is the interesting question, and this course can't answer it from what
it read. **Something that pulled the two apart** would be needed: a measure sensitive to what
somebody has rather than what they say, or a design where the two accounts predict different
outcomes. **This is the shape of a distinguishing test and not a report of one.**[3]
:::

## What the study can and can't carry

**Take the broken-glass result seriously and look at what it is made of.** Sixteen against seven
against six. **The difference between the smashed condition and the control is ten people**, out of
a hundred that were asked.

**That is a real difference and it is a very small study**, by any modern standard, and it is the
foundation of an idea now repeated in every discussion of eyewitness memory.

**Three things follow, and the third is the one this course insists on.**

**One.** The phenomenon is much better established than this study. Fifty years of work followed it,
and **this course has read almost none of it**, which means the lesson can teach the phenomenon and
must not claim the evidence.

**Two.** A lesson that leaned on the size of the effect would be overreaching. Ten people is enough
to notice something and not enough to say how big it is.

**Three.** **A famous study isn't automatically a strong study**, and this is the clearest case in
the institute. Fame is a fact about citation.

## The trace, which is this lesson's real subject

**This course tried to read the original and could not.** Here is the trail, step by step, because
walking it is more useful than the result.

1. **The paper is Loftus and Palmer, 1974**, in the *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal
   Behavior*, pages 585 to 589. That much is uncontested and appears in every citation.
2. **It isn't open access**, and no copy this course could reach turned out to be the paper.
3. **What was reachable was a teaching summary**, published by an examination board as a resource
   for students, giving both experiments, both tables and the authors' two interpretations.
4. **So the figures in this lesson are at one remove.** They are consistent with what every
   secondary source reports, and consistency across secondary sources is not verification: it is
   what you would see either way, because secondary sources copy each other.

**That is the trace, and it ended at step four.** It is one of the five endings **Habits and
Self-Discipline** lesson 7 and **Focus and Deep Work** lesson 7 both name: the trail leads somewhere
you can't get to.

**And it is the honest state of this lesson.** A reader who wants the figures verified has to open
the paper, and if they do, they will know something this course doesn't.

:::checkpoint Somebody says this is over-scrupulous. The figures are in twenty textbooks, they all agree, and nobody seriously doubts them. Why does the lesson make a fuss?
It is a fair challenge and there are two answers, one weaker and one stronger.

**The weaker answer is that agreement between secondary sources is not evidence.** They copy each
other, which is exactly how the twenty-one days in **Habits and Self-Discipline** lesson 4 became a
fact, and how the figure in **Time Management** lesson 5 came to have no printed source at all.
Twenty textbooks agreeing is one source, twenty times.

**The stronger answer is that the specific things that go missing are predictable**, and this course
has watched them go missing four times now. Not the headline figure, which survives. **The range,
the criterion, the sample and the instrument**, which are what let you tell what the figure means.
**In this case the read level is exactly the thing at risk**: it would be very easy to write "Loftus
and Palmer found" as though this course had read them, and nobody would ever know.

**And there is a cost to the fuss, which the lesson should own.** This is a real, well-replicated
phenomenon, and a reader who takes away "the broken glass study is dodgy" has learned the wrong
thing. **The study is fine as far as this course can tell.** What is uncertain is this course's
access to it, which is a statement about the course.

**So the useful version:** the effect is real and broadly supported, the figures here come at one
remove and are stated as such, and if that distinction seems fussy, it is the same distinction that
caught three travelling numbers in the two courses before this one.
:::

## Three things people get wrong about this study

**"Leading questions implant memories."** Too strong for what this experiment shows, which is
consistent with two accounts, one of which involves no change to the memory at all. The authors give
both.

**"It proves eyewitness memory is unreliable."** It shows that a question can change an account.
What
that means for a real identification, under real procedures, is lesson 5, and the answer there is
more interesting than this sentence.

**"Everyone in the smashed group reported glass."** Sixteen of fifty. The commonest
misrepresentation of the finding, and it inflates a third into everybody.

## Practice

:::exercise Trace one yourself
Take 20 minutes, and stop at twenty whether or not you have finished.

**Pick a study you have heard about but never read.** The marshmallow test, the Stanford prison
experiment, the one about posture and confidence, anything with a name.

Follow the trail, writing each step.

1. **Who says it, and what do they cite?** If nothing, write "nothing", and you are finished.
2. **Find the citation. Can you reach the paper?** Not an abstract, not a summary. The paper.
3. **If you reach it, does it say what you were told?** Check one specific figure.
4. **If you can't reach it, what is the best source you can reach**, and what level is that?

**Five endings and all of them count**: you reach the paper and it agrees; you reach it and it
doesn't; the trail ends in nothing; the trail ends somewhere you can't get to; or twenty minutes
went.

**The fourth is the commonest and it is what happened to this lesson.**
:::

:::exercise Write the question twice
Take 15 minutes.

**Think of something you witnessed this week** that somebody else also saw. A meeting, an incident
in the street, a conversation.

**Write two questions about it.** One as neutrally as you can manage. One with a word in it that
supplies a frame, the way "smashed" does.

Then three lines.

1. **The two questions.**
2. **What each one supplies** that the other doesn't: not just tone, but the specific thing a
   respondent could take up.
3. **Which one you would have asked without thinking**, honestly.

**Most people find the leading version is the natural one**, which this course expects rather than
has measured.[3] Questions that supply a frame are easier to ask because they are more specific, and
specificity is what makes them leading.
:::
