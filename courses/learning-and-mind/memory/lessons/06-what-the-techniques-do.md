Five lessons of things going wrong. This one is about the technique people actually ask for, and the
honest verdict on it is narrower than the advertising and better than you might expect after
everything that came before.

## The technique

The method of loci is the oldest technique in the subject. **Here it is as it is generally
described, which is not something the abstract this course read sets out.**[3] You take a route you
know well, a walk through your house or a street you have lived on, and you place the things you
want to remember at points along it. To recall them, you walk the route.

It is the technique the training studies are about, and the athletes the first study compared were
ranked among the world's top 50 in memory sports.[1] **What proportion of competitors use it, and
on what material, is not something this course read.**

And it has been studied with a design worth looking at, which is what the next section is.

## The study

Wagner and colleagues compared memory athletes with people who had never used mnemonics, and then
ran a [training study](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.04.29.067561v1.full).[1] **This course has read the abstract and the participant counts, from the
preprint. The published version was not located and the body was not read.**

**In the authors' words:** "Mnemonic techniques, such as the method of loci, can powerfully boost
memory. Here, we compared memory athletes ranked among the world's top 50 in memory sports to
mnemonics-naïve controls. In a second study, participants completed a six-weeks memory training,
working memory training, or no intervention. Behaviorally, memory training enhanced durable,
longer-lasting memories."[1]

****Three arms**, about fifty people across them, with the athletes of the first study counted
separately. **The retest at four months comes from the preprint page's description of the design
rather than from the sentences quoted above**, which the research file records.[1]

:::predict Before the next section. Three arms: memory training, working-memory training, and no intervention. Why would a study bother with the middle one, when the comparison everybody wants is training against nothing?
This is **Focus and Deep Work** lesson 6 arriving in a new subject, and if you did that lesson the
answer's already yours.[2]

A group that does nothing controls for almost nothing. The trained group turned up, did
something for six weeks, expected to improve, got attention from researchers, and practised being
tested. **All of those move scores on their own**, and a no-intervention group has none of them.

The working-memory arm is an active control, which is what Focus lesson 6 called a treated control
condition. It takes similar time, similar effort and similar attention, and it isn't the technique
under test. **What survives the comparison with that arm is
attributable to the technique rather than to the fact of training.**

That is why this design is better than most in this area, and it is worth saying plainly after
what Focus lesson 6 found: in the working-memory training literature, the entire far-transfer
verdict turned on whether the control group had been given something to do.

Which is not a claim that this study settles anything. It is a claim that its design does not
have the specific hole that emptied a neighbouring literature.
:::

## What the lesson will not give you

A number. The abstract this course read reports the design and the durability finding and gives
no count of words recalled, before or after, by anybody.[1] **The numbers are in the body and the
body was not opened.**

That is worth pausing on, because a lesson with a figure in it reads as more useful. It'd be very
easy to reach for one from a secondary source, or from the popular writing about memory athletes,
and print it here. Nothing would look wrong, and that's the trouble.

And it is precisely what lessons 2 and 4 are about. A figure whose provenance nobody states is
the defect this course exists to teach, and printing one here would be worse than teaching without
one.

So the honest statement of the finding is: six weeks of training in the method of loci produced
durable, longer-lasting memories, in a three-arm study of about fifty people across the arms,
retested at four months. **Which arm the effect was measured against is in the body, and the body
was not opened.**[1]

### Worked: five things on a route

**This demonstration is constructed**, and the route and the items are invented.[2] The point of it
is that you should not meet the transfer question without having seen the technique do anything.

**The route.** The front door. The hall table. The bottom stair. The bathroom sink. The bedroom
window. Five points, in the order you would walk them, in a place somebody knows without thinking.

**The list.** Five things to buy: bleach, a birthday card, batteries, bread, a bicycle pump.

**The first three, placed.** Bleach is poured over the front door, running down it and taking the
paint off. The birthday card is on the hall table, propped open and singing. The batteries are
stacked on the bottom stair, so you tread on them and they roll.

:::predict Two points left, the bathroom sink and the bedroom window, and two items, bread and a bicycle pump. Place them yourself before reading on, and notice what you do to make each one stick.
Whatever you wrote is better than what is written here, because you made it.

The version this lesson had in mind was a loaf of bread jammed in the sink with the tap running
into it, and a bicycle pump sticking out through the bedroom window like a cannon.

Now notice the two things you did, because they are the whole technique. You put each item
somewhere specific rather than thinking about it in general. And you made the picture strange,
because the ordinary version of a loaf on a worktop is not distinguishable from every other time you
have seen a loaf on a worktop.

**The strangeness is part of the standard instruction**, and nothing read for this course tests
whether it helps.[3] What is being claimed for the technique is not that strangeness is magic, but
that ordered arbitrary items given distinct places are easier to walk back through than the same
items given nothing.
:::

Walk the route and you have the list. Front door, hall table, bottom stair, sink, window. That
took two minutes to build and it is the whole of the method.

And notice what the list was. Five arbitrary items in a fixed order, which is exactly the
material the next section is about.

## The transfer question

**Focus and Deep Work** lesson 6 taught the distinction and this lesson applies it rather than
re-teaching it.[2]

**Near transfer**: you get better at the thing you practised. **Intermediate**: you get better at
something different that draws on the same system. **Far**: you get better at something out in your
life.

Where does the method of loci sit? The shape of the answer is the lesson.

The technique works on what it works on. **Ordered lists of arbitrary items**, which is the
research file's own description of what the technique is for rather than a list of materials
anything read here tested.[2] That isn't a small category and it is a specific one.

Whether it reaches the material a reader actually wants to remember is the transfer question, and
this course has not answered it.[2] A memory palace for a shopping list and a memory palace for
the argument of a book are different propositions, and nothing read here tests the second.

What makes this different from the brain-training case in Focus lesson 6 is this course's own
reading of the two claims rather than a comparison anybody has published, and it is worth stating
because the two look similar and aren't.[2] **Brain training claims that practising a component
improves the system.** The method of loci claims something much weaker and much more plausible: that
a particular way of encoding a particular kind of material works better than no method at all.
**That is a claim about a tool rather than about an organ**, and it is the sort of claim training
evidence can support.

:::checkpoint After five lessons of things going wrong, is this the lesson where the course finally gives you something that works?
Partly, and the qualifications are the useful part.

Yes, in the sense that the technique was trained and something durable came of it. A trained
group showed the durability effect after six weeks. **What material the training was tested on, and
which arm the effect was measured against, are both in the body and the body was not opened**, so
the step from the study to "the kind of list the technique is built for" is this course's and not
theirs.[2] That is still more design than most of this genre can show.

No, in the sense that the thing most readers want is not that. People want to remember what they
read, what was said in a meeting, the names of people they met last month. **The technique's
evidence is about ordered arbitrary lists**, and the step from one to the other is the transfer
question nobody in what this course read has taken.

And there is a cost the studies do not measure, which the lesson flags as its own observation.[2]
Building a memory palace takes time, and using one takes attention during encoding. A technique
that works and costs more than the thing it saves is a bad deal, and no study read here compares
the technique against simply spending the same time rehearsing.

So the honest recommendation, and it is a recommendation: if you have ordered arbitrary material you
must hold, this is the only method this course found a controlled training study for, and it is
worth an afternoon to learn. If what you want is to remember what you read, **this course cannot
tell you whether it helps**, and somebody who tells you it does is going past the evidence.
:::

### Worked: one claim, sorted

The claim is invented and the sorting is the course's own reasoning.[2]

**"Our memory course will double what you can remember. Graduates recall lists of forty items after
a single pass."**

Two sentences and two different levels.

1. **"Recall lists of forty items after a single pass."** Near transfer, on exactly the material the
   technique is for, and **this is the level of claim the technique is for**. It is the kind of
   material the technique is for, and it is the level of claim that tells you least. 2. **"Double
   what you can remember."** Far transfer, unqualified, and about everything. **Nothing in what this
   course read supports it**, and the first sentence is not evidence for it.

The sentence does its work by putting them next to each other, which is the same move Focus lesson 6
built into its own invented claim. Neither sentence is a lie. The claim is in the adjacency.

## Three things people get wrong about this

"Memory athletes have better memories." They have a technique, and the training studies are
about what that technique does to ordinary people.

"A technique that works on lists works on everything." The transfer question, unanswered here.
Nothing read for this course tests the technique on anything but the material it is for, so this is
not a claim the course can support or refute.

"Mnemonics are a trick rather than a skill." Six weeks of training, and a retest at four months.
Whatever else it is, it's learned.

## Practice

:::exercise Build one and use it
Take 40 minutes, then 5 minutes a week later.

Pick a route you know without thinking. Your home, a walk you do often, the layout of a
building you have worked in. Fix ten points along it, in order, and say them out loud until the
order's automatic.

Then take ten arbitrary things you actually need: a shopping list, ten countries, ten dates, ten
names from a team you are joining.

Place one at each point, as vividly and as strangely as you can manage. The strangeness is part
of the standard instruction for this technique, and nothing read for this course tests whether it
helps.[3]

Then walk the route in your head and write down what you find. Count how many you get.

A week later, without revisiting it, walk it again and count again. Write down both numbers.

You won't learn from this whether the technique works, because one person doing it once is
the thing this course spends six lessons explaining. **What you'll learn is what it costs**, which
is the part the studies do not report and the part that decides whether you'll use it.
:::

:::exercise Sort a memory product
Take 20 minutes.

Find something being sold on the promise of a better memory: an app, a course, a book, a training
programme.

Write down four things.

1. **The claim, word for word.**
2. **The level it claims**: near, intermediate or far.
3. **The level its evidence reaches**, if it names any evidence at all.
4. **Whether there was a control group, and whether that group did anything.**

Then one line: **which of the four did the source give you?**

If a claim names no evidence, that isn't proof it's false. It's a claim nobody can check, and
lesson 7 is about what to do with those.
:::
