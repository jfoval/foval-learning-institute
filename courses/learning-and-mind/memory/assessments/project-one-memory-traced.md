Seven lessons have given you an account of what remembering is, the founding measurement of the
subject and its sample of one, what retrieving one thing does to its neighbours, what a question
does to the memory it asks about, the best-evidenced finding in the course and the narrowest, an
honest verdict on the technique everybody asks for, and a way of reading the next claim you meet.

This project runs the account on one of your own memories. It takes about five hours, and they are
spread across the whole course rather than gathered at the end.

## Read this before lesson 1, not after lesson 6

Section 1 was written in lesson 1's first exercise, and it can't be written now. If you have not
done that exercise yet, stop and do it before you read any further, because **the rest of this page
describes what happens to that account** and knowing it changes what you would write.

If you did the exercise and then read the account again at some point since, say so in section 2 and
carry on. **A spoilt project honestly labelled is worth more than a clean one that is not true**,
which is lesson 5's point arriving in your own work.

## The problem this project has, and how it solves it

**No section here can have a key**, because the answer is your memory and nobody else has it. A
project asking you to reach the right conclusion could not be marked, and one asking you to reflect
would produce nothing anybody could check.

So every section asks for a record, then a check against something written earlier, then the gap
between them. That's the shape lesson 1 gave the whole course: an account is a self-report, a
comparison is a measurement, and the distance between the two is what the subject studies.

**One consequence, said up front so it does not feel like a trick.** The project cannot be improved
by making the two accounts agree. Section 3 asks what a reconstructive account predicts about each
difference, and a pair of accounts with no differences gives you nothing to run that on.

**And the consequence on the other side, which matters more.** A learner whose two accounts match
closely has not failed this project. **Nothing in this course predicts how much any particular
memory will change**, and a rubric that scored divergence would be teaching a result the evidence
does not support. The marks are for the comparison and the reasoning, not for the size of the gap.

## What you hand in

**One plain text or Markdown file, roughly 1,000 to 1,800 words, in four sections.** Plain text for
the reason **Digital Literacy**'s project gives.

## Section 1: the first account, dated

This is the file you wrote in lesson 1 and have not opened since. Paste it in whole, including the
three lines at the end: the date you wrote it, when you think the event happened, and your
confidence out of ten.

**Do not edit a word of it**, including the parts you now think are wrong. The parts you now think
are wrong are section 3's material.

## Section 2: the second account, written from memory

**Write the event out again, in as much detail as you can give, without opening section 1.**

Sit down for it properly rather than doing it from notes on a phone, and give it about as long as
you gave the first one. Aim for the same kind of thing you aimed for the first time: what was said,
what things looked like, how long things took, who was where.

Then the same three lines: today's date, when you think the event happened, and how confident you
are out of ten that this account is accurate.

Then **one line saying whether you read section 1 in between**. Say yes if you did, including if it
was only a glance. Nobody can check this and the whole project rests on it.

**Only after all of that, open section 1.**

## Section 3: the comparison

The longest section and the one being marked.

**Go through the two accounts item by item and build a list.** Not a summary of how they differ, a
list of the individual things that do.

For each item, four columns or four lines, whichever you find easier to write.

1. **What the first account said.**
2. **What the second account said.**
3. **Which kind of difference it is.** Something dropped, something added, something changed,
   something moved in time, something moved between people.
4. **Whether a reconstructive account predicts it**, and which lesson you're getting that from.

Aim for at least eight items. If you cannot find eight, say so, and say what that tells you about
either the memory or the accounts.

Then four paragraphs.

**The additions.** Anything in the second account that is not in the first. These are the
interesting ones: lesson 1's account says an account is built at the time of remembering from what
is available then, and lesson 4 says the availability can come from outside. **For each addition,
ask where it could have come from.** Something you were told since, something you saw, a question
somebody asked you, a general expectation about how events of that kind go.

**The detail.** Is the second account more detailed or less? **Say which, and then say what you
would have predicted before you looked**, and whether those match. Most people expect less and a
fair number find more.

**The confidence.** Compare the two numbers out of ten. Lesson 5 is the place to be careful here:
that lesson is about the relation between confidence and accuracy in one specific measured
situation, and your project is not that situation. **Say what you can conclude from your two
numbers, and say what you cannot.**

**What the account does not predict.** At least one item from your list where a reconstructive
account says nothing either way. This paragraph is worth as much as the other three. A framework
that explains every difference after the fact is the thing lesson 7 asks you to be suspicious of.

## Section 4: the decision, and what it costs

Lesson 7 gives you a way of sorting a claim and then a decision to make with it. **Make it here, on
this memory.**

Pick the thing this exercise has actually changed, if it has changed anything: how you would talk
about an old memory, whether you would repeat one as evidence, what you would do if somebody
contradicted your account of something, how you would ask somebody else about an event you both
remember.

Write it in three parts.

1. **The decision**, in one or two sentences, specific enough that somebody could tell whether you
   had kept it.
2. **What it rests on.** Which lessons, and what those lessons actually measured. **If a lesson's
   finding does not stretch as far as your decision, say so**, and make the decision anyway with the
   gap named. 3. **What it costs.** Every decision worth making has something on the other side of
   it. Treating your own accounts as reconstructions costs you something real: confidence you would
   otherwise have, arguments you would otherwise win, a certain ease about your own past. **Name
   it.**

**A decision with nothing on the other side of it is not a decision**, and it's the commonest way
this section goes wrong.

## Rubric

| Criterion | What full marks look like |
|---|---|
| The first account is untouched | Section 1 is dated, matches what lesson 1 asked for, and was not edited after |
| The second account is independent | Written without re-reading the first, and the learner says so plainly, including when the answer is no |
| The comparison is specific | Differences named item by item, at least eight, rather than summarised |
| The account is applied, not asserted | Section 3 says which differences a reconstructive account predicts and which it does not |
| Scope is respected | No figure from the course is used without what it was measured on, and section 3's confidence paragraph says what it cannot conclude |
| The decision names its cost | Section 4 has something real on the other side of it |

**Two of those six are about honesty rather than analysis**, which is deliberate. This course is
largely about what people report about their own memories, and a project on that subject that
rewarded a tidy result would be funny in the wrong way.

## If the project is spoilt

**It happens, and it is not a disaster.** If you read the first account in between, or lost it, or
never wrote it, section 2 onwards can still be done, and you say at the top what went wrong.

**What you lose is the measurement**, which is the whole point, so it is worth saying what the
replacement is and is not. Write the second account, then read the first, then do section 3 knowing
that your second account was written by somebody who knew the comparison was coming. **That's a
different experiment with a known bias in it**, and naming the bias is the part still worth marks.

**The one thing not worth doing is writing section 1 now and dating it earlier.** Nobody would catch
it, and you would have spent five hours producing a result you already knew.
