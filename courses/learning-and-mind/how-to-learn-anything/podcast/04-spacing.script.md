S1: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm John.

S2: And I'm Haley. We're in How to Learn Anything, lesson four. Spacing, when to come back.

S1: Last episode said test yourself and come back later. This one answers the question that leaves open. When is later? And it starts with a plan that feels responsible and isn't. You reviewed something yesterday, it felt solid, you'll review it again tomorrow.

S2: That's most people's idea of being diligent.

S1: And it's one of the least efficient ways to spend study time the research has measured. Here's the core finding. Take a fixed amount of study time. Spend it in one block, that's massed practice. Split it into sessions with a gap between them, that's spaced practice. At a delayed test, spaced wins, and it wins at the same total time. This is one of the oldest findings in experimental psychology and one of the best replicated. A 2006 review by Cepeda and colleagues covered three hundred and seventeen experiments.

S2: So the question isn't whether to space. It's how big the gap should be.

S1: And there's a study built to answer exactly that. Cepeda and colleagues again, in 2008, ran more than thirteen hundred and fifty people through two learning sessions with a gap between them, then a final test up to a year later. Then they plotted the best gap against how far away the test was.

S2: Give me the numbers.

S1: For a test seven days away, the best gap was one day. For thirty five days, eleven days. For seventy days, three weeks. And for a test nearly a year away, also about three weeks, with the fitted curve putting the optimum at twenty three days.

S2: So the best gap grows as the horizon grows, but not in proportion.

S1: Right. As a share of the horizon it's about a seventh for a week, closer to a third for a month or two, and down to a sixteenth for a year, so no single percentage fits every row. The working rule of thumb, from a review Carpenter and an overlapping group wrote for teachers, is a gap of roughly ten to twenty percent of the time until you need the material.

S2: And if I'm torn between two gaps?

S1: Take the longer one. That's the most useful single fact in the study. The curve is lopsided. A gap that was too short cost far more recall than a gap too long by the same amount. Miss on the short side and you've done a fluent, low value review. Miss on the long side and you've done a harder review that still mostly works.

S2: Okay, but here's what bothers me about the whole idea. Nothing happens during the gap except forgetting. Why would forgetting help?

S1: Because of what the forgetting forces. Review right after studying, and retrieval strength is still high, so the review is just recognition. Yes, I know this. Easy, fluent, and it barely moves storage strength. Wait a few days and the memory has faded a little. Now you have to reconstruct it, search for it, rebuild it from cues. And that reconstruction is retrieval practice, the strongest tool from last episode. A spaced review isn't the same review done later. It's a harder review.

S2: So forgetting a little between sessions is the price of remembering a lot.

S1: That's the account this lesson leans on, called study phase retrieval, and the honest note is that it isn't the only one. Some researchers point to changed contexts giving the memory more routes back, some to attention, some to consolidation between sessions, some of it during sleep. The 2006 review concluded no single theory fits all the data. But the practical advice is the same whichever wins.

S2: And spacing plus retrieval together?

S1: That's the combination. The spacing effect was originally studied with rereading, and spaced rereading does beat massed rereading. But a 2021 review by Latimier and colleagues looked at spaced retrieval against massed retrieval across twenty nine studies and found a large effect. So the ordering is spaced retrieval, then spaced rereading, then massed anything. Every review starts with the book closed.

S2: Make it concrete. Exam in thirty days, chapter learned today.

S1: The rule of thumb says three to six days for the first gap. Cepeda's nearest data point says eleven. Lean long, call it six. So reviews on day six, twelve, eighteen, twenty four, and a final one two days out. At each review, answer your own questions from memory, expect to miss some, that's the point, and relearn the misses. First session, three correct recalls of each item. Every later session, one. The whole plan is a little over three hours, the same total time as cramming. Only where the hours sit has changed.

S2: And what about a horizon measured in years? Say a language you want for life.

S1: Then the gap grows into months, and the evidence is a family affair. Harry Bahrick and his family relearned foreign vocabulary over nine years on two schedules. Thirteen sessions fifty six days apart, or twenty six sessions fourteen days apart. The two came out comparable. Half the sessions, four times the gap, about the same memory. Four participants, and they were the four authors, so hold it loosely, but it fits everything else in the literature.

S2: And does anything last without a schedule?

S1: Bahrick's other study tested seven hundred and thirty three people on Spanish learned at school, some fifty years earlier. Retention fell over the first three to six years, then stayed roughly unchanged for up to thirty. He called the stable part permastore. The loss is front loaded. What you still have six years out, you'll probably have at thirty six.

S2: One myth before we close. My app uses expanding gaps, one day, then three, then seven. Is that required?

S1: No. Latimier's review compared expanding schedules with fixed ones and found them about equal, a difference not distinguishable from zero. Expanding is convenient, not necessary. The effect comes from the gaps being there, and from retrieving at each review. Not from the shape of the sequence.

S2: So. The horizon sets the gap, ten to twenty percent as the starting rule, err long, and close the book at every review.

S1: And when a review goes badly, remember what the misses are. Not evidence the studying was wasted. The forgetting is what made this review count.
