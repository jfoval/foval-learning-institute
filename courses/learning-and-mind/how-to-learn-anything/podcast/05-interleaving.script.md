S1: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm John.

S2: And I'm Haley. We're in How to Learn Anything, lesson five. Interleaving and variation, mix it up, within limits.

S1: Think about the last time you worked through problem sets. A maths chapter, a grammar unit, a coding tutorial. Almost certainly the practice was arranged the way textbooks arrange it. Ten problems on one method, then ten on the next. By the tenth problem you were fast and accurate, which felt like mastery.

S2: And then the exam mixed everything together, and I stared at a question knowing I could do it and not remembering which method it wanted.

S1: That gap isn't a memory failure. It's a skill you never practised, because the arrangement of the problems did it for you. Blocked practice groups problems by type. A A A A, then B B B B. Interleaved practice mixes them. Same problems, same total time, different order. And the finding is that blocked looks better during the session and interleaved wins on a delayed test.

S2: How big is the difference?

S1: The study to hold in your head is Rohrer and Taylor, from 2007. College students learned to compute the volume of four uncommon solids, a wedge, a spheroid, a spherical cone, a half cone, each with its own formula. One group did all the wedge problems, then all the spheroids, and so on. The other group did the identical problems shuffled. During practice, the blocked group was about eighty nine percent correct and the interleaved group about sixty.

S2: So blocking wins the session.

S1: A week later, the blocked group scored twenty percent. The interleaved group, sixty three. Three times higher. It was a small study, eighteen students, but the direction has been replicated many times since. And it scaled up. A preregistered randomised trial in 2020 ran fifty four seventh grade classes over four months, and a month after practice ended the interleaved classes scored sixty one percent against thirty eight, with the effect positive for every one of the fifteen teachers.

S2: Okay, why? Same problems, different order. Why would the order matter that much?

S1: Because solving a problem has two steps, and blocked practice quietly does the first one for you. Step one is deciding which method the problem needs. Step two is executing it. In a blocked set, the heading tells you the method. When every problem on the page is a wedge problem, you never ask, is this a wedge problem? So you get very fluent at executing and get no practice at all at choosing.

S2: And every real test starts with the choosing. A patient doesn't arrive with the diagnosis written on their forehead.

S1: That's the line. Taylor and Rohrer looked at what kind of errors the blocked students made on the test, and most were discrimination errors. The right procedure, carried out on the wrong kind of problem. They could execute. They couldn't choose.

S2: Does it work when there's no procedure at all?

S1: That's the painters study we met in episode one, and this is what it was really about. People studied paintings by twelve artists, and the test was recognising the style in new paintings they'd never seen. Artists studied mixed in among the others were recognised at sixty one percent, artists studied in a block at thirty five. Seeing one artist next to a different one made the differences stand out in a way six paintings in a row never did. And still, most participants said afterwards the blocked way had been as good or better. The feeling of blocking wins even when your own test just went the other way.

S2: Now the part of the title that says within limits. Because mix everything up can't be the lesson.

S1: It isn't, and there's a meta analysis that draws the boundary. Brunmair and Richter, 2019, pooled fifty nine studies. For confusable visual categories like paintings, the effect is large. For maths problem types, moderate and well supported. For reading passages on different topics, mixed results, nothing reliable. And for word learning, things like translations and pronunciation rules, the average actually favoured blocking.

S2: So mixing my Spanish vocabulary with my chemistry flashcards buys me nothing.

S1: Worse than nothing, maybe, because the switching costs attention. The mechanism explains the boundary. Interleaving's job is discrimination and choice. Gato means cat doesn't need to be told apart from libro means book, and there's no method to select. So here's the decision rule. First question, could I confuse these things with each other? If yes, interleave. Second, does using them require choosing which one applies? If yes, interleave. If both answers are no, block, and use spacing and retrieval instead, which work regardless.

S2: There's a trap in building the practice set too, isn't there. I shuffle my items, but each one is labelled. Past perfect, translate this.

S1: Then the label does the choosing for you, and you've built blocked practice in a shuffled order. Strip the labels, and write down which type you decided each item was before you solve it. Then when you mark it, count choosing errors and executing errors separately. The choosing number is the one this whole technique exists to fix.

S2: And a word for the textbook authors, because they're not stupid.

S1: They're not. You can't mix in what hasn't been taught yet. A block shows a teacher immediately who can't do the day's skill. And novices do need a small block first to get a procedure's steps down. Rohrer's own 2015 study began each new skill with a short block before the mixing. So the practical target isn't never block. It's block briefly, then mix, and treat the drop in accuracy when you start mixing as the signal that the choosing muscle is finally being trained.

S2: The full lesson has the boundary evidence in detail, a worked example building a French tense set, and the decision rule to run on your own subject. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Go shuffle something.
