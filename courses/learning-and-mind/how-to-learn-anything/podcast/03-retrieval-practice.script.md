S1: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm John.

S2: And I'm Haley. We're in How to Learn Anything, lesson three. Retrieval practice, one of the two techniques that hold up.

S1: Lesson one was the diagnosis. What you can do right now is a poor guide to what you'll keep, and the feeling of knowing tracks fluency, not storage. Lesson two gave the machinery underneath. This lesson is the treatment. And it's simple to state. Pulling information out of memory strengthens it more than putting it in again.

S2: Testing yourself as a way of learning, not just a way of measuring.

S1: Psychologists call it the testing effect. And here's how much confidence it deserves. When Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed ten common study techniques for the Association for Psychological Science, only two came out rated high utility. Practice testing was one. Spacing your study out, which is the next lesson, was the other. Rereading, the technique that topped the list when students in one university sample were asked what they do, was rated low.

S2: We told the founding study back in episode one, the crossover. Give me the one week numbers again quickly.

S1: Roediger and Karpicke. Restudied passage, forty two percent at a week. Tested passage, fifty six. And in their second experiment, one study session plus three recall tests beat four study sessions, sixty one percent to forty, with the four time studiers more confident.

S2: Okay. But that's a lab with short passages. Does it hold in real classrooms?

S1: For a long time the honest answer was, mostly lab studies. Not any more. A meta analysis by Yang and colleagues in twenty twenty one covered two hundred and twenty two classroom studies, over forty eight thousand students, and found a solid overall effect for classroom quizzing. And they measured what made it bigger, which turns into three design rules. The effect was larger with feedback, larger when the practice quiz matched the format of the final test, and larger the more often quizzing was repeated.

S2: So always check your answers, practise in the form you'll be tested in, and quiz more than once.

S1: Those are the rules.

S2: Here's my worry though. If I test myself early and get things wrong, am I not just practising my mistakes?

S1: That fear stops a lot of people, and it's been tested directly. Kornell, Hays and Bjork gave people questions they could not possibly answer, let them guess, then showed the answer. In five of six experiments, guessing wrong and then seeing the answer beat spending the same time just reading the answer. And the wrong guesses did not stick.

S2: The condition being the feedback.

S1: That's the condition. There's a study showing that a multiple choice test without feedback can teach you some of its own wrong options. So the rule is not, don't guess. It's, guess, then always check. The failed attempt opens a gap, the feedback fills it, and the memory that results is stronger than one that never had the gap.

S2: Now the objection I'd actually make. Fine for vocabulary and bones of the hand. But I need to understand my subject, not memorise it.

S1: Then here's the study to know. Karpicke and Blunt, published in Science. Students learned a science text several ways, and the two that matter here are these. One group drew a concept map with the text open in front of them, which looks like deep structural work. Another closed the text and wrote out everything they could recall, twice. A week later, the retrieval group won. And not just on fact questions. On inference questions, the ones where you have to connect ideas.

S2: The rote looking method beat the deep looking one at the deep stuff.

S1: It gets better. In their second experiment, a hundred and twenty students learned one text each way, so every student was their own control. Eighty four percent did better on the text they'd learned by retrieval. And for half of them, the final test was itself a concept map, drawn from memory. The retrieval text still won. Practising the drawing lost to practising the recall, at drawing.

S2: How did the students expect it to go?

S1: Backwards. Three quarters of them predicted concept mapping would do as well or better. The explanation the authors give is that the mappers had the text in front of them the whole time, so nothing had to be rebuilt from memory. The retrieval students had to reconstruct not just the facts but the structure connecting them. The method that looked structural was doing less structural work than the one that looked like rote.

S2: Alright, I'm sold. But most students already quiz themselves. So what's going wrong?

S1: The reason they do it. Kornell and Bjork surveyed four hundred and seventy two UCLA students. Only eighteen percent said they self test because testing teaches. Sixty eight percent said they do it to find out how well they've learned. They're using the strongest learning tool in the field as a thermometer.

S2: What does the difference look like in practice?

S1: The checker looks at the score. High score, stop. Low score, reread the whole chapter from the start. The learner barely looks at the score. She looks at which questions she missed. Those go on a list, she rereads only the sections behind them, then retests the list. And there's a prescription for how much. Rawson and Dunlosky call it successive relearning. In the first session, keep at each item until you've recalled it correctly three times. Then in three later spaced sessions, bring each one back to a single correct recall. Miss one, and it goes back to three.

S2: Three times first, then spaced returns. And one correct recall is not the finish line.

S1: That's the other trap. Karpicke watched students run their own study and most dropped an item as soon as they'd recalled it once, and their retention suffered for it. One correct recall is the first step of a schedule, not the end of one.

S2: So the whole lesson in a sentence.

S1: Every test with feedback is a learning event. Close the book, pull it out of your head, check, and come back. And expect it to feel worse than rereading. It feels worse because your memory is being made to do something, and that effort is where the learning is.

S2: The full lesson has the classroom evidence, a worked three week routine, and an exercise where you write the exam yourself from memory. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Go test yourself on this one.
