S1: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm John.

S2: And I'm Haley. We're in How to Learn Anything, and this is the last lesson of the course. Running yourself. Attention, procrastination, sleep, and your system.

S1: Everything in this course so far has one awkward property. None of it runs itself. And the whole lesson fits in one sentence. Every technique in this course feels worse in the moment than its useless rival, so any decision you make in the moment will go the wrong way, and the only fix is to make the decision before the moment arrives.

S2: Give me the cleanest number behind that.

S1: In one study reviewed by Bjork, Dunlosky and Kornell, ninety percent of students did better after spaced practice than after massed. Asked afterwards which had worked better for them, seventy two percent still said massed. Nine in ten had just done better with spacing, and the feeling didn't update.

S2: So start with attention, and the habit almost everyone has. Studying with something else open.

S1: May and Elder reviewed the research on media multitasking in 2018, and the finding is consistent across the studies they surveyed, though much of it is associational rather than a pooled number. Multitasking goes with lower grades, worse recall, worse comprehension, in class and in private study alike. And the review found students misjudge the cost, which is lesson two's machinery. Working memory holds about four chunks, and a second stream takes some of them. What you feel is that you followed along. What you lose is the residue.

S2: And the fix isn't willpower.

S1: The fix is short blocks of single tasking with three properties. One stream. A phone you can't reach, rather than one you've resolved not to check. And an end that is a retrieval act, close the notes and write what you have. The length is whatever you can start. If twenty five minutes is too long, use fifteen.

S2: Procrastination next. Because I've written I'll study statistics this week in a planner many times, and it has never once worked.

S1: There's a name for why. Gollwitzer calls that a goal intention, and it leaves the decision of when and how to a future self who will be tired and holding a phone. The alternative is an implementation intention, which always has the form, when situation X arises, I will do Y. When I put my plate in the sink on Tuesday, I open the statistics notes. The cue happens to you, so no fresh decision is needed. A meta analysis of ninety four tests found adding an if then plan to a goal produced a medium to large improvement in follow through.

S2: And make the first step tiny.

S1: Steel's meta analysis of procrastination found the strongest predictors include how aversive the task is. Study statistics is aversive. Open the notes to question four and read it is not. Once you've read question four, you're in. And the third tactic is designing the environment. The argument from the self control researchers, and they're clear it's an argument rather than a trial result, is that strategies applied before temptation appears, phone in another room, notes already open, should beat strategies applied during it. None of this is a personality change. It's engineering around a known weakness in how people decide about future effort.

S2: Now sleep, where you promised me the evidence at its actual size.

S1: The mechanism is real and well developed. That consolidation happens during sleep is established. How much of it runs through slow wave sleep specifically is now in question. And the size is where the popular version overreaches. Cordi and Rasch, and Rasch co wrote the big review, concluded in 2021 that sleep mediated memory benefits are smaller, more task dependent and less robust than previously assumed. And across more than fifteen thousand children and adolescents, the correlation between how long they sleep and how well they do at school is about point zero seven. Tiny.

S2: But a lost night still hurts.

S1: It hurts attention first. A meta analysis of sleep deprivation found lapses in simple attention among the largest effects. So the calibrated advice is, sleep enough, because consolidation is real and a tired session is a worse one. But don't skip a review on the theory that sleep will do the work.

S2: The lesson also names the myths. Quick fire.

S1: Learning styles. The claim that matching teaching to your visual or auditory style helps. Pashler and colleagues set out the test and found virtually no evidence, even though over ninety percent of surveyed teachers believed it. Preferences are real. The benefit of matching doesn't show up. Hard fonts, no benefit in the meta analysis. Handwriting beats typing, failed to replicate in two direct attempts. Ten percent of the brain, no idle ninety percent. What does hold is lesson two's rule, notes that reconstruct beat notes that copy, whatever the medium.

S2: And growth mindset gets a different label.

S1: Contested, not a myth, and the lesson gives both camps. A preregistered national trial of over twelve thousand students found small gains, concentrated among lower achievers in schools where peer norms supported the message. The meta analyses put the average effect at about point zero five to point zero eight. One camp reads that as real effects in identifiable places, diluted by weak studies. The other reads it as a near zero effect inflated by weak studies. Same numbers, different summary. Anyone who tells you it's simply proven or simply debunked is telling you their side.

S2: And it all lands in a template.

S1: Six steps. Your honest hours. Each subject tagged by what kind of material it is, because the tag decides the technique. List like material gets spaced retrieval and never interleaving. Problem types get mixed sets. Prose gets free recall. Skills get deliberate practice with feedback. Then a horizon and a gap for each, blocks with if then cues and a definition of done, one weekly free recall session on a blank page, and a drop rule, decided now, for when the week goes wrong.
