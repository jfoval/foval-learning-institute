S1: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm John.

S2: And I'm Haley. Today we're starting the course called How to Learn Anything, with its first lesson. Learning is not performance.

S1: And it opens with a feeling everybody knows. You go over your notes the night before, everything looks familiar, you'd bet money you know it. A week later somebody asks you a question about it and there's nothing there.

S2: And the usual conclusion is, I have a bad memory.

S1: The lesson says no. You have a normal memory and a bad instrument for measuring it. The feeling of knowing. And the whole course rests on one distinction that fixes the instrument. It comes from Robert Bjork and Elizabeth Bjork, who have studied memory at UCLA for decades. Every memory, they propose, has two separate strengths, not one.

S2: Two strengths.

S1: And I'll say what the lesson says. This is the Bjorks' model, one model among several, and researchers argue about the details. The experiments we'll get to are real whichever model you prefer. But the two strengths are the clearest way to read them. Storage strength is how deeply a memory is entrenched, how woven in it is to what you already know. And in their model it does not decrease. Once something is well learned it stays learned in that sense. Retrieval strength is how accessible the memory is right now. It rises with recent use and falls with time.

S2: Give me a case where they come apart.

S1: There's a phone number you dialled every day as a child. Try to say it now. For most people nothing comes. But show it to them once and they'd have it back in seconds, and it would stay back. High storage, low retrieval. Now take a number you read off a screen thirty seconds ago. You can say it right now, and by tomorrow it's gone. High retrieval, almost no storage.

S2: Okay. So when I look over a page and think, yes, I know this, which strength am I actually measuring?

S1: Retrieval strength. Every time. And that's the problem, because what you want for an exam next month or a skill for life is storage strength, and you have no direct sense of storage strength at all. You infer it from how easy things feel right now.

S2: And ease can lie.

S1: Predictably. When you reread a chapter, the second pass is easier. The sentences flow, nothing surprises you. That ease is called fluency, and it's produced by recognition. The page triggers the memory. You never had to rebuild the memory from nothing, which is the thing that builds storage strength most. So rereading raises fluency and retrieval strength, does little for storage, and feels like learning the whole time.

S2: Is there a clean experiment on that feeling being wrong?

S1: There is. Asher Koriat and Robert Bjork used word pairs. Take the pair cats, kittens. You study it with both words in front of you, and it feels easy, because kittens obviously goes with cats. But at the test you'll see cats alone and have to produce kittens. And in word association norms, kittens brings cats to mind seventy two percent of the time, but cats brings kittens to mind two percent of the time. The link you felt was only visible because the answer was sitting next to the cue.

S2: So the confidence was made with information that won't be there at the test.

S1: They called it foresight bias. And rereading a chapter is that situation on a large scale. Every sentence is a cue with its answer sitting right next to it.

S2: Alright, so what should we do instead? And how do we know it works?

S1: Here's the founding experiment. Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke. Students read two short passages. One passage they read again. The other they closed and wrote out from memory, no feedback. Then a final test, either five minutes later, two days later, or a week later. Before I tell you the numbers, which wins?

S2: My honest intuition says reading twice. You saw it twice instead of once.

S1: And at five minutes you'd be right. Restudy scored eighty one percent, recall seventy five. But at two days it had flipped. Sixty eight percent for the recalled passage, fifty four for the restudied one. And at one week, fifty six against forty two. Rereading lost nearly half of what it had at five minutes. Recall lost a quarter.

S2: So the method that felt better was building the wrong strength.

S1: Their second experiment made it starker. Study a passage four times, or study it once and recall it three times. At a week, the repeated testers were at sixty one percent, the repeated studiers at forty. Twenty one points. And the repeated studiers were more confident they'd remember it.

S2: You'd hope seeing your own results would cure that.

S1: That's the hardest finding in the lesson. Nate Kornell and Robert Bjork had people learn the painting styles of twelve artists, so they could identify new paintings they'd never seen. Some artists' paintings came in a block, one after another. Others were spread out, mixed in among other artists. Accuracy on new paintings was sixty one percent for the spread out artists, thirty five for the blocked ones.

S2: Big gap.

S1: And then they asked people which order had helped them more. Seventy eight percent had done better with the spread out order, and seventy eight percent said blocking was as good or better. In a second version, of the seventy two people who had a preference, sixty four picked the method that had worked worse.

S2: Their own performance had just demonstrated the opposite, and the belief never registered it.

S1: Because blocked study feels better while you do it. Fluent, obvious, comfortable. The mixed order feels confusing, and confusion feels like not learning. When the feeling and the test disagreed, nearly nine in ten believed the feeling.

S2: So you can't feel your way out of this. What's the defence?

S1: Decide in advance. Trust delayed tests over feelings, and build your studying around things that measure storage strength. Recalling from nothing, after a gap. The rest of the course is how. From here on, the question, did that study session work, is never answered by how it felt. It's what you can do, cold, later.

S2: The full lesson has the numbers drawn out, Bjork explaining the model in his own words, and a practice audit of your own habits. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Go learn something that lasts.
