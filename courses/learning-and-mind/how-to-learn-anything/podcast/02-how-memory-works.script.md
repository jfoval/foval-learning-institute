S1: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm John.

S2: And I'm Haley. We're in the course How to Learn Anything, on lesson two. How memory actually works.

S1: Start with a feeling from this one too. Pick a paragraph you found impossible recently. A clause in a lease, a page of a statistics textbook, a block of code someone else wrote. You read it, you reached the end, and you had nothing. Then someone who knows the field glanced at it and said, oh, that's just saying X.

S2: And the obvious explanation is, that person has a better brain.

S1: The lesson says no. They have the same tiny working memory you do. What they have that you don't is a set of pre built bundles in long term memory. This lesson is about those bundles.

S2: Okay, so lay out the machinery.

S1: The textbook picture has two stores. Working memory is where you think. It holds whatever you're attending to right now, and it's small and brief. In a classic experiment from nineteen fifty nine, people heard three letters and were stopped from rehearsing them. Within about twenty seconds, most had lost them. Long term memory is where everything you know lives, and for practical purposes it has no capacity limit. Nobody has ever run out.

S2: And for the kind of learning this course is about, everything has to pass through the small store to reach the large one.

S1: That's the bottleneck, and most of what goes wrong in studying goes wrong right there.

S2: How small is small? I've heard seven.

S1: Everyone has. George Miller's nineteen fifty six paper put it at seven, plus or minus two. But Miller's people were allowed to rehearse and regroup. Nelson Cowan reviewed the evidence in two thousand and one and argued that when you stop people doing that, the limit is three to five. About four. Four is what you get when you meet material for the first time.

S2: Four what, though? Four words? Four facts?

S1: Four chunks. And a chunk is anything your long term memory already treats as a single unit. The letters F, B, I are three chunks to a child learning to read and one chunk to you. A working memory slot holds one chunk, and a chunk can be as big as your knowledge lets it be.

S2: So the expert and I have the same four slots, but her slots hold more.

S1: And there's a beautiful experiment proving it. Chess. In nineteen seventy three, William Chase and Herbert Simon showed a master, a club player, and a beginner positions from real games, five seconds each, then asked them to rebuild the board from memory. First attempt, the master placed about four fifths of the pieces correctly. The club player about half. The beginner a third.

S2: Better memory. That's what it looks like.

S1: Then comes the part that matters. They scattered the same pieces on the board at random and ran it again. And the master's advantage largely vanished. In their data there was no relation at all between skill and recall for random positions, and a later review of thirteen such studies found only a sliver survives.

S2: Because the patterns were gone. A real position matched things he already had stored, a castled king, a pawn chain, so it was a handful of chunks. A random board was just pieces, nearly as many for him as for anyone.

S1: Simon and a colleague estimated a master carries somewhere between ten thousand and a hundred thousand stored patterns. The figure that stuck is fifty thousand. So expertise, at the level of memory, is having bigger chunks. Not more slots. Bigger contents per slot.

S2: Alright. So how does anything get into long term memory in the first place?

S1: Not, for this kind of learning, by exposure. Daniel Willingham's summary of decades of research is one sentence. Memory is the residue of thought. You remember what you thought about. The experiment behind it is Craik and Tulving, nineteen seventy five. Show people words, ask a different question about each. Is it in capitals? Does it rhyme? Would it fit in this sentence? Then a surprise memory test. The words judged for meaning were recognised several times as often as the words judged for typeface.

S2: Couldn't that just be time? Thinking about meaning takes longer.

S1: They suspected that too, so they used a harder non meaning question, about the word's pattern of vowels and consonants, which took twice as long as the meaning question. Meaning still won. Same words, same exposure, more time on the shallow question, and meaning still won. What differed was what the person was thinking about.

S2: Which is uncomfortable. Because you can read a whole page while thinking about lunch.

S1: And you'll remember lunch. That's why rereading fails. You can reread while thinking about almost nothing, so it deposits almost nothing, while feeling fluent the whole time.

S2: The lesson also has a name for instruction that wastes those four slots.

S1: Extraneous load, from John Sweller's cognitive load theory. Load that comes from how material is presented rather than from the content. A diagram on one page and its labels on another, so you shuttle between them. A term used three sentences before it's defined. Background music. All of it occupies slots that aren't then available for the content. The test is simple. If you can rewrite the thing to be easier without changing what it teaches, the load was extraneous. If you had to cut content to make it fit, the difficulty was in the material itself, and the fix is to build the sub parts into chunks first.

S2: One more trap before we close. The lecture that made sense.

S1: This one stings. You sit in a lecture, every step follows, you nod. Next evening, alone with the problem set, you can't begin. What happened is that in the room, the lecturer was doing the assembling. She held the chunks and put them together in the right order, out loud. Your four slots only had to follow, one step at a time. But following doesn't build a chunk. Alone, you'd have to hold all the steps together yourself, and you have twelve elements and four slots.

S2: So it's lesson one's fluency illusion, seen from inside the machine.

S1: Exactly. Retrieval strength was high in the room because the cues were all present. Storage was never built because you never did the assembling. So when something feels impossible, the problem is usually not that you're not smart enough. You're missing the chunks it assumes. Find and build those. Don't stare harder.

S2: The full lesson has the diagram, the chunk counting exercise on your own material, and a contract clause read three ways. It's free at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S1: Thanks for listening. Go build a chunk.
