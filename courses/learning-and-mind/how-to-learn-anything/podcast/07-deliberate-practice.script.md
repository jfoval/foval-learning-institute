S1: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm John.

S2: And I'm Haley. We're in How to Learn Anything, lesson seven. Getting good at a skill. Deliberate practice and feedback.

S1: Start with something you've done almost every day for years. Typing, driving, cooking. If you type, you've typed for thousands of hours. Are you much faster or more accurate than you were after the first year?

S2: Honestly? Not really.

S1: That's most people, on most skills. You reach a level that's good enough, then stop improving while the hours keep piling up. This lesson is about the specific kind of practice that keeps improvement going, and it is quite different from doing the thing a lot.

S2: This is the famous violin study, isn't it.

S1: Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch Romer, 1993. Thirty violin students at a Berlin music academy, in three groups. The best, nominated by their professors as potential international soloists. A good group. And students headed for careers as teachers. By eighteen, the best had accumulated about seven thousand four hundred hours of solitary practice, the good about five thousand three hundred, the future teachers about three thousand four hundred.

S2: So the hours separated the groups. That's where the ten thousand hour rule comes from.

S1: And the rule was added later, by Gladwell, not by the paper. The ten thousand was an average for the best group at twenty, not a threshold. Half of that group hadn't reached it at that age, and they were still students with a long way to go. Ericsson rejected the rule in print. Because the paper's main idea was never the count. It was that the hours only counted when the activity had a particular structure.

S2: Which is what, exactly?

S1: Five parts. Designed to improve, not to perform, so it works on a named weakness. At the edge of current ability, tasks you can't yet do reliably. Fed back immediately, you know right away whether the attempt worked. Designed by someone who knows the domain. And effortful, not enjoyable in itself. Ericsson kept it separate from work, doing the thing for reward, and play, doing it for fun. Both add hours. Neither is designed to change what you can do.

S2: Give me the two guitarists.

S1: Two people, an hour a day for a year. The first plays songs he knows, tries a new one when he's bored, stops when it sounds rough. That's play with a bit of naive practice, and it produces a competent, stalled amateur. The second starts each session by naming one transition she can't make cleanly, say C to F barre chord. Metronome at a speed where she makes it about seven times in ten. Drills that change, only that change, for ten minutes. Records the last minute and listens back. Moves the metronome up when she's clean nine times in ten. Then plays songs at the end, partly to check the drilled change survives inside real music.

S2: Same instrument, same hours, and the difference is entirely in the structure. Why does the structure matter so much?

S1: Because every attempt at a skill is an experiment. You intended one thing and something happened. Feedback tells you the gap. Without it, you keep the gap. And here's the uncomfortable part. Repetition strengthens whatever you repeat, so practising without feedback is worse than nothing. It entrenches the version you already have, errors included. Twenty years of typing with two fingers produces a very fast two finger typist.

S2: Which explains the professionals. Twenty years of experience that somehow isn't expertise.

S1: The lesson works through a lawyer, twenty years in. Run the checklist on her working day. Designed to improve? No, the work arrives as it arrives. Edge of ability? No, most of a working day is what you can already do. That's what being competent means. Immediate feedback? A lease drafted in 2012 might first be tested in court in 2021, and by then nobody connects the outcome to the drafting. So forty thousand hours, almost none of which meet the definition. Experience is not practice.

S2: Now the debate, because I know this one got messy. How much does practice actually explain?

S1: The honest answer is, it isn't settled, and the lesson gives both sides. In 2014, Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald pooled eighty eight studies. Practice hours explained twenty six percent of the differences between people in games, twenty one in music, eighteen in sports, and under one percent in professions. Their conclusion, practice is important, but not as important as has been argued.

S2: And Ericsson's reply?

S1: That they counted the wrong thing. The pooled studies summed up virtually any activity in the domain. Team practice, lectures, play, even watching the sport on television. By his definition most of those hours aren't deliberate practice, so the meta analysis measured a mixture and reported the mixture explains little.

S2: Then there was a replication.

S1: 2019, preregistered, Macnamara and Maitra redid the violin study with thirty nine violinists in Cleveland. The good violinists had practised more than the less accomplished, as in Berlin. But the predicted ordering of best above good did not appear. The best had actually logged fewer solitary hours than the good group by eighteen, a difference in the wrong direction, though not statistically significant with thirteen per group. Practice explained twenty six percent of the variance in group membership, and the authors themselves call that not inconsequential.

S2: So what does nobody in the dispute deny?

S1: Three things. Deliberate practice, structured the way we described, is the best supported method anyone has for improving a skill. Accumulated practice, however counted, is a substantial predictor in music, games and sport. And the replication didn't reproduce the size of the original effect. What would settle the rest is a study that measures practice quality as it happens, rather than asking adults to remember childhood hours. Both sides say so. It hasn't been done.

S2: And for the listener who just wants to get better at something this week?

S1: Four lines. Name the one sub skill you can't yet do reliably, small, like opening paragraphs that state the point in one sentence. Design the drill that isolates it, slightly harder than you can manage, with a rule for making it harder when you succeed. Pick a feedback source outside your own impression, a recording, a timer, a checklist, a person. And keep it short, ten to thirty minutes, because this kind of practice is tiring. A short honest session beats a long vague one.
