S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Time Management, on the second lesson. Reading your own week.

S1: And the first thing the lesson says is that you should not add the week up.

S2: That is the obvious thing to do with a record.

S1: It is, and a total is the least informative line on the page. It tells you what you already half knew, it moves from week to week for reasons that have nothing to do with you, and the only thing it can be compared against is the prediction you wrote before you started. The information is somewhere else, and finding it takes two passes over the same sheet.

S2: What's the first pass?

S1: Put the prediction beside the record. Four numbers you wrote down before the week, four totals from the week, and a third column for the difference with its sign. That's the whole instrument.

S2: And what should I expect to see?

S1: Three things, and none of them is a failure. Some categories will agree closely, which is a real finding, because you can stop attending to them. One or two will be out by a lot. And something will turn up that was not in your four categories at all.

S2: Why do the disagreements carry the information rather than the totals?

S1: Because you predicted first. That one step turns the record from a description into a comparison, and a comparison can be wrong in a way a description cannot. Where the two agree, your model of your week works there. Where they disagree, you've found a specific place it is wrong, and the record names it. Not, I'm bad with time, which isn't a finding. But, I believed Thursday evening was three hours of study and it was forty minutes twice.

S2: And that's all one week can establish?

S1: That's exactly the limit. One week is a sample of one. It supports nothing about a rate, because a single observation cannot tell you what varies. What it can support is an existence claim, which is a different shape of sentence. At least one of my four categories is badly wrong needs one week. I spend eleven hours a week on domestic work needs many, and no reader of this course is going to collect them.

S2: Give me a case.

S1: The lesson builds one, and it says plainly that the person is constructed. Ade predicted thirty hours of paid work, twelve of domestic, fifty-two of sleep and twenty of leisure. He came back close on three. Paid work thirty-one and a half, sleep forty-nine, domestic thirteen and a half. Leisure came to nine.

S2: So he lost eleven hours of leisure.

S1: And here's the arithmetic almost everybody skips. A week has a hundred and sixty-eight hours. His four categories account for a hundred and three of them, and forty-nine of those are sleep. So fifty-four waking hours sat outside the four boxes he predicted. That's close to eight hours a day.

S2: That sounds alarming.

S1: It isn't, and this is the correction the lesson makes. Most of it is the ordinary business of being alive. Eating, washing, getting places, the twenty minutes between one thing and the next, standing in a queue. Everybody's residue is large. The four categories you chose were never going to cover a week.

S2: Then what is the finding?

S1: The largest named thing you can pull out of the residue. For Ade it came to eleven hours and it was one recognisable activity. Calls to the surgery, a form for the council, two trips to the pharmacy, an hour on hold about his mother's account, an afternoon at an appointment. He had recorded every one accurately. He had simply never had a name for them, so there was no box to predict them into.

S2: And his leisure shortfall is also eleven hours.

S1: Which is not a coincidence. Every hour that goes into an unnamed activity came from somewhere, and in his case it came out of the box he was least sure about. Watch for that on your own sheet. The category that shrinks most is often the one you were using as a mental remainder rather than as a real estimate.

S2: All right, second pass.

S1: Every recorded hour into one of three columns. Fixed, discretionary, or contested. And the lesson is straight that this three-way sort is the course's own. Nothing in the literature it read defines a discretionary hour or counts how many of them anybody has.

S2: Define them for me.

S1: Fixed is an hour you do not decide about this month. A rostered shift, the school run, a dialysis appointment, sleep, the journey between two of those. The test isn't whether the hour is pleasant, it's whether a decision of yours this month could move it. Discretionary is where the decision is genuinely yours. Not hours with nothing planned in them, which is a much larger set, but hours where you could do a different thing without somebody else's agreement.

S2: And contested?

S1: Everything you hesitated over. And instead of forcing it into one of the other two, you write the condition beside it. Saturday morning at my mother's, fixed unless my brother takes one in three. The hour after the children are down, discretionary on the nights I'm not too tired, which is about half.

S2: Why is that worth doing?

S1: Because a condition can be asked about, checked, or found to be false. A feeling cannot. Each of those was vague before it was written and is now a statement with a dependency in it.

S2: What about somebody whose hours are all fixed?

S1: The lesson takes that seriously rather than telling him to look harder. If the honest answer is two discretionary hours, then two is what the rest of the course has to work with, and knowing it's two is a different position from suspecting it's somewhere between nothing and twenty. And his week lives in the fixed column, where an hour fixed by a rota is fixed differently from an hour fixed because nobody has ever asked whether it could move.

S2: One more thing before we finish.

S1: Count the discretionary column twice. The sum, and the shape. How many pieces, and how long the longest one is. Eleven hours in three blocks and eleven hours in thirty-one pieces are not the same resource, and no total will ever tell them apart.

S2: What have we left out?

S1: A second worked case built entirely on that point, the difference between a gap measured in hours and the same gap measured as a share of what you predicted, which are usually different categories, three things people get wrong about their own records, and an exercise for anybody who hasn't finished the week yet. The full lesson is at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S2: Thanks for listening.
