S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Time Management, on the seventh lesson. A week that survives other people.

S1: This is the assembly lesson. Nothing in it is new, and everything in it comes from a sheet you already have.

S2: Six lessons, and each one handed you something.

S1: Lesson two gave you the hours that are actually yours. Lesson three gave you a number for how far your estimates run short. Lesson four gave you plans attached to cues instead of to intentions. Lesson five gave you the real shape of a working day. Lesson six gave you one refusal, in writing, with its cost named. This lesson puts them in a week, runs it, and then reads what broke.

S2: Start with the failure.

S1: Because the shape of it is specific, and recognising it is most of the lesson. And I should say the course has no measurement of when plans break. What follows is its own account, and your week is the test of it.

S2: Go on.

S1: A plan built the usual way isn't wrong on Monday. You write it on Sunday evening, you look at it, and it's fine. Everything fits, the week looks like the one you wanted. Then Tuesday. Somebody needs something. A meeting moves, a child is sick, a client sends the thing they promised on Friday. And now the plan is wrong, not slightly but structurally, because the thing that moved was load bearing and everything after it was stacked on top.

S2: And then I throw it away.

S1: That's the part worth noticing. What happens next, in this course's account, is almost never revision. It's abandonment. The plan goes in a drawer, the week is got through, and the conclusion reached on Sunday is usually about the planner rather than about the plan. I need a better system, or I'm not the sort of person who plans.

S2: Is the answer just, be realistic?

S1: That's advice, and the course adds something bigger to it. A plan is a prediction, and lesson three is entirely about what happens to your predictions. You plan the run that goes well, your history isn't in the room, and the result is systematically short in a direction you can measure. So a week plan isn't a statement of intent you either live up to or fall short of. It's a forecast, subject to every finding in this course about forecasts, and that's the relationship that survives a Tuesday.

S2: Give me the order.

S1: Six steps, and the order is the course's own rather than anything a source recommends. One. The fixed points go in first. Everything from the fixed column of your lesson two sheet. Shifts, the school run, sleep, the journey, the evening you're at your mother's. Not because they matter most, but because they're the parts of the week that are true.

S2: Two.

S1: The refusal goes in, as an absence with a name on it. Not by leaving a gap where the thing used to be, but by writing, Tuesday evening, not the committee. A gap is ambiguous and will silently fill. A named absence is a decision you can see you're keeping.

S2: Three.

S1: The discretionary hours go in against their real shape. If your eleven hours arrive in thirty-one pieces, don't plan them as three blocks. Put the work that needs continuity in the two or three genuinely long pieces you have, and match everything else to the size of the piece it'll actually get.

S2: And the last three?

S1: Four, the if-then plans go on cues that exist in the week you've just written, because you now have a sheet showing which whens are real. Five, reserve the slack before planning the rest. About a fifth of your discretionary time, and how much is the course's own judgement rather than a finding, in pieces, placed where the week historically goes wrong. Reserving it first matters, because slack added at the end is slack taken off something already planned, and that's the step nobody actually performs. And six, apply your own ratio to what's left and take something out. Lesson three gave you its scope too: your median is for the items whose length is yours to decide, and anything that waits on somebody else gets a whole morning instead.

S2: What is slack for, exactly? It sounds like rest.

S1: It isn't rest and it isn't catching up, though it can be spent on either. Slack is what lets a plan be wrong without being finished. A week with every hour committed is fully wrong by Tuesday afternoon. A week with a fifth loose absorbs the Tuesday and carries on.

S2: Show me one.

S1: The lesson builds one on a reader you met in lesson two, constructed, a self-employed bookkeeper with a six-year-old. Eleven hours twenty of discretionary time, arriving in thirty-one pieces, the longest fifty-five minutes. Fixed points go in. Two mornings at a client's, the school run twice a day, her daughter's bedtime, Thursday at her mother's, sleep. That's most of the week, and it isn't a complaint, it's the input.

S2: And the study she keeps not starting?

S1: A bookkeeping qualification. It needs continuity, so it gets her two genuinely long pieces. Tuesday nine fifteen to ten ten in the morning, and Sunday eight in the evening to eight fifty-five. An hour and fifty minutes a week.

S2: That sounds thin.

S1: It does, and the arithmetic is worth following because the lesson does it in front of you. Eleven hours twenty is six hundred and eighty minutes. Take a hundred and ten for the study and a hundred and thirty-five for the slack, and about seven and a quarter hours are left. She'd planned six items adding to about four and a half hours. Her own median is one point six, so at that ratio they need about seven and a quarter.

S2: So it fits.

S1: Exactly, and that's the trap. A plan that fits exactly has no room to be wrong, and being short is what lesson three established her forecasts systematically are, in a direction she can measure. So one item comes out, and she writes down which. The website, again, which has now been removed from three consecutive weeks. That fact is itself worth having.

S2: Where did it break?

S1: Tuesday, at nine fifteen, on the first study session of the week. And not because of a client. Her daughter's school ran a parents' coffee morning she'd known about for three weeks and hadn't put on the sheet, because it wasn't the sort of thing that goes on a sheet.

S2: She lost fifty-five minutes.

S1: She lost the only continuous slot before Sunday, which is not the same thing. The qualification got half its week. By Wednesday the plan had one wrong thing in it, and by Thursday she'd stopped looking at it.

S2: What did she change?

S1: Three things, and the third is the finding. She moved the study to Sunday and Tuesday rather than Tuesday and Sunday, because the week's most important work was riding on its most fragile slot. She put school things into the fixed column, not that event but the category, because her lesson two sheet had missed a whole class of fixed commitment. A single week's record has that hole in it by construction.

S2: And the third.

S1: She stopped treating the break as a failure. The plan converted a vague sense that the weeks get away from her into one sentence. My study time is on the most interruptible morning of the week and my sheet is missing the school calendar. She couldn't have got that sentence from a better designed plan. She got it from running a plan that broke.

S2: What did we leave out?

S1: What to do if your discretionary total is two hours, which is a section of its own. Four things people believe about week plans. And the sentence this subject quotes more than any other, as far as the course can tell, about work expanding to fill the time available, which this lesson deflates and lesson eight takes properly. The full lesson is at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S2: Thanks for listening.
