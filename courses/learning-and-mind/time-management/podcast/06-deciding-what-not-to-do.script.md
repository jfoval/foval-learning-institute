S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Time Management, on the sixth lesson. Deciding what not to do.

S1: And the lesson opens by telling you what it is, which I think is the right way round. It's the lesson with the least evidence behind it and, if the rest of the course has done its job, the most use in it.

S2: Then start with the evidence, thin as it is.

S1: Two reviews of the whole field, fourteen years apart. Claessens and colleagues in two thousand and seven say time management behaviours relate positively to perceived control of time, job satisfaction, and health, and negatively to stress, and that the relationship with work and academic performance is not clear. The two thousand and twenty-one meta-analysis, over a hundred and fifty-eight studies, found the relationship with wellbeing, and life satisfaction in particular, larger than the relationship with performance.

S2: That tells me where the benefit lands.

S1: And that's all it is. Nothing this course read in either review tests a method for deciding what to drop. So most of this lesson is the course's own reasoning, and it says so rather than dressing it up as a finding. What it rests on is the measurement you took in lesson two, which is yours and not anybody's average.

S2: All right. Most people's first move is a priority list.

S1: And the lesson's claim is that it does nothing. You write down what you're trying to do, you put it in order, sometimes with numbers, sometimes in a four-box grid. Then the week happens and all of them are still in it.

S2: Why doesn't ordering help?

S1: Because ranking is an operation on a list, and the constraint isn't in the list. It's in the hours. Lesson two asked you to count them. An ordering changes nothing about the hours. Putting something fifth doesn't remove it from the week. It tells you which thing you'll feel worst about when the week runs out.

S2: What about the four-box scheme? Urgent against important. That's the one everybody has met.

S1: The lesson's position there is the course's own, since no study it read tests the scheme. Sorting does something real: it separates whether a thing is pressing from whether it matters. What it cannot do is decide, because the cases that actually hurt are important against important. The work and the parent. The qualification and the child's bedtime. Both land in the same box, and the scheme sorts the easy cases and hands the hard ones back.

S2: So what is a decision, on this lesson's account?

S1: A pair. What I will do, and what I am accepting will not happen. Not a ranking, not an intention, not a hope that it'll all fit. Two halves, both written, and the second is the one that makes it a decision rather than a preference.

S2: And the test?

S1: If nothing is lost, nothing was decided. You can tell the difference by trying to write the second half down. If you can't name what you're giving up, you haven't yet given anything up, and the week will make the choice for you in a fortnight.

S2: Now. A lot of this assumes I have things I could drop.

S1: Which is the objection the lesson takes most seriously, and it builds a case around it. A woman working a fixed rota at a distribution centre, caring for her father four evenings a week. Her sheet came back with two discretionary hours, both on Sunday, and eleven contested hours with conditions written beside them. She's constructed, like the others here.

S2: There's nothing on her sheet to drop.

S1: Nothing. And a course that told her to identify her low-value commitments and decline them would be describing somebody else's life back to her, and she'd be right to stop reading. So the decision on offer is three things and none of them is a technique.

S2: Go on.

S1: One. Where the two hours go, decided rather than defaulted. At the moment they go wherever the Sunday goes, which usually means the washing and whatever's left. Deciding isn't a small act just because the quantity is. It's the difference between two hours that are hers and two hours that are a remainder.

S2: And the second and third?

S1: The second is the eleven contested hours, because every one has a clause beside it from lesson two and those clauses are the only things on her week that could change. The third is the one to have ready. What she would ask for. People do sometimes ask her what would help. A manager, a social worker, a brother on a good day. And the true answer, I don't know, it's just a lot, doesn't get her anything. Having one specific, small, costed request written down before anybody asks is the highest-value thing on that page for a reader in her position, because the moment somebody offers is never the moment to work it out.

S2: What's hers?

S1: One line. One Thursday in three, and I would use it to sleep. And notice she's still named a cost. Her brother has his own week, and asking means he does less of something. She's decided it's worth asking for anyway, which is the same operation as the reader with eleven discretionary hours performs, and a harder one.

S2: There's a question underneath all of this and I want to put it to you directly. Should a person be trying to do more, or trying to do less?

S1: And the lesson faces it rather than letting the machinery answer it, because everything in it is machinery for taking things out of a week and machinery has a direction built into it. Both answers have serious people behind them. The case for less is that hours are finite in a way nothing will change, that most of what gets added is paid for by something nobody notices going, and that the measured benefits of this whole subject land on control, satisfaction, health and stress rather than output.

S2: And the case for more?

S1: Gets said less often in books like this. A life is largely made of what you manage to build. Capacity is real and it grows with use. A great many things worth having are on the far side of a period when you were doing too much, and a person who optimises for a comfortable week can optimise their way out of everything that would have mattered. And the wellbeing finding doesn't touch this, because it measures how people feel rather than what they made.

S2: Where does the course land?

S1: It doesn't, and it says it has no standing to. What it offers is the same either way. Know what your hours are, decide rather than default, and write down what the decision costs. A person doing too much on purpose, with the cost named, is doing something this course has nothing to object to. What it argues against is only the third thing, which is carrying a life you haven't decided on and paying for it every week. And it names its own lean, which is the do-less side, so you can discount for it.

What you've already given up without deciding to, which is the uncomfortable half. And three more things people believe about this, beyond the sorting one we did.
