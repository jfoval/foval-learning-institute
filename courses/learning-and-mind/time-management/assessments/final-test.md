  # --- The estimate-record gap and its shape (L1) ---
  - q: >-
      Comparing survey estimates of working hours with the same people's time diaries, what was
      found, and on whom?
    options:
      - Estimates run above diaries by 5 to 10 percent on paid work, on United States and Belgian samples
      - Diaries run above estimates on United States and Belgian national samples, published in 2011
      - Estimates run above diaries by a constant that can be subtracted from any survey figure since
      - Estimates and diaries agree closely except among people working fewer than thirty-five hours
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The overestimate is on paid work, it is modest on average, and it is concentrated among people
      reporting the longest weeks. The diaries-run-higher answer inverts it for paid work, though it
      is the right direction for sleep and free time. A constant would make correction easy, which
      the uneven distribution rules out. And short-week workers are one of the groups that
      underestimates.
  - q: >-
      A man tells you he works about seventy hours a week and is quite certain of it. On what has
      been measured, what should you expect his diary to show, and what can nobody tell him?
    options:
      - Close to seventy, since somebody working that much would be unlikely to have miscounted it
      - Below seventy, and nobody can say in advance by how much, because the spread is what is wide
      - Below seventy by about five percent, which is the average gap the research reports for work
      - Above seventy, because a diary catches work that people do not think of as work at all
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      He is in the band where the gap is widest, so a shortfall is what to expect, and applying the
      average to somebody at the far end is exactly the mistake the distribution finding exists to
      stop. Reading confidence as accuracy is the first answer's error. And a diary catching unnamed
      work is a real effect pointing the other way here.

  # --- Reading a week, fixed against discretionary (L2) ---
  - q: >-
      A woman records a week, sorts it, and finds her discretionary total is close to what she would
      have guessed, but that it arrives in twenty-six separate pieces. What has the record shown
      that the total alone could not?
    options:
      - That she was right about her week, since the figure she would have guessed turned out correct
      - That her recording was too coarse, because twenty-six pieces in a week is not a plausible count
      - That the same number of hours can hold work another week of the same total could not hold
      - That she is being interrupted more than most people are, which is what the piece count measures
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Hours in twenty-six pieces and the same hours in three blocks are not the same resource, and
      the total is precisely the figure that cannot tell them apart. Being right about the total is
      half the comparison. Nothing suggests the count is implausible. And the record says nothing
      about other people's weeks or about what caused the fragmentation.
  - q: >-
      A man sorting his week writes beside Wednesday evening: "fixed, unless the rota changes in
      April". Another writes beside Thursday: "fixed". What has the first one got that the second
      has not?
    options:
      - A more accurate description, since almost nothing in a week is fixed on a long enough view
      - A reason to expect change, which makes the hour easier to bear while it stays where it is
      - A note to himself, which is what makes the difference between a record and a diary entry
      - A condition, which is something that can be asked about, checked, or found to be false
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The clause names the thing that would have to change, which turns an hour he is carrying into
      a question he could ask. Accuracy about the long view is not what the sort is for. Nothing
      here is about how the hour feels. And both entries are notes; only one of them is testable.

  # --- The planning fallacy and the two failed corrections (L3) ---
  - q: >-
      Students predicted their thesis submission three ways, including a date assuming everything
      went as badly as possible. What happened, and on whom was it measured?
    options:
      - Most met the worst-case date; thirty-seven final-year psychology students in Canada, 1994
      - Fewer than half met the worst-case date; thirty-seven final-year students in Canada, 1994
      - Fewer than half met their best estimate; a national sample of graduate students, 1994
      - Almost none met any of the three dates; thirty-seven students at one Canadian university
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      48.7 percent met the pessimistic date and its average was still nearly a week short of the
      actual. About thirty percent met the best estimate, so the third answer picks the wrong one of
      the two figures and the wrong sample. Almost half met one of the dates, so "almost none" is
      wrong. And it was one honours course at one university, not a national sample.
  - q: >-
      A tradesman who has quoted the same kind of job for fifteen years says the planning fallacy
      does not apply to him. How should that be answered?
    options:
      - It is a fair point about that kind of job, and nothing in this literature was measured on him
      - It is wrong, since the effect was found in every group the researchers looked at in the study
      - It is wrong, because being paid by the quote makes a person more optimistic rather than less
      - It is irrelevant, since the finding is about people in general and not about any one trade
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      He has a narrow band of work he has quoted hundreds of times with money attached, and the
      studies read here were students on academic projects, so the honest answer concedes the point
      and then asks how long the extension on his own house will take. The second and third answers
      claim evidence nobody has. And a finding about people in general is exactly what an individual
      case can bear on.
  - q: >-
      A woman doubles all her estimates on principle. What does the research predict about her new
      figures?
    options:
      - They will be accurate, since doubling is roughly the ratio the published studies report
      - They will be worse, because an inflated estimate causes work to expand into the extra time
      - They cannot be judged, since the size of a personal padding factor has never been studied
      - They will be less biased and no more accurate, since padding adds nothing about this job
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The instruction to be pessimistic reduced the bias and left the absolute error almost exactly
      where it was, which is the measured version of this. Doubling might land near the published
      ratios and that is luck rather than forecasting. Work expanding is an untested claim this
      course traces to a satire. And instructed pessimism is precisely what was measured.
  - q: >-
      Sixty-two students had deadlines somebody else set. Four fifths met the deadline; fewer than
      two fifths met their own earlier prediction. What follows for somebody trying to know when
      their own work will be done?
    options:
      - That predictions are worthless and only an externally imposed date should ever be trusted
      - That they should set themselves artificial deadlines, which the study shows works as well
      - That a date somebody else set is better evidence than their own forecast, where one exists
      - That they finish reliably, so the forecast does not matter much in practice either way
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Their completion times tracked the deadlines closely and their predictions barely did, so
      where a real deadline exists it is the better evidence. Calling predictions worthless
      overshoots. The study did not test self-imposed deadlines. And the forecast matters a great
      deal for everything that has no deadline at all.

  # --- Goal intentions against if-then plans (L4) ---
  - q: >-
      "I am going to be more organised about the invoices." Rewrite the form of that, in the terms
      this course uses. What has to change?
    options:
      - It needs a cue, meaning a situation that will occur anyway and that starts the behaviour
      - It needs a measurement, so that he can tell whether the invoices actually improved
      - It needs a deadline, since dates set by other people are what the evidence shows works
      - It needs a reason, because an intention without a stated reason is the kind that lapses
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      That sentence is a goal intention, an instruction about a desired outcome, and an if-then plan
      differs by naming the situation that triggers the response. Measuring and setting deadlines are
      both useful and are other techniques from other lessons. And nothing read here shows that
      stating a reason changes whether an intention lapses.
  - q: >-
      A woman's if-then plan has not fired in four weeks. Her cue is "when I feel ready after
      lunch". What is the first thing to check?
    options:
      - Whether she holds the goal, since the effect is reported as stronger where she does
      - Whether the cue is an event, since feeling ready is a judgement she has to make
      - Whether four weeks is long enough for the behaviour to have become automatic yet
      - Whether the response is too large, since a smaller one would be easier to start
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The cue is the half that does the work, and "when I feel ready" is a state she has to check
      for, which is exactly what fails on the day it matters. Holding the goal is the right question
      once the form is sound, and here it is not. Nothing read here measures how long a habit takes.
      And the size of the response is worth checking third.
  - q: >-
      Somebody reads that if-then plans have a pooled effect size of d = .65 and concludes the
      technique will work for him. What is wrong with that step?
    options:
      - Nothing, since a medium-to-large pooled effect is exactly what a person should act on
      - The figure is too small to act on, and a pooled effect of that size is close to noise
      - A pooled figure describes a body of studies and says nothing about any one person's week
      - The studies behind it were about health behaviours, which do not transfer to working life
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      An average over ninety-four tests and eight thousand people is a claim about the technique,
      and the only measurement of him is one he takes. It is a real effect, so the second answer
      understates it. And the scope of the pooled studies is not something this course has read,
      which is its own reason not to make the fourth claim.

  # --- The working day, switching and interruption (L5) ---
  - q: >-
      A man counts his own day and finds he averages 22 minutes on a piece of work before switching.
      The field study in this course measured 11 minutes 4 seconds. What has he established?
    options:
      - That he concentrates better than the information workers that study observed did
      - That his job has fewer separate pieces of work in it, which is what the gap measures
      - That his count is wrong, since the published figure is the one that has been checked
      - That his day is less fragmented than the day that study measured, on the count he took
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      He has one number about himself and it is larger, which is all of it; why the two differ is
      not something a count can say. Concentration is a cause the measurement does not carry. The
      number of working spheres is a separate count. And twenty-four people in one office is not a
      standard his own day can be wrong against.
  - q: >-
      A receptionist is told to protect two hours of uninterrupted time each morning. What is wrong
      with that advice for her in particular?
    options:
      - Nothing, provided she is firm about it, which is what the advice is really asking for
      - Being available is a large part of the job, so the advice treats her work as a defect
      - Two hours is too long, and the same advice would work for her at thirty minutes instead
      - She should protect the time in the afternoon, when fewer people come to the desk
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The advice assumes interruption is a fault in a day that could have been otherwise, and hers
      could not; the useful question is which of her work needs continuity and when in the week such
      a stretch exists. Firmness is the answer that makes it her failing. And shortening or moving
      the block keeps the same assumption.
  - q: >-
      Somebody quotes a figure for what an interruption costs, given to the second. What does this
      course say the precision itself tells you?
    options:
      - Nothing on its own, though a figure that never arrives with a page number has come loose
      - That the study behind it measured carefully, which is what a figure to the second implies
      - That the figure is false, since no real measurement of this could be that precise
      - That it has been rounded from a larger one, which is how such figures usually travel
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Precision is doing rhetorical work rather than measuring anything, and the tell is that nobody
      quoting it can say which table it is in. Precision does not imply care. Calling it false
      overshoots, since an unsourced figure can still be true. And nothing suggests rounding.

  # --- Deciding what not to do (L6) ---
  - q: >-
      A man lists his commitments in order of importance every January. What does this course say
      that does, and what does it not do?
    options:
      - It clarifies what matters and then makes the choices that follow from it much easier
      - It fails because he ranks by importance, when urgency is the dimension that decides
      - It orders the list and removes nothing from the week, which is where the constraint is
      - It works for the hard cases and is unnecessary for the easy ones, which is backwards
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Ranking is an operation on a list and his hours are unchanged by the order he wrote them in; a
      decision needs something on the other side of it. Clarity is real and is not a decision.
      Sorting by urgency has the same limitation. And the scheme handles the easy cases and hands
      back the hard ones, which is the reverse of the fourth answer.
  - q: >-
      A woman on a fixed rota with a disabled son has about ninety minutes she genuinely decides
      about. What decision does this course say is available to her?
    options:
      - None yet, because a decision requires discretion and hers would have to be created first
      - Which of her caring commitments to reduce, since every arrangement is negotiable in time
      - How to protect the ninety minutes, which is the technique the interruption lesson gives
      - Where the ninety minutes go, and what she would ask for if anybody asked what would help
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Ninety minutes is small and it is real, and the request worth having ready matters as much,
      because the moment somebody offers is not the moment to work one out. Telling her she has no
      decision until she has more hours is the move this course exists to refuse, and telling her
      the caring is negotiable is a judgement that is not the course's to make. Protecting time is a
      technique rather than a decision.
  - q: >-
      A man writes: "I am leaving the committee, because it takes two evenings a month." What is
      missing, on this course's account of what a decision is?
    options:
      - What it costs him to leave, without which nothing has actually been decided
      - A date, since a decision with no date on it is one he can defer indefinitely
      - Who else could do it, which is the question that determines whether he should
      - A measurement of the two evenings, since his estimate of them is probably short
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      He has written what he is doing and why, and not what he is accepting will not happen; if
      nothing is lost then nothing was decided. A date and a reversal condition are the other two
      lines and come after the cost. Who else could do it is a fact about the committee. And the
      estimate is not what makes this incomplete.

  # --- Building a week (L7) ---
  - q: >-
      A woman's week plan is built carefully and fits her discretionary hours exactly. What does this
      course say about that?
    options:
      - That it is well made, since fitting the hours available is what a good plan does
      - That it has no room to be wrong, and being wrong is what her forecasts reliably are
      - That she should add more, since a plan with room left in it is a plan under-used
      - That it cannot be judged until she runs it, which is the only test that means anything
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      A plan with every hour committed is fully wrong the moment anything unexpected arrives, and
      the planning lesson established that her estimates run systematically short. Fitting exactly
      is the problem rather than the achievement. Adding more makes it worse. And running it is the
      test of much else, but the absence of slack is visible before Monday.
  - q: >-
      A man's plan broke on Tuesday because of a school event he had known about for weeks and never
      written down. What is the finding, and what is not?
    options:
      - The finding is that he is disorganised; what is not is anything about the plan itself
      - The finding is that the plan was too ambitious; what is not is anything about the week
      - The finding is that Tuesday is a bad day to schedule anything he actually cares about
      - The finding is that his sheet is missing a whole class of commitment; the broken plan is not
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      School things recur, are known about in advance, and were absent from a single week's record
      by construction, which is a specific repair he can make. The plan breaking is the measurement
      rather than the result. Calling it disorganisation makes it about him. And nothing here is
      about Tuesdays.

  # --- Reading a claim (L8) ---
  - q: >-
      "Top performers protect their mornings." Sort that claim and say what would settle it.
    options:
      - Measurable, and a survey of when high performers do their work would settle the whole claim
      - False, since counter-examples are easy to produce and one counter-example is enough
      - Two claims, one measurable once the terms are defined and one causal and unsupported here
      - Unmeasurable, because "top performers" and "protect" are both undefined as they stand
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      It asserts that top performers do this and implies that doing it is why they are top
      performers, and the second half is carried in the word order and supported by nothing. A
      survey would address the first half only. The undefined terms are a real problem with that
      first half rather than with the whole claim. And it does not quite make the claim that a
      counter-example would refute.

Twenty items, covering all eight lessons. Eighteen of them apply the course to a person, a week or a
claim that appears in no lesson; two ask for a figure, and both of those ask for its scope as well,
because a figure without its scope is the defect this course spends eight lessons on.

**Nothing here turns on a figure that dates.** The gap between estimate and diary has been a
different size in every decade anybody measured, and the interruption figures belong to one office
in one year, so no item is built on either. What the items test is the reasoning, which is the part
that keeps working after the numbers move.

**Three items are deliberately cross-lesson.** The item about a plan that fits exactly pairs the
planning fallacy with the week you build, because a plan is a prediction. The item about the woman
with ninety minutes pairs the sort with the refusal, because you can only decline what was yours.
And the sorting item at the end pairs the course's opening measurement with its closing habit,
because they are the same move pointed inward and outward.

**Pass mark 0.7**, which is fourteen of twenty.
