Eight lessons have given you a way of finding out where your hours actually go, a number for how far
your own estimates run short, a plan form that fires on a cue instead of on willpower, a picture of
what a working day really looks like, a way of deciding what not to do that names its cost, and a
habit for reading claims about any of it.

**This project is the first time you use all of it at once, on your own life rather than on a
constructed case.** It takes about ten hours spread over two or three weeks, and most of those hours are the recording in section 1, which is spread out because it has to be; you can't do a fortnight in an afternoon.

## The problem this project has, and how it solves it

**No section here can have a right answer**, because the answer's your week and nobody else has seen it. A project that asked you to reach a conclusion couldn't be assessed, and one that asked you to reflect would produce nothing you could check.

**So every section asks for a prediction, then the record, then the gap between them.** The gap is
the thing that can be assessed without a key, and it is also the only thing a single fortnight can
honestly establish. If that sounds familiar it is lesson 2's argument, applied to the course rather than to a week.

**One consequence, stated up front so it doesn't feel like a trick.** A fortnight in which every
prediction turned out correct is almost certainly a fortnight whose predictions were written
afterwards. The rubric says so, and a project with an honest wrong prediction in it scores better
than a tidy one without.

## The deliverable

**One plain text or Markdown file, roughly 1,200 to 2,000 words, in five sections**, with the
records to back it.

Plain text, for the reason **Digital Literacy**'s project gives: it exercises something that course
taught you, it will still open in thirty years, and it leaves you holding a document you made rather than a page inside somebody's product. A notebook photographed at the end is fine too, as long as
the five sections and their numbers are legible.

## Section 1: two weeks recorded

**Budget: about twenty minutes of setup and five to ten minutes a day, across fourteen days.**

Before each week, write your four estimates: paid work, domestic work, sleep, and anything you would
call leisure. Before you start, not after you finish. Then record what actually happens, at fifteen-minute resolution or
finer, including the days that go badly.

**Two weeks, not one, and the reason is lesson 2's.** One week is a sample of one: it can show you a
surprise and it cannot show you a rate. **The difference between your two weeks is a finding of its
own**, and for most people it is larger than they expect.

Write, in this section: the two sets of estimates, the two sets of totals, and one paragraph on
what the two weeks did not have in common.

## Section 2: the sort

**Budget: about an hour.**

Take the second week and sort every recorded hour into **fixed**, **discretionary**, or
**contested**, with the condition written beside every contested entry.

Then the numbers: **how many genuinely discretionary hours you have, and in how many separate
pieces they arrive, and how long the longest piece is.**

Write, in this section: the three numbers, the contested list with its conditions, and one sentence
saying whether the total was more or fewer than you would have guessed before you counted.

## Section 3: five predictions and five measurements

**Budget: about half an hour of writing, spread across the fortnight.**

Five real tasks, each with a written prediction before you start and the actual time after you
finish. Then the ratio for each: actual divided by predicted, not the difference, because the ratio
is what transfers to a task of another size.

At least two of the five should be small, and at least one should be something that waits on
somebody else, because lesson 3's worked case turns on the difference between those two kinds.

Write, in this section: the five pairs, the five ratios, your median, and one sentence on whether
the ratios cluster or whether one of them is doing all the work.

## Section 4: one refusal and one if-then plan

**Budget: about an hour to write, then a week of living with them.**

**The refusal**, in lesson 6's four lines: what, why, what it costs on the other side, and what
would reverse it with a date. If your sheet has almost nothing you could decline, **write a request instead**, in the same four lines. Both are decisions and both have costs.

**The if-then plan**, in lesson 4's form, with a cue that exists in the week you recorded in section
1. Test the cue against the question: would I notice this if I were tired and in a bad mood? If you wouldn't, the cue's wrong.

Then live with both for a week and report. For the plan: **out of how many times the cue occurred,
how many times did the response happen?** A count, not an impression. For the refusal: did you keep
it, was it told to whoever needed telling, and what did it actually cost against what you predicted
it would cost?

## Section 5: one traced claim, and the estimate again

**Budget: about forty-five minutes.**

**Trace one claim about time or attention with a number in it**, as far as you can get in twenty
minutes, writing down each step. Report where the trail ended. All four endings are results,
including running out of twenty minutes.

**Then, without looking at anything**, write your four estimates again from memory, and only then
open your section 1 records and compare.

Write, in this section: the traced claim with its trail, your four remembered numbers, the
comparison, and one sentence naming the category you got wrong in the same direction both times.
**That one is your standing bias**, and it's the most useful line in the whole project.

## Rubric

| Criterion | What full marks look like |
|---|---|
| The record is real | Two weeks of actual entries, including the days that went badly and the hours you could not account for |
| Predictions are written first | Every prediction is recorded before its measurement, and **at least one of them is wrong** |
| The sort is honest | Section 2 separates hours that are genuinely fixed from hours you have decided not to contest, with a condition beside each contested one |
| Ratios rather than differences | Section 3 reports ratios, so the finding transfers to tasks of other sizes, and names the outlier rather than averaging it away |
| Something was given up | Section 4 names a real refusal or a real request with a real cost, not a hypothetical one |
| The plan names its cue | The if-then plan's cue is an event visible in the week recorded in section 1, and the report gives a count rather than an impression |
| Dated and scoped | Every figure quoted from the course carries what it was measured on and when |
| Honest about the gap | Where a prediction was wrong, the file says so, and says what the mistake was rather than explaining it away |

**The first and last rows are this course's own standards applied to you**, and it's worth saying so rather than leaving you to notice. Lesson 1 asked what a figure was measured on and when. Lesson
8 asked what happens when nobody checks. This project asks both of those about a document you wrote
yourself, which is the only way either habit becomes yours rather than something you're able to recite.

## What this project cannot tell you

**Not whether your week is good.** Nothing in this course has a view on that, and it isn't being coy, and lesson 6 says at
length why the question is yours.

**Not a rate.** A fortnight is two observations. It can show you that your model of your own life is wrong in a named place, which is worth a great deal and isn't the same as knowing what you spend on anything in a year.

**And not whether any of this worked.** That would need the fortnight run twice, months apart, with
something changed in between, and you'd still be one person. If you want to know, lesson 8 tells
you what the field's own two reviews manage to say after 158 studies, which is less than you might hope and more than nothing.
