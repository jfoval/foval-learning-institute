This is the assembly lesson. Nothing in it is new, and everything in it comes from a sheet you
already have.

Six lessons have each handed you one thing. Lesson 2 gave you the hours that are actually yours.
Lesson 3 gave you a number for how far your estimates run short. Lesson 4 gave you plans attached to
cues instead of to intentions. Lesson 5 gave you the real shape of a working day. Lesson 6 gave you
one refusal, in writing, with its cost named.

This lesson puts them in a week, runs it, and then does the part that matters, which is reading what
broke.

## Why most week plans fail on Tuesday

Start with the failure, because the shape of it is specific and recognising it is most of the
lesson. **This course has no measurement of when plans break**, and what follows is its own account
rather than a finding.[4] Your own week is the test of it, which is what the exercise is for.

A plan built the usual way isn't wrong on Monday. You write it on Sunday evening, you look at it,
and it is fine. Everything fits, the week looks like the one you wanted, and there is a reasonable
amount in it.

Then Tuesday. Somebody needs something. A meeting moves. A child is sick, a client sends the thing
they promised on Friday, a form arrives with a date on it. **And now the plan is wrong**, not
slightly but structurally, because the thing that moved was load-bearing and everything after it was
stacked on top.

The part worth noticing is what happens next. **What happens next, in this course's account of it,
is almost never revision.** It is abandonment.[4] The plan goes in a drawer, the week is got
through, and the conclusion reached on Sunday is usually about the planner rather than about the
plan.

**Neither of those is the finding.** They are both about you, and the finding is not. What is going
on is that the plan was built as though the week were yours, and lesson 2 asked you to count how
much of it actually is.

:::checkpoint So is the lesson just "be realistic"? What does this course add to that?
Two things, and the second is the useful one.

The first is that "realistic" has a measurement behind it now. You're not guessing at how much
of the week is yours. You counted it in lesson 2, and you counted its shape as well as its total,
which this course expects came out smaller and more fragmented than you had assumed. "Be realistic"
is advice. A number of discretionary hours and a count of how many pieces they arrive in is an
input.

**The second is that a plan is a prediction**, and lesson 3 is entirely about what happens to your
predictions. Everything that lesson found about a single task applies to a week: you plan the run
that goes well, your own history is not in the room while you do it, and the result is
systematically short in a direction you can measure.

So a week plan isn't a statement of intent that you either live up to or fall short of. It is a forecast,
and it is subject to every finding in this course about forecasts. That is a different relationship
to have with it, and it is the one that survives a Tuesday.
:::

## The order, and why the order is the technique

Six steps. **The order is this course's own rather than anything a source recommends**,[4] and the
order carries almost all of the value.

**One: the fixed points go in first.** Everything from the fixed column of your lesson 2 sheet.
Shifts, the school run, sleep, the journey, the appointment, the evening you are at your mother's.
Not because they matter most, but because **they're the parts of the week that are true.** A plan
that starts anywhere else is a plan about somebody else's week.

**Two: the refusal goes in, as an absence with a name on it.** Write the thing you decided in lesson
6 into the week explicitly: not by leaving a gap where it used to be, but by writing "Tuesday
evening: not the committee" for the first few weeks. **A gap is ambiguous and will silently fill.**
A named absence is a decision you can see you are keeping.

**Three: the discretionary hours go in against their real shape.** This is lesson 2's second count
doing its work, with lesson 5's explanation of why your day has that shape. If your eleven hours
arrive in thirty-one pieces, don't plan them as three blocks. Put the work that needs continuity in
the two or three genuinely long pieces you have, and match everything else to the size of the piece
it will actually get.

**Four: the if-then plans go on cues that exist in the week you have just written.** Lesson 4's
point was that the "when" half does the work, and you now have a sheet showing which "whens" are
real. If a plan's cue is not visible on this sheet, the cue is wrong and the plan will not fire.

**Five: reserve the slack before planning the rest.** How much is the course's own judgement and not
a finding.[4] A rough figure that works: **hold back a fifth of your discretionary time**, in pieces
rather than in one lump, and put it where the week historically goes wrong rather than at the end.
Reserving it first matters, because slack added at the end is slack taken off something already
planned, and that is the step nobody actually performs.

**Six: apply your own ratio to what is left, and take something out.** Lesson 3 gave you a median
for how far your estimates run short, **and it gave you its scope**: your median is for the items
whose length is yours to decide. Anything that involves waiting on somebody else gets a whole
morning instead, which is what lesson 3's outlier was about. Apply the ratio, and if what you have
planned does not fit, take one thing out and write down what it was. Not adjust, not compress.

Slack is not rest and it is not catching up, though it can be spent on either. Slack is what lets
a plan be wrong without being finished. A week with every hour committed is a week that is fully
wrong by Tuesday afternoon; a week with a fifth loose absorbs the Tuesday and carries on.

### If your discretionary total is two hours

Lesson 6 was built around you and this lesson has to be as well, because the six steps above assume a week with room to arrange, and yours hasn't got one.

Steps one and two are the same and they are most of the value. Your fixed points go in, and your
refusal or your request goes in as a named line rather than as a gap. That is a week honestly described, which is more than most people have.

**Step three changes shape.** If you have no long pieces at all, do not force one. What you have is short pieces, and the useful move is to decide **what one thing** those short pieces are for this
week, rather than letting them go to whatever's nearest. One decision, not a timetable.

**Step four still works and is where your leverage is.** An if-then plan needs a cue, not an hour,
and a fixed week is full of reliable cues. That's the one technique in this course that gets better rather than worse as discretion shrinks.

**Steps five and six mostly don't apply.** A fifth of two hours is twenty-four minutes, which is not slack, it is a rounding error, and applying a ratio to two items tells you nothing you did not
already know. Skip them, and spend the attention on the conditions beside your fixed hours instead,
which is where a week like yours will actually change.

:::checkpoint Why does step six apply your ratio to the week as a whole, rather than to each item separately, when lesson 3 measured it on single tasks?
Because what you are correcting at this level is not the length of any one item. It is the total.

Lesson 3 measured the ratio on individual tasks because that is where you can see it, and its own
warning was that a ratio is only stable within a kind of work. Applied item by item to a week, you'd be pretending to a precision the measurement doesn't support: your median is a summary of five tasks, not a multiplier for each of the next six.

Applied to the total it does something more modest and more defensible. It says: whatever you
have planned, you have historically needed about this much more of it than you thought, so plan for
less. That is a claim about the size of the week rather than about the length of any job in it, and
it survives the fact that your individual estimates vary.

And the excluded category matters more here than it did in lesson 3. One item that waits on
somebody else can swallow a morning on its own, and no multiplier's going to catch that, which is why it gets the morning rather than the ratio.
:::

### Worked: Hannah builds a week

**Hannah is the constructed reader from lesson 2**,[4] the self-employed bookkeeper with a
six-year-old. Her sheet says **11 hours 20 of discretionary time, arriving in thirty-one pieces**,
the longest 55 minutes and nineteen of them under fifteen.

She has one goal she keeps not starting, which is a bookkeeping qualification, and one refusal
written in lesson 6's four lines. **What:** she has stopped doing the monthly accounts for her
brother-in-law's business unpaid. **Why:** they were costing her about three hours a month. **What
it costs:** probably an awkward Christmas, which she's written down rather than hoped about.
**What would reverse it, and when:** if he offers to pay the going rate she'll reconsider, and she
looks at it again in June.

**The fixed points go in.** Two mornings at a client's premises. The school run twice a day. Her
daughter's bedtime. Thursday at her mother's. Sleep. That is most of the week, and it is not a
complaint, it is the input.

**The refusal goes in.** The last Wednesday evening of the month is written as *not the accounts*.

**The discretionary time goes in against its shape.** The qualification needs continuity, so it gets
the two genuinely long pieces: Tuesday 9:15 to 10:10, after the school run and before her first
client call, and Sunday 20:00 to 20:55. **An hour and fifty minutes a week of study**, which looks
thin, and lesson 8 is where that feeling gets examined.

**Her nineteen short pieces get short work.** Invoices, the one email that needs writing properly,
the thing she keeps meaning to file. Not the qualification, which will not fit into fourteen minutes
and should not be asked to.

**The slack is reserved next.** A fifth of 11 hours 20 is about two and a quarter hours, and she
holds that back in four pieces, three of them on Wednesday and Thursday, because that is where her
weeks have historically gone wrong.

**Then the ratio.** Her own median came out at 1.6, and lesson 3 warned her not to borrow anybody
else's. Do the arithmetic with her: 11 hours 20 is 680 minutes, less 110 for the study and 135 for
the slack, leaves **about seven and a quarter hours** for everything else. She had planned six
items adding to about four and a half hours; at 1.6 they need about seven and a quarter.

**Which fits exactly, and that is the trap.** A plan that fits exactly hasn't room for being wrong,
and lesson 3's whole finding is that her forecast is systematically short. **So one item comes
out**, and she writes down which: the website, again, which has now been removed from three
consecutive weeks. That fact is itself worth having.

:::predict Before you read what happened: Hannah's week is carefully built, honest about her hours, and has slack in it. Which day do you think it broke on, and what do you think broke it?
This course expects most readers to say Thursday or Friday, and to reach for something external: a client emergency, the
child being ill, a deadline moving.

Write down your own answer, because the exercise at the end of this lesson asks you to make the same
prediction about your own week before you run it, and the point of making it in writing is that you
find out afterwards whether your model of your own week is any good. That is lesson 1's move,
applied one last time to the plan itself.
:::

### Worked: the same week, after it broke

It broke on Tuesday, at 9:15, on the first study session of the week.

Not because of a client. Her daughter's school ran a parents' coffee morning that she had known
about for three weeks and had not put on the sheet, because it wasn't the sort of thing that goes on
a sheet.

Look at what that did, because the mechanism is the lesson. Tuesday 9:15 was one of her two long
pieces. Losing it didn't cost her fifty-five minutes; it cost her the only continuous slot before
Sunday, and the qualification therefore got half of its week. By Wednesday the plan had one wrong
thing in it. By Thursday she had stopped looking at it.

Three things she changed, and the third one is the finding.

She moved the study to Sunday and Tuesday rather than Tuesday and Sunday. If the first long
piece of the week is also the most exposed, then the week's most important work is riding on its
most fragile slot. Sunday evening is the one nobody else can claim.

She put the coffee morning and its relatives into the fixed column. Not that specific event: the
category. School things, three or four a term, always known about in advance, never written down.
Her lesson 2 sheet had missed a whole class of fixed commitment because none of them happened in the
week she recorded. A single week's record has that hole in it by construction, which lesson 2
said and which she has now met.

**And she stopped treating the break as a failure.** This is the one that transfers. The plan did
its job: it converted a vague sense that the weeks get away from her into one sentence, *my study
time is on the most interruptible morning of the week and my sheet is missing the school calendar.*
She couldn't have got that sentence from a better-designed plan. She got it from running a plan
that broke.

## What a plan is for

The course's position, stated plainly and labelled as its own.[4]

**A week plan is an instrument, not a promise.** You build it, you run it, and what you get back is
information about where the week pushes on you. A plan that breaks in a new place every week is
telling you something. A plan that breaks in the same place four weeks running is telling you
something much more specific, and in this course's reading it is usually a fixed point you have not
admitted is fixed.

That reframing does a particular job, and it is worth being explicit about what. **It removes the
moral content.** The relationship most people have with a week plan, this course expects, is that
keeping it means you did well and breaking it means you did badly, which is why the drawer exists.
An instrument that gives a reading you did not want is not a failed instrument.

## Four things people believe about week plans

**"A plan that survived the week was a good plan."** It might have been. It might equally have been
a quiet week that would have gone fine anyway, or a plan with very little in it. **A surviving plan
is weak evidence**, because nothing in the week tells you which of those it was, and this is the
same problem lesson 1 had with a single estimate.

**"A plan that broke was a bad plan."** The break is the measurement. The only plan that teaches you
nothing is the one you didn't run.

**"I need a system."** You need about four inputs and a sheet of paper, and you have the inputs.
Systems are a genuine interest for a good many people, and the tell for when one has stopped being
that is straightforward: if you've changed the system more often than you've run it, the system is
the thing you're doing instead. **This course is not going to recommend a tool**, and it has not
researched any.[4]

**"If I plan every hour, I will use every hour."** Behind this is the sentence this subject quotes
more than any other, as far as this course can tell: that work expands so as to fill the time
available for its completion. **This course hasn't read the essay it comes from** and has it at the
level of a search summary.[3] What that summary records is worth knowing now and is lesson 8's
subject properly: the line opens a 1955 satire, its author offers it as a commonplace observation
rather than as a result, the essay is actually about why bureaucracies grow regardless of the work
available, and no empirical test of the time-expansion claim was found when this course went
looking.

So: an amusing observation that might well be true, presented as a law, quoted for seventy years,
and, as far as this course can tell, never tested. Plan every hour if it suits you. Don't do it
because of Parkinson.

## Practice

:::exercise Build it, run it, and record the breaks
Take 45 minutes to build, then 5 minutes at the end of the week. You need your lesson 2 sheet, your
lesson 3 median ratio, your lesson 4 plans and your lesson 6 refusal.

**If you're missing one of those, don't stop.** Substitute, and mark the substitute so you know
which parts of the result are measured and which are guessed. **No lesson 2 sheet:** guess your
discretionary total and how many pieces it comes in, and treat the whole exercise as a prediction
to check later. **No ratio:** use 1.5, and write "borrowed, not mine" beside it, because lesson 3 is explicit that somebody else's ratio isn't evidence about you. **No if-then plans:** write one
now, in one line. **No refusal:** skip step 2 and note that you skipped it.

**Build, in this order and no other.**

1. **Fixed points**, from the fixed column. All of them, including sleep and travel.
2. **Your refusal**, written in as a named absence rather than as a gap. If lesson 6 made yours a
   request rather than a withdrawal, write the request and the date you will make it, which goes in
   the week the same way.
3. **Discretionary time against its shape.** Long work in the long pieces, short work in the short
   ones. Count how many genuinely long pieces you have. This course expects one or two, and some readers will have none.
4. **Your if-then plans**, each attached to a cue that already exists in the week you have just
   written down. If a plan's cue is not visible on this sheet, the cue is wrong.
5. **Reserve about a fifth of the discretionary time**, in pieces, placed where your weeks
   historically go wrong. Do this before the next step, not after it.
6. Apply your ratio to what is left, and remove one thing. Apply it to the items whose length is
   yours to decide, and give anything that waits on somebody else a whole morning. Write down what
   you removed. If you can't bring yourself to remove anything, that's the finding, and write that
   down instead.

Then, before the week starts, one line: which day do you think it will break, and what do you
think will break it? Seal it, or just write it at the top of the sheet where you will not
conveniently forget it.

**Run the week.** Don't tidy the sheet as you go.

**At the end, four lines.** What actually broke it, and when. Whether your prediction was right.
What the break cost, in the sense Hannah's did, which is not minutes but the thing that could not
then happen. And one change for next week, exactly one.
:::

:::exercise The plan you already had
Take 20 minutes, and do this one before the week you have just built rather than after.

Most people reading this will have made a week plan before, probably several. Think of the last one.
**If you never have**, take the last time you decided to start something on a Monday; it produces
the same three lines.

1. **What was it in?** Paper, an app, a calendar, your head.
2. **How long did it last?** Days rather than weeks is the answer this course expects.
3. **What did you conclude when it stopped?** Write the actual sentence you told yourself. It is usually a sentence about you rather than about the plan.
4. **Now write what this lesson would have said instead**, in one sentence, about where the week
   pushed back.

The gap between lines 3 and 4 is the whole of this lesson. This course expects you to find that you have run four or five perfectly good measurements of your own week and thrown every result away.
:::

**Back.** This lesson uses lesson 2's sort, lesson 3's ratio, lesson 4's cues, lesson 5's shape and
lesson 6's refusal, and adds nothing to any of them. Lesson 1's habit of predicting before measuring
is applied one last time, to the plan itself, which is why the exercise asks you to name the day
