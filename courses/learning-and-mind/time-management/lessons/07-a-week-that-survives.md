This is the assembly lesson. Nothing in it is new, and everything in it comes from a sheet you already have.

Six lessons have each handed you one thing. Lesson 2 gave you the hours that are actually yours.
Lesson 3 gave you a number for how far your estimates run short. Lesson 4 gave you plans attached to
cues instead of to intentions. Lesson 5 gave you the real shape of a working day. Lesson 6 gave you
one refusal, in writing, with its cost named.

This lesson puts them in a week, runs it, and then does the part that matters, which is reading what
broke.

## Why most week plans fail on Tuesday

Start with the failure, because the shape of it is specific and recognising it is most of the
lesson.

A plan built the usual way isn't wrong on Monday. You write it on Sunday evening, you look at it,
and it is fine. Everything fits, the week looks like the one you wanted, and there is a reasonable
amount in it.

Then Tuesday. Somebody needs something. A meeting moves. A child is sick, a client sends the thing
they promised on Friday, a form arrives with a date on it. **And now the plan is wrong**, not slightly but structurally, because the thing that moved was load-bearing and everything after it
was stacked on top.

Here is the part worth noticing. **What happens next is almost never revision.** It is abandonment.
The plan goes in a drawer, the week is got through, and on Sunday the conclusion is either "I need a
better system" or "I am not the sort of person who plans".

**Neither of those is the finding.** They are both about you, and the finding is not. The finding is that the plan was built as though the week were
yours, and lesson 2 established that most of it is not.

:::checkpoint So is the lesson just "be realistic"? What does this course add to that?
Two things, and the second is the useful one.

**The first is that "realistic" has a measurement behind it now.** You're not guessing at how much of the week is yours. You counted it in lesson 2, and you counted its shape as well as its total,
which for most readers came out smaller and more fragmented than they expected. "Be realistic" is
advice. **A number of discretionary hours and a count of how many pieces they arrive in is an
input.**

**The second is that a plan is a prediction**, and lesson 3 is entirely about what happens to your
predictions. Everything that lesson found about a single task applies to a week: you plan the run
that goes well, your own history is not in the room while you do it, and the result is
systematically short in a direction you can measure.

So a week plan isn't a statement of intent that you either live up to or fail. **It is a forecast,
and it is subject to every finding in this course about forecasts.** That is a different relationship
to have with it, and it is the one that survives a Tuesday.
:::

## The order, and why the order is the technique

Four steps. The order carries almost all of the value, and it is the reverse of how most people do
it.[4]

**One: the fixed points go in first.** Everything from the fixed column of your lesson 2 sheet.
Shifts, the school run, sleep, the journey, the appointment, the evening you are at your mother's.
Not because they matter most, but because **they're the parts of the week that are true.** A plan that starts anywhere else is a plan about somebody else's week.

**Two: the refusal goes in, as an absence with a name on it.** Write the thing you decided in lesson
6 into the week explicitly: not by leaving a gap where it used to be, but by writing "Tuesday
evening: not the committee" for the first few weeks. **A gap is ambiguous and will silently fill.**
A named absence is a decision you can see you are keeping.

**Three: the discretionary hours go in against their real shape.** This is lesson 5 doing its work.
If your eleven hours arrive in thirty-one pieces, don't plan them as three blocks. Put the work
that needs continuity in the two or three genuinely long pieces you have, and match everything else
to the size of the piece it will actually get.

**Four: apply your own ratio to the whole thing.** Lesson 3 gave you a median for how far your
estimates run short. Take what you have planned, apply it, and **take something out**. Not adjust, not compress. Take one thing out and write down what it was.

**Then leave slack deliberately.** How much is the course's own judgement and not a finding.[4] A
rough figure that works: **leave a fifth of your discretionary time unplanned**, in pieces rather
than in one lump, and put it where the week historically goes wrong rather than at the end.

Slack is not rest and it is not catching up, though it can be spent on either. **Slack is what lets a plan be wrong without being finished.** A week with every hour committed is a week that is fully wrong
by Tuesday afternoon; a week with a fifth loose absorbs the Tuesday and carries on.

### Worked: Hannah builds a week

**Hannah is the constructed reader from lesson 2**,[4] the self-employed bookkeeper with a six-year-old.
Her sheet says **11 hours 20 of discretionary time, arriving in thirty-one pieces**, the longest 55
minutes and nineteen of them under fifteen.

She has one goal she keeps not starting, which is a bookkeeping qualification, and one refusal from
lesson 6: she has stopped doing the monthly accounts for her brother-in-law's business unpaid, at a
cost of about three hours a month and, she has written down, probably an awkward Christmas.

**The fixed points go in.** Two mornings at a client's premises. The school run twice a day. Her
daughter's bedtime. Thursday at her mother's. Sleep. That is most of the week, and it is not a complaint, it is the input.

**The refusal goes in.** The last Wednesday evening of the month is written as *not the accounts*.

**The discretionary time goes in against its shape.** The qualification needs continuity, so it gets
the two genuinely long pieces: Tuesday 9:15 to 10:10, after the school run and before her first
client call, and Sunday 20:00 to 20:55. Ninety minutes a week of study, which looks thin next to the
advice in any book about this.

**Her nineteen short pieces get short work.** Invoices, the one email that needs writing properly,
the thing she keeps meaning to file. Not the qualification, which doesn't fit in eleven minutes and won't be made to.

**Her ratio goes on.** Her lesson 3 median was 1.4. She had planned six discretionary items; at 1.4
they need about eight and a half hours against the seven she has left after the study. **So one item
comes out**, and she writes down which: the website, again, which has now been removed from three
consecutive weeks. That fact is itself worth having.

**And the slack.** She leaves about two hours loose, in four half-hours, and puts three of them on
Wednesday and Thursday, because that is where her weeks have historically gone wrong.

:::predict Before you read what happened: Hannah's week is carefully built, honest about her hours, and has slack in it. Which day do you think it broke on, and what do you think broke it?
Most readers say Thursday or Friday, and most reach for something external: a client emergency, the
child being ill, a deadline moving.

Write down your own answer, because the exercise at the end of this lesson asks you to make the same
prediction about your own week before you run it, and the point of making it in writing is that you
find out afterwards whether your model of your own week is any good. **That is lesson 1's move,
applied one last time to the plan itself.**
:::

### Worked: the same week, after it broke

It broke on Tuesday, at 9:15, on the first study session of the week.

Not because of a client. Her daughter's school ran a parents' coffee morning that she had known
about for three weeks and had not put on the sheet, because it wasn't the sort of thing that goes on a sheet.

**Look at what that did, because the mechanism is the lesson.** Tuesday 9:15 was one of her two long
pieces. Losing it didn't cost her fifty-five minutes; it cost her the only continuous slot before
Sunday, and the qualification therefore got half of its week. By Wednesday the plan had one wrong
thing in it. By Thursday she had stopped looking at it.

**Three things she changed, and the third one is the finding.**

**She moved the study to Sunday and Tuesday rather than Tuesday and Sunday.** If the first long
piece of the week is also the most exposed, then the week's most important work is riding on its
most fragile slot. Sunday evening is the one nobody else can claim.

**She put the coffee morning and its relatives into the fixed column.** Not that specific event: the
category. School things, three or four a term, always known about in advance, never written down.
Her lesson 2 sheet had missed a whole class of fixed commitment because none of them happened in the
week she recorded. **A single week's record has that hole in it by construction**, which lesson 2
said and which she has now met.

**And she stopped treating the break as a failure.** This is the one that transfers. The plan did
its job: it converted a vague sense that the weeks get away from her into one sentence, *my study
time is on the most interruptible morning of the week and my sheet is missing the school calendar.*
**She couldn't have got that sentence from a better-designed plan. She got it from running a plan
that broke.**

## What a plan is for

The course's position, stated plainly and labelled as its own.[4]

**A week plan is an instrument, not a promise.** You build it, you run it, and what you get back is
information about where the week pushes on you. A plan that breaks in a new place every week is
telling you something. A plan that breaks in the same place four weeks running is telling you
something much more specific, and it is usually a fixed point you have not admitted is fixed.

That reframing does a particular job, and it is worth being explicit about what. **It removes the
moral content.** The usual relationship with a week plan is that keeping it means you did well and
breaking it means you did badly, which is why the drawer exists. An instrument that gives a reading
you did not want is not a failed instrument.

## Four things people believe about week plans

**"A plan that survived the week was a good plan."** It might have been. It might equally have been a quiet week that would have gone fine anyway, or a plan with very little in it. **A surviving plan is weak evidence**, because
nothing in the week tells you which of those it was, and this is the same problem lesson 1 had with
a single estimate.

**"A plan that broke was a bad plan."** The break is the measurement. The only plan that teaches you nothing is the one you didn't run.

**"I need a system."** You need about four inputs and a sheet of paper, and you have the inputs.
Systems are a genuine interest for some people and a displacement activity for rather more, and the tell is straightforward: if you've changed the system more often than you've run it, the system is the thing you're doing instead. **This course is not going to recommend a tool**, and it has not researched any.[4]

**"If I plan every hour, I will use every hour."** Behind this is the most quoted sentence in the
whole subject, that work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion. **This course hasn't read the essay it comes from** and has it at the level of a search summary.[3] What that
summary records is worth knowing now and is lesson 8's subject properly: the line opens a 1955
satire, its author offers it as a commonplace observation rather than as a result, the essay is
actually about why bureaucracies grow regardless of the work available, and no empirical test of
the time-expansion claim was found when this course went looking.

So: an amusing observation that might well be true, presented as a law, quoted for seventy years,
and, as far as this course can tell, never tested. **Plan every hour if it suits you. Don't do it because of Parkinson.**

## Practice

:::exercise Build it, run it, and record the breaks
Take 45 minutes to build, then five minutes at the end of the week. You need your lesson 2 sheet,
your lesson 3 median ratio, your lesson 4 plans and your lesson 6 refusal.

**Build, in this order and no other.**

1. **Fixed points**, from the fixed column. All of them, including sleep and travel.
2. **Your refusal**, written in as a named absence rather than as a gap.
3. **Discretionary time against its shape.** Long work in the long pieces, short work in the short
   ones. Count how many genuinely long pieces you have; for most readers it is one or two.
4. **Your if-then plans**, each attached to a cue that already exists in the week you have just
   written down. If a plan's cue is not visible on this sheet, the cue is wrong.
5. **Apply your ratio and remove one thing.** Write down what you removed. If you can't bring yourself to remove anything, that's the finding, and write that down instead.
6. **Leave about a fifth of the discretionary time loose**, in pieces, placed where your weeks
   historically go wrong.

**Then, before the week starts, one line: which day do you think it will break, and what do you
think will break it?** Seal it, or just write it at the top of the sheet where you will not
conveniently forget it.

**Run the week.** Don't tidy the sheet as you go.

**At the end, four lines.** What actually broke it, and when. Whether your prediction was right.
What the break cost, in the sense Hannah's did, which is not minutes but the thing that could not
then happen. And one change for next week, exactly one.
:::

:::exercise The plan you already had
Take 20 minutes, and do this one before the week you have just built rather than after.

Most people reading this have made a week plan before, probably several. Think of the last one.

1. **What was it in?** Paper, an app, a calendar, your head.
2. **How long did it last?** Days rather than weeks is the usual honest answer.
3. **What did you conclude when it stopped?** Write the actual sentence you told yourself. It is usually about you rather than about the plan.
4. **Now write what this lesson would have said instead**, in one sentence, about where the week
   pushed back.

The gap between lines 3 and 4 is the whole of this lesson. Most people find that they have run four
or five perfectly good measurements of their own week and thrown every result away.
:::
