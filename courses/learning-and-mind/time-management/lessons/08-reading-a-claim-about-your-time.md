This is the last lesson, and it is the one you'll use longest.

Everything before it was about your own hours. This one is about the claims other people will make
about them, and there'll be a great many: in a book somebody gives you, in a training session at
work, in an article with a confident number in the headline.

Three habits, and then the course closes on the measurement it opened with.

## Habit one: sort the claim before you argue with it

Almost every piece of advice about time is one of three things, and telling them apart takes about
thirty seconds once you've done it twice.

**Measurable.** It makes a claim a study could check, whether or not one has. "Writing a plan the
night before makes you more likely to finish what you planned." Somebody could run that, and the
useful question's whether anybody has.

**Unmeasurable as stated.** It uses a term nobody has defined, so no study could settle it either
way. "Focus on what really matters." **This is not the same as false**, and it isn't automatically
useless; it may be good advice. It is simply not the kind of sentence evidence can bear on until
somebody says what the words mean.

**False.** It makes a checkable claim that has been checked and did not hold. There are fewer of
these than you'd expect, because most of the genre's claims never get near enough to a measurement
to be wrong.

**This is Digital Literacy's move and Using AI Effectively lesson 11's, adapted again**, and the
course says so rather than presenting it as new. What changes here is the subject and one wrinkle,
which is the fourth category the sort keeps producing.

### Worked: four pieces of advice, sorted

**These four are constructed for the exercise**,[4] assembled from the shapes this kind of advice
usually takes rather than quoted from anybody.

**"Check email twice a day rather than continuously."** **Measurable**, and specifically so. You
could randomise people to the two conditions and measure what they get done and how they feel. What
the sort gets you is not a verdict, it's a question with an address: *has anybody run this, and on
whom?* **This course has not read a study of it**, which is a fact about this course and is not one
about the advice.

**"Protect your deep work."** **Unmeasurable as stated**, because "deep work" is a category the
sentence doesn't define and "protect" is not an action. Lesson 5 gives you a sharper form of the
same idea, which is that a long stretch is partly a thing that happens to you and depends on what
other people can see. **Notice that sharpening it made it measurable**, and that's generally what
happens: the unmeasurable ones are usually a measurable claim with the specifics removed.

**"An interruption costs you twenty-three minutes."** **False as usually stated**, or at least
unsupported in a way that has been checked. Lesson 5 traced it: absent from what this course read of
both papers it is attributed to, and no primary printed source found by somebody who went looking
across five papers and twenty-three posts.

**"Successful people get up at five."** **Two claims stuck together**, which is the fourth category
and the commonest one in this genre. Claim one: successful people rise early. Measurable, once
somebody defines "successful", and worth asking about. Claim two, carried in the word order and
never stated: rising early is *why*. That is a causal claim, and the sentence supplies nothing that
could support it.

**Separating the two is most of the work**, and it is what the rest of this section is for. An
unsupported causal claim riding on a measurable descriptive one is the standard shape of advice in
this subject.

:::checkpoint Somebody says the sorting is a way of dismissing advice you do not fancy taking. Is that fair?
It would be, if the sort produced verdicts. It doesn't, and this is worth being clear about because
the objection is a reasonable one.

**Sorting a claim as unmeasurable is not a criticism of it.** A great deal of good advice is
unmeasurable as stated, including most of what anybody's grandmother told them. What the sort
establishes is what kind of support the claim could have, which tells you what to do next.

Three different next steps come out of it. A measurable claim sends you looking for whether anybody
measured it. An unmeasurable one sends you looking for a sharper version, which often exists. A
claim spliced out of two sends you to separate them, and usually one half survives.

**None of those is "ignore it".** The sort doesn't tell you to drop anything. The one thing the sort does rule out is the move the genre relies
on, which is a claim that borrows the authority of evidence without having any, and you will notice
that this course's own lesson 6 is almost entirely unmeasurable advice and says so in its first
paragraph.
:::

## Habit two: trace the number

**A figure is easier to check than an argument**, which is the whole reason this habit is worth
having. You do not need to evaluate the reasoning. You just follow the citation.

Lesson 5 did one of these in full and this lesson will not repeat it. What it does instead is the
question lesson 5 left: **why does this particular subject produce so many unsourced numbers?**

Three reasons, and the third is the one you can act on.[4]

**A memorable figure travels better than a study.** "Twenty-three minutes and fifteen seconds" is
specific, surprising and easy to say. The finding it's displaced, that most interrupted work is
resumed the same day but with more than two intervening activities, is none of those things.
Precision to the second is doing rhetorical work here rather than measuring anything, and the tell
is that it never arrives with a page number.

**The books cite each other rather than the literature.** A claim enters the genre once and then
circulates inside it, each repetition inheriting its authority from the last rather than from any
study. That is not dishonesty; it's what happens when a field is written mostly by practitioners for
practitioners.

**And nobody checks, because checking looks like more work than it is.** This is the actionable one.
The trace in lesson 5 took one person an afternoon. Your own version takes twenty minutes, most
trails end after two steps, and the number of times you will need to do it before you stop believing
unsourced figures is about one.

### Parkinson's law, which is not a law

The most quoted sentence in this whole subject is that **work expands so as to fill the time
available for its completion**.

**This course has not read the essay it comes from.** The full text sits behind a paywall and the
copies found were unreachable, so what follows is at the level of a search summary and is flagged
as such.[3] At that level, four things are established and they are worth having.

**It is the opening line of an essay in *The Economist*, published on 19 November 1955.** Not a
paper, not a study, not a report of anything anybody measured.

**Its author presents it as a commonplace observation**, which is to say he isn't claiming to have
discovered it. That is a considerable distance from how it's used seventy years later.

**The essay is a satire, and it is about something else.** Its actual subject is why bureaucracies
grow regardless of how much work there is to do, and its two stated drivers are that an official
wants to multiply subordinates rather than rivals, and that officials make work for each other.

**And no empirical test of the time-expansion claim was found.** A 2008 study tests the essay's
*committee* argument, which is a different claim in the same essay.

So the most repeated sentence in this subject is a funny aside in a satirical essay about civil
service staffing, offered as an everyday observation by a man who was making a joke about something
else, never tested, and quoted for seventy years as though it were a result.

:::callout Which is not to say it is wrong
Being unsupported is not being false, and this course has been careful about that distinction all
the way through.

The claim might well be true, and lesson 3 gives you a reason it could be: if people forecast from
the run that goes well, then a generous deadline gets filled by the ordinary events an optimistic
forecast leaves out. **That's an argument for it, not evidence of it.**

**The honest statement's the boring one.** A plausible and widely believed claim, with no study
behind it that anybody has produced, and a reader who wants to know whether it holds for them can
run the experiment on themselves in a fortnight. Which is, as it happens, exactly what the project
for this course asks you to do.
:::

## Habit three: the honest answer to "does this work"

You have reached the end of a course on time management. It would be a strange place not to ask.

:::predict Before you read what they found: you have done seven lessons of this course. What do you expect two reviews of the whole field, fourteen years apart, to have concluded about whether time management improves performance, and how confident do you expect them to sound?
Two things are worth having in mind before you look.

The first is that you already know the shape of the answer, because lesson 1 told you: the measured
relationship with wellbeing is larger than the one with performance. What you may not expect is that
the two reviews disagree with each other about performance, and that the disagreement is not between
a careful paper and a careless one.

The second is the thing people get wrong in both directions. Readers who expect "it all works" are
about to be disappointed, and readers who expect "none of it works" are about to be disappointed
too. **The honest answer is in between and it is less satisfying than either.**
:::

**Two reviews of the whole field, fourteen years apart, and they do not agree.**

**Claessens and colleagues, 2007**, reviewing the literature to that point: time management
behaviours "relate positively to perceived control of time, job satisfaction, and health, and
negatively to stress. **The relationship with work and academic performance is not clear.**"[1] The
same review found no empirical study in the subject published before 1982, having searched from
1954.[1]

**Aeon, Faber and Panaccio, 2021**, pooling 158 studies, 53,957 participants and 490 effect sizes:
time management "is moderately related to job performance, academic achievement, and wellbeing", and
shows "a moderate, negative relationship with distress".[2] Job performance came out at r = .25 and
distress at r = -0.358, and the authors note that the link to job performance appears to increase
over the years.

**The newer one's larger, newer and pooled over far more studies. Both are correlational.** Neither
can tell you time management caused anything, because the studies underneath them mostly measure
people at one moment rather than following them through a change. And the 2021 authors add a
limitation that is unusually frank: studies "often don't explain what, exactly, is taught in time
management training seminars",[2] so the pooled effect is the effect of something not well
specified.

**So the honest answer is that something is there, that it is probably not what the genre sells, and
that nobody has shown it is causal.**

That middle clause is the one to carry away. Both reviews point at the same region: control,
satisfaction, health, stress. The 2021 paper puts it directly, finding that time management enhances
wellbeing, and life satisfaction in particular, **to a greater extent than it does performance**,
with the effect on life satisfaction about 72 percent stronger than the effect on job
satisfaction.[2]

Lesson 1 opened by telling you that, and refused on those grounds to promise you more output. You
now have the evidence it was standing on, and you can check whether the course kept its word.

## Three things people believe about this literature

**"Somebody must have checked."** Lesson 5's trace is the answer, and the general form is worse than
the specific one. The subject's best-known figure has no printed source anybody can produce, and
it's repeated daily in rooms full of professionals. **Assume nothing has been checked until you have
checked it**, which is a cheap assumption, because checking takes twenty minutes.

**"An old study is out of date."** Sometimes, and the distinction matters. **Figures that describe a
time and place go stale**: the estimate-against-diary gap was 6.2 hours a week in 1985 and 2.4
across 2003 to 2007, so quoting either without its year is a defect. **Findings about how people
reason do not obviously go stale the same way.** That people forecast by imagining the run that goes
well is a claim about how forecasting works, and 1994 being a long time ago is not in itself an
argument against it. Ask which kind of claim you are looking at. That distinction is **Using AI
Effectively** lesson 11's and it holds here for the same reason.

**"The science says..."** There is less science than the phrase implies. The 2007 review searched
from 1954 and found no empirical study before 1982, and concluded that time management "has made its
way into the literature without being accompanied by empirical research".[1] The literature arrived
after the practice, which is the reverse of the usual order of things and explains a good deal about
the state of it.

## Practice

:::exercise Trace one
Take 20 minutes, and stop at twenty whether or not you have finished.

Find one claim about time or attention with a number in it. A headline, a training slide, a post,
something in a book. Write it down word for word, with where you found it.

Then follow it back, one step at a time, writing each step down:

1. **What does this source cite?** If nothing, write "nothing" and you are finished.
2. **Open what it cites. Is the number in there?** Not something like it. The number.
3. **If it cites something further back, repeat.** Most trails are two steps.

Then write your finding in one sentence, whatever it is. **All four endings are results.** None of
them is a failed exercise. The number is in a study, and the study measured what the claim says. The
number is in a study and the study measured something narrower. The trail ends in nothing. Or you
ran out of twenty minutes, which tells you the claim is harder to check than it is to repeat.

If you did lesson 5's version of this exercise, put the two sheets side by side and notice whether
the second one was faster.
:::

:::exercise Close the loop on lesson 1
Take 25 minutes. This is the last thing the course asks, and the order of the steps matters.

**First, without looking anything up**, write down four numbers: how many hours you spent last week
on paid work, on domestic work, on sleep, and on anything you would call leisure. From memory. Don't
open your record.

**Then open the record you kept in lesson 1**, and the sort you did in lesson 2, and put the two
sets of numbers side by side.

Three questions in writing.

1. **Are your four numbers closer to the record than they were the first time?** Some readers find
   they are, and some find one category is far worse because the week in question was unusual.
2. **Which category did you get wrong in the same direction as before?** That one is your standing
   bias, and it is the single most useful line in this exercise.
3. **What would you now say to somebody who told you they know roughly where their hours go?**

Writing it from memory before opening the record is not a formality. **How to Learn Anything**
lesson 3 is about why retrieving something first changes what the comparison is worth, and this is
that argument applied to this course's own material at the end of it.
:::
