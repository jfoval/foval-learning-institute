**The `unread:` line above is the build's enforcement and it is unusually long for this subject, for
a reason worth stating at the top.** Almost everything written about time management is a trade
book, and the trade books were not opened for this course. They are named in lessons only as things

## The first thing Stage 1 found, and it shapes the whole course

**There is no canonical textbook for this subject and there are no university syllabi to consult.**
That is not a gap in the search. Time management is taught in corporate training rooms and sold in
airport bookshops; it is not a field with a graduate text and a standard sequence. The academic
literature that exists is scattered across organisational psychology, and the most useful single
document in it says so in as many words.

**Claessens, van Eerde, Rutte and Roe, "A review of the time management literature", *Personnel
Review* 36(2), 2007, pp. 255 to 276.** Read in part: the abstract and the opening of the review
(pp. 255 to 257), in the author-posted copy. **This is the closest thing the subject has to a
textbook chapter, and the course should lean on it heavily.**

What it establishes, verbatim:

- **The name is wrong.** "The term 'time management' is actually misleading. Strictly speaking, time
  cannot be managed, because it is an inaccessible factor. Only the way a person deals with time can
  be influenced." (p. 256)
- **The literature arrived after the practice, not before it.** "we found no empirical studies
  published before 1982. Obviously, time management has made its way into the literature without
  being accompanied by empirical research." (p. 257) The review searched from 1954, "when time
  management was introduced".
- **Training works on the skill and not reliably on the outcome.** "Time management training seems
  to enhance time management skills, but this does not automatically transfer to better
  performance." (abstract, p. 255)
- **The headline finding.** "The review demonstrates that time management behaviours relate
  positively to perceived control of time, job satisfaction, and health, and negatively to stress.
  The relationship with work and academic performance is not clear." (abstract, p. 255)
- **The measurement problem.** "time management has been defined and operationalised in a variety of
  ways. Some instruments were not reliable or valid, which could account for unstable findings."
  (abstract, p. 255)
- **Who was studied.** Respondents per study ranged "from four to 701, with an average of 90", in
  three kinds of group: employees of different organisations, students following psychology classes,
  and employees with a double workload. "The majority of respondents were recruited among students
  in psychology classes." (p. 257)

**One internal inconsistency, recorded so a drafter does not trip over it.** The abstract says the
review includes **32** empirical studies; the Method section says **35** were selected (p. 257). A
lesson citing a count must say which part of the paper it took the count from, or avoid the count.

## The measured results this course can stand on

### 1. People do not know where their time goes, and the error has a shape

**Robinson, Martin, Glorieux and Minnen, "The overestimated workweek revisited", *Monthly Labor
Review*, June 2011, pp. 43 onwards.** Read in part: the standfirst and pp. 43 to 45, from the
Bureau of Labor Statistics' own PDF. Free and citable at work, which matters for this course the way
NIST mattered for Using AI Effectively.

- The standfirst, verbatim: "Data from the American Time Use Survey (ATUS) and a Belgian national
  survey using weekly diaries indicate that, when asked to estimate their number of work hours,
  employed respondents tend to overestimate their work hours by 5–10 percent in relation to the work
  hours they report in their time diaries; most of the overestimation is accounted for by
  respondents who estimate longer work hours".
- **The shape of the error is the teachable part.** "workers within the range of 35- to 45-hour work
  weeks tend to report relatively similar work hours when filling out time diaries and when
  answering questions asked with the time-estimate approach, but the higher the respondent's
  estimated number of hours per workweek, the larger is the gap between the estimates obtained with
  the two approaches. Workers estimating 50- to 80-hour workweeks had progressively greater gaps
  between this estimate and what they reported in their diaries." (p. 45) The authors summarise it
  as "The greater the estimate, the greater the overestimate."
- **The gap over time**, in hours per week: 1.3 in 1965; 3.6 in 1975; 6.2 in 1985; 2.7 for 1993 to
  1995; 3.7 for 1998 to 2001; 2.4 for 2003 to 2007. (p. 45) **A lesson quoting any one of these must
  date it**, and the fact that the gap itself moves is a better teaching point than any single
  figure.
- **It is not only work.** "Estimates across all, or almost all, activities ultimately sum to more
  than 168 hours per week." And, on a study by Chase and Godbey of swimming and tennis club members
  checked against the clubs' own sign-in systems: "for both types of clubs, almost half of all
  respondents overestimated the actual number of times they participated by more than 100 percent."
  (p. 45)
- **Why the diary is the better instrument**, in the authors' words: respondents "are not asked to
  make complex, vague, or subjective calculations, but simply to recall their activities
  sequentially for a specific period", and the method has "an implicit constraint on the numbers of
  hours in a day (all activities must add up to exactly 24 hours)". (p. 44)

**This is a contested question and the dissent is in the same article**, which is why the source is
so useful. "Jerry Jacobs has challenged this notion of inaccurate estimates, arguing that the gap
was simply a result of the familiar 'regression to the mean' phenomenon", and "Using more recent
data from the ATUS, Harley Frazis and Jay Stewart found no notable difference between diary data and
data from estimate questions, also arguing that any gaps might result from regression to the mean."
(p. 44) The authors answer that the gap's size has changed over the decades in a way regression to
the mean would not produce, and that Frazis and Stewart's results "were mainly for days in
'reference weeks' during the month and not for days in other weeks of the month." (pp. 44 to 45)

### 2. Estimates of how long work will take run short, and the correction is not the obvious one

**Buehler, Griffin and Ross, "Exploring the 'Planning Fallacy': Why People Underestimate Their Task
Completion Times", *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1994, Vol. 67, No. 3, pp. 366 to
381.** **Read in substantial part**: the abstract, Studies 1 and 2 in full with their tables, and
Study 4 with its table and discussion. Extracted from the copy hosted at MIT. **This is the
best-evidenced thing in the course and the spine of at least one lesson.**

The abstract, verbatim: "This study tested 3 main hypotheses concerning people's predictions of task
completion times: (a) People underestimate their own but not others' completion times, (b) people
focus on plan-based scenarios rather than on relevant past experiences while generating their
predictions, and (c) people's attributions diminish the relevance of past experiences."

**Study 1** (pp. 369): 37 psychology students in the final semester of the Honors Thesis course at
the University of Waterloo, asked by telephone to predict when they would submit their thesis; the
coordinator recorded the actual date. Means are based on 33 subjects.

| | Best estimate | "Everything went as well as it possibly could" | "As poorly as it possibly could" |
|---|---|---|---|
| Predicted days | 33.9 | 27.4 | 48.6 |
| Actual days | 55.5 | 55.5 | 55.5 |
| Finished in predicted time | 29.7% | 10.8% | 48.7% |

**The figure to carry out of this study is the last cell.** Fewer than half of the respondents
finished by the date they had named on the assumption that *everything went as badly as it possibly
could*. The paper puts it: "Interestingly, fewer than half of the respondents (48.7%) finished by
the time they had predicted assuming that 'everything went as poorly as it possibly could.'"

And the correction that does **not** work, from the same page: "Although the instructions to make a
pessimistic prediction decreased the optimistic bias in prediction, it did not increase the accuracy
of respondents' forecasts." The absolute error was the same whether people were told to be
pessimistic or accurate (23.2 days against 22.6).

**Study 2** (pp. 370 to 371): 104 undergraduates, predictions for one academic and one nonacademic
project they intended to finish in the coming week.

| | Academic | Nonacademic |
|---|---|---|
| Predicted days | 5.8 | 5.0 |
| Actual days | 10.7 | 9.2 |
| Finished in predicted time | 37.1% | 42.5% |

Confidence, which rules out the "they were only saying what they hoped" reading: "subjects reported
feeling 74.1% certain that they would meet their forecasts for academic projects and 69.9% certain
for nonacademic tasks." (p. 371)

**The deadline sub-analysis is the single most practical thing in the paper** (p. 371). Of the 62
subjects with external deadlines on their academic projects, **80.6% finished in time to meet the
deadline**; projects were due in 12.9 days on average and were reported finished in 11.0. And yet
they had predicted 5.9 days, "well in advance of both the deadline and the reported completion
time; only 38.7% of these subjects finished in the predicted time." The authors' reading:
"subjects underestimated the importance of deadlines in determining when they would finish their
projects." Their predicted times correlated only weakly with the deadlines (r = .23) while their
reported completion times correlated strongly with them (r = .82).

**Study 4** (pp. 374 to 376) is the one a lesson on fixing this has to carry, because it separates
two things that sound identical. 123 students, a computer assignment, three thought-focus
conditions.

| | Control | Recall | Recall-relevant |
|---|---|---|---|
| Predicted days | 5.5 | 5.3 | 7.0 |
| Actual days | 6.8 | 6.3 | 7.0 |
| Finished in predicted time | 29.3% | 38.1% | **60.0%** |

*Recall* subjects described their past experiences with similar assignments just before predicting.
*Recall-relevant* subjects did that **and** answered questions forcing them to connect those
experiences to this assignment: when they would finish if they completed it as far before its
deadline as they typically do, and a plausible scenario based on their past experiences that would
result in their completing the assignment at their typical time.

The finding, verbatim: "The absence of an effect in the recall condition is rather remarkable. In
this condition, subjects first described their past performance with projects similar to the
computer assignment and acknowledged that they typically finish only 1 day before deadlines.
Following a suggestion to 'keep in mind previous experiences with assignments,' they then predicted
when they would finish the computer assignment. Despite this seemingly powerful manipulation,
subjects continued to make overly optimistic forecasts. Apparently, subjects were able to
acknowledge their past experiences but disassociate those episodes from their present predictions."
(p. 376)

**And the honest limit, which a lesson must carry with the 60%**: "Predictions were no more accurate
in the recall-relevant condition than in the other conditions." (p. 375) Less biased, not more
accurate. The same shape as Study 1's pessimistic instruction.

**Why it happens**, from the thought-listing (p. 375): "Almost all subjects (93.5%) reported
considering future plans and scenarios for the computer assignment. Most of these future plans
concerned how subjects would successfully complete the project. Only 9.8% of the subjects mentioned
potential impediments... very few subjects (8.9%) reported thinking about their past experiences.
Even within the recall-relevant condition, only 12.5% of the subjects mentioned their past
experiences."

**Provenance of the idea.** The paper attributes the term to **Kahneman and Tversky (1979)**,
"Intuitive Prediction: Biases and Corrective Procedures", *TIMS Studies in Management Science* 12,
pp. 313 to 327. **Search-summary level only; not opened.** A lesson may say Buehler and colleagues
credit them with the term and must not quote them.

### 3. Switching between tasks, and the most-quoted number in the field

**Mark, Gonzalez and Harris, "No Task Left Behind? Examining the Nature of Fragmented Work", CHI
2005, Portland, Oregon, pp. 321 to 330.** Read in part: the abstract, the research setting, and the
results on fragmented work (pp. 321 to 325). A field study, not an experiment: 24 information
workers at one outsourcing company, observed to the second.

- The abstract, verbatim in the parts a lesson would want: "We found work to be highly fragmented:
  people average little time in working spheres before switching and 57% of their working spheres
  are interrupted... Though most interrupted work is resumed on the same day, more than two
  intervening activities occur before it is."
- Informants worked in an average of **11.7 different working spheres** (sd 2.4). (p. 324)
- "The average length of time that the informants spent in central and peripheral working spheres
  was **11 min. 4 sec.** (sd=18 min. 9 sec.) before switching to another working sphere or being
  interrupted." (p. 324) An earlier study by the same group found switching "averaging every three
  minutes" at the level of individual work events. (p. 322)
- **57.1%** of working sphere segments were interrupted; central spheres 60.3%, peripheral 41.7%.
  (p. 324)
- A finding that inverts the usual moral: "segments that *were* interrupted lasted significantly
  longer (12 min. 40 sec.) than those not interrupted (8 min. 58 sec.)", and "the longer one works
  in a working sphere, the longer is the interrupting event". The authors call this "the irony of
  the work day". (p. 325)
- Collocated workers spent longer in a sphere (11 min 56 sec against 9 min 56 sec) **and** were
  interrupted more (62.9% against 49.3%). (p. 325)

**The "23 minutes 15 seconds" figure is not in this paper, and this course should say so.** I
searched the text myself; it is not there, and the paper's recovery finding is about intervening
activities rather than about minutes. It is usually attributed instead to:

**Mark, Gudith and Klocke, "The Cost of Interrupted Work: More Speed and Stress", CHI 2008.** Read
in part: the abstract and the experimental design (pp. 1 to 2). **It is not there either, and the
paper's actual finding is close to the opposite of the popular one.** The abstract, verbatim: "We
found that context does not make a difference but surprisingly, people completed interrupted tasks
in less time with no difference in quality. Our data suggests that people compensate for
interruptions by working faster, but this comes at a price: experiencing more stress, higher
frustration, time pressure and effort."

The design matters for how far it travels: a laboratory experiment, 48 subjects, 81% German
university students with a mean age of 26, answering twelve simulated emails as a fictional human
resource manager, interrupted every two to five minutes by the experimenter by telephone or instant
message, paid 12 euros. It is not a study of a working day.

**A third-party trace of the figure**, read in full and treated as what it is: a blog post rather
than a source. [oberien, "Interruptions cost 23 minutes 15 seconds,
right?"](https://blog.oberien.de/2023/11/05/23-minutes-15-seconds.html) (2023) reviewed five papers
and twenty-three blog posts and concluded: "So in the end, where do the 23 minutes and 15 seconds
come from? They are mentioned in interviews multiple times by Gloria Mark. But I wasn't able to find
a primary printed source." **A lesson may cite this only for the claim that the author looked and
did not find one**, and must rest the substance on the two papers above, which this course has read.

**Sophie Leroy, "Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching
between work tasks", *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 109(2), 2009, pp. 168 to
181.** **Search-summary level only; paywalled and not opened.** On the unread line. The idea, that
attention to a prior task persists into the next one and that the effect is worse when the prior
task was unfinished, is widely reported and this course has not verified it. If a lesson wants it,
read the paper first.

### 4. Does any of this work?

**Aeon, Faber and Panaccio, "Does time management work? A meta-analysis", *PLOS ONE* 16(1),
e0245066, 2021.** Open access. Abstract read verbatim; effect sizes and limitations read through the
journal's own page.

- The abstract, verbatim and complete: "Does time management work? We conducted a meta-analysis to
  assess the impact of time management on performance and well-being. Results show that time
  management is moderately related to job performance, academic achievement, and wellbeing. Time
  management also shows a moderate, negative relationship with distress."
- **158 studies, 53,957 participants, 490 effect sizes.** Job performance r = .25. Distress
  r = -0.358.
- **The finding that should reframe the course**: time management "enhances wellbeing, in particular
  life satisfaction, to a greater extent than it does performance", and the effect on life
  satisfaction was reported as 72% stronger than on job satisfaction. The usual promise of the genre
  is the one the evidence supports least well.
- The limitation to carry: most of the studies are cross-sectional, which restricts causal
  inference, and "studies often don't explain what, exactly, is taught in time management training
  seminars."

**Read this beside Claessens 2007, because they are fourteen years apart and they disagree in
tone.** Claessens: "The relationship with work and academic performance is not clear." Aeon and
colleagues: moderately related, and the link to job performance "seems to increase over the years".
A lesson should present both and say the second is larger, newer and still correlational.

### 5. The one technique with a meta-analysis behind it

**Gollwitzer and Sheeran, "Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta-Analysis of Effects
and Processes", *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38, 2006, pp. 69 to 119.**
Search-summary level for the meta-analysis itself: **d = .65 across 94 independent tests and more
than 8,000 participants.**

**The authors' own chapter-length statement of the idea was read in full** (Gollwitzer and Sheeran,
"Implementation Intentions", posted by the US National Cancer Institute, pp. 1 to 4). Verbatim:

- The definition: implementation intentions "are **if-then plans that link situational cues (i.e.,
  good opportunities to act, critical moments) with responses that are effective in attaining goals
  or desired outcomes** ('If situation Y is encountered, then I will initiate behavior Z in order to
  reach goal X!')." (p. 1)
- The distinction: "**Goal intentions can be defined as the instructions that people give themselves
  to perform particular behaviors or to achieve certain desired outcomes**... and are measured by
  items of the form, 'I intend to achieve X!'" (p. 2)
- The gap being closed, with numbers: goal intentions "accounted for 28% of the variance in
  behavior, on average, across 422 studies"; a review of health behaviour matrices "found that
  people translated their 'good' intentions into action only 53% of the time"; and in experiments
  that successfully changed intentions, the change in behaviour was "only small-to-medium (R² =
  .03)". The conclusion, in bold in the original: "**forming even strong goal intentions does not
  guarantee goal attainment**." (pp. 2 to 3)
- **The limit a lesson must carry**: "Implementation intention effects are stronger when
  self-regulatory problems beset goal striving, and when if-then planning is supported by strong
  activated goal intentions." (p. 1) It is a bridge from wanting to doing. It does nothing about
  whether you should want it.

### 6. The most quoted law in the subject is a joke, and was never a finding

**C. Northcote Parkinson, "Parkinson's Law", *The Economist*, 19 November 1955.** Not read in the
original; the full text is behind a paywall and the copies found were unreachable. Established at
search-summary level, and recorded here because the *provenance* is the teaching point rather than
the content:

- The famous sentence, "work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion", is the
  essay's opening and Parkinson presents it as **"a commonplace observation"**, not as a result.
- The essay is a satire, and the rest of it is about something else entirely: the growth of
  bureaucracies regardless of the work to be done, driven by two factors Parkinson states as "An
  official wants to multiply subordinates, not rivals" and "Officials make work for each other".
- **No empirical test of the time-expansion claim was found.** A 2008 study by Klimek, Hanel and
  Thurner tests the *committee* version, not this one.

**A lesson may use this only if it opens the essay first**, or it must say plainly that the course
has not read it and is reporting its provenance rather than quoting it.

## What the field considers essential

There is no expert consensus to report, for the reason at the top of this file. What follows is
**this course's judgement about what an evidence-led treatment must contain**, built from the
sources above and flagged as a judgement rather than as a finding.

1. **Measurement before method.** The subject's one robust empirical foundation is that people's
   accounts of their own time are wrong in a measurable, directional way. Every technique below is
   worthless applied to a false picture of the week. This is also the only part of the course a
   reader can verify on themselves in a week.
2. **Why estimates run short, and what actually corrects it.** Buehler and colleagues, including the
   two negative results: being told to be pessimistic, and being told to remember, both fail.
3. **The difference between a goal and a plan**, which is the one technique in the subject with a
   meta-analysis behind it.
4. **Deciding what not to do.** The literature has almost nothing on this and it is the part a
   reader most needs. Any treatment must be honest that it is reasoning rather than evidence.
5. **Switching, interruption and the working day**, with the field data rather than the folklore.
6. **Reading the genre.** The subject is dominated by trade books making claims of a kind this
   course teaches its readers to sort. The most-quoted law is a joke; the most-quoted number has no
   printed source. **This is Using AI Effectively's lesson 11 in a new subject**, and Digital
   Literacy's sorting before that, and it belongs at the end.

## Common misconceptions

Sourced where the source exists; marked as the course's own where it does not.

- **"I know roughly where my time goes."** Measured, and the error grows with the estimate. Robinson
  and colleagues, 2011.
- **"I am just bad at estimating; other people manage it."** Buehler and colleagues found the bias
  in their Study 1 subjects and *not* in observers predicting for others, which is the opposite of
  the folk model: you are normal, and the asymmetry is between predicting for yourself and
  predicting for somebody else.
- **"So I should just pad my estimates."** Study 1's pessimistic condition reduced the bias and did
  not improve accuracy. Padding moves the number without improving the forecast.
- **"I should remember how long it took last time."** Study 4's recall condition did almost nothing.
  Remembering is not connecting.
- **"Deadlines are the problem."** Study 2: 80.6% of subjects with deadlines met them. Deadlines
  worked; the predictions ignored them.
- **"Interruptions are what other people do to me."** Mark and colleagues found internal
  interruptions to be a large share, and found that longer stretches of work attract longer
  interruptions.
- **"An interruption costs 23 minutes."** Not in either paper it is attributed to. **Course's own,
  and verified by reading both.**
- **"Being busy means being productive."** Adjacent to the status point Robinson and Godbey make
  about why people inflate work hours; recorded here at search-summary level and not to be asserted
  without a source.
- **"Time management will make me perform better at work."** The meta-analysis finds the wellbeing
  effect larger than the performance effect. That is the reverse of the genre's promise.

## Contested questions and the positions on each

1. **Do people misreport their own time, or is the diary-estimate gap a statistical artefact?**
   Standards 3.1 case 2: genuinely open, both positions held by working researchers.
   *For misreporting:* Robinson, Martin, Glorieux and Minnen, 2011, with the changing size of the
   gap over decades as their argument against the artefact reading.
   *For the artefact:* Jerry Jacobs on regression to the mean; Frazis and Stewart finding no notable
   difference in ATUS data.
   *What would settle it:* both measures collected from the same respondents across all weeks of the
   month, which the 1998 to 2001 diary study did for household tasks and found a real gap.
   **This course should give both and not resolve it.**

2. **Does time management improve performance?** Standards 3.1 case 2. Claessens 2007: "not clear".
   Aeon and colleagues 2021: moderately, r = .25, and rising over time. Both are correlational.
   *What would settle it:* randomised training trials with behavioural outcomes, which the 2021
   authors note are largely absent and whose content is usually undocumented.

3. **Should a person aim to do more, or to do less?** Standards 3.1 case 3: **a value question**, and
   the course must describe rather than prescribe. The trade literature splits on it, and this
   course's own `course.yaml` currently takes the second position in its summary. **That has to be
   either defended from evidence or restated as a stance the course takes and names**, and Stage 2
   must settle which. The evidence available touches it only obliquely, through the meta-analysis
   finding the wellbeing effect larger than the performance effect.

4. **Is the working day's fragmentation a problem to be fixed or a feature of the work?** Mark and
   colleagues are careful: "Task switching may be beneficial. It could serve to refresh one and
   provide new ideas. On the other hand, too much task switching with too many different activities
   could be detrimental." Their 2008 experiment found interrupted tasks finished *faster*, at a cost
   in stress. **The course must not present interruption as simply bad**, which is what most of the
   genre does.

## Best existing free resources (to link, not duplicate)

- **[The American Time Use Survey](https://www.bls.gov/tus/)**, from the US Bureau of Labor
  Statistics. The raw material for "where do people's hours actually go", free, and the one place a
  reader can check a claim about how much time anybody spends on anything. United States only, which
  a lesson must say.
- **[The overestimated workweek revisited](https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2011/06/art3full.pdf)**, the
  source above, free, four pages, and readable without any statistics.
- **[Does time management work? A meta-analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0245066)**,
  open access, and the only survey of the whole question a reader can get to.
- **[Exploring the "Planning Fallacy"](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/67_J_Personality_and_Social_Psychology_366,_1994.pdf)**,
  hosted openly at MIT. The tables are readable on their own.

## Open concerns about scope

- **Nearly every participant in this literature is a student or an office worker**, and most of the
  students were in psychology classes. Nobody in these studies is a shift worker, a carer, a
  self-employed tradesperson or a parent of small children, and those readers are a large part of
  who this course is for. **This gap must be named in lesson 1**, the way Using AI Effectively names
  its own, rather than discovered by a reader in lesson 6.
- **The subject is almost all American and European**, and the time-diary work is heavily United
  States and Belgium. Anything about hours, norms or working patterns carries a country.
- **This course cannot avoid the trade canon and has not read it.** Allen, Covey, Drucker, Lakein,
  Burkeman and Newport are the books a reader will have heard of, and a course that never names them
  looks evasive while a course that cites them unread breaks rule 2 of the defect list. The honest
  middle is to name them as widely read, say the course has not assessed them, and teach the reader
  to sort their claims. **Stage 2 should decide whether one of them is worth opening properly**; if
  so, Burkeman is the one that argues against the genre's own premise and would be the most useful
  to have read.
- **There is a real risk of this course becoming a sermon.** Points 3 and 4 above are value
  questions with a lot of personality behind them in the trade literature. The institute's standard
  is to describe the trade and decline to make it, which is exactly what Using AI Effectively's
  lesson 9 does with a much better evidence base. **Copy that lesson's handling.**
