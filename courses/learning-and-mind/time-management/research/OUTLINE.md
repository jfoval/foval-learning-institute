# Outline: Time Management

*Stage 2, 2026-09-19. Built from `research/SOURCES.md`. Sequence for how ideas build, not how the
trade books list chapters.*

## Eight lessons, and why eight

The subject has one robust empirical foundation and a large trade literature resting on almost
nothing. **An honest course is therefore short, and it spends its first two lessons on measurement
rather than on method**, because every technique in the genre is worthless applied to a false
picture of the week.

Eight lessons, in three movements. **One and two** are about finding out what is true of your own
week, and they are the only lessons whose content a reader can verify on themselves. **Three to
six** are the four things with evidence behind them: why estimates run short, what an if-then plan
does, what the working day actually looks like, and deciding what not to do. **Seven and eight**
are the week you build and the genre you will meet, and eight is where everything perishable lives.

## Six decisions this outline settles

Eight lessons written in different sessions have to agree. These are settled here and a drafter may
not re-open them.

**1. The course says "your hours" and never "productivity".** The meta-analysis finds time
management does more for wellbeing than for performance, which is the reverse of the genre's
promise, and a course that led with performance would be promising the thing its own evidence
supports least. Lesson 1 states this and the rest of the course keeps to it.

**2. Every figure carries what it was measured on and when.** This is Digital Literacy's convention
and Using AI Effectively's with one field added, and this subject needs it more than either: the
diary-estimate gap itself changed size in every decade measured. A figure with no year is a defect.

**3. The course describes the value question and declines to answer it.** Whether a person should
aim to do more or to do less is standards 3.1 case 3, and the trade literature is full of
personality on both sides. **Copy Using AI Effectively's lesson 9**, which handles the same shape
with a better evidence base: describe the trade, give both costs, and say plainly that the decision
is the reader's and that the course has no standing to make it. This also means the course's own
`course.yaml` summary, which currently leans one way, is a stance the course must name rather than
smuggle. **Lesson 1 names it.**

**4. Nothing from the trade canon is cited for a claim.** Allen, Covey, Drucker, Lakein, Burkeman
and Newport are on the `unread:` line and the build fails on a citation to any of them. They are
named in lesson 8 as widely read, and the reader is taught to sort their claims. If a future session
opens one properly, Burkeman is the one to open, because he argues against the genre's own premise.

**5. Every exercise produces a number or a written comparison, never a resolution.** The reader
already knows from Using AI Effectively that their impression of their own week is not evidence, and
this course is the one where they can act on that cheaply. An exercise whose deliverable is "reflect
on" has failed.

**6. The reader may not have discretion over their hours.** A shift worker, a carer and a
self-employed tradesperson are not the office worker the literature studied, and a course that
assumes an empty calendar and a door that closes is useless to most of its readers. **Every exercise
must work for somebody whose week is mostly fixed**, and lesson 6 is built around exactly that
reader rather than treating them as an exception.

## Lesson sequence

### 1. You do not know where your hours go (~70 min)

- **Objectives:** state what the measured gap between estimated and recorded time is and which
  direction it runs; say what this course promises and what it refuses to promise; start a record of
  one week of your own time.
- **Core ideas:** the diary-against-estimate gap, at 5 to 10 percent on work hours; **the shape of
  the error, which is the teachable part**, since the gap is small for 35 to 45-hour weeks and grows
  with the estimate, "the greater the estimate, the greater the overestimate"; the gap's own history
  by decade, which is the argument for dating everything; the club sign-in study, where almost half
  of respondents overestimated their attendance by more than 100 percent; and the honest opening
  about what the course is for, which per decision 1 is your hours rather than your output.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why a diary beats an estimate, in the authors' own terms. An estimate
  asks you to interpret "work", search your memory for every episode over a week, and add the
  lengths up, in a few seconds. A diary asks you to recall activities in sequence, and it has an
  arithmetic constraint the estimate does not: the day must add up to twenty-four hours.
- **Worked examples:** (a) *simple:* one person's estimate of their working week set beside the same
  week recorded, with the categories that moved and the two that did not. (b) *with a wrinkle:* a
  reader whose estimate is accurate, because they work a 37-hour fixed week and the gap is small in
  that band. **The wrinkle is that the technique's value depends on which band you are in**, and a
  course that told everybody to keep a diary regardless would be over-selling it.
- **Misconceptions:** "I know roughly where my time goes"; "this is about squeezing more in"; "time
  management improves your work performance" (the meta-analysis says wellbeing more than
  performance); and the one this lesson must handle with care, "the diary is just as unreliable",
  which is a real position held by working researchers and is lesson 8's contested question in
  miniature.
- **Practice:** *Record one week.* Not a plan, a record, in whatever medium the reader will actually
  keep. **Before starting, write down your estimate for four categories**, which is the prediction
  decision 5 requires. At the end, the gap between the two is the deliverable, and it is the input
  to every later lesson.
- **Sources:** Robinson, Martin, Glorieux and Minnen 2011; Aeon, Faber and Panaccio 2021; Claessens
  and colleagues 2007 for what the field is and is not.
- **Connects:** leads to 2, which is what to do with the record. Leans on **Using AI Effectively**
  lesson 11 (an impression is not evidence, and a measurement of two is better than a feeling) and
  on **Logic and Argument** lesson 9 for reading a figure.
- **Names the gap in its own evidence**, per the standard this institute set in Using AI
  Effectively: every participant in this literature is a student or an office worker, and most of
  the students were in psychology classes.

### 2. Reading your own week (~65 min)

- **Objectives:** identify, from your own record, the three largest differences between what you
  recorded and what you expected; distinguish the hours you control from the hours you do not; state
  what a week's record can and cannot tell you.
- **Core ideas:** the fixed and the discretionary, which is the distinction the rest of the course
  runs on, and the one decision 6 exists to protect. Most readers have far fewer discretionary hours
  than the genre assumes. Second: a record of one week is a sample of one, which the reader already
  knows how to think about from Logic and Argument lesson 5 and Using AI Effectively lesson 3.
  Third: the categories you chose before recording shaped what you saw, which is why the record's
  surprises are more informative than its totals.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why the surprises are where the information is. You predicted before you
  recorded. Where the two agree you have learned that your model was right there and can stop
  looking; where they disagree you have found a place your model of your own life is wrong, which
  is the only thing a single week can establish.
- **Worked examples:** (a) *simple:* a record where the surprise is a category that did not exist in
  the prediction at all, which is the commonest outcome and the reason to record rather than to
  tick boxes. (b) *with a wrinkle:* a record whose totals look fine and whose distribution is the
  problem, because the discretionary hours are real but arrive in eleven-minute pieces. **That
  wrinkle is lesson 5's subject arriving early**, and it is deliberate.
- **Misconceptions:** "the total is the finding"; "I should keep doing this forever" (the cost is
  real and the return falls); "my week is typical".
- **Practice:** *Sort the week.* Every recorded hour into fixed, discretionary, or contested, with
  the condition written beside every contested one. Then one sentence: **how many genuinely
  discretionary hours do you have, and is that more or fewer than you thought?**
- **Sources:** lesson 1's, plus the method discussion in Robinson and colleagues.
- **Connects:** builds on 1; leads to 4 and 6, both of which operate only on discretionary hours.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's gap, now measured on the reader rather than on a sample.

### 3. Why it always takes longer (~85 min)

- **Objectives:** state the measured size of the planning fallacy in at least one study, with its
  scope; explain why being told to be pessimistic does not fix it; explain why being told to
  remember does not fix it either.
- **Core ideas:** Buehler, Griffin and Ross, and this lesson is the best-evidenced in the course.
  Study 1: thirty-seven students, predicted 33.9 days, took 55.5, and **only 48.7 percent finished
  by the date they named on the assumption that everything went as badly as it possibly could.**
  Study 2: 5.8 predicted against 10.7 actual on academic projects, with subjects 74.1 percent
  certain. **And the deadline finding, which is the most practical thing in the paper**: 80.6
  percent of those with deadlines met them, while only 38.7 percent finished by their own
  prediction. Their predictions correlated weakly with their deadlines (r = .23) and their actual
  completion times correlated strongly (r = .82).
- **Mechanism to explain:** the thought-listing result, which is the whole explanation in one
  figure. 93.5 percent of subjects reported considering future plans and scenarios; 9.8 percent
  mentioned any impediment; 8.9 percent thought about their own past. **You forecast by imagining
  the run that goes well, because that is what planning is**, and your history is not in the room.
- **Worked examples:** (a) *simple:* the thesis study worked as a table, with the three predictions
  and the one actual, so the reader can see that the pessimistic column is still short. (b) *with a
  wrinkle:* the deadline sub-analysis, because it inverts the usual moral. **These people were not
  bad at finishing. They were bad at predicting, and the deadline did the work their forecast
  should have done.**
- **Misconceptions:** "I should pad my estimates" (Study 1: the pessimistic instruction reduced the
  bias and did not improve accuracy); "I should remember how long it took last time" (Study 4's
  recall condition, which did almost nothing); "other people manage this" (the bias is in predicting
  for yourself, and observers over-estimate); "deadlines are the problem".
- **Practice:** *Predict five, then measure five.* Five real tasks this week, a written prediction
  before each, the actual after, and **the ratio rather than the difference**, because the ratio is
  the thing that transfers to a task of a different size.
- **Sources:** Buehler, Griffin and Ross 1994, Studies 1, 2 and 4.
- **Connects:** builds on 1's argument that impressions of your own time are unreliable; leads to 4,
  which is the one thing that does help.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's prediction-then-measure move, now on tasks rather than on
  categories.

### 4. Plans that survive the moment (~75 min)

- **Objectives:** distinguish a goal intention from an implementation intention and write one of
  each for the same goal; state what the meta-analysis found and what it did not; apply the one
  correction to the planning fallacy that has been shown to work, and say what it costs.
- **Core ideas:** two things, joined. First, Gollwitzer and Sheeran's if-then plan: d = .65 across
  94 tests and over 8,000 participants, and the honest framing that **"forming even strong goal
  intentions does not guarantee goal attainment"**, with the numbers behind that claim (28 percent
  of the variance across 422 studies; intentions translated into action 53 percent of the time;
  experiments that changed intentions moved behaviour by R² = .03). Second, Buehler's Study 4: the
  recall-relevant condition took the share finishing on time from 29.3 to 60.0 percent, and the
  **limit that must travel with that figure, which is that predictions were less biased and no more
  accurate.**
- **Mechanism to explain:** why the if-then form does something a resolution does not. It names a
  cue, so the plan is triggered by the world rather than retrieved by you at the moment you are
  least able to retrieve it. The three failures it addresses are Gollwitzer's own: remembering to
  act, seizing the moment, and second thoughts at the critical moment.
- **Worked examples:** (a) *simple:* one goal written as a goal intention and then as an if-then
  plan, with the cue named and tested against the question "would I notice this cue if I were
  tired?". (b) *with a wrinkle:* **a plan that fails because the goal intention behind it was weak**,
  which is the moderator Gollwitzer states in the same paragraph as the effect. If-then planning
  helps most where self-regulatory problems beset a goal you actually hold; it does nothing about
  whether you should hold it.
- **Misconceptions:** "if-then plans are just goals with extra words"; "a bigger effect size means it
  will work for me"; "this is willpower"; and the one to be careful with, "so planning works", which
  overshoots what a d of .65 on goal attainment establishes.
- **Practice:** *Three if-then plans, one week, one count.* Written before the week, counted after,
  with the cue for each named. Plus the recall-relevant drill from Buehler Study 4 applied to one
  task: what happened last time, when would you finish if this went like that, and what scenario
  would produce that.
- **Sources:** Gollwitzer and Sheeran 2006 and the authors' own chapter; Buehler and colleagues,
  Study 4.
- **Connects:** builds on 3; leads to 7, where the plans go into a week.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 3's finding that remembering is not connecting.

### 5. What a working day actually looks like (~80 min)

- **Objectives:** state what a field study measured about how long people work before switching, and
  on whom; distinguish the two kinds of interruption and say which was more common; say what the
  measured cost of an interruption is and is not.
- **Core ideas:** Mark, Gonzalez and Harris, on twenty-four information workers observed to the
  second: 11.7 working spheres each, an average of **11 minutes 4 seconds** before switching or
  being interrupted, and **57.1 percent** of segments interrupted. Then the two findings that
  invert the folklore: **segments that were interrupted lasted longer than those that were not**
  (12 min 40 sec against 8 min 58 sec), and collocated workers both worked longer stretches and were
  interrupted more. And the honest position on cost, which is the 2008 experiment: interrupted
  tasks were completed *in less time with no difference in quality*, at a price in stress,
  frustration, time pressure and effort.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why "the longer you work, the longer the interruption" is not a paradox.
  The authors call it the irony of the work day: the longer you spend in a piece of work, the more
  involved in it you become and the more likely you are to be interrupted, because involvement is
  visible to other people. **The course's own reading, labelled as such**: this is why advice to
  "protect a long block" fails for somebody whose colleagues can see them.
- **Worked examples:** (a) *simple:* one reader's recorded day from lesson 1 counted into working
  spheres and switches, set against the study's figures. (b) *with a wrinkle:* **a case where
  interruption is the job.** A receptionist, a ward nurse, a support desk. The study's figures
  describe a cost that person cannot avoid and should not be told to, and the useful question for
  them is a different one: which of your work needs a continuous stretch, and when in the week does
  a stretch exist at all?
- **Misconceptions:** "an interruption costs twenty-three minutes" (**the worked provenance case:
  the figure is in neither paper it is attributed to, and the course has checked both**);
  "interruptions are what other people do to me" (internal ones are a large share); "interruption is
  simply bad" (the authors are explicit that switching can be beneficial); "open offices are the
  cause" (the collocation finding is two-sided).
- **Practice:** *Count your own switches for two days*, using the record from lesson 1 rather than
  starting again, and mark each switch internal or external. Then one sentence on which of the two
  you could act on.
- **Sources:** Mark, Gonzalez and Harris 2005; Mark, Gudith and Klocke 2008; the blog trace, used
  only for the claim that its author looked for a primary source and did not find one.
- **Connects:** builds on 2's finding that discretionary hours arrive in pieces; leads to 6 and 7.
- **Spaced review of:** Using AI Effectively lesson 11's habit of asking what was measured, on whom.

### 6. Deciding what not to do (~80 min)

- **Objectives:** produce a written decision to stop or decline something, with its cost named;
  explain why a list of priorities without a list of refusals is not a decision; identify, for
  somebody with little discretion over their hours, what the decision actually is.
- **Core ideas:** **this is the lesson with the least evidence behind it and the most use in it**,
  and the lesson must say so in its first paragraph. What the literature offers is Claessens's
  finding that time management behaviours relate to perceived control of time, job satisfaction and
  health rather than to performance, and the meta-analysis's larger wellbeing effect. Neither is a
  method. The rest is this course's reasoning, labelled at the point of use, and the shape is
  borrowed openly: **every decision names what it costs on the other side**, which is Using AI
  Effectively lesson 9's handling of the same kind of question.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why a priority list does nothing on its own. Ranking five things does
  not remove any of them from the week, and the week is where the constraint lives. A decision is
  only a decision when something is on the other side of it, and the honest form is therefore a pair:
  what I will do, and what I am accepting will not happen.
- **Worked examples:** (a) *simple:* a reader with genuine discretion, declining one recurring
  commitment, with the cost written down and a date to review it. (b) *with a wrinkle, and this one
  carries decision 6:* **a reader with almost no discretion.** A carer, or somebody on a fixed
  shift. The decision available is not what to drop but where the remaining hours go, and what to
  ask for. **A course that had nothing to say to this reader would be a course for people who
  already have the thing it teaches.**
- **Misconceptions:** "I need to be more disciplined" (the record from lesson 2 usually says
  otherwise); "urgent against important solves this" (**the course's own position, stated as such:
  the scheme is useful for sorting and does not decide anything, because the hard cases are
  important against important**); "saying no is a skill I lack" (it is a decision with a cost, and
  the cost is the part to write down); "this is about ambition".
- **Practice:** *One refusal, in writing.* What, why, what it costs, and what you would need to see
  to reverse it. Plus, for a reader with no discretion, the mirror: **what you have already given
  up without deciding to**, which is the same exercise run backwards and is usually more
  uncomfortable.
- **Sources:** Claessens and colleagues 2007; Aeon and colleagues 2021; and the course's own
  reasoning, labelled.
- **Connects:** builds on 2's fixed-and-discretionary split; leads to 7.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 4's point that an if-then plan does nothing about whether the goal is
  worth holding.

### 7. A week that survives other people (~75 min)

- **Objectives:** build a week that names its fixed points before anything else; state what your week
  gave up; run it once and record where it broke.
- **Core ideas:** the assembly lesson. Fixed points first, because lesson 2 established that most of
  the week is not yours; discretionary hours placed against the switching picture from lesson 5
  rather than against an imagined empty afternoon; if-then plans from lesson 4 attached to cues that
  exist in the week you actually have; and the refusal from lesson 6 written into it, so that the
  plan records a decision rather than a wish. **And the estimate correction from lesson 3 applied to
  the plan itself**, since a week's plan is a prediction and is subject to everything lesson 3 said.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why a plan that ignores other people's claims on your time fails in a
  particular way, which is that it is not wrong at the start of the week and becomes wrong by
  Tuesday, at which point it is abandoned rather than revised. A plan with slack in it survives
  being wrong.
- **Worked examples:** (a) *simple:* a week built from one reader's lesson 2 sort, with the fixed
  points in first and the discretionary hours placed last. (b) *with a wrinkle:* **the same week
  after it broke**, with what broke it and what the reader changed. The second version is the
  lesson; the first is the set-up.
- **Misconceptions:** "a plan that survived the week was a good plan" (it may have been an easy
  week); "a plan that broke was a bad plan"; "I need a system"; "if I plan every hour I will use
  every hour", which is where **Parkinson's law** gets its first mention and its deflation.
- **Practice:** *Build it, run it, and record the breaks.* With a written prediction of which day it
  will break, because decision 5 requires one and because the prediction is usually wrong in an
  informative direction.
- **Sources:** all of the above; nothing new.
- **Connects:** builds on 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6; leads to 8.
- **Spaced review of:** deliberately the whole course, through the build.
- **Must not re-teach:** **How to Learn Anything** lesson 8 already builds a weekly study plan and
  covers attention and procrastination, and **Focus and Deep Work** and **Habits and
  Self-Discipline** are Term 2 and are not this course's subject. This lesson points at all three
  and takes none of them.

### 8. Reading a claim about your time (~70 min)

- **Objectives:** sort a piece of time-management advice into measurable, unmeasurable or false;
  trace a widely repeated figure to its source, or establish that it has none; state what the
  evidence does and does not show about whether any of this works.
- **Core ideas:** **this is the lesson that carries everything perishable, by design**, the way
  Using AI Effectively's lesson 11 does. Three habits. First, the sorting, which is **Digital
  Literacy**'s move and Using AI Effectively's adapted again, and the lesson says so. Second, trace
  the number: the twenty-three minutes, and Parkinson's law, which is the opening line of a 1955
  satire, is called by its own author "a commonplace observation", is actually about bureaucratic
  growth, and has never been tested. Third, the honest answer to "does time management work", which
  is two papers fourteen years apart that disagree in tone: Claessens's "the relationship with work
  and academic performance is not clear" and the 2021 meta-analysis's moderate relationship, both
  correlational, with the wellbeing effect larger than the performance effect in the newer one.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why this genre in particular produces unsourced numbers. A memorable
  figure travels better than a study, the trade books cite each other rather than the literature,
  and nobody checks, which is a claim the reader can test in ten minutes on the twenty-three
  minutes.
- **Worked examples:** (a) *simple:* four pieces of advice sorted, one of each kind plus one that is
  really two claims stuck together. (b) *with a wrinkle:* **the twenty-three minutes, traced
  properly.** Where it is attributed, what those papers actually say, and what the 2008 paper found
  instead, which is close to the opposite. The reader watches a number fail to have a source.
- **Misconceptions:** "somebody must have checked"; "an old study is out of date" (the findings
  about people do not go stale the way the figures do, which is Using AI Effectively lesson 11's
  sentence and is true here for the same reason); "the science says" about a genre with no science
  before 1982.
- **Practice:** *Trace one.* One claim about time, from anywhere, traced as far as the reader can
  get in twenty minutes, with the finding written down whatever it is. Then: **write your lesson 1
  estimate again from memory, and compare it with the record.** That is the course closing on the
  measurement it opened with.
- **Sources:** Claessens and colleagues 2007; Aeon and colleagues 2021; Parkinson at
  search-summary level and flagged as such; the two Mark papers; the blog trace.
- **Connects:** builds on the whole course; leads out of it.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's record, retrieved before it is compared, which is **How to Learn
  Anything**'s argument applied to this course's own material at the end of it.

## Assessments

### Course-end test

**Twenty items.** At least fourteen must be application to material in no lesson: a described
person, a described week, a described claim. **No item may turn on a figure that dates** (the
diary-estimate gap by decade, any interruption figure), because the test would then need revising
whenever the literature moves.

| Area | Items | Kind |
|---|---|---|
| The estimate-record gap and its shape (L1) | 2 | 1 recall with scope, 1 application to a described estimate |
| Reading a week, fixed against discretionary (L2) | 2 | both application |
| The planning fallacy and the two failed corrections (L3) | 4 | 1 recall of the figures with scope, 3 application |
| Goal intentions against if-then plans (L4) | 3 | all application: rewrite, diagnose a failure, name a limit |
| The working day, switching and interruption (L5) | 3 | all application, one on a job where interruption is the work |
| Deciding what not to do (L6) | 3 | all application, one for a reader with no discretion |
| Building a week (L7) | 2 | both application |
| Reading a claim (L8) | 1 | the sorting item |

**Three items must be cross-lesson:** one pairing lesson 3's planning fallacy with lesson 7's weekly
plan, because a plan is a prediction; one pairing lesson 2's discretionary hours with lesson 6's
refusal, because you can only decline what was yours; and one pairing lesson 1's measurement with
lesson 8's claim-reading, because they are the same move pointed inward and outward.

### Project: the fortnight

**Deliverable.** One plain text or Markdown file of roughly 1,200 to 2,000 words, written by the
learner, in five sections, with the records to back them. Plain text for the reason **Digital
Literacy**'s project gives and Using AI Effectively's repeats: it exercises an earlier course and it
leaves the learner holding a document they made.

**Budget about ten hours over two or three weeks**, most of it the recording in section 1, which is
spread out because it has to be.

**The design problem this solves.** No section can have a key, because the answer is the learner's
own week. **So every section asks for a prediction, then the record, then the gap**, which is Using
AI Effectively's project solving the same problem, and the gap is assessable without a key.

**Steps.**

1. **Two weeks recorded**, with the four-category estimate written before each. Not one week: two,
   because lesson 2 establishes that one is a sample of one, and the difference between the two
   weeks is a finding of its own.
2. **The sort**, fixed against discretionary against contested, with the condition written beside
   every contested entry, and the count of genuinely discretionary hours.
3. **Five predictions and five measurements**, with the ratio for each and one sentence on whether
   the ratios cluster.
4. **One refusal and one if-then plan**, both in writing, both with what they cost, and both
   reported on after a week.
5. **One traced claim**, and the estimate from section 1 rewritten from memory and compared.

**Rubric.**

| Criterion | What full marks look like |
|---|---|
| The record is real | Two weeks of actual entries, including the days that went badly and the hours that are missing |
| Predictions are written first | Every prediction is recorded before its measurement, and at least one is wrong |
| The sort is honest | Section 2 distinguishes hours that are genuinely fixed from hours the learner has decided not to contest |
| Ratios rather than differences | Section 3 reports ratios, so the finding transfers to tasks of other sizes |
| Something was given up | Section 4 names a real refusal with a real cost, not a hypothetical one |
| The plan names its cue | The if-then plan names a cue that exists in the week recorded in section 1 |
| Dated and scoped | Every figure quoted from the course carries what it was measured on and when |
| Honest about the gap | Where a prediction was wrong, the file says so and says what the mistake was |

**The first and last rows are the course's own standards applied to the learner**, and the rubric
says so out loud: a fortnight in which every prediction was correct is a fortnight whose predictions
were written afterwards.

## Neutrality notes

This course carries `sensitive_domain: false`, and the call is worth stating. Standards 3.4's list
does not reach it: there is no politics, no religion, no recent event, and the health material is
one correlation reported as a correlation. **The neutrality audit is therefore not mandatory**, and
two things still need care.

- **Lesson 6 (what not to do)** is standards 3.1 case 3, a value question. What a person should
  trade their hours for is not something this course can settle, and the reader's circumstances vary
  more than in any other course in Term 1. Describe, give both costs, decline to prescribe.
  **Decision 3 and decision 6 exist for this lesson.**
- **The whole course has an obvious temptation**, which is to sell the genre's promise while
  disclaiming it. The test to apply: if a sentence would sit comfortably on the back of a trade
  paperback, it needs either a figure with a scope attached or deletion.

## What is deliberately left out, and where it goes instead

- **Procrastination**, attention and the psychology of not starting. **How to Learn Anything**
  lesson 8 covers it and Term 2's **Focus and Deep Work** and **Habits and Self-Discipline** are
  built for it. This course names the boundary in lesson 7 and does not cross it.
- **Tools and apps.** No product is named, for Using AI Effectively's reason: the market moves
  faster than a lesson, and a course that reads as an advertisement is doing something this
  institute does not do.
- **Team and organisational time.** Meetings, delegation and project scheduling are somebody else's
  course; this one is about a person's own week.
- **The trade canon, read properly.** Named in lesson 8, unread, on the `unread:` line, and flagged
  in SOURCES.md as the obvious next Stage 1 job if John wants it.

## What downstream courses will need this one to have taught

**Focus and Deep Work** and **Habits and Self-Discipline**, both Term 2, can assume: that the
learner has recorded a week and knows the estimate-record gap; that they can tell a fixed hour from
a discretionary one; that they know what an if-then plan is and what its evidence is; and that they
have made at least one written refusal with a cost attached. Neither should re-teach any of those.
