# Outline: Focus and Deep Work

*Stage 2, 2026-09-19. Written from `research/SOURCES.md` and from `npm run taught
courses/learning-and-mind/focus-and-deep-work`, which lists 92 lessons across ten written courses
that a learner reaching this point has already had.*

## How many lessons, and why

**Seven.** The same count as Habits and Self-Discipline, and for the same reason: this subject has
one honest measurement, three contested claims that each need a lesson to do properly, one
constructive lesson, and a closing lesson on reading the literature.

**The count is set by the contested questions rather than by the techniques.** A course of
techniques here would be four lessons of advice with almost nothing behind it, which is what the
genre already provides. What this subject actually offers a learner is three worked cases of a
famous finding meeting a later synthesis, and those do not compress.

**What the seven are.**

1. What this course is for, and what attention means when somebody measures it
2. What an interruption actually costs
3. The mind that is not on the task at all
4. Does multitasking damage your attention?
5. The phone on the desk
6. Can attention be trained, and what would that even mean?
7. Reading a claim about your attention

## Six decisions, settled here so no lesson re-opens them

**Decision 1. The course will not hand out a focus technique it cannot support.** Lesson 1 says so,
in the form Habits lesson 1 used. The one constructive section in the course is in lesson 6, it is
built from what survives lessons 2 to 5, and **every part of it that is the course's own reasoning
is labelled as such at the point of use**. A reader who finishes wanting a method has been told
honestly why there is not one to give.

**Decision 2. Every figure carries what it was measured on and when, in the body, at each
appearance.** This course's figures are worse in this respect than Habits' were: 46.9 percent comes
from iPhone volunteers in 2010, the interruption result from 48 mostly German students in a
90-minute lab session, and the pooled effects from literatures that are overwhelmingly student
samples. **Lesson 1 names the gap once, as a section, and each lesson restates the scope where its
own figure appears.**

**Decision 3. The boundary with Time Management lesson 5 is drawn in lesson 1 and held.** That
lesson teaches Mark's 2005 field study, how long a work segment runs, and the trace of the
twenty-three-minute figure. **This course does not re-teach any of the three**, and where a lesson
needs one it points at Time Management rather than repeating it. Lesson 2 uses a different paper by
one of the same authors and must say which.

**Decision 4. Where a claim rests on a source this course did not open, the lesson says so in the
body at the point of use**, not only in a footnote. This applies to Ophir, Nass and Wagner, to Ward
and colleagues, to Ruiz Pardo and Minda, to the mindfulness syntheses, and to Leroy. **Habits lesson
4's opening read-level note is the model.**

**Decision 5. The self-report and performance split is the course's through-line, introduced in
lesson 1 and used in four later lessons.** It is what makes the media-multitasking meta-analysis
intelligible, it is why the brain-drain domains differ, and it is the reason "I am more distractible
than I used to be" is not the kind of sentence that settles anything. **It is the one idea this
course most wants a learner to keep.**

**Decision 6. No lesson may tell a reader that their attention has got worse, or that it has not.**
Nothing read for this course measures change over time in individuals; every source is
cross-sectional or a single session. Lesson 7 says this in terms, and it is the course's main
refusal.

## Lesson 1. What this course is for, and what attention means when somebody measures it

**Objectives.**

- Distinguish the two instruments this subject uses, self-report and task performance, and say why
  the same question answered by each can give different results
- Say what this course promises and what it refuses to promise, and identify a claim about attention
  that the course could not support
- Name the population nearly every figure in this subject was measured on, and say what that does
  and does not license

**Core ideas.** Attention is not one thing and the word covers at least sustained attention,
selective attention and the control of switching. **What matters for reading this subject is not the
taxonomy but the instrument**: a questionnaire asking how distractible you are, and a task counting
how often you miss a target, are measuring different things and they do not agree. Source 3's split
is the demonstration and lesson 4 gives it properly; lesson 1 only plants it.

**Mechanism to explain.** Why the two instruments come apart. A self-report is a judgement about
oneself made from memory, and a task is a count. Reading Well lesson 8 and Logic and Argument lesson
5 both bear on this and the lesson should use them rather than re-teaching them.

**Worked examples.** (a) The same person answering "how often do you get distracted at work?" and
then doing a task that measures it, with the two answers set out and the reasons they could differ
listed. **Constructed, and labelled.** (b) The scope table for this course's own four main sources:
what each was measured on, how many people, and when. **This is real and it is the section decision
2 exists for.**

**Misconceptions.** "Attention is one capacity." "If I feel more distractible, I am." "Studies of
students tell you nothing" — which over-corrects, and the lesson says what a student sample does and
does not license.

**Practice.** (1) Write down your own answer to three self-report items about your attention, then
predict how you would do on a task measuring the same thing, and say which you would trust. (2) Take
one claim about attention you have met and write down which instrument would have to have produced
it.

**Sources.** 1, 2, 3, 4 for the scope table. **Connections.** Back to Time Management lesson 5 for
the boundary, and Habits lesson 1 for the shape of a course that names what it will not do. Forward
to every lesson.

## Lesson 2. What an interruption actually costs

**Objectives.**

- State what the CHI 2008 experiment measured and found, with its sample and its design
- Explain why a measure that looks at time and errors alone can miss the cost of an interruption
- Predict, for a described case, what an interruption would and would not show up in

**Core ideas.** The finding inverts the expectation: **interrupted tasks were finished faster**, in
about 20.3 and 20.6 minutes against 22.8 uninterrupted, with no difference in errors or politeness.
The price was stress, frustration, time pressure and effort, all rated higher in both interruption
conditions. And the work changed: emails were shorter when interrupted.

**Mechanism to explain.** Compensation. People speed up to make up the lost time, and the speeding
up is what costs them. **The lesson's central move is to ask what a manager reading only the
completion times would conclude**, which is that interruptions are free, and why that is wrong.

**Worked examples.** (a) The experiment itself, laid out with its three conditions, its numbers and
its scale. (b) A described worker whose output is measured on volume and error rate and who is
interrupted constantly. **Constructed and labelled.** The wrinkle: her numbers look fine, and the
lesson has to say precisely what is and is not established about her by that.

**Misconceptions.** "An interruption costs the time it takes plus the time to get back" — Time
Management lesson 5 covers the recovery-time literature and this lesson points there rather than
re-arguing it. "Same-topic interruptions are less disruptive" — tested here and not supported.
"Working faster is good."

**Practice.** (1) For one day, note each interruption and rate stress and effort at the end of each
work block, against a block with none. (2) Take a measure somebody applies to your work and write
down what it would miss.

**Sources.** 1 primarily; 6 named and not relied on. **Connections.** Back to lesson 1's instruments
and to Time Management lesson 5. Forward to lesson 3, which is the interruption that comes from
inside.

## Lesson 3. The mind that is not on the task at all

**Objectives.**

- State what the experience-sampling study measured about mind-wandering, with its sample, its
  method and its date
- Distinguish an interruption from mind-wandering, and say why the second is harder to measure
- Say what the study's time-lag analysis does and does not establish about causation

**Core ideas.** 46.9 percent of samples, and at least 30 percent during every activity measured
except one. The happiness finding, and then **the direction-of-causation question, which is where
this lesson earns its place**: the authors' own phrase is that time-lag analyses "strongly
suggested" mind-wandering was "generally the cause", in observational data.

**Mechanism to explain.** What experience sampling is, why it gives a higher number than the lab,
and why that is a feature rather than a flaw. The authors say so themselves and the lesson quotes
it.

**Worked examples.** (a) A reader's own hour reconstructed against the study's question, showing how
the count works. **Constructed.** (b) The causal claim taken apart: three readings of "mind
wandering makes you unhappy", and what each would need.

**Misconceptions.** "Half your life is wasted." "Mind-wandering is a failure of discipline." "The
study showed mind-wandering causes unhappiness" — stated more strongly than the paper does.

**Practice.** (1) Sample yourself hourly for one day with the study's own question and four options,
and compare your proportion with 46.9 percent, with a line on why yours is not comparable. (2) Take
one causal claim from a newspaper and write down what design would be needed to support it.

**Sources.** 2. **Connections.** Back to lesson 2, and to Habits lesson 2 for the shape of a diary
study and the self-report problem. Forward to lesson 6.

## Lesson 4. Does multitasking damage your attention?

**Objectives.**

- State what the 2009 study claimed and what the 2021 meta-analysis found, with the scale of each
- Explain what a publication-bias correction does, and what happened to the performance-based effect
  when one was applied
- Explain why the self-report and performance halves of this literature disagree, and what that
  means for a reader assessing their own attention

**Core ideas.** This is the course's central lesson and the one decision 5 was written for. 118
assessments; overall z = .138; performance-based z = .091; self-report z = .200; and then
trim-and-fill takes the performance-based effect to z = .032 with an interval crossing zero.
**Same literature, two instruments, two answers.**

**Mechanism to explain.** Publication bias and what trim-and-fill does, at the level Habits lesson 5
introduced it and no deeper. Then the more interesting question: why would self-report find
something performance does not? Three candidate answers, with the lesson saying which are testable.

**Worked examples.** (a) The meta-analysis's own split, laid out as a table. (b) A person who is
certain their attention has been ruined by their phone, and what the evidence does and does not say
about them. **The wrinkle**: the honest answer includes that their self-report is itself the
measure that shows the association, which is not nothing, and the lesson has to hold both.

**Misconceptions.** "Multitasking rewires your brain." "The original study was debunked" — too
strong, and the meta-analysts say the self-report half holds. "A small effect is no effect."

**Practice.** (1) Find one article about multitasking and attention, and identify which instrument
its claim rests on. (2) Write the strongest version of the case *for* the association, using the
meta-analysis's own words, and then the strongest against.

**Sources.** 3 primarily. **Connections.** Back to lesson 1's instruments, which this lesson pays
off. Forward to lesson 5, which is the same move on a smaller claim.

## Lesson 5. The phone on the desk

**Objectives.**

- State what the brain-drain study claimed, what the preregistered replication found, and what the
  meta-analysis pooled
- Read a subgroup analysis, and say what it means that the effect is absent in one region's samples
- Say what this course can and cannot tell somebody deciding where to put their phone

**Core ideas.** 22 studies, 43 effects, g = −0.14 overall. Memory g = −0.23; **attention g = −0.07
with an interval crossing zero**, which matters because "brain drain" is a claim about attention.
And the regional split: Asian samples −0.39, European −0.20 not significant, **North American −0.03
and nowhere near significant**, in the country where the original was run.

**Mechanism to explain.** What a subgroup analysis is, and the discipline of not reading a
significant subgroup as a discovery. This is the lesson's hardest teaching problem and it has to be
done without either dismissing the regional finding or treating it as explained.

**Worked examples.** (a) The three-row domain table and the three-row region table, read properly.
(b) A reader deciding whether to put their phone in another room, worked all the way to a decision,
with what the evidence contributes and what it does not. **The wrinkle**: the honest answer is that
the evidence barely bears on it, and the decision is still worth making on other grounds, which the
lesson must say without pretending those grounds are findings.

**Misconceptions.** "Face down is enough." "The effect was debunked" — a pooled effect exists.
"Meta-analysis settles it."

**Practice.** (1) Run the comparison on yourself across four work blocks, and write down why the
result is not evidence. (2) Take the meta-analysis's regional table and write one paragraph on what
would explain it, marked as speculation.

**Sources.** 4. **Connections.** Back to lesson 4, which taught the pooled-evidence move. Forward to
lesson 6.

## Lesson 6. Can attention be trained, and what would that even mean?

**Objectives.**

- Distinguish near, intermediate and far transfer, and say why the distinction decides the question
- State what the working-memory training meta-analysis found, including what it says about the
  studies' evidential value
- Design one working hour from what survives this course, and say which parts are evidence and which
  are the course's own judgement

**Core ideas.** 87 publications, 145 comparisons. Near transfer large, intermediate transfer real
but modest, **far transfer not convincingly there at all when compared against a treated control**.
The mediation finding: improvement on the trained task did not predict far transfer. And the
sentence a lesson should quote in full, that there is "no evidential value" from the treated-control
studies.

**Mechanism to explain.** Transfer, taught properly with examples, and why a treated control is the
thing that matters. **How to Learn Anything** taught deliberate practice and this lesson should use
it rather than re-teaching it.

**Then the constructive section**, which is the only one in the course. What survives: a reliable
context, which is Habits lesson 3 and is cited as such; removing interruptions rather than
compensating for them, from lesson 2; and the honest position that nothing read here shows attention
itself can be trained. **Everything in this section that is the course's own reasoning is labelled
inline**, per decision 1.

**Worked examples.** (a) A brain-training claim taken apart by transfer level. (b) One working hour
designed, with each element labelled evidence or judgement. **The wrinkle**: at least two elements
must come out as judgement, and the lesson says so plainly rather than dressing them up.

**Misconceptions.** "Focus is a muscle." "Meditation is proven to improve attention" — small effects
and this course has not read the syntheses, which the lesson says. "If training does not transfer,
nothing helps."

**Practice.** (1) Design your hour and run it three times, recording one number. (2) Take a
brain-training product's claim and sort it by transfer level.

**Sources.** 5 primarily; the mindfulness note at its stated read level; Habits lesson 3 for the cue.
**Connections.** Back to lessons 2 to 5, all of which feed the constructive section. Forward to
lesson 7.

## Lesson 7. Reading a claim about your attention

**Objectives.**

- Sort a claim about attention into measurable, unmeasurable as stated, or false, and say what would
  settle it
- Identify which instrument a claim rests on, and say what follows from that
- Decide, for one working arrangement of your own, what to change and what to leave, with what each
  costs

**Core ideas.** This is the lesson that carries everything perishable, by design, the way Using AI
Effectively lesson 11, Time Management lesson 8 and Habits lesson 7 do. **Its own move is the
instrument question**, which is this course's addition to the institute's standing sorting habit:
before asking whether a claim is true, ask what would have had to be measured.

Then the three claims this course has taken apart, set side by side with what each one's later
synthesis found, so the pattern is visible: a striking result, a decade of writing, and a pooled
estimate the writing has not caught up with.

Then the trade books, named as widely read and cited for nothing, with the `unread:` exemption.

Then decision 6's refusal, faced: **this course cannot tell you whether your attention has got
worse**, because nothing it read measures change within a person over time, and the honest thing is
to say what would.

**Worked examples.** (a) Four claims sorted, one of each kind plus one that is two claims stuck
together, each worked rather than asserted. (b) The three-synthesis table.

**Misconceptions.** "Somebody must have checked." "The science says." "A meta-analysis is the last
word."

**Practice.** (1) Trace one claim about attention in twenty minutes, with the five endings Habits
lesson 7 uses. (2) The decision: one working arrangement, changed, kept, or abandoned, with the cost
of each named. **All three endings must be worked**, per the neutrality note below.

**Sources.** All. **Connections.** Back to the whole course. Out to **Memory** and **Note-Taking**,
both unwritten.

## Assessments

### Course-end test

**Eighteen items.** At least thirteen must be application to material in no lesson. **No item may
turn on a figure whose scope is the teaching point** in a way that rewards recalling the number
rather than the scope.

| Area | Items | Kind |
|---|---|---|
| Instruments, and what attention means measured (L1) | 3 | 1 recall, 2 application |
| The cost of an interruption (L2) | 3 | 1 recall with scope, 2 application |
| Mind-wandering and the causal claim (L3) | 3 | all application |
| Media multitasking and the bias correction (L4) | 3 | 1 recall with scope, 2 application |
| The phone, and reading a subgroup analysis (L5) | 3 | all application |
| Transfer and training (L6) | 2 | both application |
| Sorting a claim (L7) | 1 | the sorting item |

**Three items must be cross-lesson**: one pairing lesson 1's instruments with lesson 4's split,
because that is the course's spine; one pairing lesson 2's cost with lesson 6's hour, because the
design follows from the finding; and one pairing lesson 3's causal hedge with lesson 5's subgroup,
because both are about reading a result more strongly than it was stated.

### Project: four weeks, one hour

**Deliverable.** One plain text or Markdown file, roughly 1,000 to 1,800 words, in four sections,
with the log behind it.

**Budget about six hours across four weeks**, nearly all of it the hour itself.

**The design problem this solves.** No section can have a key, because the answer is the learner's
own week. **So every section asks for a prediction, then the record, then the gap.**

**Steps.** (1) The design: one hour, one context, what is removed and what is not, with a written
prediction of how many of the sixteen sessions will happen and how they will feel. (2) Four weeks of
the log: happened or not, one interruption count, one effort rating. (3) The reading: prediction
against record, with the interruption count and the effort rating plotted or tabulated separately,
because lesson 2 is about exactly that gap. (4) The decision: keep, change or stop, with what each
costs.

**Rubric.**

| Criterion | What full marks look like |
|---|---|
| The hour has a context, not an intention | Section 1 names a place and a time the learner reliably meets |
| Predictions are written first | Both predictions recorded before week 1, and at least one is wrong |
| The record is real | Missed sessions present and marked |
| Two measures, read separately | Section 3 does not collapse the interruption count into the effort rating |
| Scope is respected | No figure from the course is quoted without what it was measured on |
| The decision names its cost | Section 4's decision has something on the other side of it |

**A learner whose hour did not work has not failed the project.** Nothing in this course predicts
that any particular arrangement will work for any particular person, and a rubric that scored only
success would be teaching the opposite of lessons 4 to 6.

## Neutrality notes

`sensitive_domain: false`, and the call is worth stating: standards 3.4's list does not reach this
subject. Three things still need care.

- **Lessons 4 and 5 are debunking-shaped and must not be debunkings.** In each case a pooled effect
  exists, the self-report half of the multitasking literature holds, and the meta-analysts themselves
  argue for caution about phones. **Habits lesson 5's failure is the one to avoid**: it described a
  model entirely in the words of the papers undercutting it, and the fix was to go and find the best
  available statement of the other side. Do that here at Stage 2, not at Stage 4.
- **Lesson 7's decision is standards 3.1 case 3**, and all three endings must be worked, which is
  the note Habits lesson 7 passed on and Time Management lesson 6 failed.
- **Decision 6 is a refusal a reader will push on.** Everybody believes their attention has got
  worse. The lesson has to decline without implying the reader is wrong, which is a harder sentence
  than it looks and should be drafted carefully.

## What is deliberately left out

- **Flow.** Csikszentmihalyi is on the `unread:` line and the measurement literature was not read.
  Named in lesson 1 as something the course is not doing. **If a later session researches it
  properly, it is an eighth lesson.**
- **ADHD and clinical attention.** A different literature, real harm available if a general course
  touches it lightly. Lesson 1 says so in a line.
- **The neuroscience of attention networks.** Real, not read, and a lesson of diagrams would be
  decoration. Named in lesson 1, as Habits lesson 1 named the basal ganglia.
- **Notification and interface design.** It is the applied half of source 1's own literature and
  this course is about the person rather than the software.

## What downstream courses will need this one to have taught

**Memory** (T2, unwritten) will need the instrument distinction and the transfer material, and
should not have to re-teach either. **Note-Taking and Personal Knowledge** (T2, unwritten) will need
lesson 2's finding about what a measure misses. Neither exists yet, so this course assumes nothing
from them.
