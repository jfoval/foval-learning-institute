S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. The last lesson of Focus and Deep Work. Reading a claim about your attention.

S2: The one that outlives the rest.

S1: Everything before it was about attention. This one is about the claims you'll meet about it, and you'll meet a great many.

S2: Three habits, you said.

S1: Ask what would have had to be measured. Check whether the claim is about change. And separate the report from the general claim. Those three are this course's own way of organising the job rather than a procedure anybody has published.

S2: Start with the first.

S1: The institute's standing sort is three categories: measurable, meaning a study could check it; unmeasurable as stated, meaning it uses a term nobody has defined; and false, meaning it's been checked and didn't hold. This course adds a question in front of that one. Which instrument would have had to produce this claim?

S2: Work one.

S1: People who check their phone constantly are worse at concentrating. Before sorting it, ask what would have been measured. A questionnaire about phone-checking and a questionnaire about concentration? A questionnaire and a task? Two tasks? The claim is compatible with all three and they aren't the same claim, and lesson four is a case where two of them give different answers.

S2: Give me an unmeasurable one.

S1: A specimen this course wrote rather than a sentence it's quoting, because it hasn't opened the books that define this. An ability to focus without distraction on a cognitively demanding task. What would be measured? Nothing yet: there's no criterion for when somebody has the ability and when they merely did the task once.

S2: Which isn't the same as useless.

S1: It's a definition, and definitions are conventions.

S2: And a fourth kind.

S1: The commonest. Turn off notifications, because every interruption costs you twenty-three minutes. That's two claims: a recommendation and a figure. Time Management lesson five traced the figure and found no printed source anybody has been able to produce. The recommendation survives the figure being wrong, on lesson two's evidence, and a sorter who threw out both has thrown away the half that holds.

S2: Habit two. Change.

S1: This subject has one failure mode of its own, and naming it is the course's own reading rather than a finding. The previous course's numbers failed by arriving stripped of their scope. These claims fail by being about change when nothing measured change.

S2: Attention spans are shrinking.

S1: Nobody can read a book any more. Your attention span is now shorter than a goldfish's. Every one of those is a claim about something that used to be different, and a claim about change needs the same people, or comparable populations, measured twice on the same instrument.

S2: And nothing here does that.

S1: Every source is a single session or a snapshot of different people at one moment. The two thousand and eight experiment ran ninety minutes. The mind-wandering study sampled people over days. Both syntheses pool studies that measured people once, and one of the two says in terms that its designs are cross-sectional.

S2: So the refusal isn't modesty.

S1: It's arithmetic. A body of evidence made entirely of snapshots can't answer a question about change, however many snapshots there are.

S2: What study would settle it, and why hasn't anybody run it?

S1: The study is easy to describe. Take a sample, measure them on a fixed task, wait ten or twenty years, measure the same people again on the same task.

S2: And why hasn't anybody?

S1: Three reasons, and this course's own reading is that the third is the binding one. Duration: a twenty-year study needs funding across careers. Instruments change: a task written in two thousand and five runs on hardware nobody uses, and rewriting it breaks the comparison, which is the point.

S2: And the third?

S1: Nobody would agree what to measure. You'd have to settle, in advance and for both dates, what attention span means as a number. Committing to a criterion is what makes a claim checkable and it's also what makes it contestable, which is why so many claims in this genre avoid it.

S2: Habit three.

S1: Somebody tells you a technique works for them. You've looked and found no evidence for it. Those are two claims, and almost every argument about this subject is two people disputing different sentences.

S2: Unpack it.

S1: It works for me is a report about one person, in one situation, usually without a comparison. It's weak evidence about the world and it isn't nothing, and it's the same kind of evidence as the self-report half of lesson four's literature, which is the half where the associations hold. It works is a general claim and needs the general kind of evidence.

S2: So what does the lesson ask of me?

S1: To say which one you're disputing, out loud, before you disagree. Most of the time the honest sentence is: I believe you about your mornings, and the general claim hasn't been tested.

S2: Put the three claims side by side for me.

S1: The first two columns of this are the syntheses'; the third, what survives, is this course's own reading of what each leaves standing, and it says to hold that loosely. Media multitasking damages attention: a hundred and eighteen assessments, overall small, and the performance half fails a publication-bias correction. What survives is the self-report association, consistently. The phone's mere presence drains cognitive capacity: twenty-two studies, a pooled effect size of minus nought point one four, memory yes, attention crossing zero, null in North American samples. What survives is a small pooled effect in memory and the authors' recommendation of caution. Attention can be trained: eighty-seven publications, no convincing far transfer against treated controls. What survives is large near transfer, which is not what anybody is buying.

S2: In all three cases something survives.

S1: And in all three it's narrower than the claim it came from. None of the three is a fraud and none of them is what it's sold as.

S2: What do they have in common?

S1: In each case the synthesis is more informative than the original study, and in each case it's the thing nobody has read. The original is famous. The paper that pooled it is where the answer is.
