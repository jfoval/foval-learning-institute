S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Lesson four of Focus and Deep Work. Does multitasking damage your attention?

S2: You've called this the lesson the course was built around.

S1: And the reason isn't the subject. It's that this one literature does something almost no other does: it answers the same question two ways and gets two answers, and both have been counted.

S2: Start with the claim I've met.

S1: In two thousand and nine, Ophir, Nass and Wagner published the study everybody in this subject cites. They built a questionnaire called the media multitasking index, split people into heavy and light multitaskers by their scores, and reported that the heavy multitaskers did worse on tests of filtering out distraction.

S2: Have you read it?

S1: No, and the lesson says so where the study is described. What exactly the index asks isn't something this course can tell you. The figures come from a meta-analysis that read it and a hundred and seventeen other assessments.

S2: What happened in between?

S1: The ordinary and admirable thing. Other people tried it. Some found the association and some didn't, and after a decade somebody pooled the lot.

S2: Who?

S1: Parry and le Roux, in twenty twenty-one. This course has read its abstract, its overall result, its moderator analyses and its conclusion, which is enough for what follows and isn't enough to have weighed every study in it.

S2: Give me the scale.

S1: In their words: following a systematic search and selection process, one hundred and eighteen assessments were included in the meta-analysis.

S2: And the headline?

S1: The effect size for the association between media multitasking and cognitive control is small, at z equals nought point one three eight, with an interval from nought point one nought seven to nought point one seven, and highly heterogeneous, with an I-squared of nearly eighty percent.

S2: Three things and no more, you said.

S1: The association is there. It's small. And the studies disagree with each other a great deal, which is what the heterogeneity figure says.

S2: And one thing the sentence doesn't say.

S1: Which this course has to. Those hundred and eighteen assessments pool studies that are overwhelmingly student samples. So every figure here is a figure about students, and the sentence people who multitask are more distractible is already wrong at the second word.

S2: All right. You said two answers.

S1: The hundred and eighteen used two kinds of measure. Questionnaires asking people about their own distractibility, and tasks counting their performance. Which do you expect showed the stronger association?

S2: The task. It's the more serious instrument.

S1: That's what most people say, and it's the other way round. For self-report measures the pooled effect size is small and statistically significant, at z equals nought point two. For performance-based assays, the effect size is negligible but statistically significant, at nought point nought nine one.

S2: And the measurement approach moderated the result.

S1: Which is the technical way of saying the difference between those two numbers is itself a finding rather than noise.

S2: You said there was one more step.

S1: And it's the one that changes the conclusion. A pooled estimate is only as good as what got published, and studies finding nothing are less likely to be written up. There are tests for whether a literature looks like it's missing its null results, and one is Egger's test.

S2: What did it say?

S1: Significant for the performance-based assays and not for the self-report ones. So the authors ran a correction called trim-and-fill, which imputes the studies the pattern suggests are missing and re-pools. In their words: in this sensitivity analysis, the pooled effect for performance-based assays was no longer statistically significant, at nought point nought three two, with an interval from minus nought point nought two four to nought point nought eight eight.

S2: Read the interval rather than the estimate.

S1: It runs from below zero to above it. Once the pattern of missing nulls is corrected for, the performance-based literature can't distinguish the effect from nothing.

S2: So the task half loses and the questionnaire half wins.

S1: That's where a worse lesson would stop and declare a winner, and this course won't. Three explanations for the split, and all three are live. First, the two instruments ask about different stretches of time: a task measures twenty minutes in a quiet room, a questionnaire asks about a life.

S2: Second.

S1: The self-report may be picking up something real that isn't attention. Somebody who multitasks heavily may also have more demanding work, more people contacting them, or less control over their day. All of that shows up on a distractibility questionnaire and none of it on a filtering task.

S2: Third.

S1: The belief itself is doing work. A person who has concluded their attention is ruined behaves differently from one who hasn't, and the questionnaire is the only instrument of the two that can see it.

S2: None of those needs anybody to be lying.

S1: And the first two are testable. A study measuring the same people in a quiet room and in their own working week would separate them. Nothing this course read separates any of them.

S2: What do the meta-analysts themselves say?

S1: Ten years on from Ophir and colleagues, the picture is not any more transparent, and based on the papers reviewed we are no closer to understanding cognitive control in media multitaskers.

S2: That sounds like a debunking.

S1: Except the same paragraph carries the other half. However, complicating matters, studies adopting a different measurement approach than this first investigation have consistently produced results supporting the claim that media multitasking is negatively associated with everyday executive functioning.

S2: Both sentences are theirs.

S1: And they belong together. A meta-analysis that had found nothing would say so. This one says the picture is unclear and that one half of it consistently finds something.

The full lesson is at Foval Learning Institute dot org.
