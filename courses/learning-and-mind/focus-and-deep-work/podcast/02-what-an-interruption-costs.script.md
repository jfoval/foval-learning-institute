S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Lesson two of Focus and Deep Work. What an interruption actually costs.

S2: Everybody knows interruptions are expensive.

S1: And this lesson is about an experiment that measured the expense and found it somewhere nobody looks.

S2: Before that, you wanted to draw a line.

S1: Time Management lesson five already covers part of this subject, and it gives this experiment's sample, its design and its abstract. If you've met it there, you've met who was in it and what they concluded. What you haven't met is the condition-by-condition numbers, and that's where the teaching is.

S2: All right. The experiment.

S1: Mark, Gudith and Klocke, published at a computing conference in two thousand and eight. Forty-eight subjects, eighty-one percent of them German university students, mean age twenty-six.

S2: Doing what?

S1: Playing a human-resources manager who'd just come back from holiday, answering twelve emails from staff, with a fact sheet to answer from. They were instructed and given incentives, in the paper's words, to answer all emails in their inbox as quickly, correctly and politely as possible.

S2: And the conditions?

S1: Three, counterbalanced so everybody did all three. No interruption. Interruption on the same topic as the task. Interruption on a different topic. The supervisor interrupting them was the experimenter in another room, by telephone or message.

S2: What did they measure?

S1: Time to complete the task, with the time spent on interruptions subtracted. Errors, counted as spelling mistakes and typos. A politeness measure. And a workload index, on which people rated mental workload, stress, frustration, time pressure and effort, each on a twenty-point scale.

S2: So before you tell me. Interrupted people had to stop, answer a question and come back. What happened to the time and to the errors?

S1: Write down your prediction. This course expects most readers to predict the same two things, which is an expectation rather than a measurement, and one of the two is right.

S2: Errors the same, time longer.

S1: Errors the same. That part is the less surprising of the two: the emails were short, the fact sheet was in front of them, and there's no obvious reason an interruption would make somebody misspell a name.

S2: And the time?

S1: Interrupted tasks were finished faster. Not by a trivial amount, and not in one condition only.

S2: Say the numbers.

S1: Twenty-two point seven seven minutes with no interruption. Twenty point three one with the same-topic interruption. Twenty point six zero with the different-topic one. The uninterrupted condition was the slowest.

S2: In the authors' words?

S1: We found that context does not make a difference but surprisingly, people completed interrupted tasks in less time with no difference in quality.

S2: Context does not make a difference. That was their actual question.

S1: It was, and it's worth knowing, because the idea that same-topic interruptions are less disruptive circulates widely. Whether the interruption was about your task or about something else didn't change the time, and it didn't change any workload measure either.

S2: So what did it cost?

S1: The workload ratings, one to twenty. Mental workload ten point nought two at baseline, against ten point eight three and eleven point five. Stress six point nine two, against nine point four six and nine point one three. Frustration four point seven three, against six point six three and six point four eight. Time pressure eleven point nought two against twelve point six nine and twelve point one seven. Effort nine point five against eleven point nought four and eleven point five two.

S2: Every one higher.

S1: Every one higher in both interruption conditions, and all five differ at conventional levels of significance, four of them at the stricter one. The authors say it plainly in their discussion: after only twenty minutes of interrupted performance, people reported significantly higher stress, frustration, workload, effort and pressure.

S2: And the mechanism?

S1: Compensation, and it's theirs. People know they've lost time, they speed up, and the speeding up is what costs them. There's a second piece of evidence for it in the table and it's easy to miss. The emails were longest in the uninterrupted condition, at about thirty-one and a half words on average against twenty-nine and thirty.

S2: People wrote less.

S1: Which means the work wasn't identical, even though the error count and the politeness rating were. Two measures said nothing had changed, and a third said something had.

S2: Let me put the obvious objection. A manager reads this and concludes interruptions are fine. Work gets done faster, quality holds.

S1: Something is right about that, which is why it's worth doing carefully. On the two measures most workplaces actually use, output and error rate, this study found no damage and a small gain. If your whole account of the cost was that things take longer and go wrong, this experiment doesn't support you.

S2: And what's wrong with it?

S1: The inference from two measures to no cost. The study measured five more things and all five moved. Stress went from about seven to about nine and a third. Effort from nine and a half to about eleven and a third. A manager who sees the output and concludes the interruptions are free has drawn a conclusion the measurements don't support, because the cost landed in the column she isn't looking at.

S2: Is there anything the study can't tell her?

S1: A lot, and one thing in particular, which the lesson flags as its own reading rather than a finding. The session ran about ninety minutes. Nothing in it tells you what happens to somebody compensating like that for a week or a year, and the authors say in their discussion that they don't know whether people would cope over time or whether these measures would only increase.

S2: What's in the lesson that we haven't touched?

S1: A worked case of a team lead whose dashboard has exactly the two measures that didn't move, and what she may not be seeing; a personality finding worth a paragraph and not more; the error counts and the standard deviations from the paper's own tables; an exercise that has you keep the column a dashboard doesn't have, on two of your own work blocks; and a second exercise on finding the missing measure in something that gets measured about you. The full lesson is at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S2: Thanks for listening.
