S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Lesson three of Focus and Deep Work. The mind that is not on the task at all.

S2: Lesson two was somebody else taking your attention.

S1: This one is the times nobody did. And they're different problems, because only one of them has a tally. An interruption has a phone call attached to it, or a colleague, or a notification, and somebody standing behind you could count them.

S2: And a wandering mind?

S1: Leaves no trace at all. That's this course's own way of separating the two rather than a distinction the study draws, and it's why these are two lessons.

S2: So how do you measure a thing with no witness?

S1: You ask, at moments the person didn't choose. Killingsworth and Gilbert built a web application for the iPhone that contacted people at random during their waking hours and asked three things: what they were doing, how they felt, and one question about attention.

S2: What was the question?

S1: Are you thinking about something other than what you're currently doing? Four answers available: no; yes, something pleasant; yes, something neutral; yes, something unpleasant.

S2: That's the whole instrument.

S1: One question, four options, at a moment nobody chose. It's called experience sampling, and it exists because asking somebody at the end of the day how often their mind wandered gets you a judgement from memory rather than a count.

S2: Who were they?

S1: Samples from two thousand two hundred and fifty adults, fifty-nine percent male, seventy-four percent residing in the United States, mean age thirty-four. Volunteers who'd installed a happiness-tracking application on an iPhone, in twenty ten.

S2: And what did it find?

S1: Mind wandering occurred in forty-six point nine percent of the samples, and in at least thirty percent of the samples taken during every activity except making love.

S2: It's the second half of that sentence you want me to keep.

S1: Not that the average is high, but that there's a floor. Across almost everything these people were doing, at least three sampled moments in ten were spent somewhere else. Mind-wandering was not concentrated in the dull parts of the day.

S2: Does the paper hedge its own headline?

S1: It does, and this almost never travels with it. The frequency of mind wandering in our real-world sample was considerably higher than is typically seen in laboratory experiments.

S2: Which is a statement about instruments.

S1: A lab study interrupts somebody doing a task the experimenter chose, in a room with nothing else in it. This one asked people in the middle of their lives. The two produce different numbers, and the authors say so rather than leaving you to find out.

S2: They also asked how happy people were.

S1: And people were less happy when their minds were wandering than when they were not, at a slope of minus eight point seven nine, and this was true during all activities, including the least enjoyable.

S2: There's a second result there I want.

S1: And it's the one that makes the first interesting. Minds wandered to pleasant topics in forty-two and a half percent of samples, to neutral in thirty-one, to unpleasant in twenty-six and a half. People were no happier when thinking about pleasant topics than about their current activity. Neutral and unpleasant topics were both sharply worse.

S2: So it isn't simply that unhappy thoughts make you unhappy.

S1: Wandering to something pleasant didn't help. Which is what makes the authors' framing about the wandering rather than about its content.

S2: Now the sentence everybody quotes wrongly.

S1: Here it is in full. Although negative moods are known to cause mind wandering, time-lag analyses strongly suggested that mind wandering in our sample was generally the cause, and not merely the consequence, of unhappiness.

S2: Take it apart.

S1: Three things are doing work. Time-lag analyses means they used the order of the samples: does wandering at one moment predict lower mood at the next, more than the reverse? In observational data, that's the strongest move available.

S2: Second.

S1: Strongly suggested is the authors' own hedge, and it isn't decoration. A time-lag analysis doesn't rule out a third cause that moves first.

S2: And generally.

S1: Admits the other direction exists in the data.

S2: So what can you say?

S1: That in this sample, the order of events fits wandering leading unhappiness better than the reverse. What you can't say is that mind-wandering causes unhappiness, full stop, which is how it's almost always reported. The gap between those two sentences is the lesson.

S2: Let me put the obvious objection. Two thousand people who downloaded a happiness app tells you nothing about anybody else.

S1: They've identified the right problem and drawn the wrong conclusion. The problem is self-selection, not sample size: two thousand two hundred and fifty is large for this kind of work. What shapes it is who installs a happiness-tracking application in twenty ten.

S2: So what does that rule out?

S1: The sentence people spend forty-six point nine percent of their time thinking about something else. That sentence is everywhere and it isn't what the study supports. What survives is more interesting: the floor across activities would be remarkable in almost any sample, and the instrument itself, which is the thing worth taking away.

S2: What's the honest form?

S1: In a large self-selected sample of adults in twenty ten, minds were elsewhere in about half the moments sampled, and in at least three in ten during every activity but one.

S2: What's in the lesson that we haven't touched?

S1: A worked case of two moments that look identical from outside and are different data points only the person could report; the comparison of how much of people's happiness was explained by what they were thinking against what they were doing; an exercise that has you sample yourself for a day with the study's own question and then write down why your number and theirs aren't comparable; a second exercise on taking any causal claim apart in four questions; and three things people get wrong about the finding. The full lesson is at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S2: Thanks for listening.
