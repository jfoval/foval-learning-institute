S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're opening a new course, Focus and Deep Work, with its first lesson. What this course is for, and what attention means when somebody measures it.

S2: There is more advice about attention than about almost anything.

S1: And less measurement behind it than almost anywhere else you'll read. So this course starts somewhere unhelpful, with how anybody counts it, because the single most useful thing in the subject is knowing which instrument produced a claim.

S2: Instruments. Go on.

S1: Almost every finding about attention comes from one of two places. The first is a questionnaire. Somebody is asked how often they get distracted, how much media they use at once, how hard they find it to stay on a task.

S2: And the answer is a judgement they make about themselves.

S1: Usually over weeks or months of remembered experience. The second is a task. Somebody sits down and does something that produces a count: how many targets they missed, how often they responded to something they were told to ignore.

S2: Two routes to the same fact.

S1: They aren't. In the two thousand and twenty-one synthesis of the media-multitasking literature, the two halves give different answers about the same question. The association is small but holds where people reported on themselves, and it doesn't survive a standard correction where a task did the measuring.

S2: So one of them is wrong.

S1: That's the conclusion the lesson refuses. Try this first: a questionnaire asks a hundred people how distractible they are, and a task measures the same hundred. Why might the two disagree?

S2: The obvious one is that people are bad at judging themselves.

S1: That's the first of three and it isn't quite it. A questionnaire asks for a judgement and a task produces a count. One consults an impression built over months, the other counts what happened between two o'clock and half past. Those can come apart without anybody being wrong or dishonest.

S2: What's the second?

S1: They're about different stretches of time. The questionnaire is about a life. The task is about half an hour in a room with nothing else in it, which isn't a setting most distraction happens in.

S2: And the third?

S1: This is the course's own reading rather than a finding, and it's the one worth having. A person who believes their attention is poor will answer the questionnaire accordingly, and that belief is itself a real thing with real effects that the questionnaire is picking up.

S2: So the self-report isn't a failed attempt at the task's measurement.

S1: It's a different measurement, of how somebody experiences and describes their own attention. Which is why the course doesn't simply say trust the task.

S2: All right. What does the course promise?

S1: Not to make you more focused, and that's a statement about the state of the evidence rather than modesty. What the research can tell you is what's been measured: what an interruption costs, how often a mind is elsewhere, what happens to two famous claims about devices when the studies are pooled, and whether anything calling itself attention training transfers to anything.

S2: That's a lot, and it's not a method.

S1: There's one constructive section in the whole course, in lesson six, and it's built only from what survives lessons two to five. Where it reasons past the evidence it says so in the sentence.

S2: Now, the thing everybody wants to know. Has my attention got worse?

S1: The course can't tell you, and the reason isn't modesty either. No source it read measures the same people on the same instrument years apart. Some follow people for hours or weeks. None follows anybody across the stretch of time the question is about.

S2: Somebody's going to say that's precious. They know their attention is worse and they don't need a study.

S1: And half of that is right. Their impression is evidence of something, and the whole self-report half of this literature is built on exactly that kind of report. Nobody here is telling them they've imagined it.

S2: And the other half?

S1: A memory of how easy it was to read a book in twenty fifteen is a comparison between a current experience and a remembered one, and nobody has measured how well that comparison tracks. It's also confounded with everything else that changed: their job, their sleep, their age, how many people can now reach them.

S2: So the honest sentence is narrow.

S1: This course can't tell you whether your attention has declined, because nobody it read designed a study that could. Somebody could, and that's the shape of a real answer to a real question.

S2: Let me ask about the samples, because you flagged them.

S1: Four main sources. The interruption experiment is forty-eight people, eighty-one percent German university students, mean age twenty-six, in two thousand and eight. The mind-wandering study is two thousand two hundred and fifty adults, seventy-four percent in the United States, mean age thirty-four, sampled through an iPhone application in twenty ten. The two syntheses pool studies that are overwhelmingly student samples.

S2: Three of four are students or volunteers.

S1: And the fourth pools studies that mostly are. That's a real limit on everything the course tells you, and it's the ordinary state of this literature rather than a failure of these particular papers.

S2: Does that mean we should ignore them?

S1: That's the over-correction and it would throw away most of what's known. A student sample makes a finding narrower rather than empty. What it rules out is the sentence beginning "people are". So instead of "people spend half their time thinking about something else", the sentence the evidence supports is: in a large group of volunteers who chose to track their own happiness in twenty ten, minds were elsewhere in forty-six point nine percent of sampled moments.

S2: Duller.

S1: And defensible, which is the trade this whole course is about.
