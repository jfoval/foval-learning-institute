S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Lesson six of Focus and Deep Work. Can attention be trained, and what would that even mean?

S2: Three lessons have taken three famous claims apart.

S1: And this one asks the question all three were really about. Can you get better at this? It's also the only lesson in the course with something constructive in it, and the constructive part comes last and is smaller than you want.

S2: Start with the word that decides it.

S1: Transfer. You practise one task, and the question is what else improves. The literature separates three levels and the separation isn't a technicality, it's the whole argument.

S2: Level one.

S1: Near transfer. You get better at the thing you practised, or something almost identical. You do a number-sequence game every day and your score on that game rises.

S2: Two.

S1: Intermediate transfer. You get better at a different task that taxes the same system. Your score rises on a working-memory test you weren't practising.

S2: Three.

S1: Far transfer. You get better at something out in your life that the system is supposed to support. Reasoning. Reading comprehension. Arithmetic. That's the only level anybody is buying, and it's the level the advertising implies.

S2: Before the evidence. Imagine a programme that produces large near transfer, real intermediate transfer, and no far transfer at all. What would that mean?

S1: That the training works exactly as advertised on its own terms and does nothing you wanted. You get better at the game. You get somewhat better at similar tests. Your reading, your work and your reasoning are untouched.

S2: Is that a coherent thing for the world to be like?

S1: That's the part worth sitting with. There's no law that practising a component improves the system that contains it. Getting better at scales doesn't straightforwardly make you a better musician.

S2: And is it what the evidence shows?

S1: Melby-Lervåg, Redick and Hulme pooled the working-memory training literature in twenty sixteen. This course has read its abstract verbatim and not the body. Eighty-seven publications with a hundred and forty-five experimental comparisons.

S2: Give me the result in full.

S1: Immediately following training there were reliable improvements on measures of intermediate transfer, verbal and visuospatial working memory. For measures of far transfer, which they list as nonverbal ability, verbal ability, word decoding, reading comprehension and arithmetic, there was no convincing evidence of any reliable improvements when working memory training was compared with a treated control condition.

S2: Treated control. Unpack that, because it sounds technical.

S1: It carries the whole argument. A treated control is a group that does something else: another activity, another programme, anything taking similar time and attention. Comparing training against a group that did nothing measures the expectation of improvement, the attention from researchers, and the practice at being tested, all of which move scores on their own.

S2: So a do-nothing comparison can't separate them.

S1: Which is why the meta-analysis restricts its far-transfer verdict to studies with a treated control.

S2: What else did they check?

S1: The finding the lesson would keep if it could keep only one. Mediation analyses indicated that across studies, the degree of improvement on working memory measures was not related to the magnitude of far-transfer effects found.

S2: That's awkward.

S1: If training works by strengthening a system, and a stronger system carries improvement outwards, then the people who improved most on the trained measure should show the most far transfer. They didn't.

S2: And there's a blunt sentence.

S1: Unusually blunt for a meta-analysis. Analysis of publication bias shows that there is no evidential value from the studies of working memory training using treated controls. Their conclusion: these results seriously question the practical and theoretical importance of current computerized working memory programs as methods of training working memory skills.

S2: Right. The question everybody asks next. Meditation.

S1: And this course has to answer it carefully, because it hasn't read the evidence. Two syntheses from twenty twenty-one are worth naming, one by Yakobi and colleagues and one by Verhaeghen. Both report small effects, with significant results on some attention measures and not others.

S2: Give me the figures.

S1: No. And that's the point rather than an evasion. The research file says in terms that a course at this read level must not put a figure in a learner's hands, and a number remembered is a number quoted. What this course can tell you is the direction and the read level: reported effects are small, the syntheses exist, and somebody who wants an answer should read them rather than take this course's word.

S2: Somebody's going to call this defeatist. Three lessons said the famous claims don't hold up, and now the training doesn't work either.

S1: It's a fair question and the answer isn't consoling. The course is for reading this subject, and that's a skill with a use. A person who knows what happened to those three claims won't spend money on the next thing, won't conclude their attention is broken on the strength of a headline, and won't pass any of it on.

S2: Is it empty of things that hold?

S1: No. Lesson two's finding is real, measured, and inverts what people expect. Lesson three's measurement is real. Lessons four and five both found something and were careful about what. What the course lacks is a method, because the evidence doesn't support one.

S2: And the hour?

S1: Six elements, every one labelled. A context you meet reliably, which is evidence plus a step this course takes from it. Remove the interruptions rather than compensating for them, which is evidence plus a step. An hour, which is the course's own judgement. The same hour each day, judgement. One measure written down, judgement. And the phone, which is explicitly not supported.

S2: Two evidence, three judgement, one not supported.

S1: And a step away from the evidence in both of the first two. That's what an honest constructive section looks like in this subject, and a course offering you seven techniques has either read something this one hasn't or isn't telling you which is which.
