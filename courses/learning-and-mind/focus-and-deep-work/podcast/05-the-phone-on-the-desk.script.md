S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Lesson five of Focus and Deep Work. The phone on the desk.

S2: Smaller than lesson four, you said, and more personal.

S1: Because it's about a decision nearly everybody has made without evidence. Where should your phone be while you work?

S2: Start with the claim.

S1: In two thousand and seventeen, Ward, Duke, Gneezy and Bos published a study that gave the subject its phrase. People did working-memory tasks with their own phone on the desk, in a bag, or in another room. Those with the phone in another room performed best, even though nobody was using it. They called it brain drain: the mere presence of the phone consuming cognitive capacity.

S2: Have you read it?

S1: No, and the lesson says so. What this course holds at search-summary level is the shape of the claim and the name.

S2: What happened next?

S1: In twenty twenty-two, somebody ran it again properly. Ruiz Pardo and Minda published a preregistered direct replication of Ward's second experiment, crossing phone location with whether the phone was switched on, six conditions in all. The effect did not replicate: phone location made no difference.

S2: Also not opened by this course.

S1: Also not, and also said in the lesson. Which is where a pooled estimate earns its keep.

S2: Go on then.

S1: Böttger, Poschik and Zierer, twenty twenty-three. In their words: in a database search we identified twenty-two studies with a total of forty-three relevant effects that could be assigned to the categories memory, attention, and general cognitive performance.

S2: And overall?

S1: The pooled effect across all studies was g equals minus nought point one four, with a ninety-five percent confidence interval of minus nought point two four to minus nought point nought three. Heterogeneous, at an I-squared of about sixty-two.

S2: So something is there.

S1: The interval sits entirely below zero, the direction is the one the original claimed, and a course that stopped here would say the effect is real and small.

S2: But it doesn't stop.

S1: Because the authors ran two subgroup analyses and both matter more than the headline. The phrase is brain drain and the claim is about attention. Which of their three categories do you expect the effect to be in?

S2: Attention, presumably.

S1: That's where it's weakest. Memory carried it: minus nought point two three, with an interval from minus nought point three six to minus nought point one, entirely below zero. Attention did not: minus nought point nought seven, with an interval from minus nought point two one to nought point nought six, crossing zero. General cognitive performance came out slightly positive with an enormous interval.

S2: So the capacity the phrase names is the one with the weakest evidence.

S1: That isn't a refutation and it isn't nothing either. Memory is a real cognitive capacity. What it means is that the phone is ruining your attention is a sentence the pooled evidence doesn't support, and the phone is associated with slightly worse memory performance in these studies is one it does.

S2: And the second split?

S1: By region, because an earlier study had reported different effects for Asian and European participants. So it was a check on a prior claim rather than a pattern found in their own data. Asian samples: minus nought point three nine, significant. European: minus nought point two, not significant. North American: minus nought point nought three, with an interval straddling zero.

S2: And the original was run where?

S1: The United States. In the pooled North American samples the effect is about as close to nothing as a meta-analysis gets.

S2: What do you do with a split like that?

S1: You say what it is and not what it means. A subgroup analysis tells you that something differs between these literatures. It doesn't tell you what. At least four candidates are live and this course can't choose: the populations differ, the studies differ, publication practice differs, or chance.

S2: The authors give you anything?

S1: They report that the Asian subgroup was much the least heterogeneous of the three, which makes chance less comfortable than it would otherwise be, because the studies inside it are agreeing rather than scattering. It doesn't make it impossible, and they call for more research rather than offering a mechanism. Neither does this course.

S2: Now, you could read all that as a debunking.

S1: And the people who did the pooling don't. Their abstract ends: in view of the present research results, it seems important that people in general, and especially children and adolescents in schools and classrooms, learn how to deal with the distracting potential of smartphones.

S2: From a paper whose attention subgroup crossed zero.

S1: Both things are true at once, and their case is better than the intervals alone suggest. There's a real pooled effect in memory. And the measures in these studies are cognitive-capacity tasks, which isn't the same thing as what a phone does to a classroom: the distracting potential they're worried about is largely not what the pooled measures were testing. That second half is this course's reading of their discussion rather than a sentence they write.

S2: So where do I put my phone?

S1: Two halves. The first is what the evidence contributes, and it's very little. Minus nought point one four, in memory rather than attention, absent in the samples from the region where the original was done, with a preregistered direct replication that failed. Nobody should change where they keep their phone on the strength of that, in either direction.

S2: And the second half?

S1: The decision is still worth making. You know what you do when it's next to you, you know whether you pick it up, and you can run the comparison on yourself for a fortnight. None of that is evidence in the sense this course has been using the word, and it's exactly the right basis for a decision about your own desk.

S2: So the research can't tell me.

S1: The research can't tell you where to put your phone. You can, and the honest thing is to say which of the two you're relying on when you tell somebody else what to do with theirs.

S2: What's in the lesson that we haven't touched?

S1: What a subgroup analysis actually is, before the discipline of reading one; a constructed case of a sleep meta-analysis splitting by lab and home, worked through a four-step reasoning order; an exercise that has you run the phone comparison on yourself and then write down three reasons it proves nothing; a second exercise on finding a subgroup claim in the wild; and three things people get wrong about all of it. The full lesson is at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S2: Thanks for listening.
