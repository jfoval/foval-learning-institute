Seven lessons have given you two instruments and the difference between them, a measured cost of
interruption that lands where nobody looks, a measurement of how often the mind is elsewhere, two
pooled literatures on the things people blame, an answer about training that nobody wants, and a way
of reading the next claim you meet.

**This project runs the one constructive thing the course has on your own week.** It takes about six
hours across four weeks, and nearly all of that is the hour itself.

## The problem this project has, and how it solves it

**No section here can have a right answer**, because the answer's your week and nobody else has seen
it. A project asking you to reach a conclusion couldn't be marked, and one asking you to reflect
would produce nothing anybody could check.

**So every section asks for a prediction, then the record, then the gap between them.** That's
lesson 1's instrument question turned on your own life: a prediction is a self-report, a record is a
count, and the gap between them is the thing this course has been about from its first page.

**One consequence, said up front so it doesn't feel like a trick.** Four weeks in which both
predictions came out right is almost certainly four weeks whose predictions were written at the end.

**And a second, which matters more here than in any other project in this term.** **A learner whose
hour didn't work hasn't failed this project.** Nothing in this course predicts that any particular
arrangement will work for any particular person. Lesson 6 found no convincing far transfer from
training, lessons 4 and 5 found the popular claims thinner than they are sold, and a rubric that
scored only success would be teaching the opposite of all three.

## What you hand in

**One plain text or Markdown file, roughly 1,000 to 1,800 words, in four sections**, with the log
behind it. Plain text for the reason **Digital Literacy**'s project gives.

## Section 1: the design, written before week 1

Six lines, matching lesson 6's six elements, and **each one labelled evidence or judgement**. If you
write evidence, name the lesson.

1. **The context.** A place and a time you meet reliably, tested against both bars from **Habits and
   Self-Discipline** lesson 3: would you notice it tired and in a bad mood, and do you actually meet
   it nearly every day?
2. **What is removed**, and what isn't. Be specific about what you're not removing and why.
3. **The length**, and whose judgement that is.
4. **Whether it is the same hour each day**, and why.
5. **What you will record.** Two things: something counted, and something rated.
6. **The phone**, and on what basis you decided.

**Then two predictions, in numbers, written before the first session.** How many of the sixteen
sessions will happen. And what your average effort rating will be, on one to twenty.

**Do not edit section 1 after week 1 starts.** It is the only part of this project that cannot be
reconstructed afterwards, and it is the part everything else is measured against.

## Section 2: four weeks of the log

**Sixteen sessions, four a week.** For each one, three things and no more.

- **Did it happen?** A tick or a blank. **Don't tidy the blanks away**: they're data, and section 3
  is largely about them.
- **How many interruptions?** A tally, kept during the session. Count anything that took your
  attention away, including the ones that came from inside your own head.
- **One effort rating**, one to twenty, written at the end of the session rather than afterwards
  from memory. Lesson 1 is why that distinction matters.

## Section 3: reading it

Three questions, and the second is the one this course most wants you to answer.

1. **Predictions against record.** How many sessions happened against how many you predicted, and
   what your average effort rating was against what you predicted. Say by how much each was out and
   in which direction.
2. **The two measures, read separately.** Plot or tabulate the interruption tally and the effort
   rating side by side. **Do not average them together and do not treat one as explaining the
   other.** Lesson 2's whole finding is that a cost can sit in one measure while another shows
   nothing, and this section is where you look for that shape in your own week.
3. **The missed sessions.** For each blank, one line on what was happening. **A pattern in the
   blanks is a finding about your context**, which is element one, and it is the commonest thing
   this project turns up.

## Section 4: the decision, and its cost

1. **Keep, change, or stop**, and say which.
2. **What it costs.** Keeping costs the four weeks it takes to find out something you may already
   know. Changing costs the comparison you were building. Stopping costs whatever you wanted the
   hour for. **A decision with nothing on the other side of it hasn't been made.**
3. **What would have to be true for this to have been the wrong call?** One sentence, specific
   enough that you would recognise it if it happened.
4. **Which of your six labels turned out to be wrong.** Go back to section 1. **Anything you wrote
   as evidence that turned out to be judgement is the most valuable line in this project**, and it
   is the thing the whole course was for.

## Rubric

| Criterion | What full marks look like |
|---|---|
| The hour has a context, not an intention | Section 1 names a place and a time the learner reliably meets, tested against both bars |
| Every element is labelled | All six carry evidence or judgement, and the evidence ones name a lesson |
| Predictions are written first | Both are recorded before week 1, and at least one of them is wrong |
| The record is real | Missed sessions are present and marked, not tidied away or filled in later |
| Two measures, read separately | Section 3 does not collapse the interruption tally into the effort rating |
| The blanks are read | Section 3 says what was happening at each missed session, and whether there is a pattern |
| Scope is respected | No figure from the course is quoted without what it was measured on |
| The decision names its cost | Section 4's decision has something real on the other side of it |
| The labels are revisited | Section 4 says which of the six labels turned out to be wrong, or that none did and why |

**Not on this rubric: whether the hour worked.** Read it row by row and you'll see that a learner
whose four weeks produced nothing they wanted can score full marks on every line. **That's
deliberate**, and it is lessons 4, 5 and 6 applied to the assessment rather than only taught in it.
