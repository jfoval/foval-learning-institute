  # --- Instruments, and what attention means measured (L1) ---
  - q: >-
      What are the two instruments almost every finding about attention comes from?
    options:
      - A questionnaire asking about yourself, and a task producing a count
      - A laboratory task and a field observation by a trained researcher
      - A self-report and a physiological measure such as eye tracking
      - An interview with the person and a report from somebody who works with them
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      One asks for a judgement built from remembered experience; the other counts what happened in
      a session. Field observation exists and is much rarer than either. Physiological measures are
      real and are not what this course's sources used. And third-party reports are not a standard
      instrument in this literature.
  - q: >-
      A recruiter claims a screening test measures a candidate's ability to concentrate at work.
      What is the strongest objection this course supplies?
    options:
      - That concentration cannot be measured at all, so no test could do this
      - That the test was probably validated on students, which limits what it shows
      - That a test counts a session, and the claim is about months of working life
      - That self-reports are more accurate than tasks for a question of this kind
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The gap between what a task counts and what the claim covers is the instrument question, and
      it is the objection that does not depend on anything about this particular test. Concentration
      is measured throughout this course. The student-sample point is real and narrower. And neither
      instrument is simply more accurate than the other.
  - q: >-
      Why does this course refuse to say whether a reader's attention has got worse?
    options:
      - Because the evidence on devices is contested, so no direction at all can be claimed here
      - Because no source it read measures the same people on the same instrument years apart
      - Because self-reports of attention are unreliable and cannot be used for this
      - Because the question is about a capacity that psychology has not defined precisely
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      A claim about change needs a measurement at two times, and every source here is a single
      session or a snapshot of different people. The device evidence is contested and is a different
      question. Self-reports are one of the two instruments the course takes seriously. And the
      definition problem is real and is not what stops this particular question.

  # --- The cost of an interruption (L2) ---
  - q: >-
      What did the CHI 2008 experiment find about time and errors?
    options:
      - Interrupted tasks took longer, with no difference in the number of errors
      - Interrupted tasks took longer and contained significantly more errors
      - Interrupted tasks were finished faster but contained more errors as a result
      - Interrupted tasks were finished faster, and errors did not differ at all
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      About 20.3 and 20.6 minutes against 22.8 uninterrupted, with error counts that did not differ,
      is the result that makes the experiment worth teaching. Every other option is the folk model in
      one form or another, and the researchers themselves expected the first of them.
  - q: >-
      A school measures teaching quality by lessons delivered and complaints received. On this
      experiment, what would constant interruption of teachers do to those two numbers?
    options:
      - Leave both looking acceptable, with the cost landing where nothing is measured
      - Push complaints up, which is how interruption usually shows itself in practice
      - Push lessons delivered down, since interrupted work takes longer to complete
      - Push both the wrong way, which is why interruption is worth managing at all
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Output held and errors held in the study, while stress, frustration, time pressure and effort
      all rose, so a two-measure dashboard would show nothing. The other three options all assume
      the cost appears in output or in quality, which is what the experiment contradicts.
  - q: >-
      The experiment compared same-topic with different-topic interruptions. What did that
      comparison establish?
    options:
      - That same-topic interruptions cost less, which is the basis for batching work
      - That different-topic interruptions cost more, by a small but reliable margin
      - That context made no difference, which was the question the study set out to test
      - That the comparison could not be made, since topics could not be matched properly
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The authors say in the abstract that context does not make a difference, and it is worth
      knowing because the same-context-is-easier idea circulates widely. Two options assert a
      difference in one direction or the other. And the comparison was made, with counterbalanced
      conditions.

  # --- Mind-wandering and the causal claim (L3) ---
  - q: >-
      A man reads that minds wander in 46.9 percent of sampled moments and concludes that half of
      everybody's life is wasted. What has he got wrong?
    options:
      - The figure itself, which was closer to a third once the sampling was corrected for
      - Two things, since wandering is not waste and the sample was iPhone volunteers
      - Nothing, since the finding does support a claim about most people's days
      - The direction, since minds wandered less often than people expect them to
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Planning, remembering and anticipating all count as wandering by the study's own definition,
      and the sample chose to install a happiness application in 2010. The figure is right as
      published. And the study's own point is that the rate is higher than laboratory work suggests.
  - q: >-
      The study's authors wrote that time-lag analyses "strongly suggested" wandering was
      "generally" the cause of unhappiness. What do those two words do?
    options:
      - They indicate the finding was statistically significant but small in size
      - They mean the authors disagreed with each other about how to report it
      - They mark the claim as applying to most people but not to every individual
      - They hedge a claim about direction made in data that was not experimental
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      A time-lag analysis is the strongest move available in observational data and it does not rule
      out a third cause that moves first, which is what the hedging acknowledges. Significance was
      reported separately. Disagreement between authors is not what hedged language indicates. And
      the second word does concern generality, which is why the answer names both together.
  - q: >-
      Why is mind-wandering harder to study than interruption?
    options:
      - Because it leaves no external trace, so the only witness is the person
      - Because it happens far less often, so studies need much larger samples
      - Because it has no agreed definition, so no study can operationalise it
      - Because it only occurs during tasks people find boring or repetitive
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      An interruption has a caller or a notification attached and a third party could count them; a
      wandering mind is visible only from inside. It is more frequent, not less. It was
      operationalised by one question with four options. And it occurred in at least 30 percent of
      samples during every activity measured but one.

  # --- Media multitasking and the bias correction (L4) ---
  - q: >-
      What happened to the performance-based half of the media-multitasking literature when a
      publication-bias correction was applied?
    options:
      - It grew, since the imputed studies were on the side of larger effects
      - It stayed the same, since the funnel plot showed no asymmetry at all
      - It fell to z = .032, with a confidence interval that crosses zero
      - It reversed, showing multitaskers performing better than everybody else
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Egger's test indicated asymmetry for the performance-based assays, trim-and-fill imputed the
      missing studies, and the corrected pooled effect could no longer be distinguished from
      nothing. The self-report half passed the same test unchanged. And no reversal was reported.
  - q: >-
      A study finds that people who report heavy multitasking also report more distraction. Which
      half of that literature does it belong to, and what follows?
    options:
      - The performance half, so it is the more direct evidence of the two
      - The self-report half, where the association holds and survives a bias test
      - Neither, since reporting both variables makes it a different kind of study
      - Both, since reporting on yourself about a behaviour is a performance measure
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Two questionnaires make it a self-report study, and that is the half where the pooled
      association is consistent. A performance measure counts something the person does in a task.
      Studies reporting both variables by questionnaire are the bulk of the self-report half rather
      than a separate category.
  - q: >-
      What did the meta-analysts themselves say about the state of this question after a decade?
    options:
      - That the original finding was confirmed once the literature was properly pooled
      - That the association is now settled, with a small but reliable effect on control overall
      - That the question should be abandoned, since the measures are too inconsistent
      - That the picture is no more transparent, and they are no closer to understanding it
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      That is their closing paragraph, said alongside their observation that one measurement
      approach consistently supports an association with everyday executive functioning. Neither a
      confirmation nor a settlement. And they call for more research rather than for abandonment.

  # --- The phone, and reading a subgroup (L5) ---
  - q: >-
      The brain-drain effect is a claim about attention. Where did the pooled effect actually sit?
    options:
      - In memory, at g = −0.23, while attention's interval crossed zero
      - In attention, at g = −0.07, exactly where the phrase points
      - In general cognitive performance, at g = 0.10 across the pooled studies
      - Evenly across all three domains, which is why the overall effect is negative
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Memory is the only domain whose interval sits entirely below zero, and attention's runs from
      −0.21 to 0.06. General cognitive performance came out slightly positive with a very wide
      interval. And the three domains differ, which is why the subgroup analysis was reported.
  - q: >-
      A meta-analysis finds an effect significant in one region's samples and not in two others.
      What is the disciplined reading?
    options:
      - The effect is real in that region and absent in the others, as the figures show
      - The split is noise, since subgroup analyses generate spurious findings routinely
      - Something differs between those literatures, and the subgroup does not say what
      - The regions differ culturally, which is the explanation such splits usually have
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      A subgroup locates a difference without explaining it, and the candidates include the
      populations, the tasks, the publication practices and chance. Reading the location as the
      cause is the commonest error. Dismissing it entirely ignores that such splits are sometimes
      planned checks on a prior claim. And supplying a cultural mechanism is speculation.
  - q: >-
      Somebody asks whether to keep their phone in another room while working. What does this course
      let you tell them?
    options:
      - Yes, since the pooled effect across all 22 of the studies was significantly negative
      - Very little from the evidence, and the decision is still worth making on other grounds
      - No, since a preregistered replication of that original study found nothing at all here
      - That it depends on which region's research applies best to their situation
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      A small pooled effect, in memory rather than attention, null in one whole region's samples,
      with a failed direct replication, is thin ground for a recommendation either way. The first
      two answers each pick one result and ignore the rest. And region is a property of research
      samples rather than of a person.

  # --- Transfer and training (L6) ---
  - q: >-
      A company claims its daily brain game improves concentration at work. Its evidence is that
      users' scores on the game rise steadily. What level of transfer does that evidence support?
    options:
      - Far transfer, which is what an improvement in concentration at work would be
      - Intermediate transfer, since a game taxes the same system that work does
      - No transfer at all, since practice effects are not a kind of transfer
      - Near transfer only, which is improvement on the practised task itself
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Getting better at the thing you practised is the first rung, and the claim being sold is the
      third. Intermediate transfer would need a different task tapping the same system. And
      improvement on the trained task is a real result that simply does not reach the claim.
  - q: >-
      Why does comparing a training programme against a group that did nothing overstate its effect?
    options:
      - Because expectation, researcher attention and test practice all move scores
      - Because a do-nothing group is usually a good deal smaller and therefore noisier
      - Because people who do nothing get worse over the period of the study
      - Because a do-nothing group cannot be randomised in the same way
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      A treated control does something that takes similar time and attention, which is what isolates
      the training from everything that comes with being in a study. Group size and randomisation
      are separate issues. And there is no general finding that untrained groups decline.

  # --- Sorting a claim (L7) ---
  - q: >-
      "Modern life has cut the average attention span from twelve seconds to eight." Sort that
      claim and say what to do next.
    options:
      - Measurable, and the diary and sampling studies have already settled it
      - False, since the pooled evidence shows attention has not changed at all
      - A claim about change, so ask what was measured twice and on whom
      - Unmeasurable, since attention span is not a quantity anybody could count
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Change needs a measurement at two times on the same instrument, and that is the question the
      claim's form makes urgent. Nothing this course read measures change, so neither settling it nor
      calling it false is available. And attention can be operationalised, which is what makes the
      question worth asking rather than dismissing.
