## The first thing Stage 1 found, and it shapes the course

**This subject has a genuinely good empirical literature and a genuinely bad popular one, and the
gap between them is larger here than in Time Management.** That is the opposite of what the search
suggests: the first twenty results for almost any query are trade summaries, and they agree with
each other because they are copying each other rather than the papers.

Two things follow for the course. **The habit half is in better shape than a reader expects**, with
a measurement paradigm, a published methodology guide, and researchers who are open about what they
have not established. **The self-control half is in worse shape than a reader expects**, because the
account that made it famous did not survive a multi-lab replication, and the course has to say so
without overclaiming in the other direction.

## The measured results this course can stand on

### 1. What a habit is, in the terms researchers use

**Gardner, Lally and Wardle, "Making health habitual: the psychology of 'habit-formation' and
general practice", *British Journal of General Practice* 62(605), December 2012, pp. 664 to 666.**
**Read in full**, open access at PMC3505409. Written for GPs, three pages, and it is the single most
useful document found. Two of its three authors are authors of the study everybody quotes.

Verbatim, and this is the definition the course should use:

- **The definition, and it is not the everyday one.** "While often used as a synonym for frequent or
  customary behaviour in everyday parlance, within psychology, 'habits' are defined as actions that
  are triggered automatically in response to contextual cues that have been associated with their
  performance". Their two examples: "automatically washing hands (action) after using the toilet
  (contextual cue), or putting on a seatbelt (action) after getting into the car (contextual cue)".
- **The mechanism.** "Decades of psychological research consistently show that mere repetition of a
  simple action in a consistent context leads, through associative learning, to the action being
  activated upon subsequent exposure to those contextual cues". And then the sentence that makes
  habit worth the trouble: "Once initiation of the action is 'transferred' to external cues,
  dependence on conscious attention or motivational processes is reduced. Therefore habits are
  likely to persist even after conscious motivation or interest dissipates."
- **The three phases**, which give a lesson its spine: an "initiation phase" where the behaviour and
  its context are chosen, a "learning phase" of repetition in that context, and a "stability phase"
  "at which the habit has formed and its strength has plateaued, so that it persists over time with
  minimal effort or deliberation".
- **The advice, in one sentence.** "Habit-formation advice is ultimately simple — repeat an action
  consistently in the same context."
- **The patient-facing tool in Box 1** is a ten-week tick sheet with a weekly automaticity rating
  from 1 to 10. It tells the patient: "Be consistent: choose a time and place that you encounter
  every day of the week", and "within 10 weeks you should find you are doing it automatically
  without even having to think about it". **A course exercise can be modelled on this directly**,
  and it has the advantage of being a real instrument a health service hands to real patients.

### 2. How long a habit takes, and the figure everybody quotes

**Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts and Wardle, "How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the
real world", *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40(6), 2010, pp. 998 to 1009.** **Paywalled at
Wiley and not opened.** Everything below is at one remove, from the two sources in sections 1 and 3,
both of which have Lally as an author. **A lesson may report it at that level and must say so.**

At that level, established:

- **Design.** 96 volunteers each chose an eating, drinking or activity behaviour to perform daily in
  the same context, for 12 weeks, completing the Self-Report Habit Index each day and recording
  whether they did the behaviour. 82 provided enough data to analyse; curves were fitted for 62, of
  whom 39 showed a good fit.
- **The famous number, with the thing that is always dropped.** Automaticity rose along an
  asymptotic curve, plateauing after **an average of 66 days**, with individual times ranging from
  **18 days to a predicted 254**. Gardner, Lally and Wardle 2012 state it as "an asymptotic increase,
  with an initial acceleration that slowed to a plateau after an average of 66 days".
- **Complexity matters.** "Automaticity strength peaked more quickly for simple actions (for
  example, drinking water) than for more elaborate routines (for example, doing 50 sit-ups)."
- **The finding that matters most practically, and that almost nothing quotes**, verbatim from
  Gardner, Lally and Wardle 2012: "Missing the occasional opportunity to perform the behaviour did
  not seriously impair the habit formation process: automaticity gains soon resumed after one missed
  performance."

**Gardner, Rebar and Lally, "How does habit form? Guidelines for tracking real-world habit
formation", *Cogent Psychology* 9:1, 2022, article 2041277.** **Read in substantial part**, open
access, seventeen pages. This is the closest thing the subject has to a methods chapter, and it is
unusually candid. What it gives the course:

- **What habit does in daily life**, verbatim: "in the ebb and flow of daily life, fluctuations in
  motivation, attention or memory lead habits to tend to dominate over conscious intentions". And
  the two directions that cuts: "A runner, for example, may go for their morning run out of habit,
  despite poor weather having weakened their running intentions", but also, after the 2007 smoking
  ban in UK public houses, "many smokers reported finding themselves lighting a cigarette as an
  unintentional habitual response to drinking alcohol".
- **Why habit is proposed as the mechanism for maintenance**: "Initially successful behaviour change
  attempts often fail over the long-term, but theory proposes that a behaviour that becomes habitual
  will likely persist even when motivation erodes."
- **The shape of the growth curve is itself disputed**, which is a good lesson-level nuance. Lally
  et al. "portrayed habit formation using an exponential asymptotic growth curve, with habit
  strengthening immediately in response to initial repetitions, and slowing as a plateau was
  reached"; Fournier and colleagues in 2017 "depicted formation using a logistic function, with
  earliest repetitions expected to have negligible impact, followed by a period of pronounced, rapid
  growth, which decelerated as habit plateaued".
- **A standing warning this course should carry.** Their Criterion 2: "Habit formation tracking
  studies should not infer effects of repetition from measures of time." **Days are not repetitions**,
  because a day the behaviour did not happen is still a day. Every popular retelling of the 66 days
  makes exactly this error.
- **And they name the problem with the field's own study.** These studies are "resource-intensive,
  typically requiring multiple assessments, lengthy tracking periods, and complex statistical
  methods", and "There is, to our knowledge, no explicit guidance available on how to conduct such
  studies", which is why the paper exists. The guidance is "based on expert opinion".

### 3. How much of a day is habitual, and the figure that is usually quoted wrong

**Wood, Quinn and Kashy, "Habits in Everyday Life: Thought, Emotion, and Action", *Journal of
Personality and Social Psychology* 83(6), 2002, pp. 1281 to 1297.** **Read in part**: the abstract
and the results of the diary studies, from the copy on Wendy Wood's own USC page. Two hourly-diary
studies with student participants.

- **The abstract's finding**, verbatim: "When participants were engaged in habitual behavior, defined
  as behavior that had been performed almost daily in stable contexts, they were likely to think
  about issues unrelated to their behavior, presumably because they did not have to consciously guide
  their actions." And: "the self-regulatory benefits of habits were apparent in the lesser feelings
  of stress associated with habitual than nonhabitual behavior".
- **The 43 percent, and what the paper actually says around it.** Verbatim: "between a third and a
  half of all behaviors listed were classified as habits, given that they were performed just about
  every day and usually in the same location. This estimate was greater in Study 2 (43%) than in
  Study 1 (35%)". **The figure quoted everywhere is the higher of two**, and the authors explain the
  difference: the Study 2 procedure of listing multiple behaviours "would encourage participants to
  include more activities performed habitually".
- **This is the course's own worked provenance case**, and it is better than Time Management's
  because the number is real and the paper is honest. What travels is "43 percent of what you do is
  habit". What the paper says is between a third and a half, on students, by a definition the paper
  states, with two studies disagreeing for a reason the authors name.
- **Which behaviours were most habitual**: "attending to hygiene and appearance and sleeping and
  waking activities".

### 4. That a habit can beat what you actually want

**Neal, Wood, Wu and Kurlander, "The Pull of the Past: When Do Habits Persist Despite Conflict With
Motives?", *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* 37(11), 2011, pp. 1428 to 1437.** **Read in
part**: the abstract and Study 1's design and participants.

- The abstract, verbatim: "Habitual popcorn eaters at a cinema were minimally influenced by their
  hunger or how much they liked the food, and they ate equal amounts of stale and fresh popcorn."
- **The two conditions that broke it.** Participants "ate out of habit, regardless of freshness, only
  when currently in the context associated with past performance (i.e., a cinema; Study 1) and only
  when eating in a way that allowed" the habitual response. Study 2 disrupted it by having people
  eat with the non-dominant hand.
- **Scope, which a lesson must carry**: a campus cinema, 98 participants in the cinema condition and
  60 in a meeting room condition, paid $6, watching movie trailers. It is a field experiment on
  students, not a study of eating in general.
- **Why this is the best single demonstration in the subject.** It separates the two things people
  conflate. The popcorn eaters were not weak-willed; they were in a context, and the context was
  doing the work. Taken out of the cinema, the same people ate much less stale popcorn.

### 5. Self-control as a limited resource, and what happened to it

**This is the course's contested question and it has to be handled the way Using AI Effectively and
Time Management handled theirs: both sides at strength, in their own words, with the course
declining to declare a winner where the field has not.**

**The claim, and the meta-analysis that made it respectable.** Hagger, Wood, Stiff and Chatzisarantis
in 2010 meta-analysed 198 published experiments and reported the depletion effect as
"medium-to-large": **d = 0.62, 95% CI [0.57, 0.67]**. That figure is quoted verbatim from Carter and
colleagues' description of it, below; the 2010 paper itself was not opened.

**The re-analysis.** **Carter, Kofler, Forster and McCullough, "A Series of Meta-Analytic Tests of
the Depletion Effect: Self-Control Does Not Seem to Rely on a Limited Resource", *Journal of
Experimental Psychology: General* 144(3), 2015.** **Abstract read verbatim** from the authors' own
posted copy. Their conclusion, in their words: "We find very little evidence that the depletion
effect is a real phenomenon, at least when assessed with the methods most frequently used in the
laboratory. Our results strongly challenge the idea that self-control functions as if it relies on a
limited psychological or physical resource." **Note the hedge in the middle of that sentence**, which
is dropped everywhere it is repeated.

**The replication.** **Hagger, Chatzisarantis and 55 others, "A Multilab Preregistered Replication of
the Ego-Depletion Effect", *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 11(4), 2016, pp. 546 to 573.**
**Abstract and the limitations section read verbatim.** Note that Hagger and Chatzisarantis are the
proposing authors of the replication *and* two of the four authors of the 2010 meta-analysis it
undercuts, which is worth telling a reader.

- The result, verbatim: "Multiple laboratories (k = 23, total N = 2,141) conducted replications of a
  standardized ego-depletion protocol based on a sequential-task paradigm by Sripada et al.
  Meta-analysis of the studies revealed that the size of the ego-depletion effect was small with 95%
  confidence intervals (CIs) that encompassed zero (d = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.15]."
- **The protocol was vetted by Roy Baumeister**, the originator of the model being tested, which is
  recorded on the paper's own front matter. That single fact does more for a reader's trust in the
  result than any argument about it.
- **And the authors state their own limitations at length**, which the course must carry. Among them:
  the depletion version of the letter "e" task "did not include an initial period in which
  individuals familiarized themselves with the no-depletion version", which is supposed to induce the
  habitual response participants then override; and "It is possible that the letter 'e' task was
  sufficiently arduous but not of sufficient duration to deplete individuals' self-control
  resources." They judge the first unlikely to have been decisive and leave the second open.

**What replaced it, from its own proponents.** **Inzlicht and Schmeichel, "What Is Ego Depletion?
Toward a Mechanistic Revision of the Resource Model of Self-Control", *Perspectives on Psychological
Science* 7(5), 2012, pp. 450 to 463.** **Abstract read verbatim.** Their proposal: "exerting
self-control at Time 1 causes temporary shifts in both motivation and attention that undermine
self-control at Time 2". They are explicit about the cost of the change: "Though the process model
of depletion may sacrifice the elegance of the resource metaphor, it paints a more precise picture of
ego depletion". **This is the honest shape of the dispute**: not "willpower is a myth", but that the
resource metaphor was doing work the evidence did not support, and something less tidy is proposed
in its place.

**One more, at search-summary level only.** Watts, Duncan and Quan, "Revisiting the Marshmallow
Test", *Psychological Science*, 2018, a conceptual replication of the delay-of-gratification work.
Reported: the bivariate correlation was about half the size of the original and was reduced by about
two thirds once family background, early cognitive ability and the home environment were controlled
for, and most of the variation in adolescent achievement came from being able to wait at least 20
seconds. **Not opened**, and a lesson using it must say so.

### 6. How habit is measured, which is where the whole literature rests

**Verplanken and Orbell, "Reflections on Past Behavior: A Self-Report Index of Habit Strength",
*Journal of Applied Social Psychology* 33(6), 2003, pp. 1313 to 1330.** **Search-summary level only;
not opened.** A twelve-item self-report index built on three features of habit: a history of
repetition, automaticity (lack of control and awareness, efficiency), and expressing identity. It is
the instrument Lally's daily ratings used a subset of.

**Worth a paragraph in a lesson rather than a footnote**, because it is the weak joint in the whole
subject: habit strength is measured by asking people how automatic something feels. The researchers
know this and say so; the trade books do not mention it.

### 7. The twenty-one days, which was never a study

**Maxwell Maltz, *Psycho-Cybernetics*, 1960.** **Not read in the original; search-summary level
only.** Recorded here because the *provenance* is the teaching point rather than the content, which
is the same footing Time Management put Parkinson's essay on.

At that level, consistently reported across sources: the sentence is in the book's preface, and
reads that "It usually requires a minimum of about 21 days to effect any perceptible change in a
mental image", with the observation that after plastic surgery it takes about twenty-one days for
the average patient to get used to a new face.

- **It is an observation by a clinician, not a result.** No study produced it and none is cited.
- **It is about something else.** Adjusting to a changed appearance is not forming a habit.
- **"A minimum of about" is the part that fell off in transmission**, which turns a floor into a
  deadline. That is the mechanism Time Management's last lesson teaches, arriving in a second
  subject.

**A lesson may use this only at search-summary level and must say so in the body.** Lesson 4 does.

## What the field considers essential

This course's judgement, built from the above, in the order it builds:

1. **What a habit is**, in the psychological sense, which is not the everyday sense. A cue, a
   response, and an association built by repetition in a stable context. Without this the rest is
   self-help.
2. **What habit buys you**: independence from motivation, and cognitive efficiency. This is the
   answer to "why bother".
3. **How much of life is like this**, honestly stated, with the 43-percent figure traced.
4. **How long it takes**, with the 66 days given its range, its scope, and its behaviour-complexity
   moderator, and the twenty-one days traced to a 1960 self-help book.
5. **What happens after a missed day**, which is the highest-value fact in the subject and the least
   repeated.
6. **The cue**, treated properly: choosing one, testing it, and why an existing reliable event beats
   a resolution. This is where **Time Management lesson 4** hands over.
7. **Self-control**: what was claimed, what replicated, what did not, and what is proposed instead.
8. **What to do when it fails**, and what the evidence does and does not license you to conclude
   about yourself.

## Common misconceptions

Sourced where a source exists; marked as the course's own where not.

- **"It takes twenty-one days."** Traced to Maxwell Maltz's 1960 *Psycho-Cybernetics*, at
  search-summary level and **the book has not been opened**. The reported original is that "It
  usually requires a minimum of about 21 days to effect any perceptible change in a mental image",
  an observation about plastic-surgery patients adjusting to their faces, not a study of habits.
  **The words "a minimum of about" are what fell off in transmission**, which is the same shape as
  Time Management's Parkinson case and should be taught the same way.
- **"It takes sixty-six days."** Better, and still wrong as usually stated: 66 is an average with a
  range from 18 to 254, on 96 volunteers choosing their own simple behaviours, and simple actions
  plateaued sooner than elaborate ones.
- **"Miss a day and you start again."** Measured, and false: automaticity gains resumed after one
  missed performance. Course's own note: this is the single most useful thing in the subject and is
  almost never the thing people are told.
- **"Forty-three percent of what you do is habit."** Between a third and a half, on students, and the
  43 is the higher of two figures the same paper reports.
- **"Willpower is a muscle that runs out."** The model that says so did not survive a preregistered
  multi-lab replication whose protocol its originator vetted.
- **"So willpower is a myth."** Overshoots in the other direction, and the course must not let a
  reader leave believing it. The replication tested one paradigm; its authors list their own
  limitations; and a process account in terms of motivation and attention is proposed rather than
  nothing.
- **"Habits are about discipline."** The popcorn study is the counter-case: same people, same
  popcorn, different room, different behaviour.
- **"A habit is a behaviour you do often."** Frequency is not automaticity. This is the definitional
  error the whole popular literature is built on.

## Contested questions and the positions on each

1. **Does self-control behave like a depletable resource?** Standards 3.1 case 2, genuinely open,
   both positions held by working researchers, though the balance has moved a long way.
   *For:* the 2010 meta-analysis of 198 experiments, d = 0.62; the strength model's originators.
   *Against:* Carter and colleagues' bias-corrected re-analyses, 2015; the 23-lab preregistered
   replication, 2016, d = 0.04 with a CI spanning zero.
   *A third position:* Inzlicht and Schmeichel's process model, which keeps the phenomenon and
   rejects the metaphor.
   *What would settle it:* preregistered replications across several paradigms rather than one, with
   the task durations the proponents specify.
   **The course gives all three and does not declare a winner**, and it says plainly that the
   evidence has moved against the resource account.

2. **What shape is the habit growth curve?** Standards 3.1 case 2, and a smaller question.
   Exponential asymptotic (Lally) against logistic (Fournier and colleagues), which differ in whether
   the earliest repetitions do a lot or almost nothing. *What would settle it:* denser measurement
   over longer periods, which Gardner, Rebar and Lally call for.

3. **Is self-report a defensible way to measure automaticity?** Not contested in the sense of two
   camps arguing, but genuinely unresolved, and the course should name it rather than let a reader
   assume the figures come from observation.

4. **Should a person build habits at all, or decide differently?** Standards 3.1 case 3, **a value
   question**, and the one this course must describe rather than answer. Habit buys independence from
   motivation at the cost of doing something without deciding to each time, and there are people for
   whom that trade is wrong in either direction. **Time Management's lesson 6 handled the same shape**
   and this course should borrow it openly.

## Open concerns about scope

- **The participants are students, again.** Wood's diary studies are students; the popcorn study is a
  campus cinema; the depletion replications are undergraduates. Lally's 96 volunteers are the
  broadest sample here and are a self-selected convenience sample in London. **This must be named in
  lesson 1**, the way Time Management named its own, rather than discovered by a reader later.
- **Almost everything is Western and much of it is one research group.** Wood, Neal and Quinn account
  for a large share of the habit literature; Gardner, Lally and Wardle for another. That is not a
  criticism, and it does mean the field is smaller than the volume of writing about it suggests.
- **This course cannot avoid the trade books and has not read them.** Duhigg, Clear and Fogg are what
  a reader will have met. The honest middle is Time Management's: name them as widely read, say the
  course has not assessed them, and teach the reader to sort their claims. **If a later session opens
  one, Fogg is the one to open**, because his is the only one of the three written by a researcher
  about his own research programme, so it can be checked against papers.
- **The course must not become a productivity course.** Its own `course.yaml` promises what a habit
  is and what the evidence shows, and the pull towards "here are seven habits to build" will be
  constant. Time Management's decision 1 is the model: name the thing the course will not do, in
  lesson 1, and hold it.
- **One thing this course will be asked and cannot answer**: how to break a habit. The literature on
  breaking is thinner than the literature on forming, and what exists is mostly about context change.
  **Stage 2 should decide whether that is a lesson or a paragraph**, and either way the course has to
  say the evidence is thinner rather than improvise.
