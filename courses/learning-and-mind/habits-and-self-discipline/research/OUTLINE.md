# Outline: Habits and Self-Discipline

*Stage 2, 2026-09-19. Built from `research/SOURCES.md`. Sequence for how the ideas build, not how the
trade books list chapters.*

## Seven lessons, and why seven

The subject splits cleanly into two halves that most books run together, and the course is clearer
for keeping them apart. **Lessons 1 to 4 are about habit**, which has a definition, a measurement and
a decent empirical literature. **Lessons 5 and 6 are about self-control**, where the famous account
did not survive replication and the course has to teach what happened as well as what is known.
**Lesson 7 is what to do with both**, and it is where everything perishable lives.

Seven rather than eight, because this course has genuinely less to stand on than Time Management
did. Stretching it to eight would mean a lesson of technique with no evidence under it, which is what
the genre already provides.

## Six decisions this outline settles

Seven lessons written in different sessions have to agree. These are settled here and a drafter may
not re-open them.

**1. A habit is defined by automaticity, never by frequency.** The whole popular literature runs
these together and the course cannot. Lesson 1 gives the research definition, and every later lesson
holds to it: a behaviour you do often but decide about each time is not a habit in this course's
sense, and a drafter who lets "habit" drift back to "thing you do a lot" has broken the course.

**2. Every figure carries what it was measured on and when.** This is Time Management's decision 2
inherited, and this subject needs it at least as much, because three of its best-known numbers are
misreported in the same way. A figure with no scope is a defect.

**3. The course reports read levels in the body, not in footnotes alone.** `SOURCES.md` records that
the Lally study itself is paywalled and reported at one remove through two papers by its own author.
**Lessons must say so where the 66 days appears.** This is not modesty; it is the same standard the
course asks the reader to apply to everybody else.

**4. Nothing from the trade canon is cited for a claim.** Duhigg, Clear, Fogg, Maltz and
Baumeister-Tierney are on the `unread:` line and the build fails on a citation to any of them. They
are named in lesson 7 as widely read, and the reader is taught to sort their claims. If a future
session opens one, Fogg is the one, because his is the only one written by a researcher about his own
programme.

**5. The course describes the value question and declines to answer it.** Whether a person should
want their life run by habit at all is standards 3.1 case 3. Habit buys independence from motivation
at the price of doing things without deciding to each time, and there are readers for whom that trade
is wrong in either direction. **Copy Time Management lesson 6's handling**, which is the institute's
own worked example of this shape: describe the trade, give both costs, say the decision is the
reader's. **Lesson 1 names it and lesson 7 faces it.**

**6. The reader may have tried this before and failed.** Most people opening this course have run
several habit attempts that did not stick, and the genre's answer is that they lacked discipline.
**The course's answer is that the attempt is usually mis-specified**, and every lesson must be
readable by somebody who has failed at this repeatedly without implying it was a character problem.
Lesson 4 is built around exactly that reader.

## Lesson sequence

### 1. What a habit actually is (~70 min)

- **Objectives:** state the research definition of a habit and say what distinguishes it from a
  behaviour you simply repeat; identify, in a described case, whether a behaviour is habitual in that
  sense; say what this course promises and what it refuses to promise.
- **Core ideas:** the definition, verbatim from Gardner, Lally and Wardle: actions "triggered
  automatically in response to contextual cues that have been associated with their performance",
  with their two examples, handwashing after the toilet and the seatbelt after getting into the car.
  Then the mechanism in one sentence: repetition of a simple action in a consistent context leads,
  through associative learning, to the action being activated on exposure to the cue. Then **what
  that buys**, which is the reason to care: once initiation transfers to external cues, "dependence
  on conscious attention or motivational processes is reduced", so habits "are likely to persist even
  after conscious motivation or interest dissipates".
- **Mechanism to explain:** why frequency is not automaticity. Two people both go to the gym three
  times a week. One decides each time; one finds themselves changing at half past six without having
  decided. Same frequency, different thing, and only the second is a habit in the sense that makes
  any of the research apply.
- **Worked examples:** (a) *simple:* three described behaviours sorted against the definition, one
  clearly habitual, one frequent but deliberated, one automatic but not frequent enough to have been
  learned that way. (b) *with a wrinkle:* **a behaviour that is habitual and unwanted**, because the
  definition has no view on whether the habit is good. The smoking-ban case from Gardner, Rebar and
  Lally is in the research file and is exactly this: smokers "finding themselves lighting a cigarette
  as an unintentional habitual response to drinking alcohol".
- **Misconceptions:** "a habit is a behaviour you do often"; "habits are about discipline"; "a habit
  is something good you build" (the definition is neutral); and the course's promise, which is not to
  make the reader disciplined.
- **Practice:** *Sort five of your own.* Five behaviours you repeat, each marked habitual or
  deliberated by the definition, with the cue named where there is one. **Decision 5 requires a
  deliverable**: the count of how many of the five turned out to be habits, which for most readers is
  fewer than they expect.
- **Sources:** Gardner, Lally and Wardle 2012; Gardner, Rebar and Lally 2022; Wood, Quinn and Kashy
  2002 for the definitional care.
- **Connects:** leads to 2. Leans on **Time Management** lesson 4, which gave the reader if-then
  plans and said explicitly that a plan is a bridge from wanting to doing and does nothing about
  whether you want the thing. **This course starts one step further on.**
- **Names the gap in its own evidence**, per the standard set in Using AI Effectively and kept in
  Time Management: the participants in this literature are overwhelmingly students and
  self-selected volunteers, and one research group accounts for a large share of it.

### 2. How much of your day runs itself (~65 min)

- **Objectives:** state what the diary studies measured about how much behaviour is habitual, with
  the scope and both figures; explain why the number usually quoted is the higher of two; identify,
  in your own day, the behaviours that would meet the study's definition.
- **Core ideas:** Wood, Quinn and Kashy's two hourly-diary studies. The sentence the course teaches
  from is the paper's own: "between a third and a half of all behaviors listed were classified as
  habits, given that they were performed just about every day and usually in the same location", and
  then that the estimate "was greater in Study 2 (43%) than in Study 1 (35%)", with the authors' own
  explanation of why. Then the finding that gives habit its point: people engaged in habitual
  behaviour "were likely to think about issues unrelated to their behavior", and reported less stress.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why the definition drives the number. The 43 percent is not a fact about
  human nature; it is a count of behaviours meeting a stated criterion, in a diary, by students, and
  changing the criterion changes the number. **This is the course's first provenance case and it is
  deliberately early**, because the reader needs the habit before lesson 7 asks for it.
- **Worked examples:** (a) *simple:* the same behaviour counted under two different criteria, showing
  the count move. (b) *with a wrinkle:* **the figure as it appears in the wild**, stated as "43% of
  what you do is habit", set beside the sentence in the paper. The gap is the lesson.
- **Misconceptions:** "43 percent of what you do is habit"; "most of life is automatic"; "the studies
  observed people" (they are self-report diaries, which matters).
- **Practice:** *Count your own.* One waking day, every behaviour listed hourly, each marked against
  the study's criterion. Then the reader's own percentage, and one sentence on how the criterion
  changed what they counted.
- **Sources:** Wood, Quinn and Kashy 2002.
- **Connects:** builds on 1's definition; leads to 3. **Spaced review of** lesson 1's definition,
  applied rather than recited.

### 3. What the cue is doing (~70 min)

- **Objectives:** explain what the context does that motivation does not; predict, for a described
  case, whether a habitual behaviour will persist when the person's motive changes; choose a cue for
  a behaviour of your own and test it.
- **Core ideas:** the popcorn experiments. Habitual popcorn eaters at a cinema "were minimally
  influenced by their hunger or how much they liked the food, and they ate equal amounts of stale and
  fresh popcorn", and two things broke that: being outside the cinema, and eating with the
  non-dominant hand. **The second is the one to dwell on**, because it separates the cue from the
  person: nothing about their preferences changed, only whether the response could run off
  automatically.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why this is not a story about weakness. The same people, out of the
  cinema, ate much less stale popcorn. The context was doing the work, which means changing the
  context is a lever and trying harder is not.
- **Worked examples:** (a) *simple:* a cue chosen well and a cue chosen badly for the same behaviour,
  tested against the question Time Management lesson 4 already taught, which is whether you would
  notice it while tired. **This course adds what that course could not**: the cue has to be a context
  you meet reliably, and the research reason is that the association is built by repetition in it.
  (b) *with a wrinkle:* **a cue that works and a behaviour that still fails**, because the cue is
  only half of it.
- **Misconceptions:** "willpower beats context"; "if I want it enough the cue does not matter";
  "habits are triggered by feelings" (the research cue is a context, not a mood, and a mood is
  exactly the cue Time Management warned against).
- **Practice:** *Two cues, one behaviour.* Write the same behaviour against two candidate cues, test
  both against reliability and noticeability, and predict which will fire more often in the coming
  week. Then count.
- **Sources:** Neal, Wood, Wu and Kurlander 2011; Gardner, Lally and Wardle 2012 for the initiation
  phase.
- **Connects:** builds on 1 and 2; leads to 4, which is the timeline. **Leans explicitly on Time
  Management lesson 4** and says what it is adding.

### 4. How long it takes, and what happens when you miss (~75 min)

- **Objectives:** state what has been measured about how long habit formation takes, with its range,
  its scope and the read level this course holds it at; state what was measured about missing a day;
  trace the twenty-one-day claim to its source.
- **Core ideas:** the Lally study, reported honestly at one remove. 96 volunteers, self-chosen
  eating, drinking or activity behaviours, daily in the same context, 12 weeks, daily automaticity
  ratings. An asymptotic rise plateauing after **an average of 66 days**, ranging from **18 to a
  predicted 254**, with simple actions plateauing sooner than elaborate ones. **And the finding this
  course exists to deliver**: "Missing the occasional opportunity to perform the behaviour did not
  seriously impair the habit formation process: automaticity gains soon resumed after one missed
  performance."
- **Mechanism to explain:** why days are the wrong unit, which is Gardner, Rebar and Lally's own
  criterion: "Habit formation tracking studies should not infer effects of repetition from measures
  of time." A day you did not do it is still a day. **Every popular retelling of the 66 days makes
  this error**, and a reader who sees it once will see it everywhere.
- **Worked examples:** (a) *simple:* the curve described in words and in a table, so the reader can
  see that the early repetitions buy the most and the last ones buy least. (b) *with a wrinkle:*
  **two readers who both "did 66 days"**, one of whom repeated the behaviour 64 times and one 31
  times, and what the research does and does not let you say about them.
- **Misconceptions:** "twenty-one days" (**the worked provenance case: a 1960 self-help book, an
  observation about plastic-surgery patients, and the words "a minimum of about" dropped in
  transmission; the book has not been opened and the lesson says so**); "sixty-six days" as a rule
  rather than an average with a range; "miss a day and you start again"; "it gets easier every day"
  (the curve flattens).
- **Practice:** *The ten-week sheet.* Modelled openly on the tick sheet in the BJGP paper, which is a
  real instrument handed to real patients: one behaviour, one cue, a daily tick, and a weekly
  automaticity rating from 1 to 10. **The deliverable is the reader's own curve**, and decision 5's
  requirement is met by the numbers rather than by a reflection.
- **Sources:** Lally et al. 2010 at one remove; Gardner, Lally and Wardle 2012; Gardner, Rebar and
  Lally 2022; Maltz at search-summary level and flagged.
- **Connects:** builds on 3; leads to 5. **Spaced review of** lesson 2's provenance move, now on a
  number the reader is about to rely on.
- **Written for the reader decision 6 protects.** The missed-day finding is the reason this lesson
  exists in this position: a reader who has failed at this before has almost certainly been told, or
  has told themselves, that a break means starting over.

### 5. Self-control, and what happened to the famous account (~80 min)

- **Objectives:** state what the resource model of self-control claimed and what evidence supported
  it; state what the preregistered multi-lab replication found, with its numbers; explain why "so
  willpower is a myth" overshoots what was shown.
- **Core ideas:** the arc, told straight. The strength model and the sequential-task paradigm. The
  2010 meta-analysis of 198 experiments reporting d = 0.62, CI [0.57, 0.67]. Carter and colleagues'
  bias-corrected re-analyses in 2015, whose own conclusion carries a hedge in the middle of it:
  "very little evidence that the depletion effect is a real phenomenon, **at least when assessed with
  the methods most frequently used in the laboratory**". Then the 2016 registered replication: 23
  labs, N = 2,141, **d = 0.04 with a confidence interval spanning zero**, on a protocol vetted by
  Baumeister himself.
- **Mechanism to explain:** what a confidence interval spanning zero means and does not mean, which
  is where **Logic and Argument** lesson 6 is cashed in. It does not mean the effect is zero. It means
  this design, at this size, could not distinguish it from zero.
- **Worked examples:** (a) *simple:* the three figures set out in order with their designs, so the
  reader watches an effect shrink as the method tightens. (b) *with a wrinkle:* **the authors' own
  limitations**, at length and in their words, because a reader who only meets the headline is being
  handed the same kind of claim the course is teaching them to refuse. They name the missing
  habit-forming familiarisation period and the possibility that the task was not long enough.
- **Misconceptions:** "willpower is a muscle"; "willpower is a myth"; "the replication proved the
  effect does not exist"; "this means self-control does not matter" (the outcome correlations the
  original abstract lists are not what was tested).
- **Practice:** *Read the null result.* The reader is given the replication's abstract and its
  limitations section and asked three questions: what was tested, what was found, and what the
  authors themselves say their result cannot settle. **This is Reading Well lesson 8 applied to a
  paper that matters to them.**
- **Sources:** Hagger et al. 2016 (abstract and limitations, read verbatim); Carter et al. 2015
  (abstract); Hagger et al. 2010 at one remove.
- **Connects:** builds on the course's first half by contrast; leads to 6.

### 6. What is actually going on instead (~70 min)

- **Objectives:** state what the process account proposes in its proponents' terms; explain what it
  keeps and what it gives up compared with the resource account; apply it to a described case of
  self-control failing.
- **Core ideas:** Inzlicht and Schmeichel's proposal, verbatim: "exerting self-control at Time 1
  causes temporary shifts in both motivation and attention that undermine self-control at Time 2".
  And their own statement of the cost: "Though the process model of depletion may sacrifice the
  elegance of the resource metaphor, it paints a more precise picture". **The honest framing of the
  whole dispute is here**: not that willpower is a myth, but that a tidy metaphor was carrying weight
  the evidence did not support.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why a metaphor can be wrong and useful at once, and why "it feels like
  running out" is evidence about the feeling rather than about a resource. This is the same move
  lesson 2 made about the 43 percent.
- **Worked examples:** (a) *simple:* one described evening read twice, once in resource terms and
  once in motivation-and-attention terms, with what each predicts. (b) *with a wrinkle:* **a case
  where the two accounts predict different things**, which is what makes the disagreement a real one
  rather than a relabelling.
- **Misconceptions:** "it is just motivation, so try harder" (the process account is not a moral
  claim); "the science reversed itself, so nothing is known"; "self-control and habit are the same
  thing" (habit removes the need for the decision; self-control is what happens when a decision is
  needed).
- **Practice:** *One failure, two readings.* The reader takes one occasion their own self-control
  failed and writes it under both accounts, then says which fits better and what would distinguish
  them.
- **Sources:** Inzlicht and Schmeichel 2012; Watts, Duncan and Quan 2018 at search-summary level and
  flagged.
- **Connects:** builds on 5; leads to 7. **Spaced review of** lesson 5's distinction between an
  effect that is absent and an effect that could not be detected.

### 7. Reading a claim about your habits (~75 min)

- **Objectives:** sort a piece of habit advice into measurable, unmeasurable or false; trace a
  repeated figure in this subject to its source or establish that nobody can produce one; decide, for
  one behaviour of your own, whether to build a habit or to do something else.
- **Core ideas:** **this is the lesson that carries everything perishable**, by design, the way Using
  AI Effectively lesson 11 and Time Management lesson 8 do. Three habits, and the lesson says outright
  that the sorting move is borrowed from those courses rather than new here. Then the trade books,
  named as widely read and cited for nothing. Then the value question from decision 5, faced.
- **Mechanism to explain:** why this subject in particular produces numbers that come loose. The
  three best-known figures in it are all real measurements that were transmitted with their scope
  removed, which is a different failure from invention and is harder to see.
- **Worked examples:** (a) *simple:* four pieces of habit advice sorted, one of each kind plus one
  that is two claims stuck together. (b) *with a wrinkle:* **the three numbers side by side** — 21,
  66 and 43 — with what each actually is. The reader has met all three by now and traced one, so this
  is assembly rather than new work.
- **Misconceptions:** "somebody must have checked"; "the science says"; "an old study is out of date"
  (the same distinction Time Management lesson 8 draws, and true here for the same reason).
- **Practice:** *Trace one*, twenty minutes, any claim about habits with a number in it. Then: **the
  decision.** One behaviour the reader keeps meaning to do, with a written choice between building a
  habit, deciding to do it deliberately each time, and crossing it off, and what each costs. **That is
  Time Management lesson 6's form applied to this subject, and the lesson credits it.**
- **Sources:** everything, plus Wood, Quinn and Kashy for the 43 and Maltz at search-summary level
  for the 21.
- **Connects:** builds on the whole course; leads out of it. **Spaced review of** the whole course
  through the assembly.
- **Must not re-teach:** **Time Management** lesson 8 already teaches the three-way sort and the
  tracing method, and **Digital Literacy** lesson 8 and **Using AI Effectively** lesson 11 teach the
  sort in their own subjects. This lesson points at all three and takes none of them; what it adds is
  this subject's own three numbers.

## Assessments

### Course-end test

**Eighteen items.** At least thirteen must be application to material in no lesson: a described
person, a described behaviour, a described claim. **No item may turn on a figure whose scope is the
teaching point** (66, 43, 21) in a way that rewards recalling the number rather than the scope.

| Area | Items | Kind |
|---|---|---|
| What a habit is, automaticity against frequency (L1) | 3 | 1 recall of the definition, 2 application |
| How much of a day is habitual, and the 43 percent (L2) | 2 | both application, one on the provenance |
| The cue and the context (L3) | 3 | all application, one on a behaviour that persists against the person's motive |
| Timeline, the range, and the missed day (L4) | 4 | 1 recall with scope, 3 application |
| The resource model and its replication (L5) | 3 | 1 recall of the figures with scope, 2 application |
| The process account (L6) | 2 | both application |
| Reading a claim (L7) | 1 | the sorting item |

**Three items must be cross-lesson:** one pairing lesson 1's definition with lesson 5's self-control,
because the whole point is that they are different mechanisms; one pairing lesson 3's cue with lesson
4's missed day, because both are about what the association does and does not need; and one pairing
lesson 2's provenance with lesson 7's sorting, because they are the same move at two scales.

### Project: ten weeks, one behaviour

**Deliverable.** One plain text or Markdown file of roughly 1,000 to 1,800 words in four sections,
with the tick sheet behind it. Plain text for the reason **Digital Literacy**'s project gives.

**Budget about eight hours across ten weeks**, nearly all of it a few seconds a day.

**The design problem this solves.** No section can have a key, because the answer is the learner's own
behaviour. **So every section asks for a prediction, then the record, then the gap**, which is Using
AI Effectively's project solving the same problem and Time Management's after it.

**Steps.**

1. **The choice**, with the definition applied: the behaviour, the cue, why that cue, and a written
   prediction of how many days out of seventy it will happen and how many weeks until it feels
   automatic.
2. **Ten weeks of the sheet**, a daily tick and a weekly automaticity rating from 1 to 10.
3. **The curve**, drawn or tabulated from the weekly ratings, with the missed days marked, and one
   paragraph on what happened after each miss.
4. **The comparison and the decision**: prediction against record, and a written decision to
   continue, change the cue, or stop, with what each costs.

**Rubric.**

| Criterion | What full marks look like |
|---|---|
| The behaviour meets the definition | Section 1 names a cue, not a goal, and the cue is a context the learner meets reliably |
| Predictions are written first | Both predictions are recorded before week 1, and at least one is wrong |
| The record is real | Missed days are present and marked, not tidied away |
| The curve is read, not just drawn | Section 3 says where the gains were largest, which is early if the research holds |
| The missed days are the finding | Section 3 reports what happened after each miss, against what the learner expected |
| Dated and scoped | Every figure quoted from the course carries what it was measured on and when |
| The decision names its cost | Section 4's decision has something on the other side of it |

**A learner whose behaviour never became automatic in ten weeks has not failed the project.** The
range in the study reaches 254 days, and a project that scored only success would be teaching the
opposite of its own lesson 4. The rubric says so.

## Neutrality notes

This course carries `sensitive_domain: false`, and the call is worth stating. Standards 3.4's list
does not reach it: no politics, no religion, no recent event. Two things still need care.

- **Lesson 7's value question** is standards 3.1 case 3. Whether a person should want their life run
  by habit is not something the evidence decides, and the pull towards "more habits are better" is
  the direction this whole genre leans. **Time Management lesson 6's review failed it for giving one
  side all the worked cases and all the exercises**, and this course must not repeat that: if lesson
  7's exercise offers three endings, all three must be worked.
- **Lesson 5 is about a scientific dispute in which one side lost ground**, and the temptation is to
  write it as a debunking. The authors' own limitations are in the research file for exactly this
  reason, and lesson 5's second worked example is built on them. **A reader who finishes lesson 5
  believing willpower is a myth has been taught badly**, and the misconception list says so.

## What is deliberately left out

- **How to break a habit.** The literature on breaking is thinner than the literature on forming and
  is mostly about context change. The course says so in lesson 3, where context change is already on
  the table, and does not improvise a lesson out of it. **If a later session researches it properly,
  it is an eighth lesson.**
- **Addiction.** Out of scope, different literature, and a course that touched it lightly would do
  harm. Lesson 1 says so in a line.
- **Neuroscience.** The basal-ganglia account is real and this course has not read it, and a lesson
  of brain diagrams would be decoration. Named in lesson 1 as a thing the course is not doing.
- **Identity-based habit advice**, which is the current trade framing. There is a research strand
  behind it, in the Self-Report Habit Index's third component, and this course has not read enough to
  teach it. Lesson 7 names it as something it has not assessed.

## What downstream courses will need this one to have taught

**Focus and Deep Work** (T2, unwritten) will need the cue-and-context material and the honest state
of the self-control literature, and should not have to re-teach either. **Mental Fitness** (T2,
unwritten) will need lesson 5's arc as a worked case of a psychological finding that did not hold.
Neither exists yet, so this course assumes nothing from them.
