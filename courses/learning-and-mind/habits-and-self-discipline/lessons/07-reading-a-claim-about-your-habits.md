This is the last lesson, and it's the one that stays useful after the rest of the course has dated.

Everything before it was about habits. This one is about the claims you'll meet about them, and
you will meet a great many, because this is a subject with far more written about it than measured in
it.

Three habits of reading, and then the decision the course has been deferring since lesson 1.

## Habit one: sort the claim

Almost every piece of advice about habits is one of three things.

**Measurable.** It makes a claim a study could check. "Stacking a new behaviour onto an existing
routine makes it stick faster than putting it at a fixed time." Somebody could run that, and the
useful question is whether anybody has.

**Unmeasurable as stated.** It uses a term nobody has defined, so no study could settle it either
way. "Become the kind of person who does this." **That isn't the same as useless**, and it may be
good advice; it is simply not the kind of sentence evidence bears on until somebody says what the
words mean.

**False.** It makes a checkable claim that has been checked and didn't hold. "Miss a day and you
start again" is the clearest example this course can give you, and lesson 4 gave it.[1]

**This sort isn't new here and the course says so.** It is **Digital Literacy** lesson 8's move and
**Using AI Effectively** lesson 11's, and **Time Management** lesson 8 applied it to a different
subject. What is new is this subject's own failure mode, which is the next section.

## Habit two: trace the number, and expect the same failure

Lesson 2 traced one number and lesson 4 traced another. Put all three of this subject's travelling
numbers side by side and something becomes obvious that none of them shows alone.[1]

| The claim | What the source says | What fell off |
|---|---|---|
| 21 days to form a habit | "A minimum of about 21 days" to change a mental image, in a 1960 self-help book, about patients adjusting after surgery | "A minimum of about", and that it was not about habits |
| 66 days to form a habit | An average, with individuals from 18 days to a predicted 254, on 96 volunteers doing self-chosen simple behaviours | The range, the sample, and that simple actions plateaued sooner |
| 43% of what you do is habit | "Between a third and a half", 43% in one study and 35% in another, on students, by a stated criterion | The range, the criterion, and the second study |

**None of these was invented.**[2] That's the point of putting them together. Every one is a real
thing somebody measured or observed, reported honestly in its source, and every one arrives at you
with the part that made it meaningful stripped away.

**That's a harder failure to catch than invention**, and it is why lesson 2 came so early. A course
that taught you only to spot fabrications would leave you defenceless against all three of these.

:::predict Before reading on: of the three, which do you think is the most misleading as it travels, and why?
There is a case for each, and the one this course would argue for is the 66.[3]

**Twenty-one days is the most wrong and the least dangerous**, because it has no study behind it at
all, and once you know that you can discard it whole.

**Forty-three percent is misleading and doesn't change what you do.** Believing a third when it is
between a third and a half costs you nothing in practice.

**Sixty-six is the one that changes behaviour.** It sounds like a finding, because it is one. It
sounds precise, because it's a real average. And a reader who takes it as a rule will conclude at
week ten that they've failed, when the study they're relying on contains people who took eight
months. **A number that is true and stripped of its range is worse than a number that is simply
wrong**, because there's nothing about it to distrust.
:::

## Habit three: separate the advice from its reason

This one is particular to this subject, because half of it has just changed underneath.

Lesson 5 showed that the resource model of self-control lost most of the evidence that supported
it.[1] An enormous amount of practical advice was built on that model: decide important things in
the morning, do not change two things at once, do not go shopping hungry, save your discipline for
what matters.

**The reason has gone. The advice hasn't necessarily gone with it.**

"Don't start two habits at once" was argued from a willpower budget. It can also be argued from
something this course has established: habits are built by repeated pairings, attention is finite,
and two attempts at once halve the attention available for noticing either cue. **Same advice,
different support, and the second one survives lesson 5.**

**So a piece of advice and its stated reason are two separate things to check**, and finding the
reason unsupported does not settle the advice.[3] What it does tell you is that nobody has checked
the advice either, and that you're on your own with it, which is what the exercises are for.

## The decision this course has been deferring

Lesson 1 said there was a question the course wouldn't answer, and that lesson 7 would come back to
it. Here it is.

**Should you want your life run by habit?**

**The case for is most of this course.** A habit is behaviour that survives your worst weeks. It
costs nothing to run, it doesn't depend on your wanting it that day, and it frees attention for the
things that need deciding. A life with more of them spends less of itself on getting started.

**The case against is in the definition.** A habit is by construction something you do without
deciding to. The mechanism is indifferent to whether the thing is worth doing, it built your phone
habit exactly as it would build your reading habit, and a person can automate their way into a life
they never chose, one cue at a time. There is a smaller version of the same worry too: a life with
very few decisions left in it may be a life somebody stopped examining.

**This course has no standing to settle that**, and it is worth saying why rather than just saying
it. Nothing in the evidence this course read bears on the question. The studies measure how habits
form, not whether a person should form them, and a course that answered it would be handing you a
preference dressed as a finding.

**What it offers instead is the same in either direction.** Know which of your behaviours are
habitual and which aren't. Know what a cue is, and how long an association takes. And decide, rather
than default, which is the one thing both answers require.

:::checkpoint This course has spent seven lessons being careful about what the evidence supports. So apply that to the course. Which of the things you have been told here should you hold most loosely, and which most firmly?
Worth doing, and the ordering is not the obvious one.[3]

**Hold most firmly: the definition, and the distinction between automaticity and frequency.** A
definition is a convention rather than a finding, so it cannot be wrong in the way a figure can, and
this one is what makes the rest of the literature intelligible. Nothing that happens to the numbers
will touch it.

**Hold firmly: the cue.** Two field experiments with a mechanism, a clean disruption, and a result
that inverts what people expect. It is the best-evidenced practical thing in the course.

**Hold with its scope attached: the timeline.** The 66 days and the missed-day finding are the
strongest practical results here **and they come from a study this course has not opened**, reported
at one remove through two papers by its own author. That is a real limitation and lesson 4 says so
where the figures appear.

**Hold most loosely: what lesson 6 proposed.** The process account is the newest thing in the course
and the least established. Its authors call their paper a blueprint for future research and say
where evidence is lacking, and this course read its abstract and two passages. **It is in the course
because leaving a hole where the resource model used to be would have been worse**, not because it is
settled.

**And hold this lesson somewhere in the middle.** The three-number table is the course's own
assembly, and the argument that a true number stripped of its range is worse than a wrong one is
reasoning rather than a finding.
:::

## The books you will be handed

You will meet this subject mostly through four very widely read books: Duhigg's, Clear's, Fogg's, and
Baumeister and Tierney's. A course that never named them would be pretending you were not going to.

**This course hasn't opened any of them.**[4] That is on the `unread` line of its research file and
enforced by the build, and it means this section is not an assessment of them. No claim anywhere in
these seven lessons rests on any of the four.

**What to do when somebody hands you one** is the three habits above. Sort each claim. Trace each
figure, and expect the failure in the table rather than invention. And check whether the advice
survives its reason, because the fourth of those books argues from the model lesson 5 is about.

**One of them is worth naming separately**, on the same search-summary basis as everything else this
course knows about them. If a later reader opens one properly, the one to open is Fogg's, because
it is the only one of the four written by a researcher about his own research programme, which means
its claims can be checked against papers with his name on them. That is a note about where a reading
would go furthest rather than a recommendation.[4]

## Three things people believe about this literature

**"Somebody must have checked."** Three of this subject's best-known numbers reached you with their
conditions removed, and the checking took twenty minutes each. Nobody had done it.

**"The science says."** There is less science here than the phrase implies and more than there is in
most of the genre. The habit half has a measurement paradigm and researchers who publish their own
methodological doubts. The self-control half had a famous model that did not survive testing.
**Saying which half a claim comes from is most of reading this subject well.**

**"An old study is out of date."** Sometimes, and the distinction is the one **Time Management**
lesson 8 drew. Figures describing a time and a place go stale. A finding about how association works
is a claim about a mechanism, and 2002 being a long time ago is not in itself an argument against it.

## Practice

:::exercise Trace one
Take 20 minutes, and stop at twenty whether or not you've finished.

Find one claim about habits with a number in it. An app's onboarding, a book, a poster, an article.
Write it down word for word, with where you found it.

Then follow it back, writing down each step:

1. **What does this source cite?** If nothing, write "nothing", and you are finished.
2. **Open what it cites. Is the number there?** Not something like it. The number.
3. **If it is there, what is attached to it in the source that is not attached to it in the claim?**
   A range, a criterion, a sample, a qualifier. **This is the question the table above exists to
   prompt**, and it is the one most traces in this subject end on.

Write your finding in one sentence. **Five endings are all results**: the number is there with its
conditions; the number is there stripped of them; the trail ends in nothing; the trail leads
somewhere you cannot get to, which is what happened to this course with the Lally paper; or twenty
minutes ran out.
:::

:::exercise The decision
Take 30 minutes. This is the last thing the course asks, and it is the point of all of it.

Take one behaviour you have been meaning to take up and have not. Something real, that you have carried
for months or years.

**Three endings, and all three are legitimate.** Write whichever you choose in four lines.

**One: build the habit.** What is the cue, tested against both bars from lesson 3? What is the
smallest version of the behaviour? What will you record, and for how long? And what comes out of
your week to make room, because something must.

**Two: keep deciding each time.** Not everything should be automatic, and lesson 1's second worked
case was somebody whose monthly accounts are more reliable than most people's habits and are not
habitual at all. What makes this one worth deciding about each time? What will you put in place to
make the decision arrive, since it will not arrive by itself? And what does it cost you to keep
deciding?

**Three: stop carrying it.** Write down that you are not going to do it, what that costs, and what
would bring it back. **This is a real answer and the genre almost never offers it.** Carrying an
undecided intention has a price that is paid every week and buys nothing.

Then one line, whichever you chose: **who has to be told?**

**If you cannot choose, that is a result too.** Write down what you would need to know in order to choose,
and whether it is something you could find out in an afternoon.
:::
