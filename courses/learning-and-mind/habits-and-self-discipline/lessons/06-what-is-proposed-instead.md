Lesson 5 left a gap. The resource model lost most of the evidence that supported it, and something
still needs explaining, because things really do feel harder after a hard morning. That experience
is not in dispute and it's what the model was invented for.

This lesson is the leading proposal for what is going on instead. It is a proposal rather than a
finding, its authors say so, and being clear about that difference is half of what this lesson
teaches.

## The proposal

Inzlicht and Schmeichel, writing in 2012, propose what they call a **process model**. Their
statement of it's one sentence: exerting self-control at Time 1 "causes temporary shifts in both
motivation and attention that undermine self-control at Time 2".[1]

**Two shifts, and they describe each one.**

**The first is a shift in motivation.** In their words, it is "a shift in motivational orientation
away from suppressing and inhibiting desires and toward approaching and gratifying them". Unpacked,
and also in their words: "initial acts of control lead people to become less motivated to engage in
further deliberative control and more motivated to engage in things that are more personally
rewarding, interesting, and enjoyable".[1]

**The second is a shift in attention**, and it runs alongside the first: "a shift in attention away
from cues signaling the need to exert control and toward cues signaling gratification".[1]

**And the two feed each other.** The model "assumes that motivation and attention each affect the
other".[1]

### What that means on a Tuesday afternoon

Strip the vocabulary off and the proposal is this. After a morning of holding yourself to something,
two things change, and neither of them is that you have less of a substance.

**What you want changes.** Not what you want in general, and not what you'd say if asked about your
goals. What is appealing right now moves towards the rewarding and away from the effortful.

**And what you notice changes.** The things that would have prompted you to exercise control, the
signs that you are off track, become less salient, and the things that promise a reward become more
so.

**That second one's the part the resource metaphor has no room for**, and it is worth dwelling on,
because it is a different kind of failure. On the resource account you see the biscuit, you want to
resist, and you can't. On this account you may simply not register that resisting was on the table.

## What it keeps and what it gives up

This is where the lesson earns its place, because the authors are unusually direct about the trade.

**What it keeps is the phenomenon.** They are not saying nothing happens between Time 1 and Time 2.
The whole paper is an attempt to say what.

**What it gives up is the metaphor**, and they say so themselves: "Though the process model of
depletion may sacrifice the elegance of the resource metaphor, it paints a more precise picture of
ego depletion and suggests several nuanced predictions for future research."[1]

**Read "elegance" as a real loss rather than as false modesty.** A limited resource is a wonderful
idea: everybody's met a battery, the metaphor explains itself in four words, and it generates
obvious advice. Two shifts that feed each other explains itself in nobody's four words and generates
advice that is harder to state.

**What they claim in exchange is precision, and precision means testability.** A metaphor can absorb
almost any result you give it. A claim about motivation shifting predicts specific things, including
things the resource account cannot accommodate, which the next section is about.

:::predict Here is the test of whether the two accounts genuinely differ or whether one is a relabelling of the other. Imagine an experiment: people do a demanding first task, and then, before the second task, half of them are offered money for doing well on it. What does each account predict?
**The resource account has a problem here.** If the first task spent a store, then an offer of money
before the second task doesn't refill it. Whatever capacity was used is still used. You might
predict people try harder and still do worse, but you cannot easily predict the effect going away.

**The process account predicts it can go away.** If what happened was a shift in motivation towards
the rewarding, then supplying a reward acts directly on the thing that shifted. The account is about
what you want, and an incentive is about what you want.

**So the two are not a relabelling**, and that's the point of the exercise. They differ about what
changed, which means they differ about what would change it back.

**One caution, and it matters here.** This is the shape of a distinguishing test rather than a report of
one. The reasoning is this course's own, from the two accounts as stated,[3] and where the evidence
stands is the subject of the section after next.
:::

### Worked: one evening, read twice

**Nadia is constructed.**[3] She spends her working day on a task that requires constant care, gets
home, intends to spend an hour on her Open University course, and spends it on her phone instead.

**On the resource account.** The day's careful work drew down a limited store of self-control. By
the evening there was not enough left to override the easier option. The prediction that follows is
about quantity: a lighter day should leave more, a rest before the evening should help, and there is
a finite budget to be spent wisely.

**On the process account.** Two things happened. Her motivation shifted away from deliberative
control and towards what is rewarding, so the course became less appealing relative to the phone
than it would have been at nine in the morning. And her attention shifted: the reminders that she is
behind on the course were less salient, the phone more so. **She may not have experienced a struggle
at all**, which is worth noticing, which is a specific prediction, and on the resource account is
harder to explain.

**Notice which account describes what she would report.** Most people in Nadia's position do not
describe a fight they lost. They describe the evening going, which is closer to the second
description than the first.

**That's a point in the process account's favour and it is not evidence**, which is the distinction
this course keeps making. What people report is a thing to explain, and not a thing that settles an
explanation.

## Where the evidence actually stands

Be careful here, because the temptation is to treat the new account as established simply because
the old one is not.

**The process account is a proposal.** Its authors describe the paper as highlighting "evidence in
support of this model" but also "where evidence is lacking, thus providing a blueprint for future
research".[1] **A blueprint for future research is not a result**, and they chose that phrase.

**And this course hasn't read much of it.**[1] The argument summarised above is from that abstract
and from the two passages stating the shifts; the rest of a fourteen-page paper has not been opened.
That is a thin read level for a lesson built on it, and a reader should weight the lesson
accordingly.

**So the honest position is the one the quiz ends on.** The phenomenon is better attested than
either explanation of it. Things get harder after a hard morning, and that isn't in doubt. Why, is.

:::checkpoint Somebody objects that the process account is just saying people stopped wanting to try, dressed up in vocabulary. It sounds to him like a judgement about character wearing a lab coat.
It is a fair objection, it's the commonest one, and answering it properly needs two moves.

**The first is that the shift is described as something that happens, not something chosen.** The
proposal is that exerting control *causes* the shift. Nobody in the model decides to become less
motivated, any more than somebody decides a biscuit looks appealing. **Motivation in this sense
is a state that moves, not an effort a person elects to make**, and the whole force of the objection
comes from reading the word the second way.

**The second's the attention half, which the objection leaves out entirely.** "You stopped wanting
to try" is a description of motivation alone. The account pairs it with a shift in what you notice,
and that half is not a story about wanting at all. It predicts the cue to exercise control becomes
less salient, which is a claim about perception.

**And there is a reason to hold this carefully rather than to dismiss it.** The objection has real
force against a sloppy version of the account, the version that says "you just did not want it
enough". If you meet the process model stated that way, in a book or an article, **the objection is correct against that version**, and the authors' own statement is not it.
:::

## Four things people get wrong here

**"The resource account is dead, so the process account is right."** These are two separate
questions. One model losing evidence does not confirm another, and the process account's own authors
describe theirs as a blueprint.

**"It is just motivation, so try harder."** The account describes a shift that happens rather than
an effort withheld, and "try harder" is advice aimed at the thing the account says has moved.

**"The science reversed itself, so nothing is known."** Lesson 5 answered this and it is worth
repeating: what is known is that things get harder after exertion. What is contested is why.

**"Self-control and habit are the same thing."** They're nearly opposites in this course's
structure. **A habit removes the need for the decision.** Self-control is what happens when a
decision is required, and the whole reason lessons 1 to 4 came first is that the most reliable way
to need less self-control is to need fewer decisions.

## Practice

:::exercise One failure, two readings
Take 25 minutes.

Take one occasion in the last fortnight when your own self-control failed. Something small and real:
the evening that went, the thing you said, the purchase.

**Write it twice.**

1. **In resource terms.** What had you spent the day doing? What was the store you were drawing on,
   and what would have refilled it? Be specific about what the account would predict.
2. **In process terms.** What did you want at the moment it happened, as opposed to what you wanted
   that morning? And what were you *not* noticing? The second question is harder and is the one
   worth the time.

Then two more lines.

3. **Which reading fits what you actually remember?** Not which one sounds better. Whether you remember a
   struggle you lost or an evening that went.
4. **What would have distinguished them at the time?** If somebody had offered you twenty pounds to
   do the thing, would you have done it? **You cannot run that experiment retrospectively**, and
   answering honestly is still informative.
:::

:::exercise Spot the sloppy version
Take 20 minutes.

Now that you have both accounts, go and find one piece of writing about willpower and work out which
it is using, if either.

Write down the passage, then answer three questions.

1. **Which account is it using?** Resource, process, something else, or a mixture that does not
   notice it is one.
2. **If it uses the process account, is it the authors' version or the sloppy one?** The tell is the
   attention half. A version that talks only about wanting has dropped half the proposal and is
   fairly open to the objection in the checkpoint above.
3. **Does the advice depend on the account being true?** As in lesson 5's exercise, often it doesn't, and a piece of advice can be worth taking with its stated reason wrong.
:::
