S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. The last lesson of Habits and Self-Discipline. Reading a claim about your habits.

S2: And this is the one that outlives the rest.

S1: Everything before it was about habits. This one is about the claims you'll meet about them, and you'll meet a great many, because this is a subject with far more written about it than measured in it.

S2: Three habits of reading, you said.

S1: Sort the claim. Trace the number. And separate the advice from its reason.

S2: Start with sorting.

S1: Almost every piece of advice about habits is one of three things. Measurable, meaning a study could check it. Unmeasurable as stated, meaning it uses a term nobody has defined. Or false, meaning it's been checked and it didn't hold.

S2: Work one for me.

S1: Stacking a new behaviour onto an existing routine makes it stick faster than putting it at a fixed time. Something to count? Yes, how automatic the behaviour feels, which is how this literature measures sticking. Two conditions to compare? Yes. So it's measurable, and the useful question isn't whether it sounds right but whether anybody has run it. Nothing this course read has.

S2: And an unmeasurable one.

S1: Become the kind of person who does this. What's to be counted? A kind of person. By what criterion? None is given, and there's no way of telling somebody who's become that kind of person from somebody who merely does the behaviour every day. Without that, there's no result the advice could fail.

S2: Which isn't the same as useless.

S1: It may be perfectly good advice. It just isn't the kind of sentence evidence bears on until somebody says what the words mean.

S2: And false.

S1: Miss a day and you start again. What would make it true? That automaticity ratings after a missed day resume from lower than where they were. What was found? That gains soon resumed after one missed performance. So it's not merely unsupported: it's contradicted by the one study this course has read on the point, which is the strongest thing you can say about a piece of advice and the rarest.

S2: You said three kinds and then gave me a fourth.

S1: The commonest of all: two claims stuck together. Do it first thing in the morning, because that's when your willpower is highest. One claim about when to act, one about why. The second is the model lesson five is about. The first would survive somebody's morning being reliably quiet whatever willpower turns out to be.

S2: Right. Habit two. Tracing.

S1: And this subject has its own failure mode, which you only see when you put all three of its travelling numbers side by side.

S2: Go.

S1: Twenty-one days to form a habit. What the source says is a minimum of about twenty-one days to change a mental image, in a nineteen sixty self-help book, about patients adjusting after surgery. What fell off is the phrase a minimum of about, and that it wasn't about habits at all.

S2: Second.

S1: Sixty-six days. What the source says is an average, with individuals from eighteen days to a predicted two hundred and fifty-four, on ninety-six volunteers doing self-chosen eating, drinking or activity behaviours. What fell off is the range, the sample, and that simple actions plateaued sooner. And this course hasn't opened that study: every figure from it is reported at one remove, through two papers by its own author.

S2: Third.

S1: Forty-three percent of what you do is habit. What the source says is between a third and a half, forty-three percent in one study and thirty-five in another, on students, by a stated criterion. What fell off is the range, the criterion, and the second study.

S2: None of those was invented.

S1: That's the point of putting them together. Every one is a real thing somebody measured or observed, reported honestly in its source, and every one arrives at you with the part that made it meaningful stripped away.

S2: Which is harder to catch than invention.

S1: Much harder, and it's why the course traced its first number in lesson two. A reader taught only to spot fabrications is defenceless against all three.

S2: Which of the three would you say is the most misleading?

S1: There's a case for each, and the one this course would argue for is the sixty-six. Twenty-one is the most wrong and the least dangerous, because once you know there's no study behind it you can discard it whole. Forty-three misleads and changes nothing you do.

S2: And sixty-six?

S1: Sounds like a finding, because it is one. Sounds precise, because it's a real average. And a reader who takes it as a rule will conclude at week ten that they've failed, when the study they're relying on fitted curves running out to eight months. A number that's true and stripped of its range is worse than a number that's simply wrong, because there's nothing about it to distrust.

S2: Habit three.

S1: Separate the advice from its reason, and this one is particular to this subject because half of it changed underneath. A lot of practical advice was built on the resource model: decide important things in the morning, don't go shopping hungry.

S2: The reason has gone.

S1: The advice hasn't necessarily gone with it. Don't start two habits at once was argued from a willpower budget. It can also be argued from something the course did establish: what builds a habit is repeated pairings, and two behaviours started at once split whatever attention you have for noticing either cue, so each gets fewer usable repetitions.

S2: Same advice, different support.

S1: And the second one survives lesson five. So finding the reason unsupported doesn't settle the advice. What it tells you is that nobody has checked the advice either.

S2: Now the question you've been putting off since lesson one. Should I want my life run by habit?

S1: The case for is most of the course. A habit is behaviour that survives your worst weeks. It costs nothing to run, it doesn't depend on your wanting it that day, and it frees attention for the things that need deciding. A life with more of them spends less of itself on getting started.

S2: And against?

S1: It's in the definition. A habit is by construction something you do without deciding to. The mechanism is indifferent to whether the thing is worth doing, it built your phone habit exactly as it would build your reading habit, and a person can automate their way into a life they never chose, one cue at a time.

S2: Where does the course come down?

S1: It doesn't, and it's worth saying why rather than just saying it. Nothing in the evidence this course read bears on the question. The studies measure how habits form, not whether a person should form them, and a course that answered it would be handing you a preference dressed as a finding.

S2: What's in the lesson that we haven't touched?

S1: A checkpoint that turns the course's method on the course itself and says which of its own claims to hold firmly and which loosely; what to do when somebody hands you one of the four trade books, none of which this course has opened; an exercise that has you trace one claim in twenty minutes, with five endings that all count as results; and a last exercise that asks you to take one thing you've been meaning to do and decide, with all three endings worked, including stopping. The full lesson is at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S2: Thanks for listening. That's the course.
