S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Lesson five of Habits and Self-Discipline. Self-control, and what happened to the famous account.

S2: The course changes subject here.

S1: Lessons one to four were about habit, which is the case where a behaviour has stopped needing a decision. This lesson and the next are about what happens when a decision is needed after all, which is most of life, and which is what people call willpower.

S2: Start with what was claimed.

S1: On the resource or strength model, self-control is a limited resource that gets depleted by a period of exertion, and self-control failure is what happens when it runs low. That's this course's wording rather than the paper's, because the research file doesn't hold the sentence verbatim.

S2: Use it and there's less of it.

S1: Resist a biscuit this morning and you have less left for the argument this afternoon.

S2: How was it tested?

S1: In what's called the sequential-task paradigm. People do one task requiring self-control, then a second unrelated one, and the prediction is that the first makes the second worse. The state that's supposed to produce has a name: ego depletion.

S2: And this wasn't fringe.

S1: A two thousand and ten meta-analysis of a hundred and ninety-eight published experiments reported the effect as medium-to-large, at d equals nought point six two, with a confidence interval from nought point five seven to nought point six seven. That paper wasn't opened by this course either; the figure comes at one remove.

S2: That's a lot of experiments.

S1: And here's a fact worth having before the rest: two of that meta-analysis's four authors are the proposing authors of the replication we're about to discuss. The people who assembled the best case for the effect are the people who then ran the test it failed.

S2: All right, hold one thing steady for me first.

S1: Say it.

S2: What's under test is the mechanism, not whether self-control matters.

S1: Exactly that, and the replication's own abstract opens by granting the point: good self-control has been linked to better health, to more cohesive relationships, to success at work and school, and to less susceptibility to crime and addiction. That summary is this course's, not a quotation. None of it was ever in question.

S2: So what happened to the model? You said three steps.

S1: Each one tightened the method. First, somebody asked what publication bias would do to that meta-analysis. Small studies tend to report larger effects, and the usual explanation, which this course is giving you as the standard account rather than as something it verified, is that a small study finding nothing is less likely to be written up and accepted. Carter, Kofler, Forster and McCullough ran a series of meta-analyses correcting for exactly that.

S2: And found?

S1: Read the middle of their sentence. We find very little evidence that the depletion effect is a real phenomenon, at least when assessed with the methods most frequently used in the laboratory.

S2: At least when assessed with those methods.

S1: Which is a real hedge, from the authors, and it never travels with the finding.

S2: Second step.

S1: Twenty-three laboratories ran one preregistered protocol on two thousand one hundred and forty-one participants. Meta-analysis of the studies revealed that the size of the ego-depletion effect was small, with ninety-five percent confidence intervals that encompassed zero: d equals nought point nought four, interval from minus nought point nought seven to nought point one five.

S2: And the detail that does most of the work.

S1: The paper's own front matter records that the protocol was vetted by Roy Baumeister, who originated the model being tested.

S2: Let me try the two wrong readings on you. The effect is zero.

S1: No. An interval containing zero means the data are compatible with zero. They're also compatible with a small positive effect and a small negative one. Nothing here demonstrates an absence.

S2: The effect is real but small, because the estimate is above zero.

S1: Also no, and that's the more tempting error. An estimate that far inside its own interval isn't evidence of direction.

S2: What did the replication's authors say against themselves?

S1: They spend pages on it, and that's the third thing worth noticing. Their depletion task did not include an initial period in which individuals familiarized themselves with the no-depletion version. What that period is for, and what they make of leaving it out, are this course's summary rather than their words: it's supposed to induce the habitual response participants then override, and they judge the omission unlikely to have been decisive. On length they quote directly: it is possible that the letter e task was sufficiently arduous but not of sufficient duration to deplete individuals' self-control resources. They raise it themselves and leave it open.

S2: Now, you've given me three papers all pointing the same way.

S1: And that's a real limitation of the lesson, which it says. This course read no source written by a defender of the resource model. So take the defence from the fairest place available, which is the paper proposing the replacement, whose authors say they admire and have contributed to the evidence for the limited-resource view.

S2: What do the defenders say?

S1: That the model has been stretched to accommodate such results by suggesting that initial acts of self-control only partially deplete the resource, and that ego depletion occurs because people are unwilling to draw further from their reserves.

S2: So not empty. Holding back.

S1: That's called an energy-conservation account, and it changes the predictions: performance should come back when there's a reason to spend. Anybody arguing only with willpower is a muscle is arguing with the popular version rather than the position.

S2: So does this mean willpower is a myth?

S1: It overshoots, and the replication's authors would be the first to say so. What was tested is one proposed mechanism in one paradigm, and an interval spanning zero isn't a demonstration of absence. And the experience the model was invented to explain is real. Things do feel harder after a hard morning. What's been called into question is the explanation, not the experience.

S2: And the reading you'd most warn against?

S1: Science reversed itself, so nothing is known. It feels like sophistication and it's the opposite. What happened here is the process working: a claim was made, it was tested with better methods, the estimate didn't hold, and the people involved published the result and their own doubts about it.

S2: What's in the lesson that we haven't touched?

S1: A table setting the three estimates side by side, and what this course reads off them, which is that an effect shrinking as the method tightens is the signature of a literature rather than of a phenomenon; an exercise that has you read the replication's abstract and limitations and answer four questions, including what would change your mind; and a second exercise on advice built on the model, and whether it survives without it. The full lesson is at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S2: Thanks for listening.
