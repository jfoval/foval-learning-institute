S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Lesson three of Habits and Self-Discipline. What the cue is doing.

S2: This is the first lesson with a technique in it.

S1: And the technique is short. Most of the lesson is why it works, because a cue chosen without understanding what a cue is for is the commonest way habit attempts fail.

S2: Start with the evidence, then.

S1: Neal, Wood, Wu and Kurlander ran two field experiments in two thousand and eleven, and this course rates them the clearest demonstration in the subject. A field experiment is one run on real people in the place the behaviour normally happens, which buys realism and costs control.

S2: What was the setup?

S1: In study one, ninety-eight students at a campus cinema, and sixty more in a meeting room. Everyone got a box of popcorn and was told they were rating movie trailers, and everyone was paid six dollars. Half of them got fresh popcorn. Half got stale.

S2: And they'd measured something about the people beforehand.

S1: Separately, how strong each person's habit of eating popcorn at the cinema was.

S2: What happened?

S1: In the authors' words. Habitual popcorn eaters at a cinema were minimally influenced by their hunger or how much they liked the food, and they ate equal amounts of stale and fresh popcorn.

S2: Sit with that.

S1: Not that they preferred stale popcorn, and not that they couldn't taste it. Their hunger did not predict how much they ate. Their liking did not predict how much they ate. Stale popcorn and fresh popcorn came out the same.

S2: So what would you change to make them eat less of the stale? Because my instinct is to tell them it's stale.

S1: That's most people's instinct, and it's the wrong place to look. Everything in that direction is about the person: give them a reason to care, make them pay attention.

S2: And the two things that actually worked were neither.

S1: One was moving them out of the cinema. In the meeting room, the same kind of habitual eaters ate much less stale popcorn than fresh, exactly like everybody else.

S2: And the other?

S1: Stranger, and it's the one worth remembering. In the second experiment, participants at the cinema were told which hand to eat with, and half were given their non-dominant one. That alone was enough. Habitual eaters using the wrong hand ate less stale than fresh popcorn, like people without the habit.

S2: Nothing about the popcorn changed.

S1: Nothing about the popcorn, and nothing about their preferences. What changed was whether the response could run off the way it always had. And I should say this course has read study one's design and participants and not study two's, so how many people took part in the second experiment isn't something we can tell you.

S2: How do the authors put both conditions together?

S1: In one sentence. People ate out of habit, regardless of freshness, only when currently in the context associated with past performance, which was the cinema in study one, and only when eating in a way that allowed the habitual response to run, which in study two meant eating with the dominant hand.

S2: Two requirements, and both had to hold.

S1: The cue had to be present, and the response had to be performable in its usual form. Remove either and the behaviour came back under the person's control.

S2: And I should say who this is a finding about.

S1: You should. The people whose eating stopped tracking their hunger and their liking were the ones with a strong cinema popcorn habit. The same box of stale popcorn, in the same room, was eaten one way by people with the habit and another way by people without it.

S2: Right. So the technique.

S1: Time Management lesson four already gave you most of it: the if-then plan. Name a situation, attach a response, and test the situation with the question, would I notice this if I were tired and in a bad mood.

S2: And this course adds something.

S1: It adds why that works and what it therefore requires. The cue is not a reminder. It's one half of an association built by repeated pairings of cue and response rather than by elapsed time, and the building only happens when the two keep occurring together. So a cue has to clear two bars rather than one.

S2: First bar.

S1: It has to be noticeable. That's Time Management's bar, and the test is unchanged.

S2: Second.

S1: It has to be reliable, and that one is this course's inference from the mechanism rather than a finding anybody has reported. It has to be a context you actually meet, at a rate that lets the association form. A cue you encounter twice a month isn't a bad reminder; it's a context that will take years to build anything.

S2: Is there a practical version of that in the research?

S1: Gardner, Lally and Wardle put it in a sheet meant for patients. Be consistent: choose a time and place that you encounter every day of the week.

S2: Let me test it. A man who's read on the same commuter train for six years moves to a job he walks to, and finds he no longer wants to read in the morning. What happened?

S1: The cue went. The association is between a context and a response, so removing the context removes the trigger without touching what was learned. Which is why the pull often comes back on the first train journey afterwards.

S2: So the habit isn't broken.

S1: Nothing in what this course has read says an association decays because its cue stopped appearing. What you've lost is the thing that fires it.

S2: One misconception before we go.

S1: That the best cue is a time of day. A clock time is a weak context on its own, because your relationship to eight o'clock varies. An event is usually better: after you put the kettle on, after you finish brushing your teeth.

S2: What's in the lesson that we haven't touched?

S1: Two worked cases, one of a cue chosen well and one of a good cue that failed anyway for a reason worth knowing; the order in which to run the three checks when a plan in the right form fails; an exercise that has you write the same behaviour against two different cues and count which one actually fires; a second exercise on a behaviour you'd rather not have; and a section on why none of this is a story about weak-willed people, and why changing the context is a lever when trying harder mostly isn't. The full lesson is at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S2: Thanks for listening.
