S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're opening a new course, Habits and Self-Discipline, with its first lesson. What a habit is.

S2: Which sounds like the sort of thing you'd skip.

S1: It does, and the lesson's claim is that almost every confusion in this subject comes from skipping it. If you aim at the everyday meaning of the word, the techniques later in the course are aimed at nothing.

S2: So what's the ordinary meaning?

S1: Something you do a lot. Biting your nails, checking your phone, a glass of wine on a Friday.

S2: And the research meaning?

S1: Gardner, Lally and Wardle put it in a piece for general practitioners. Habits, they say, are defined as actions that are triggered automatically in response to contextual cues that have been associated with their performance.

S2: Say that again slowly, because there are several things in it.

S1: There are three. A cue, which is a context: a place, a time of day, an event, the end of another behaviour. A response, which is the action. And an association between the two, which is the thing that was built and the thing that does the triggering.

S2: And what's not in the list.

S1: You, deciding. That absence is the whole point of it.

S2: Give me their examples.

S1: Automatically washing hands after using the toilet. Or putting on a seatbelt after getting into the car. Both things nobody ever set out to learn.

S2: How does the association get built?

S1: The same paper says it. Decades of psychological research consistently show that mere repetition of a simple action in a consistent context leads, through associative learning, to the action being activated upon subsequent exposure to those contextual cues.

S2: Three phrases doing the work there.

S1: Repetition, because one pairing builds next to nothing. Consistent context, because a shifting one gives the learning nothing to attach to. And associative learning, which doesn't require you to intend any of it.

S2: All right. Here's my objection. If I run three mornings a week, I have a running habit. What more do you want?

S1: The lesson has two people for exactly that, and both are constructed, because nothing this course read describes an individual's habits. Both run three mornings a week. The first thinks about it each morning, talks herself into it some mornings and not others, and over a year it comes out at about three. The second is usually out of the door before the question of whether to go has properly formed.

S2: Same behaviour, same frequency.

S1: And only the second is a habit in the sense that makes this research apply, because only there has the decision dropped out. Frequency is not automaticity, and that distinction is the single most useful thing in the lesson.

S2: How do I tell which I've got?

S1: Pick one thing you do most days and ask whether, on a typical morning, the question of whether to do it actually arises. Not whether you enjoy it, and not whether you could stop. Whether you consult yourself about it.

S2: And what do people find?

S1: That far fewer of their behaviours are habits in this sense than they expected, and that the ones which are tend not to be the ones they're proud of. Brushing your teeth usually is. The gym usually isn't, even after years. Reading before bed, for most people, isn't, which is why it goes first in a bad week.

S2: So what does a habit actually buy you?

S1: Two things. Once initiation of the action is transferred to external cues, the paper says, dependence on conscious attention or motivational processes is reduced. Therefore habits are likely to persist even after conscious motivation or interest dissipates.

S2: That's the offer.

S1: That's the offer. Not that you'll want things more, but that for one behaviour the wanting stops being load-bearing.

S2: And the second thing?

S1: A habit is cheap to run. In two hourly diary studies of students published in two thousand and two, the researchers counted a behaviour as habitual when it had been performed almost daily in stable contexts, which is a test of frequency and context rather than the automaticity test we just did. On that count, people doing something habitual were likely to think about issues unrelated to their behavior, presumably because they did not have to consciously guide their actions, and they reported less stress than during behaviour that wasn't habitual. Lesson two takes that difference properly.

S2: You've been careful not to say any of this is good.

S1: Because the definition is a mechanism, and mechanisms are indifferent. The uncomfortable case is the smoking ban in United Kingdom public houses in two thousand and seven. Many smokers reported finding themselves lighting a cigarette as an unintentional habitual response to drinking alcohol.

S2: The drinking being the cue.

S1: The drinking was the cue, the lighting was the response, and the association was not consulted about the ban. And I should say where that comes from. It's quoted from a two thousand and twenty-two methods paper this course has read, which is reporting other researchers' work, and the course hasn't gone back to the study behind it.

S2: What else does the definition not say?

S1: It says nothing about discipline. A habit isn't a thing disciplined people have and undisciplined people don't; it's an association, and the mechanism builds them in everybody. And it says nothing about the habit being chosen, because nobody sets out to learn to check their phone when they sit down.

S2: Somebody's going to say this is word games. They do the same things whatever you call them.

S1: The definition isn't a label, it's a prediction about what happens when things get hard. If your running is habitual, the research says it should survive a week when you don't feel like running. If it's deliberated, it predicts no such thing. Those are different predictions about the same person doing the same number of runs, and you can check which is true of you.

S2: Give me the sorting case that surprised you.

S1: A woman does the household accounts on the first Saturday of the month, and has for six years without missing one. She's constructed too. She puts it in the calendar, and when the first Saturday is awkward she moves it and does it anyway.

S2: So it's not a habit.

S1: It's not, and she's more reliable than most people's habits. Reliability and automaticity are different achievements, and the sorting has nothing to do with which you'd want.

S2: What does the course promise, then?

S1: Not to make you more disciplined, and that's a statement about what the research is rather than modesty. What the evidence describes is a mechanism by which a behaviour stops needing a decision, which isn't the same as becoming someone who does hard things.

S2: We've left things out. What's in the lesson that we haven't touched?

S1: Two more sorted cases, including a teenager and an app; two exercises, one of which asks you to find a habit you never chose; a gap the lesson names up front, that nearly every participant in this literature is a student or a self-selected volunteer; three things left out on purpose, including how to break a habit; and one question the course refuses to answer, which is whether you should want your life run by habit at all. The full lesson is at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S2: Thanks for listening.
