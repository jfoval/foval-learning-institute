S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Lesson four of Habits and Self-Discipline. How long it takes, and what happens if you miss a day.

S2: Before the numbers, you wanted to say something about where they come from.

S1: I do, because it governs the rest. The study everybody quotes is Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts and Wardle, published in two thousand and ten. This course hasn't opened it. It's behind a paywall that couldn't be got past, so every figure here is at one remove, from two open-access papers that have Phillippa Lally as an author and that describe her own study.

S2: Weaker than quoting the paper.

S1: Weaker, and not bad: the reporting comes from the person who ran it. But it isn't the same thing, and a course that asks you to check where claims come from should say where its own stand.

S2: Give me the design.

S1: Ninety-six volunteers each chose an eating, drinking or activity behaviour to do daily in the same context, and were followed for twelve weeks. Each day they recorded whether they'd done it and rated how automatic it felt.

S2: And how many made it into the result?

S1: Eighty-two provided enough data to analyse. Curves were fitted for sixty-two of them, of whom thirty-nine fitted well.

S2: Ninety-six to thirty-nine.

S1: Which is normal for this kind of study, and it's the sort of attrition that disappears when a finding is retold.

S2: What shape was the curve?

S1: Automaticity didn't rise in a straight line. The papers describe an asymptotic increase, with an initial acceleration that slowed to a plateau.

S2: So fast at first.

S1: And that shape is more useful than the number, because it predicts something you can check on yourself. The first fortnight should feel like more progress than the sixth, not because you're losing momentum but because that's what the curve does.

S2: Now the number.

S1: The plateau was reached after an average of sixty-six days, with individuals ranging from eighteen days to a predicted two hundred and fifty-four.

S2: Eighteen to two hundred and fifty-four.

S1: The fastest person in that sample got there in under three weeks. The slowest, on the model, would have taken more than eight months. Sixty-six is a mean with an enormous spread around it.

S2: And complexity moved it.

S1: Automaticity strength peaked more quickly for simple actions, for example drinking water, than for more elaborate routines, for example doing fifty sit-ups.

S2: So when somebody quotes you sixty-six days, what's fallen off?

S1: The range, and it goes for the same reason lesson two's range went. Eighteen to two hundred and fifty-four isn't usable as advice. It doesn't tell a reader what to expect and it makes the finding sound less like a finding.

S2: You said there's a second thing that falls off.

S1: And it's worse, because losing it changes what you do. Sixty-six days isn't sixty-six ticks on a calendar.

S2: Explain that.

S1: What builds a habit is the pairing: the cue occurs, the response happens, the association strengthens a little. The thing that accumulates is pairings. Elapsed time only tracks pairings if you never miss, and nobody never misses.

S2: Do the researchers say that themselves?

S1: In their guidance to other researchers. One of their criteria for a well-run habit study is that it should not infer effects of repetition from measures of time. The reason, in this course's words rather than theirs, is that a day the behaviour didn't happen is still a day, so a graph plotted against time counts it as progress.

S2: Give me the worked case.

S1: Two constructed people over seventy days. Asha picks a cue she meets every day and does the behaviour on sixty-four of them. Ben picks the same behaviour and a cue he meets about four days in seven, and does it on thirty-one.

S2: Both have done seventy days.

S1: On a calendar, they're in the same place. Ask how many times the cue and the response have occurred together, and one has had more than twice as much learning as the other.

S2: So what can Ben honestly say after ten weeks?

S1: Not that it didn't work for him. That he's had about thirty-one repetitions, which the curve says is early, and that his cue is the thing to look at rather than his character.

S2: All right. The big one. What happens when you miss a day?

S1: Here's the sentence. Missing the occasional opportunity to perform the behaviour did not seriously impair the habit formation process: automaticity gains soon resumed after one missed performance.

S2: Read it twice.

S1: One missed day did not set the process back. Gains resumed. The curve carried on from about where it was.

S2: Against everything an app has ever told me.

S1: That a broken streak is a failure, that you're back at the beginning, that consistency means never missing. None of that is in the research.

S2: Does that mean it doesn't matter whether I miss?

S1: No, and the distinction is worth having. A missed day is a lost repetition, so it slows you by about one repetition. What the folk belief adds, and what's false, is that it does something else as well: undoes progress, resets a counter, breaks something. It doesn't. You're not back at the start. You're one repetition short of where you'd have been.

S2: And the practical cost of believing otherwise.

S1: Is that people stop. Stopping is the only thing here that actually costs you everything you've built.

S2: Last thing. Twenty-one days.

S1: Doesn't come from a study. As far as this course can establish, and it holds this at search-summary level because the book hasn't been opened, it comes from Maxwell Maltz's Psycho-Cybernetics, in nineteen sixty. A plastic surgeon, writing about how long patients took to adjust to a new face. It usually requires a minimum of about twenty-one days to effect any perceptible change in a mental image.

S2: A minimum of about.

S1: A minimum of about, and it wasn't about habits. The qualifier went missing somewhere between nineteen sixty and the poster you saw, and so did the subject.

S2: What's in the lesson that we haven't touched?

S1: The curve set out stage by stage, with the part that's measured separated from the part this course infers; a ten-week tick sheet, modelled on a real instrument a doctor can hand to a patient, that runs all of this on your own behaviour; a second exercise that takes whatever number you were given and traces it back to what it cites; and the point that even the study's own average is an average number of days in people who were asked to perform daily, which is the closest days ever get to repetitions. The full lesson is at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S2: Thanks for listening.
