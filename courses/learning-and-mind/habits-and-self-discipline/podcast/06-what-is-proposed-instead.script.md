S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Lesson six of Habits and Self-Discipline. What's proposed instead.

S2: Last time we left a hole. The resource model of self-control lost most of the evidence behind it.

S1: And a course that stopped there would have taught you a debunking rather than a subject. Something happens between a hard morning and a hard afternoon. The question is what.

S2: Who's proposing an answer?

S1: Inzlicht and Schmeichel, writing in two thousand and twelve, propose what they call a process model. Their statement of it is one sentence: exerting self-control at Time one causes temporary shifts in both motivation and attention that undermine self-control at Time two.

S2: Two shifts. Take them one at a time.

S1: The first is a shift in motivational orientation away from suppressing and inhibiting desires and toward approaching and gratifying them.

S2: Unpack that.

S1: They do, in their own words. Initial acts of control lead people to become less motivated to engage in further deliberative control and more motivated to engage in things that are more personally rewarding, interesting, and enjoyable.

S2: And the second shift?

S1: Runs alongside the first. A shift in attention away from cues signaling the need to exert control and toward cues signaling gratification.

S2: So not just wanting it less.

S1: Not noticing that the question arose. And they say the two feed each other: the model assumes that motivation and attention each affect the other.

S2: Give me the difference on an ordinary evening.

S1: The lesson has a constructed case. Nadia spends a working day on a task requiring constant care, gets home, intends to spend an hour on her Open University course, and spends it on her phone.

S2: On the old account?

S1: The day's careful work drew down a limited store. By evening there wasn't enough left to override the easier option, and the prediction that follows is about quantity: a lighter day leaves more, a rest should help, there's a budget to spend wisely.

S2: And on the new one?

S1: Two things happened. Her motivation shifted away from deliberative control and towards what's rewarding, so the course became less appealing relative to the phone than it would have been at nine in the morning. And her attention shifted: the reminders that she's behind were less salient, the phone more so.

S2: Which means she may not have felt a struggle at all.

S1: That's a specific prediction, and it's harder to get out of the resource account.

S2: Here's the test I want. People do a demanding first task, and half are offered money before the second. What does each account predict?

S1: The process account predicts the effect can go away, because if what happened was a shift in motivation towards the rewarding, then supplying a reward acts directly on the thing that shifted.

S2: And the resource account?

S1: Its simplest version says no: if the first task spent a store, money doesn't refill it, and you might expect people to try harder and still do worse.

S2: But that's not the version its defenders hold.

S1: And this is the part worth learning. The resource model was stretched years ago to say that initial acts of self-control only partially deplete the resource, and that what looks like depletion is people being unwilling to draw further on what's left. That's the energy-conservation account, and on it an incentive is exactly the sort of thing that makes a person willing to spend.

S2: So the test doesn't separate them.

S1: Not as cleanly as it looks, which is why the lesson gives it as a caution rather than a test. And the reasoning there is this course's own, from the two accounts as stated. The paper itself says the experiment hasn't been run: of the studies that used motivational incentives, none offered them for the first task. They put it forward as an avenue for future research.

S2: What does the new account give up?

S1: The metaphor, and they say so themselves. Though the process model of depletion may sacrifice the elegance of the resource metaphor, it paints a more precise picture of ego depletion and suggests several nuanced predictions for future research.

S2: Read elegance as a real loss.

S1: A limited resource is a wonderful idea. Everybody's met a battery, the metaphor explains itself in four words, and it generates obvious advice. Two shifts that feed each other explains itself in nobody's four words.

S2: What do they claim in exchange?

S1: Precision, and precision means testability. A metaphor can absorb almost any result you give it. A claim about motivation shifting predicts specific things.

S2: Somebody's going to say this is just saying people stopped wanting to try, dressed up. A judgement about character in a lab coat.

S1: It's the commonest objection and it needs two answers. The first is that the shift is described as something that happens to a person, not something chosen. The second is the attention half, which isn't a moral category at all. A version that talks only about wanting has dropped half the proposal, and is fairly open to the objection.

S2: How well evidenced is any of it?

S1: This is where to be careful, because the temptation is to treat the new account as established simply because the old one lost its evidence. It's a proposal. Its authors describe the paper as highlighting evidence in support of this model but also where evidence is lacking, thus providing a blueprint for future research.

S2: A blueprint isn't a result.

S1: And they chose that phrase. This course has read the abstract, the section setting out the model, the section on motivation, the conclusion and one footnote. The parts not read are the evidence review and the attention half, so the lesson's account of where things stand rests on the authors' own summary rather than on this course having weighed it.

S2: So what's the honest position?

S1: The phenomenon is better attested than either explanation of it. Things get harder after a hard morning, and that isn't in doubt. Why, is.

S2: What's in the lesson that we haven't touched?

S1: What Nadia would actually report about that evening, and why the course counts that as a point in the account's favour and not as evidence; a restatement of both shifts in the terms of an ordinary Tuesday afternoon; an exercise that has you take one of your own failures and read it twice; a second exercise on spotting the sloppy version of the process account in the wild; and the four things people most often get wrong here, including the one where self-control and habit get treated as the same thing. The full lesson is at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S2: Thanks for listening.
