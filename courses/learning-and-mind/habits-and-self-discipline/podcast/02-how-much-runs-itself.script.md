S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Lesson two of Habits and Self-Discipline. How much of a day runs itself.

S2: You've probably met the number this one's about.

S1: Forty-three percent of what you do is habit. It's everywhere, it comes from a real study, and it almost always arrives without the sentence it came from.

S2: So what did the study do?

S1: Wood, Quinn and Kashy ran two studies in two thousand and two and reported them together. Both were hourly diary studies with student participants. A prompt once an hour, and at each prompt the participant wrote down what they were doing, what they were thinking and how they felt.

S2: And the two studies differed how?

S1: In the form. Study one took one behaviour per entry. Study two let a participant list several at once.

S2: That sounds like a detail.

S1: It's the whole thing, and we'll get there. I should say first that this course read the abstract and the diary-study results rather than the Method section, so how many people took part and how long they recorded for aren't reported in the lesson.

S2: All right. What counted as a habit?

S1: A diary entry counted if the behaviour was performed just about every day and usually in the same location.

S2: Every day, same place. That's it?

S1: That's it. And notice it's a workable proxy for lesson one's definition rather than the definition itself. Lesson one's habit is an action triggered automatically by a cue. You can't see a trigger in a diary, so the researchers used the two things that produce one: repetition, and a stable context.

S2: Which means the number is a count against that criterion.

S1: Any figure you ever meet about how much of life is habitual is a count against a criterion like this one, and the criterion is doing at least as much work as the finding.

S2: Give me the sentence, then.

S1: Between a third and a half of all behaviors listed were classified as habits, given that they were performed just about every day and usually in the same location. This estimate was greater in Study two, at forty-three percent, than in Study one, at thirty-five.

S2: So forty-three is the higher of two figures.

S1: The higher of two, from one study, with a range around it that the paper states, and a gap between its two studies that the paper explains.

S2: Why does the forty-three travel and the range doesn't?

S1: A range is hard to repeat, and that's most of it. Between a third and a half doesn't fit in a headline, doesn't sound like a finding, and can't be put on a slide. One number can be all three. And of the two available, the larger one makes the more interesting claim.

S2: What do the authors say about the gap between their own two studies?

S1: They don't leave it unexplained. Their account is that the study two procedure of listing multiple behaviours would encourage participants to include more activities performed habitually.

S2: Read that twice.

S1: A diary asking for one behaviour an hour tends to catch the one you were paying attention to, and the thing you were paying attention to is the least habitual thing you were doing.

S2: So the eight-point gap is a difference between two forms.

S1: Same kind of participant, same criterion, and the only thing that changed is how many behaviours a person could write down at once.

S2: The lesson has a worked case for that, doesn't it?

S1: A constructed one. A student at eleven in the morning, in a library, reading for an essay, drinking coffee, with music in her ears. On the study one form she has one line, and she writes reading for the essay, which isn't something she does every day in the same place. So her entry contributes one behaviour and none of it is habitual. On the study two form she writes three, and two of them are.

S2: Same hour, same person, different number.

S1: Different number.

S2: What else did the studies find?

S1: The bit that connects to lesson one. When people were doing something habitual they were likely to think about issues unrelated to their behavior, presumably because they did not have to consciously guide their actions. And they reported lesser feelings of stress associated with habitual than nonhabitual behavior.

S2: Not happier.

S1: Not happier, and not more satisfied. Less stressed, which is a narrower and more believable claim.

S2: And which behaviours were the habitual ones?

S1: The ones you'd guess. The paper names attending to hygiene and appearance and sleeping and waking activities as most likely to be classified as habitual. Those are the behaviours most tightly tied to one place and one moment, which is exactly what the criterion asks for.

S2: Here's the thing I want to put to you. If it's between a third and a half, then most of what those students listed was not habitual. That's the opposite of how the number gets used.

S1: On the arithmetic, you're right, and a reader who came away thinking most of life runs itself has the finding backwards.

S2: But.

S1: But don't over-collect on it either. The criterion required a behaviour to happen just about every day in the same location. That's strict. Something you do automatically three times a week, or automatically in two different places, fails it and is still a habit in lesson one's sense.

S2: So the figure is a floor.

S1: A floor rather than an estimate, and that reading is this course's, not a claim the authors make. The honest sentence is duller than either version you'll meet.

S2: Last thing. People say these studies observed people.

S1: They didn't. These are hourly self-report diaries, filled in by students at an hourly prompt. The researchers were careful about it, and it's still people telling you what they did. Lesson seven comes back to that, because the whole habit literature measures automaticity by asking, and it's the weakest joint in the subject.

S2: What's in the lesson that we haven't touched?

S1: An exercise that has you count one day of your own against the study's criterion; a second exercise on tracing a figure to its source; the two percentages that same hour in the library produces on the two forms, which are zero and sixty-seven; and the two other travelling numbers this course traces, sixty-six and twenty-one, which are lessons four and seven. The full lesson is at Foval Learning Institute dot org.

S2: Thanks for listening.
