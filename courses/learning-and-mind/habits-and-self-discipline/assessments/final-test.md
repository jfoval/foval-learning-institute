  # --- What a habit is, automaticity against frequency (L1) ---
  - q: >-
      What definition of a habit does the research this course read actually use?
    options:
      - A behaviour triggered automatically by a cue in a context where it has been repeated
      - A behaviour a person performs at least once every day over a sustained stretch of weeks
      - A behaviour a person continues without enjoying it, which is what makes it a habit
      - A behaviour that has become part of how a person thinks of themselves over time
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The cue and the automatic triggering are the whole of it, and everything else in the course
      hangs off them. Daily repetition is what produces a habit rather than what one is, which is
      lesson 1's frequency-and-automaticity distinction. Enjoyment is not in the definition either
      way. And the identity framing is the current trade version, which this course says it has not
      read enough to teach.
  - q: >-
      A man has eaten the same lunch at his desk every workday for four years and says he chooses it
      fresh each morning. What does the course's definition let you say?
    options:
      - It is a habit, because four years of daily repetition is exactly what a habit is made of
      - It is not a habit, since anything a person can describe in words cannot be automatic
      - Possibly a habit, and what settles it is whether the choosing is actually happening
      - It is not a habit, because he says he chooses, and choosing is the opposite of one
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Repetition in a stable context is how habits get built, so the case is a good candidate, but
      the definition turns on whether the behaviour is triggered rather than decided, and his report
      is evidence rather than proof. Treating the repetition as settling it skips the definition.
      Treating his description as settling it in either direction gives self-report more weight than
      lesson 2 says it can carry.
  - q: >-
      Somebody says he has no self-discipline and so cannot build habits. What is wrong with that
      inference?
    options:
      - Nothing, since the two are the same capacity and the research treats them together
      - They are different mechanisms, and habit forming needs repetition rather than resolve
      - Self-discipline is a myth, so no claim that rests on it can support any conclusion
      - The resource model was replicated, so his self-control is not the thing limiting him here
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      A habit is built by pairing a cue with a response often enough, which is why lesson 3 works on
      the cue rather than on the person, and self-control is the separate question lessons 5 and 6
      are about. Treating them as one capacity is the conflation the course exists to undo. Calling
      self-discipline a myth overshoots what the replication showed, which lesson 5's misconception
      list says in terms. And the resource model is the thing that failed to replicate.

  # --- How much of a day is habitual, and the provenance of the figure (L2) ---
  - q: >-
      An app tells you that 43 percent of daily behaviour is habit. Traced back to the paper, what
      will you find?
    options:
      - A figure from two studies that agreed closely, so the single number is a fair summary
      - A figure produced by observing participants rather than by asking them to report it
      - A figure that later work has revised downward, which the app has not yet caught up with
      - The higher of two figures, against a stated criterion, on students, in a stated range
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The paper reports between a third and a half, gives 43 percent and 35 percent, and explains
      why the two differ, so the range and the criterion are what fell off on the way to the app.
      The two studies did not agree closely; the eight-point gap is the interesting part. These were
      hourly self-report diaries rather than observation. And nothing has been revised: the paper was
      right where it was published.
  - q: >-
      Two diary studies using the same criterion returned 35 percent and 43 percent. What accounts
      for the gap, and what does it show?
    options:
      - The forms differed in how many behaviours could be listed, so method moved the number
      - The samples differed in age, so the number tracks who happened to be in the study
      - The second study ran longer, so more of each participant's routine was captured
      - The criterion was applied more loosely in the second, so the studies are not comparable
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The authors say the Study 2 procedure of listing multiple behaviours would encourage
      participants to include more activities performed habitually, so a change to the form moved
      the count by eight percentage points with everything else held still. Both studies used
      students. The criterion was the same in both, which is what makes the comparison informative
      rather than useless. And the duration is not the explanation the authors give.

  # --- The cue and the context (L3) ---
  - q: >-
      A woman wants to floss daily and plans to do it when she remembers in the evening. Why does
      this course expect that to fail?
    options:
      - Flossing is too small a behaviour to build, and small behaviours do not become habits
      - Evenings are the worst part of the day for a new behaviour, on what the research shows
      - Remembering is not a context, so there is nothing for the response to get paired with
      - She has not allowed enough time, and the research says to give it at least sixty-six days
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      A cue has to be a context she meets whether or not she is thinking about it, and remembering
      is the thing a cue is supposed to replace. Small and simple behaviours reached automaticity
      faster in the study, not slower. Nothing in what this course read compares times of day. And
      sixty-six days is an average with an enormous range, not a minimum to serve.
  - q: >-
      A man who dislikes the coffee at his office buys one every morning on the way in. What does
      the popcorn experiment suggest is going on?
    options:
      - He is deceiving himself about disliking it, since nobody buys what they do not like
      - His self-control is depleted in the morning, which is when the resource is at its lowest
      - He has not tried hard enough to stop, which is what breaking a habit actually requires
      - The context is cueing the response, and liking stops predicting what habitual people do
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      That is the finding: habitual popcorn eaters were minimally influenced by hunger or by how
      much they liked the food. Reinterpreting his dislike keeps the assumption the experiment
      overturns. The resource account is a different mechanism and lesson 5 is about what happened
      to it. And effort is exactly the wrong place to look, since moving the same people out of the
      cinema worked where persuading them did not.
  - q: >-
      A woman's chosen cue occurs about three days a week, and she says she has been at it for ten
      weeks. What do lessons 3 and 4 together let you tell her?
    options:
      - Ten weeks is past the average, so the behaviour is unlikely to become automatic for her
      - She has had about thirty pairings, not seventy, and the cue is what to look at first
      - Missing days has reset her progress repeatedly, which is why nothing has built up yet
      - Her rate is within the published range, so the only thing she needs to do is continue
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Pairings are what accumulate, so ten weeks against an unreliable cue is a fraction of the
      repetitions ten weeks looks like, and the cue failing the reliability bar is the diagnosis.
      Reading the average as a deadline is what the range exists to stop. Missed days cost a
      repetition each rather than resetting anything, which is lesson 4's central finding. And
      telling her to continue leaves the cue, which is the thing actually limiting her, untouched.

  # --- Timeline, the range, and the missed day (L4) ---
  - q: >-
      What did the 66-day figure come from, and what has to travel with it?
    options:
      - An average in one study, with individuals from 18 days to a predicted 254 days
      - The point at which most participants in that study said the behaviour felt automatic
      - A median across several studies of daily behaviours, which is why it is so widely cited
      - The longest any participant took, which is why it is quoted as how long to persist for
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      It is a mean from one study of 96 volunteers, and the spread around it is the part that
      changes what a reader does. It is not a point most participants reached, since the curves were
      modelled individually and thirty-nine of them fitted well. It is not a median and not a
      pooling of studies. And it is nowhere near the longest, which was a predicted 254 days.
  - q: >-
      A man misses one day in week three and concludes he has to start over. What does the research
      this course read say?
    options:
      - He is right, since the association needs unbroken repetition in order to build at all
      - He is wrong, because a missed day makes no difference at all to how long it will take
      - It depends how strong the habit already was, since strong ones survive a miss and weak ones do not
      - Gains resumed after one missed performance, so he is one repetition short, not back at zero
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Missing the occasional opportunity did not seriously impair the process, and that is the most
      useful and least repeated finding in the subject. It does cost him something, though, which is
      why the second answer overshoots: a missed day is a lost pairing and slows him by about one.
      Unbroken repetition is the folk belief the finding contradicts. And prior strength is a
      reasonable guess that nothing this course read tests.
  - q: >-
      Somebody plots their weekly automaticity ratings against calendar days, sees a straight line,
      and concludes the research's curve is wrong. What is the likelier explanation?
    options:
      - The curve was measured on a different kind of behaviour, so it does not apply to theirs
      - Automaticity ratings are too coarse to show a curve, so a line is what anybody would get
      - Days are not repetitions, and a graph against time counts a missed day as progress made
      - They have not run it long enough, since the curve only becomes visible near the plateau
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The methods guidance says in terms that studies should not infer effects of repetition from
      measures of time, and plotting against the calendar is that error drawn. Behaviour type moves
      how fast the plateau arrives rather than the shape. The same ratings produced the published
      curve, so coarseness is not it. And the early repetitions are where the gains are largest, so
      waiting longer would show less, not more.
  - q: >-
      A woman's behaviour still does not feel automatic after twelve weeks. What does this course say
      about that?
    options:
      - She should change the behaviour, since twelve weeks without automaticity means a bad fit
      - It is inside the published range, which reaches a predicted 254 days for some people
      - She has probably missed too many days, which is the usual reason a curve fails to form
      - Twelve weeks is the point the research stops at, so the behaviour will not become one
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Eighteen days to a predicted 254 is the spread, so twelve weeks is unremarkable and tells her
      almost nothing on its own. Changing the behaviour on that evidence discards work for no
      reason. Missed days are worth counting and are a different question from elapsed weeks. And
      the study's twelve-week follow-up is how long the researchers watched, not how long a habit is
      allowed to take.

  # --- The resource model and its replication (L5) ---
  - q: >-
      What were the two headline figures in the depletion literature, and what was each measured on?
    options:
      - d = 0.62 across 23 laboratories, then d = 0.04 in a meta-analysis of published experiments
      - d = 0.62 and then d = 0.04, both from meta-analyses of the same published experiments
      - d = 0.04 on 198 published experiments, then d = 0.62 in one preregistered replication
      - d = 0.62 on 198 published experiments, then d = 0.04 across 23 preregistered laboratories
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      The 2010 meta-analysis of published experiments gave the medium-to-large effect; the 2016
      multilab replication, one protocol run in 23 laboratories, gave an effect whose interval
      encompassed zero. The other options swap the two designs, swap the two figures, or treat both
      as meta-analyses, and which design produced which number is the whole reason the second one
      carried weight.
  - q: >-
      The pooled replication estimate was d = 0.04 with a confidence interval from −0.07 to 0.15.
      What does that permit you to say?
    options:
      - That the data are compatible with no effect, and with a small one in either direction
      - That the effect is zero, which is what an interval containing zero is there to show
      - That the laboratories disagreed with each other, which is what a wide interval means
      - That the effect is real but small, since the point estimate sits above zero by 0.04 or so
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      An interval that encompasses zero rules out a large effect and leaves everything inside it on
      the table, which is a statement about what the data can distinguish. Reading it as zero
      asserts more than the data support, and so does reading the point estimate as a finding when
      the interval spans it. And a confidence interval expresses uncertainty about the pooled
      estimate rather than disagreement between the labs.
  - q: >-
      A friend reads about the replication and says willpower is a myth. What is the strongest
      objection?
    options:
      - The replication's protocol was vetted by the model's originator, so its result stands
      - Meta-analyses cannot overturn experiments, so the earlier 198 studies still hold good
      - A mechanism failing a test is not the capacity failing to exist, and the authors say so
      - The effect was found to be small rather than absent, so the model survives in weaker form
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The replication's own abstract opens by granting that good self-control predicts better health,
      relationships and work, and what it tested was one proposed mechanism for how exertion carries
      over. The vetting is a reason the result is credible, which is an argument on your friend's
      side rather than against them. Meta-analyses summarise experiments rather than being overturned
      by them. And an interval containing zero is not a finding that the effect is small.

  # --- The process account (L6) ---
  - q: >-
      On the process account, why does somebody who has just resisted something find the next
      resistance harder?
    options:
      - A store of self-control has been drawn down and has not had time to refill again yet
      - Motivation has shifted towards gratification and attention away from cues for control
      - The habit of resisting has not been built yet, so there is nothing to fall back on
      - Their attention has narrowed, which is the whole of what the process account proposes
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Two shifts, and its proposers say the two feed each other. The store is the resource model,
      which is the account this one was offered to replace. Habit formation is the other half of the
      course and is not what the process model is about. And attention is one of the two shifts
      rather than the whole proposal, which is the detail that makes the account testable.
  - q: >-
      What kind of result would tell against the resource account and for the process account?
    options:
      - Offering a reward restoring performance, which a spent store should not respond to
      - Performance falling further the longer the first task ran, as both accounts predict
      - Performance recovering after a rest, which is what refilling a store would look like
      - People reporting they felt tired, which is what a depleted resource would feel like
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      A motivational shift can be offset by changing what is on offer, whereas something that has
      been spent should not come back because somebody offers money, and lesson 6 gives this as the
      course's own reasoning about the two accounts rather than as an experiment anybody has
      reported. Recovery after rest is what the resource account predicts. Duration effects and
      reported tiredness are compatible with both, which is what makes them useless as a test.
