Seven lessons have given you a definition that separates automaticity from frequency, a count of how
much of a day meets it, a cue with two bars to clear, a timeline with a range around it and a
finding about the day you miss, an account of what happened to willpower, the proposal that replaced
it, and a habit for reading any claim about the lot.

**This project runs the course's own timeline on your own behaviour**, which is the only way to find
out what the 66 days means for you rather than for 96 volunteers. It takes about eight hours across
ten weeks, and nearly all of that is a few seconds a day.

## The problem this project has, and how it solves it

**No section here can have a right answer**, because the answer's your behaviour and nobody else has
seen it. A project asking you to reach a conclusion couldn't be marked, and one asking you to
reflect would produce nothing anybody could check.

**So every section asks for a prediction, then the record, then the gap between them.** The gap is
the thing that can be assessed without a key. If that sounds familiar it is lesson 2's argument
applied to your own life rather than to a diary study, and it's what **Using AI Effectively** and
**Time Management** do in their projects for the same reason.

**One consequence, said up front so it doesn't feel like a trick.** A ten weeks in which both your
predictions came out right is almost certainly a ten weeks whose predictions were written at the
end. The rubric says so, and a project carrying one honest wrong prediction scores better than one
carrying none.

**And a second, which matters more here than in any other project in the institute.** **A learner
whose behaviour never became automatic in ten weeks hasn't failed this project.** The range in the
study reaches a predicted 254 days. A project that scored only the people who got there would be
teaching the opposite of lesson 4, and the rubric is built so that it cannot.

## What you hand in

**One plain text or Markdown file, roughly 1,000 to 1,800 words, in four sections**, with the tick
sheet behind it. Plain text for the reason **Digital Literacy**'s project gives: a format you'll
still be able to open in twenty years, that nobody can take away from you, and that shows its own
history.

## Section 1: the choice, written before week 1

This section is finished before the first tick, and **that is the whole of its value**. Write it,
save it, and do not edit it afterwards.

1. **The behaviour.** One thing, small, daily. The two examples the papers give are drinking water
   and doing 50 sit-ups, and the first of those is much closer to what the study's participants
   mostly picked. Small isn't a lack of ambition here: simple actions reached their plateau sooner.
2. **The cue**, and this is the part the course spends a whole lesson on. Name a context, not a
   goal and not a feeling. Then test it against both bars from lesson 3: **would you notice it
   tired and in a bad mood**, and **do you actually meet it nearly every day?**
3. **Why that cue.** Two or three sentences. If the honest answer's that it was the only one you
   could think of, write that, and expect section 4 to come back to it.
4. **Two predictions, in numbers.** How many days out of seventy the behaviour will happen. How many
   weeks until it feels automatic. **Write both down before you start**, because a prediction
   recalled afterwards isn't a prediction.

## Section 2: ten weeks of the sheet

Seven columns by ten rows is seventy boxes. Paper, a notes file or a spreadsheet, and the medium
matters less than whether it's to hand at the moment the cue occurs.

**Each day: did it happen? A tick or a blank.** No half marks, no catching up at the weekend, and
**don't tidy the blanks away**. The blanks are data, and section 3 is largely about them.

**Each week: one automaticity rating from 1 to 10.** How much did it feel like something you did
rather than something you decided to do? Rate it on the same day each week, and don't look back at
last week's number before writing this week's.

## Section 3: the curve, and the missed days

Now draw what you recorded. A line, a bar chart or a ten-row table of the weekly ratings, with the
missed days marked on it.

Then answer three things in writing.

1. **Where were the gains largest?** The research says early: an initial acceleration slowing to a
   plateau. Say whether yours did that, and **if it didn't, say so plainly** rather than reading a
   curve into a line.
2. **What happened after each miss?** Take each blank in turn and look at the days around it. The
   study found that automaticity gains soon resumed after one missed performance. Say what yours
   did. **This is the finding of the project**, and a learner who missed nothing has less to say
   here, not more.
3. **What was your pairing count, against the calendar count?** How many days had passed, and how
   many times did the cue and the response actually occur together? If those two numbers differ, you
   have lesson 4's whole argument in your own handwriting.

## Section 4: the comparison, and the decision

1. **Both predictions against the record.** The number of days, and the number of weeks. Say by how
   much each was out, and in which direction.
2. **What the gap was caused by**, as best you can tell. A cue that fired less often than you
   thought. A behaviour more elaborate than it looked. A fortnight when something else was going on.
   **Not knowing is a legitimate answer** if you say what you'd have had to record to know.
3. **The decision**, and all three of these are real answers. **Continue** as you are. **Change the
   cue**, naming the new one and why it clears both bars better. Or **stop**, which is what lesson
   7's second exercise puts on the table and what the genre almost never allows.
4. **What the decision costs.** Every one of the three costs something: continuing costs the ten
   weeks it'll take to find out; changing the cue costs the pairings you have already built;
   stopping costs whatever you wanted the behaviour for. **A decision with nothing on the other side
   of it hasn't been made.**

## Rubric

| Criterion | What full marks look like |
|---|---|
| The behaviour meets the definition | Section 1 names a cue rather than a goal, and the cue is a context the learner meets reliably |
| Predictions are written first | Both predictions are recorded before week 1, and at least one of them is wrong |
| The record is real | Missed days are present and marked, not tidied away or filled in afterwards |
| The curve is read, not just drawn | Section 3 says where the gains were largest, and says so even when the shape is not the published one |
| The missed days are the finding | Section 3 reports what happened after each miss, against what the learner expected to happen |
| Pairings are distinguished from days | Section 3 gives both counts and notices the difference between them |
| Dated and scoped | Every figure quoted from the course carries what it was measured on and when |
| The decision names its cost | Section 4's decision has something real on the other side of it |

**Not on this rubric: whether the habit formed.** Read that row by row and you'll see that a learner
whose behaviour is no more automatic in week ten than in week one can score full marks on
every line. That is deliberate, and it's lesson 4 applied to the assessment rather than only taught
in it.
