S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're opening the course called Note-Taking and Personal Knowledge, with its first lesson. What a note is for.

S1: And the lesson starts with something startling. You have taken notes for years, and nobody has ever told you what a note is for.

S2: I'd have said it was obvious. You write things down so you don't forget them.

S1: That's one of the two answers, and the research has had names for both since the nineteen-seventies. The meta-analysis this course leans on gives the definitions. The first is the encoding function, which is what taking the note does to you at the time. Their words: the process of taking notes, versus listening without note-taking, aids lecture learning.

S2: So the writing itself does something.

S1: That's the claim. And the second is the external storage function, which is what having the note lets you do afterwards. Again their words: the product of taking notes is an external document of text and images that can be reviewed any time following the lecture to better commit noted information to memory.

S2: Both of those sound true. Does the field say which matters more?

S1: It does, and this is the sentence most people have never heard. The same paper says that when the two are compared, students who take and review their notes tend to achieve more than those who take but do not review. And then the line this course keeps. Such findings indicate that the primary benefit of taking lecture notes emerges from the review of notes.

S2: Say that again, because I think I heard it backwards.

S1: The reading afterwards is where most of the benefit is. Not the writing. Almost everybody believes it's the writing.

S2: I believed it was the writing. So does that mean the writing does nothing?

S1: No, and the lesson is careful here. Both functions aid learning. The writing is real and it's the smaller half. But if the review is where the benefit mostly is, then the variable that matters most in your own life is whether you review anything at all, and almost nobody has ever measured whether they do.

S2: Which I imagine is uncomfortable.

S1: That's what the course's project measures, and its first section is in this lesson. Before you take a single note, you write down how many you expect to take this week, how many you expect to go back to within a month, and which kinds. Then you leave it somewhere you won't edit it.

S2: All right. Give me the finding that made you take this seriously.

S1: Morehead, Dunlosky and Rawson ran the most famous study in this subject again, and extended it. And the course read their abstract, not the paper, which it says at the point of use. They added a condition nobody had included before. A group who took no notes at all.

S2: And?

S1: In their words, performance did not consistently differ between any groups, in experiments one and two, including a group who did not take notes. And the no-notes group was in experiment two.

S2: The people who wrote nothing did as well as the people who wrote something.

S1: On that test, in that experiment, at that delay. And I want to put the fences up straight away, because the lesson does. That is not a finding that note-taking is useless.

S2: Why not? It sounds like one.

S1: Because of what was compared. That's the encoding function measured against nothing, on a test soon afterwards. The storage function isn't what that comparison was about, and the same abstract says group differences shrank further after students studied their notes.

S2: So what does it actually show?

S1: That the encoding function isn't the obvious, large, reliable thing everybody assumes. If simply writing during a lecture produced a big benefit, a no-notes group would have shown it.

S2: Every figure you've given me is a student in a lecture.

S1: Every one, and the lesson has a table that makes it unavoidable. Six sources, what each measured, on whom, and how far the course got into each. Five of the six are students in a lecture, tested within the hour or within the week. The sixth is thirty-six people writing single words, with no learning outcome measured at all.

S2: And most of us are not in a lecture.

S1: You're in a meeting, or reading a report, or keeping a notebook for years. So a sentence starting note-taking improves retention is out of scope at the third word, unless the person means a student in a lecture hall.

S2: Is that a way of saying the research is useless to me?

S1: It isn't, and the lesson says so in both directions. A narrow finding isn't an empty one. The two thousand and twenty-four meta-analysis pools twenty-four studies. What the scope rules out is the words so you should, aimed at somebody who isn't in that room.

S2: There's a case in the lesson with three people in a meeting. Can you do that one?

S1: I can, and I'll say first that the case is constructed, which the lesson says too. No source it read reports individual notes and what happened to them later. So. Three people, same hour-long planning meeting. Priya writes four lines, in her own words, each a decision and who owns it, and never opens the page again. Encoding only. Whatever the writing did for her happened in the room.

S2: Second.

S1: Tom types nearly everything. Six hundred words, into a shared folder. Two weeks later somebody asks what was decided about the supplier and he finds it in forty seconds. External storage, and it worked.

S2: And the third.

S1: Daniel writes a page of neat headings with two or three words under each, feels organised, and can't reconstruct a single decision a week later. Neither function. And that one is the course's own reading of the two definitions rather than a finding.

S2: What strikes me is what the sorting didn't depend on.

S1: That's the point of it. Not neatness, not length, not typed against handwritten. It depended on what happened to the note afterwards, and what was in it.

S2: So I'll ask the lazy question. If the review is where the benefit is, can I stop worrying about how I write the note and just make sure I reread it?

S1: No, on two counts. Rereading a note you can't understand is worth nothing, which is Daniel, and that sentence is the course's reasoning from the definitions rather than a measured result. And rereading isn't reviewing. The earlier course, How to Learn Anything, spends a whole lesson on that: looking at a page again is the weakest thing you can do with it, and testing yourself against it is much stronger.

S2: So the honest version has three parts.

S1: The review carries most of the benefit. The review needs a note worth reviewing. And what you do in the review matters as much as that you do it. The thing everybody attends to is the writing, and it's in none of the three.
