S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're finishing Note-Taking and Personal Knowledge with its eighth lesson. Reading a claim about your notes.

S1: And it starts by admitting that the numbers have a shelf life. Five meta-analyses will become six. Somebody will run the EEG study again with two hands on the keyboard. Within a few years the numbers in lessons three to six will be the wrong numbers.

S2: So what survives?

S1: One operation, which the seven lessons were quietly a training in. Taking a sentence somebody says about notes and working out what would have had to happen for it to be true.

S2: Start with the sort, then.

S1: The institute's standing three-way sort, used here rather than taught again. Digital Literacy introduced it and four courses since have put it to different subjects. Measurable: a study could check it, and the sentence says enough for you to imagine the study. Unmeasurable as stated: it turns on a word nobody has defined, so no result could disagree. False: somebody checked and it didn't hold.

S2: Work one through for me.

S1: Four worked claims, all invented for the lesson rather than quoted, and how each one gets sorted is the course's reasoning rather than a finding. First: handwriting beats typing for lecture notes. Measurable, and measured more often than almost anything else in this subject. Lesson five has five pooled answers from plus nought point nought nought eight to plus nought point two five zero, three of which cleared zero.

S2: So the sort doesn't finish the job.

S1: A claim can be measurable, measured five times over, and still unsettled. The sort's only promise is that the disagreement is about evidence rather than about words.

S2: Second.

S1: A good note-taking system makes you think more clearly. Unmeasurable as stated, and worth being precise about why. It isn't that the claim is silly. It's that nothing in the sentence says what would count as thinking more clearly, or which systems are good ones, so no result could contradict it.

S2: Can you repair it?

S1: Name the system, name an outcome somebody could score, name the comparison, and you have a study. The repair is usually available and usually not made, because the vaguer sentence is the one that sells.

S2: Third.

S1: Typing is worse because you write too much. False, in the specific sense that the reason given has been checked and points the other way. Higher word count was associated with better quiz performance in the two thousand and fourteen study and again in its direct replication.

S2: So the typing half is right and the because is wrong.

S1: Typists do write more, and that reproduces reliably. The bit welded onto it, that writing more is what hurts them, is the bit the data contradicts.

S2: And the fourth.

S1: The shape you'll meet most. Don't type your notes, because handwriting activates more of the brain. Two claims stuck together, and they want separate verdicts. The second half is lesson six's EEG study, which found more elaborate connectivity patterns while writing single words by hand than while typing them, on thirty-six university students. That half is a measurement.

S2: And the first half.

S1: Advice about your working life, and nothing in that study is about your working life or about learning at all, since nobody in it learned anything that was tested. A reader who accepts both halves together, or rejects both together, has done neither.

S2: You said each course adds a question of its own to the sort.

S1: Memory added the sample: how many people, and who. Focus and Deep Work added the instrument. This course adds the setting.

S2: Meaning where it was measured.

S1: Every measurement of note-taking behind these eight lessons happened in a lecture hall, to a college student, with the outcome taken inside the hour or inside the week. The one exception is the EEG study, which is a laboratory with no lecture and no outcome at all, and that's a different gap: lesson six's rather than this one's.

S2: And that's not a criticism.

S1: It isn't a coincidence either. A lecture is a controllable event with a natural comparison group sitting in it, which is what an experiment wants. But hold it against the claims you actually meet: somebody in a meeting, somebody reading at their desk, somebody keeping a notebook for eleven years.

S2: None of which is a lecture.

S1: And none of which is tested on Friday. So the setting question is three questions and takes about ten seconds. Where did the measurement happen? How long after? And what was the task?

S2: What if a claim fails one of them?

S1: It may still be true of you. What it isn't is evidenced for you, and the difference between those two is this course's whole subject.

S2: There's a table in the lesson of what each source licenses.

S1: Six rows, and the useful column is the last: what each source does not support. Read it on its own and in four of the six rows what the source cannot support is close to what a reader will have met it saying, which is the course's reading rather than a count anybody has made.

S2: Now the books. The lesson expects most readers meet this subject through a book rather than a paper, and says that's its expectation rather than something anybody counted.

S1: Ahrens, Forte, Luhmann, Buzan. None of the four was opened for this course. The research file names all four as unread and the build takes that seriously: a lesson citing one of them for a claim fails, and this lesson is exempted only so it can say their titles.

S2: So what can you tell somebody holding one?

S1: Stage one searched for peer-reviewed work measuring whether any personal knowledge management system improves anything, and found none. That's reported as a finding rather than as a gap in the searching, because the searching was the ordinary kind and this is a large and commercially successful literature.

S2: And an absence of tests is not a failed test.

S1: Which is the distinction lesson five's two non-significant rows exist to protect, and this section would defend it in either direction.

S2: Last thing. What won't this course do?

S1: Nothing in these eight lessons will tell you whether your own notes are any good. Not last week's meeting page, not the system you've been building for three years.

S2: That sounds like modesty.

S1: It's closer to arithmetic. Every result this course has is an average over a room of people doing a task somebody chose, and an average over a room says nothing about any one person in it. The thing that can say something about your own practice is the project, and the reason it asks you to log a week rather than read a table is that a table can't do that job.

S2: And the two ways to get this wrong?

S1: Mirror images, and both ordinary. One reader finishes lesson four, reads that a no-notes group did no worse, and stops taking notes. Another takes the largest of the five pooled effects and rebuilds a working life around a pen. Each has taken a number measured on somebody else and spent it on themselves.

S2: John, what's in the lesson that we haven't done?

S1: The licence table row by row, since we only gave you its headline. The trace exercise, which gives you six endings for following a claim back to its source, including running out of time. And the decision exercise, which is where you change one thing, keep one thing deliberately, or stop deciding about it.
