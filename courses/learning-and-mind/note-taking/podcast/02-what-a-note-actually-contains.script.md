S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Note-Taking and Personal Knowledge, lesson two. What a note actually contains.

S1: Last time we said a note does two things, and that the review carries most of the benefit. This one is about the raw material both of those have to work with.

S2: Meaning what's actually on the page.

S1: And there's a measured answer, from the same two thousand and twenty-four meta-analysis, in a section about what the studies it pooled failed to report. Their words. The existing literature that does report the total number of ideas and images presented during lectures suggests that college students record a small fraction of lecture-presented ideas, about thirty percent.

S2: Thirty percent.

S1: About three ideas in ten. And the same sentence continues, for the other half: and images, about ten to twenty percent.

S2: So somewhere between one image in ten and one in five.

S1: That's it.

S2: My instinct is to call that bad note-taking.

S1: And the lesson won't let you. That isn't a finding about careless students in those lecture halls. That's what note-taking looked like wherever anybody counted. The speaker is still speaking while you write.

S2: So selecting is the job.

S1: Which I should flag as the course's own reasoning rather than a finding. Nothing it read measures writing speed against speech. But if you cannot write everything, selecting is the whole job.

S2: All right. How solid is the thirty percent?

S1: Thinner than you'd like, and the lesson says so rather than leaning on it. The same section reports how little of its own literature counted anything, and the course read that section rather than the whole paper. Their words: only nine of the studies included in our analysis reported the number of lecture ideas students recorded in notes. And later in the same passage: just three reported how many lecture images were recorded.

S2: Nine studies, and three.

S1: And the thirty percent comes from three cited papers, and the meta-analysis doesn't say whether those are among the nine and the three. Reading that as a thin base is the lesson's judgement, not the authors' statement.

S2: So why build a lesson on a number you're calling thin?

S1: Because the number isn't the lesson. Here's the thing that is. Suppose a study tells you students recorded an average of thirty-four ideas from a lecture. Is that a lot?

S2: I want to know how many students.

S1: That's where most people go, and the sample size isn't the problem here. The number you need is how many ideas the lecture presented. Thirty-four out of forty is a different world from thirty-four out of two hundred.

S2: And the study doesn't say.

S1: The meta-analysts make exactly that complaint about their own field, and they use that same figure. Their words. Knowing, for example, that students recorded an average of thirty-four lecture ideas is inconsequential without knowing the number of lecture ideas presented. Yet most of the studies reviewed did not indicate the total number of ideas or images presented during lectures. And the number they give for most is fifteen.

S2: Fifteen studies with no denominator at all.

S1: And their own verdict on that, in their words: neglecting to do so obscures treatment effects and neglects to ascertain note-taking's true achievement effect.

S2: This feels familiar from the earlier courses.

S1: It's the same question in a third disguise, and the lesson says so. Focus and Deep Work lesson one called it the instrument question: before you ask whether a claim is true, ask what would have had to be measured. Memory lesson seven called it the sample question: how many people, and who. This course's version is the denominator. Ask what the count is a count of.

S2: Give me the case.

S1: And the case is constructed, which the lesson says too. No source it read reports an individual note against the material it came from. So. A forty-minute talk contains, on a careful count, sixty distinct ideas. Ranjit writes down eighteen.

S2: Eighteen out of sixty. Thirty percent. He's average.

S1: Against the talk, yes, and by that measure his note is unremarkable. Now let me ask you what the lesson asks. Is there any number you could be given that would make that a good note instead?

S2: If it's not about the talk, then it's about him. What he wanted.

S1: That's it. Ranjit went for one reason. He needed to know what the speaker recommended about pricing. The talk contained four ideas about pricing. His note has all four.

S2: So against what he came for, he got a hundred percent.

S1: Same note, same talk, same afternoon, two numbers, opposite verdicts. And neither is wrong. They answer different questions. How much of the talk is in the note, and how much of what he came for is in the note.

S2: Why don't studies use the second one?

S1: Because a study needs a denominator it can define in advance, and a reader's own purpose isn't that.

S2: Then here's the lazy conclusion. Aim at the thing you came for and ignore the rest.

S1: Half right, and the honest answer has two halves. Yes, because a purpose gives you a denominator you can actually meet. Four pricing ideas is a target. Sixty ideas isn't, and aiming at them gives you a longer, worse note and takes your attention out of the room.

S2: And the other half.

S1: You don't always know what you came for. A note aimed narrowly at what you expected has nothing in it about what you didn't expect, and the second is often why the hour was worth attending.

S2: So what can the course actually say here?

S1: That notes capture a minority of what's presented, in college lectures, in the studies that counted both sides. It can't tell you what to aim at, because nothing it read compares a narrow aim with a broad one on any outcome. And that sentence is the lesson stating its own limit rather than reporting a result.

S2: Which leaves a question rather than a rule.

S1: Before the next thing you take notes on, decide what the denominator is. And notice how rarely anybody does.

S2: Give me one misconception before we stop.

S1: More words means a better note. Typed notes do contain more words than handwritten ones, and lesson five is where that gets its evidence. The short version is that the extra volume has not bought typists an advantage over handwriters, and word count on its own has tracked better quiz scores in two studies. So the length is not the thing to read.

S2: John, what's in the lesson that we haven't done?

S1: Two of the three misconceptions, including the one about a good note-taker getting most of it down. The argument about why a long note isn't evidence of a good one and a short note isn't evidence of a careless one. And both exercises, of which the first is the one I'd actually do: find a talk you can replay, take notes as you normally would, then watch it again and count both sides.
