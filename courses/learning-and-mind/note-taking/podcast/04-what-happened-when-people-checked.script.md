S2: Welcome in. This is the Foval Learning Institute podcast, where the two of us talk a lesson through. I'm Haley.

S1: And I'm John. Today we're in Note-Taking and Personal Knowledge, lesson four. What happened when people checked.

S1: Lesson three left the famous study with a claim in two halves. Typists transcribe more, and transcribing more costs you. Two teams checked.

S2: Start with the first one.

S1: Two thousand and twenty-one. Heather Urry and her research methods class published a direct replication of the first of the three original studies. And again, the course read the abstract, not the paper.

S2: What did they do?

S1: Participants watched a lecture taking notes either on a laptop, seventy-four of them, or longhand, sixty-eight. After a brief distraction, with no opportunity to study, they took a quiz.

S2: And?

S1: Two sentences, and between them they're the lesson. First: as in the original study, laptop participants took notes containing more words spoken verbatim by the lecturer, and more words overall, than did longhand participants.

S2: So the mechanism showed up again.

S1: It replicated. Second sentence: however, laptop participants did not perform better than longhand participants on the quiz.

S2: Hold on. Did not perform better? The original said laptops did worse.

S1: You've caught the odd phrasing, and the lesson points at it too. On its own that sentence only rules out laptops being ahead. The one that closes the gap is the abstract's last: results do not support the idea that longhand note taking improves immediate learning via better encoding of information.

S2: I've heard this called a failed replication.

S1: And it throws away the interesting part. A claim of this shape has two components, and the split is the course's rather than a division either team makes. The mechanism is what the intervention does to the thing in the middle: what typing does to your notes. The outcome is what that's supposed to cost you: the test score.

S2: And a replication can confirm one and not the other.

S1: Which is what happened. The thing typists were supposed to do to their notes, they did, in a replication of a hundred and forty-two people. The cost that should have followed didn't show up.

S2: So the question changes.

S1: From do typists transcribe to does transcribing cost anything. Narrower, and more answerable than the thing everybody was arguing about.

S2: Let me predict something. If writing more is the problem, then in their data more words should go with worse scores.

S1: That's the mechanism stated as a correlation, and it's checkable in the same dataset. What they report is the opposite. Their words: in both the original study and our replication, higher word count was associated with better quiz performance.

S2: In both. Including the original.

S1: Including the study whose own authors proposed the mechanism.

S2: Is there any part of it that did hold?

S1: The verbatim half pointed the way the mechanism predicts, and the same sentence says what happened to it. Higher verbatim overlap was associated with worse quiz performance, but the latter finding was not robust in our replication.

S2: So the correlation that survived is the one nobody quotes.

S1: And the one everybody quotes didn't.

S2: There's a constructed case in the lesson. Do that one, because I want the structure away from notes.

S1: Which is why it's there, and it's invented rather than taken from a source. Somebody claims a new checklist reduces errors on a ward, because it forces staff to state the patient's allergies out loud.

S2: Mechanism, the stating out loud. Outcome, the errors.

S1: And they replicate differently. The mechanism you answer by watching: do staff say allergies out loud more often. The outcome you answer by counting: are there fewer errors.

S2: Four combinations, then.

S1: Four, and the sort is the course's own. Both replicate, and the claim stands. Neither does, and the original is in trouble. The mechanism replicates and the outcome doesn't, which is the laptop case. Or the outcome replicates and the mechanism doesn't.

S2: That last one is the strange one.

S1: It means the thing works and not for the reason given, and people find it hardest to accept. The course hasn't read anything counting how often it happens.

S2: And what does the laptop case not license?

S1: It doesn't say the checklist is useless. It says the route from the checklist to the errors isn't the route described. And it doesn't say the original researchers were careless: they proposed the most plausible account of a real result, which is what a discussion section is for.

S2: You said two teams. What did the second one do?

S1: Morehead, Dunlosky and Rawson replicated the original and extended it, and the extension is the useful part, which is the course's judgement rather than their claim. Again the course read the abstract and not the paper. Their words: we conducted a direct replication of Mueller and Oppenheimer and extended their work by including groups who took notes using eWriters and who did not take notes.

S2: The no-notes group again.

S1: With the qualification attached this time. Some trends in their data favoured longhand, and then: performance did not consistently differ between any groups, in experiments one and two, including a group who did not take notes, and that group was in experiment two.

S2: What's their own conclusion?

S1: Careful, and worth hearing as written. Based on the present outcomes and other available evidence, concluding which method is superior for improving the functions of note-taking seems premature.

S2: Premature.

S1: Not unanswerable. It says the evidence in twenty nineteen wasn't enough, which is a statement about a literature rather than about the world. Lesson five is what that literature looked like once somebody pooled it.

S2: So why isn't the course just saying the original was wrong?

S1: Because that isn't what happened. The original reported something real, one team measured the note-taking difference and found it, and nothing this course read disputes that typists transcribe more. What didn't travel is the step to the test score, on an immediate test with no chance to study. What happens once notes are reviewed is a different question, and the second team reports group differences decreasing further after students studied their notes, which is a hint rather than an answer.

S2: And I should be careful in the other direction too.

S1: Two studies finding no difference isn't evidence of no difference, and lesson five has twenty-four studies pooled with a different answer. A reader who leaves this lesson certain the medium doesn't matter has overshot as far as the one who arrived certain it does.

S2: One last thing. Eighty-eight authors.

S1: One researcher who led it, the rest her research methods class. People read that two ways and both are wrong. It isn't a reason to trust it less, because an author list says nothing about a design or a sample. And eighty-eight people checked it is not what an author list means.

S2: So what is it?

S1: Information about incentives, and this part is the course's reading rather than anything reported. A class replicating a famous study has nothing invested in the answer: they didn't propose the mechanism and weren't going to be embarrassed either way. The record also shows it was preregistered, and that is a fact.

S2: John, what's in the lesson that we haven't covered?

S1: Urry's abstract in full, which the lesson prints because it's all the course has of that paper. And both exercises: finding a claim with a mechanism attached and naming the two halves, and then going back to the trace you ran in lesson three.
