S1: Six lessons have been about what a note is and what the evidence does and doesn't say. This one is the constructive half, and it starts where the evidence points rather than where the advice does.

S2: Which is the review, from lesson one.

S1: The field's own summary, worth having again: such findings indicate that the primary benefit of taking lecture notes emerges from the review of notes. And that was measured on college students taking lecture notes, tested inside a week, which is the scope every figure in this course carries.

S2: And this lesson is about meetings and reports.

S1: It applies that to a meeting, a report and a notebook kept over a year, which is a step past the evidence. The course takes it deliberately and says so, because you aren't in a lecture and a course that only spoke about lectures would be no use.

S2: All right. What does a review routine need?

S1: If you did How to Learn Anything, you already have two of the three parts. Lesson three of that course is retrieval practice and lesson four is spacing.

S2: And what's new here?

S1: The object. Those lessons were about material; this is a document, and the difference shows up in one operation. Material is either learned or not. A document is also a record you have to trust, so a note can need checking rather than testing.

S2: Give me the three parts.

S1: A retrieval step, from that course's lesson three. Cover the note and say what's on it, or write the question it answers on the back. An interval, from lesson four: long enough that you have to work, short enough that you still can. And a scope you'll actually keep. Not everything you've written. One project, one subject, one notebook.

S2: And the third is yours.

S1: It's the course's contribution and the place routines die. A routine covering everything covers nothing by the second week, and that's this course's observation rather than a finding anybody reported.

S2: Show me a week.

S1: Constructed, and the lesson says so. Monday, a meeting about a supplier: four decisions, who owns each, one number. Tuesday, reading notes on a report, two pages, mostly paraphrase. Wednesday, somebody explains how the billing system handles refunds, one page written fast. Thursday, nothing worth keeping. Friday, an idea of your own about the refunds problem, half a page.

S2: Four notes. What does the routine do?

S1: Fifteen minutes the following Wednesday, doing something different to three of them. The billing explanation gets the retrieval step, because it's the one you'll need and understood least. Cover it, say out loud how a refund is handled, look, note what you missed.

S2: And the meeting note?

S1: Gets checked rather than tested, because it isn't material to be learned. Are the four decisions still what you think they were, and has anybody acted? That's the operation only a document needs.

S2: And the reading notes.

S1: Nothing this week. They're storage, and you'll come to them when the report matters. And the Friday idea gets five minutes of writing, because an idea you don't develop within a week is usually an idea you've lost.

S2: Let me ask about the note I can't use. What would have had to be true, eight months ago, for me to use a note now?

S1: Two things, and the lesson says most people name the second and miss the first. And here the course is honest that it's reasoning rather than reporting: nothing it read measures findability, context, or what makes a note usable a year later.

S2: Go on.

S1: One line of context at the top: what this is, when, and why you wrote it. Fifteen seconds, and the thing this course would put first. Then one place, decided in advance, and not the best place but the place. And something to search by, whatever you'll type in eight months. A project name or a person's name usually beats a topic. All three cost you at the time of writing, which is why they don't get done.

S2: Now the part everybody wants. Zettelkasten, second brains, linked notes.

S1: None of the books that teach those was opened for this course, so what follows is a description rather than a reading of them, and it comes from general knowledge rather than from the research file.

S2: Understood. What are they, and what's claimed for them?

S1: A Zettelkasten is a collection of small notes, each holding one idea, each given an identifier, and linked so that reading one leads you to the related ones. The second-brain family is the same idea with the emphasis on capture and retrieval rather than linking. What's claimed is that over years they compound: the links do work you didn't do deliberately, and a large well-linked collection produces connections you wouldn't otherwise have made.

S2: What's the evidence?

S1: A search for peer-reviewed work measuring whether any personal knowledge system improves anything returned nothing. That's the finding, and it's worth stating as one rather than as a gap.

S2: So is a Zettelkasten worth building or not?

S1: A practice with no evidence is not a practice shown not to work. Nothing read here tests these systems either way, and a lesson saying no evidence, therefore no would be making the mistake lesson five's two non-significant rows exist to prevent, in its stronger form: there somebody measured and found nothing, here nobody measured.

S2: What does the advice literature offer instead?

S1: The output of Niklas Luhmann, whose card index of about ninety thousand slips the method is modelled on, and who was extraordinarily productive. Which isn't an argument that it doesn't work. It's an argument that his output isn't evidence that it does.

S2: So what would you back?

S1: Two halves, and they aren't equally backed. Going back to notes on purpose is review, and that's the one part with a finding behind it. A note that makes sense on its own is context, and nothing read here measures it. What the course can't back are the parts people spend the time on: the identifiers, the link graph, the taxonomy.

S2: You've got two costed systems in the lesson.

S1: Invented figures, and the shape is the point. System A takes twelve minutes a day. Every note gets an identifier, links and a place in a structure, and a year in you could find anything. System B takes two minutes a day plus fifteen minutes once a week, which is the review itself.

S2: Over a year?

S1: A costs about seventy-three hours. B about twenty-five hours: twelve hours for the daily minutes and thirteen for the weekly review. And A has no review step at all, which is the first thing wrong with it.

S2: Before you tell me, let me guess which is still running in November.

S1: That's the lesson's question too.

S2: The rough one.

S1: Because A doesn't run for a year. It runs until the first week when twelve minutes a day isn't available, which is a week you can probably name in advance. Then it stops, and a system that has stopped returns nothing whatever the quality of what's in it.

S2: So maintenance cost is the variable.

S1: Which is the course's reasoning rather than something anybody has measured. A system's benefit arrives over years and its cost arrives every week.
