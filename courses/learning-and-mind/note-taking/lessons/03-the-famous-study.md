This is the study you have heard of. Your colleagues have heard of it, the person who told you to
write by hand has heard of it, and almost nobody has read it, including this course.

That last part isn't an aside. It's what the lesson is about.

## What it says

Mueller and Oppenheimer published it in *Psychological Science* in 2014.[1] **This course read the
abstract and did not open the paper**, so everything below is at that level and the lesson will keep
saying so.

Their abstract, quoted in full, because it is the whole of what this course has:[1]

> Taking notes on laptops rather than in longhand is increasingly common. Many researchers have
> suggested that laptop note taking is less effective than longhand note taking for learning. Prior
> studies have primarily focused on students' capacity for multitasking and distraction when using
> laptops. The present research suggests that even when laptops are used solely to take notes, they
> may still be impairing learning because their use results in shallower processing. In three
> studies, we found that students who took notes on laptops performed worse on conceptual questions
> than students who took notes longhand. We show that whereas taking more notes can be beneficial,
> laptop note takers' tendency to transcribe lectures verbatim rather than processing information
> and reframing it in their own words is detrimental to learning.

Four things are in there and it's worth separating them, because they travel together and they
aren't equally strong.

The result. In three studies, laptop note-takers did worse on conceptual questions.

The scope. Conceptual questions, not everything. The abstract says so and almost nobody quoting
it does.

The condition. Even when laptops were used solely to take notes. That is the sentence that made
the paper matter, because it separates the claim from the older one about distraction.

The mechanism, which is a proposal rather than a result. Typing is fast enough to transcribe,
transcription is shallower than reframing, and the shallower processing is what costs you. Notice
the word "suggests" in their own second-to-last sentence about it.

## The trade the mechanism describes

The proposed account has two sides and most retellings keep one.

Typing gets more of the talk onto the page, which lesson 2 says is the numerator and which the
abstract itself concedes: "taking more notes can be beneficial".[1] Handwriting gets less onto the
page and forces you to decide what goes there, which is the reframing.

So the mechanism is a trade rather than a defect. More words and less processing, against fewer
words and more processing. A retelling that says "typing is worse" has dropped the half the authors
put in their own abstract.

:::predict Before you read on. If that mechanism is right, what would you expect to see in the notes themselves, separately from the test scores? Write one sentence.
The prediction is specific and it matters in lesson 4, so it is worth having in your own words
first.

You would expect typed notes to contain more words, and more of the speaker's exact words. That
is what "transcribe lectures verbatim" means as a measurable thing, and it is a property of the
document rather than of the person.

Which gives the claim two halves that can come apart, and that's the part to hold onto. One
half is about the notes: typists transcribe more. The other half is about the outcome: transcribing
more costs you on conceptual questions.

A study could confirm the first and fail to find the second, and if it did, the claim would be in
an interesting state rather than simply refuted. **Lesson 4 is what happened.**
:::

## The read level, and why this lesson makes it the point

This course could not open the paper. What it has is the abstract, from the bibliographic record.[1]

So there's no sample size in this lesson, and no effect size. The abstract gives neither. If you've
seen a number attached to this study, it came from somewhere else, and this course can't tell you
whether that somewhere was the paper.

That restriction is worth more than the numbers would be, and here's why. The sentences
everybody repeats about this study are exactly the sentences in the abstract. **The conditions
everybody drops are also in the abstract**, three lines from the sentence they're dropping. Nobody
who repeats "the pen is mightier" without "on conceptual questions" was defeated by a paywall.

### Worked: the trace

**This is an account of what this course actually did**, not a demonstration, and it is here because
walking it is more useful than the result.[2] It has four steps, in the form **Memory** lesson 4
used.

1. **The paper is Mueller and Oppenheimer, 2014**, *Psychological Science* 25(6), pages 1159 to
   1168. Every record this course saw agrees on those details.
2. **The full text was not reached.** What was reached was the bibliographic record, which carries
   the abstract in full.
3. **The record also carries something the retellings do not**: a link to a published corrigendum,
   in the same journal, four years later.
4. **So this lesson holds the study at abstract level**, and holds the corrigendum at the level of
   its existence and its subject.

That's the trace and it ended at step four. It is one of the five endings **Habits and
Self-Discipline** lesson 7 and **Focus and Deep Work** lesson 7 both name, recast here for a paper
rather than a number: the trail leads somewhere you cannot get to.

## The corrigendum

In 2018 the authors published a correction to the 2014 paper, in the same journal.[1]

What this course knows about it, at search-summary level, is what class of thing it concerns:
the z scores in the data files deposited with the paper were computed before some participants were
excluded and were never recalculated, and one study's z scores had been computed differently from
the other two.

What this course doesn't know is what difference that makes, because nobody here has read the
corrigendum. **The lesson won't tell you**, and a lesson that guessed would be doing the thing the
whole course is against.

Three things are worth saying about corrigenda in general, because most readers have never had
to think about one.

A corrigendum isn't a retraction. The paper stands. The authors are correcting something in it.

A corrigendum is published by the authors, which is different from a comment, where somebody
else raises an objection the authors may reject. Lesson 6 has one of those.

**And a corrigendum is a good sign about the authors and an awkward sign about the literature.**
Correcting your own paper four years later is what you'd want everybody to do. The awkward part is
that the paper had been cited thousands of times by then, and a citation doesn't update.

:::checkpoint So should you stop believing the famous study? It is held here at abstract level, its conditions are routinely dropped, and it carries a correction.
No, and the reasons are more interesting than a verdict.

Nothing above is a finding against the study. Not being able to open a paper is a fact about
this course. A correction published by the authors is a fact in the paper's favour. And conditions
being dropped in retellings is a fact about the retellings.

The study is a real experiment reporting a real result, and lesson 5 will show you that when
twenty-four studies of the same question are pooled, the direction it reported is the direction they
all point.

**What you should stop doing is quoting it without its conditions.** "Laptop note-takers performed
worse on conceptual questions, in three studies, and the authors propose verbatim transcription as
the reason" is one sentence longer than what people say, and it is what the abstract says.

And you should hold the mechanism more loosely than the result. The result is what they
measured. The mechanism is what they propose to explain it, and a proposal is the part of a paper
most likely to be wrong. Lesson 4 is the case.
:::

## Three things people get wrong about this study

"It proved handwriting is better." It reported worse performance on conceptual questions in
three studies, and proposed a reason.

"It was about distraction." The abstract says the opposite in terms: the claim is about laptops
used solely to take notes.

"The correction means it was wrong." A corrigendum is a correction by the authors to a paper that
stands. What this one changes isn't something this course can tell you.

## Practice

:::exercise Trace a study you have heard of
Take 20 minutes, and put it down at twenty whether or not the trail has ended.

Pick a study everybody in your field quotes and nobody you know has read.

Follow it back, writing each step.

1. **Who cites it, and what do they cite?** If nothing, write "nothing", and you are finished.
2. **Find the record. Can you reach the full text?** Not an abstract, not a summary.
3. If you reach it, does it say what you were told? Check one specific claim.
4. **Does the record show a correction, a comment or an erratum?** Most people have never looked.

Five endings and all of them count: you reach it and it agrees; you reach it and it does not;
the trail ends in nothing; the trail ends somewhere you cannot get to; or twenty minutes went.

The fourth is the commonest and it's what happened to this lesson.
:::

:::exercise Write it twice
Take 15 minutes.

Take a paragraph of something you have not read before. A news article, a page of a report, a
section of a manual.

Write it out verbatim, as much as you can in four minutes.

Then, from the paragraph again, write it in your own words in four minutes.

Then three lines.

1. **How many words each one is.**
2. **Which one you could reconstruct the paragraph from a week later**, as a guess.
3. **Which one was harder**, and where exactly the effort went.

This isn't a test of the mechanism. One person doing it once can't test anything, which **Memory**
lesson 2 explains at length. What it gives you is the feel of the trade the authors describe, which
is worth having before lesson 4 tells you what happened when people checked it.
:::
