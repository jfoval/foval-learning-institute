Lesson 1 said a note serves two functions and that the review carries most of the benefit. This one
is about the raw material both functions have to work with, and the number is worse than anybody
guesses.

## The number

The 2024 meta-analysis, in a section about what the studies it pooled failed to report, gives the
figure this lesson is built on.[1] In their words: "The existing literature that does report the
total number of ideas and images presented during lectures suggests that college students record a
small fraction of lecture-presented ideas (about 30%)".[1] They give three citations for that, and
then the same again for the other half: "and images (about 10–20%)".[1]

About three ideas in ten. About one image in six.

That isn't a finding about bad note-takers. It's what note-taking is. The speaker is still
speaking while you write, and writing is much slower than speech, so selecting is the whole job.

And the caveat is theirs, not this lesson's. That figure comes from the minority of studies
that counted both sides. In their words: "Only nine of the studies included in our analysis
reported the number of lecture ideas students recorded in notes... and just three reported how many
lecture images were recorded."[1]

Three studies. That is the evidentiary base for the image figure, and the lesson prints it because
hiding it would make the number look sturdier than it is.

:::predict Before you read on. Suppose a study tells you students recorded an average of thirty-four ideas from a lecture. Is that a lot? Write down what you would need to know to answer.
Most people reach for the sample size, and the sample size isn't the problem here.

**The number you need is how many ideas the lecture presented.** Thirty-four out of forty is a
different world from thirty-four out of two hundred, and the study as described doesn't tell you
which.

The meta-analysts make the same complaint about their own field, in their own words: "Knowing, for
example, that students recorded an average of 34 lecture ideas is inconsequential without knowing
the number of lecture ideas presented. Yet, **most of the studies reviewed (n = 15) did not indicate
the total number of ideas or images presented during lectures**."[1]

Fifteen studies reported a numerator with no denominator. Their own verdict on that, verbatim:
"Neglecting to do so obscures treatment effects and neglects to ascertain note-taking's true
achievement effect."[1]

And this is the same question in a third disguise. **Focus and Deep Work** lesson 1 called it
the instrument question: before asking whether a claim is true, ask what would have had to be
measured. **Memory** lesson 7 called it the sample question: ask how many people, and who. This
course's version is the denominator: ask what the count is a count of.
:::

## Why the denominator is the lesson and the 30 percent is not

The figure will date. The habit won't, and it's worth being precise about what the habit is.

A count of what is in a note is a numerator. It tells you how much was written. On its own it tells
you nothing about how much was available to write, so it tells you nothing about whether the note is
complete, thin, or as full as anybody's could be.

Notice that this cuts against the direction most people assume. **A long note isn't evidence of a
good one, and lesson 4 has a measured version of that: typed notes reliably contain more words than
handwritten ones and there is no evidence the extra words buy anything.

And it cuts against the other direction too. A short note isn't evidence of a careless one. If
the thing worth keeping was four decisions, a four-line note is complete.

### Worked: two notes, two denominators

**This case is constructed**, because no source read for this course reports an individual note
against the material it came from.[2]

A forty-minute talk contains, on a careful count, sixty distinct ideas. Ranjit writes down
eighteen of them.

Against the talk, Ranjit captured thirty percent, which is about what the research reports for
students in lectures.[1] By that measure his note is unremarkable.

Now change the denominator. Ranjit went to the talk for one reason: he needed to know what the
speaker recommended about pricing. The talk contained four ideas about pricing. **His note has all
four.**

Against what he needed, Ranjit captured a hundred percent. Same note, same talk, same
afternoon, and two numbers that would lead to opposite verdicts.

Neither number is wrong. They answer different questions: how much of the talk is in the note,
and how much of what he came for is in the note. **The second question is almost never the one
studies ask**, because a study needs a denominator it can define in advance, and the reader's own
purpose isn't that.

:::checkpoint If a note only ever contains a third of what was said, why bother with the other two thirds? Should a note-taker just aim at the thing they came for and ignore the rest?
It's the right question and the honest answer has two halves.

Yes, in the sense that a purpose gives you a denominator you can actually meet. Ranjit's four
pricing ideas are a target. Sixty ideas aren't, and aiming at them produces a note that's longer,
worse, and takes your attention away from the room.

And no, in the sense that you don't always know what you came for. A note aimed narrowly at
the thing you expected is a note with nothing in it about the thing you did not expect, and the
second is often why the hour was worth attending.

What this course can say and what it cannot is worth being exact about. It can say that notes
capture a minority of what is presented, in college lectures, in the studies that counted both
sides.[1] ****It can't tell you what to aim at**, because nothing read for this course compares a
narrow aim with a broad one on any outcome. **That sentence is the lesson's own statement of its
limit** rather than a finding.[2]

So the useful version is a question rather than a rule: before the next thing you take notes on,
decide what the denominator is. And notice how rarely anybody does.
:::

## Three things people get wrong about what is in a note

"A good note-taker gets most of it down." About thirty percent of ideas, in the studies that
counted both sides.[1] Nobody gets most of it down.

"More words means a better note." Typed notes contain more words than handwritten ones, and
lesson 5 has the evidence on whether that buys anything. It does not.

"If it isn't in my notes it wasn't said." Two thirds of it was said.

## Practice

:::exercise Count both sides
Take 25 minutes, in two sittings.

Find a talk you can replay. A recorded lecture, a conference talk, a long podcast segment.
Anything between fifteen and forty minutes.

**Sitting one: watch it once and take notes as you normally would.** Don't adjust your habits for
this exercise, because the habits are what you're measuring.

**Sitting two: watch it again with the notes beside you**, and count two things.

1. **The ideas the talk presented.** You will have to decide what counts as one idea, and **write
   your rule down before you start counting**, because deciding as you go gives you the number you
   expected. 2. **The ideas that made it into your notes.**

Then the ratio, and two lines.

- **What kind of thing you dropped.** Examples, qualifications, numbers, anything said quickly.
- ****Whether you'd want any of it back**, which is a different question from whether you missed it.

A low ratio is the usual result and it isn't a verdict on you. The point of the exercise is
that you now know your own number and have some idea what falls out of it.
:::

:::exercise The denominator you cannot recover
Take 15 minutes.

Take one page of notes you made more than a month ago, from something you cannot replay: a meeting,
a conversation, a talk that was not recorded.

Write down three things.

1. **What you can still tell from the note**, listed.
2. **What the denominator was**, as far as you can now reconstruct it: what else was said that day.
3. **How much of that reconstruction is guesswork.**

Then one line: **what you would have had to write at the time to make that page readable now?**

Usually it isn't more detail. It's a sentence of context at the top, which costs fifteen seconds
and is the thing nobody writes, because at the time the context is obvious.
:::
