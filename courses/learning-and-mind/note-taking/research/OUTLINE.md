# Outline: Note-Taking and Personal Knowledge

*Stage 2, 2026-09-19. Built from `research/SOURCES.md`. Sequence for how the ideas build, not how
the subject is usually listed.*

## How many lessons, and why

**Eight.** The subject divides cleanly and the division is the course's main decision.

**Lessons 1 and 2 are about what a note is**, which the research answers and almost nobody has been
told: a note serves two functions, most of the benefit sits in the second one, and a note is a much
more partial record than anybody assumes.

**Lessons 3, 4 and 5 are one argument in three parts**, and they are the spine. The most famous
result in the subject, what happened when two teams checked it, and five meta-analyses of the same
question giving five answers. **That is a worked example of how a question gets settled and does not
get settled**, run on a claim the reader has already met, and it is the reason this course exists
rather than a page of tips.

**Lesson 6 is the contested question**, and it is unusually clean: a striking brain-imaging result
and a published commentary objecting to it on three grounds, both open access, both by their own
proponents.

**Lesson 7 is the constructive one.** What to do after the note is written, which is where the
benefit is, and it is also where the personal knowledge systems belong, because a Zettelkasten is
the maximal version of the external-storage function.

**Lesson 8 is the closing move**, which this institute's courses all make: how to read the next
claim you meet, with the whole course behind you.

**Deliberately not nine.** An earlier draft of this plan gave the advice literature its own lesson.
It does not earn one. **Nothing read for this course tests any of it**, and a whole lesson on a
practice with no evidence would either pad or drift into opinion. It goes inside lesson 7, where the
reader meets it as one answer to a real question, and lesson 8 sorts the claims made for it.

---

## Six decisions, settled here so no lesson re-opens them

**Decision 1. The boundary with How to Learn Anything and Memory is drawn in lesson 1 and held.**
How to Learn Anything teaches retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving and the two strengths.
Memory teaches reconstruction, retrieval-induced forgetting, what a question does to a memory, and
the sample question. **This course uses all of them and re-teaches none.** Where a lesson needs one
it points, in the form Memory lesson 1 used for its own boundary. Lesson 7 is where the two earlier
courses meet this one most directly.

**Decision 2. Every figure carries what it was measured on, in the body, at each appearance.** This
course's evidence is college students taking lecture notes, tested within the hour or the week.
**No lesson generalises to meetings, to reading, to professional practice or to children**, and the
reader is doing at least the first three. Lesson 1 names the gap once as a section and each lesson
restates the scope where its figure appears.

**Decision 3. The read level for the famous study is the abstract, and lesson 3 makes that its
teaching point.** Mueller and Oppenheimer was not opened; what this course has is its abstract,
verbatim, and the existence of a corrigendum nobody here has read. **The lesson says so at the
point of use and teaches the trace**, the way Memory lesson 4 did with Loftus and Palmer. A lesson
that hid it would be worse than one that did not use the study.

**Decision 4. No lesson tells the reader which medium to use.** Five meta-analyses, three clearing
zero and two not, pooled effects from +0.008 to +0.250. **The course's answer is the table**, and the reader's
own decision is the project's. A course that picked a side here would be teaching against its own
lesson 5.

**Decision 5. The advice literature is named, never cited, and never mocked.** Ahrens, Forte,
Luhmann and Buzan are on the `unread:` line and the build enforces it. **A practice with no
evidence is not a practice shown not to work**, which is standards 3.1 case 2, and Memory lesson 6's
treatment of the method of loci is the template, including its closing recommendation, which was
real and narrow. Lesson 8 is exempted on the `unread:` line so it can name all four and say the
course read none of them.

**Decision 6. No lesson tells a reader that their own notes are not worth keeping.** The course
reports what has been measured about note-taking in general, under conditions somebody arranged.
**It is not a tool for auditing anybody's notebook**, and lesson 8 says so in terms. This is the
course's refusal and it is milder than Memory's, because the stakes are lower and the temptation is
the same.

## Lesson sequence

### 1. What a note is for (~100 min)

**Objectives.**
- Distinguish the encoding function of a note from the external-storage function, and say which one
  a described note is serving
- State what the field's own summary says about which function carries most of the benefit, and at
  what read level this course holds it
- Say what this course's evidence was measured on, and name one claim the course could not support

**Core ideas.** A note does two separate things. **The encoding function** is what taking it does to
you at the time. **The external-storage function** is what having it lets you do afterwards. Both
help, and the field's own summary is that the review is where most of the benefit is.[5]

Then the boundary. How to Learn Anything already taught retrieval practice and spacing, and Memory
already taught reconstruction. **This course is about the document**, which neither of them is.

Then the scope table, in the form Memory lesson 1 used: six sources, what each measured, on whom,
when, and at what read level this course holds it. **Read the "on whom" column.** College students,
lectures, tests within the hour or the week, thirty-six people in the brain study.

**Mechanism to explain.** Why the two functions can pull apart: a note optimised for writing fast is
not a note optimised for reading in a month, and the same page cannot be both.

**Worked examples.** (a) Simple: three notes from the same meeting, one serving encoding, one
serving storage, one serving neither, sorted with the reason. Constructed, labelled. (b) With a
wrinkle: **the finding that a group who took no notes at all did not perform worse than any
note-taking group**, in Morehead and colleagues' second experiment.[3] Work it honestly: it does not
show note-taking is useless, it shows that the encoding function measured on its own, in one
experiment, did not separate the groups.

**Misconceptions.** "The act of writing is what helps." "Everybody knows notes help, that is not in
question." "A note is a record of what was said."

**Practice.** (1) **This is section 1 of the course project and cannot be done later**: before the
week starts, predict in numbers how many of the notes you take this week you will go back to, and
which kinds. (2) Sort five of your own recent notes by function, and mark which ones you have
reopened since.

**Sources.** 5 for the two functions and the field's summary; 3 for the no-notes group; 1, 2, 4 and
6 for the scope table. **Connects:** builds on How to Learn Anything lessons 1 and 3 and Memory
lesson 1; leads to lesson 2.

**Why this one runs to 100 measured minutes**, against the ceiling of about 90. Fifty of them are
the two exercises, and the first of those is the project's own first section, which cannot be done
later and so cannot be moved out of lesson 1. The teaching itself is about 2,800 words. **The
alternative is to split the scope table into its own lesson**, which would be a lesson with one
table in it, so the length is the better of the two.

### 2. What a note actually contains (~70 min)

**Objectives.**
- State what proportion of lecture ideas and images students record in their notes, with the caveat
  its own authors attach
- Explain why a count of what a note contains means nothing without the denominator
- Apply the denominator question to a note of the reader's own

**Core ideas.** **About 30 percent of the ideas presented, and 10 to 20 percent of the images.**[5]
That is the figure this lesson is built on, and the sentence that makes it teachable is the
methodological complaint underneath it: most studies that counted what students wrote did not count
what was presented, so the number was uninterpretable.

**The denominator is the lesson.** "Students recorded an average of 34 lecture ideas" says nothing
until you know how many were offered. That is the instrument question from **Focus and Deep Work**
lesson 1 and the sample question from **Memory** lesson 7, arriving in a third place.

**Mechanism to explain.** Why a note is partial by construction rather than by carelessness: writing
is slower than speech, and selecting is the work.

**Worked examples.** (a) Simple: a short passage, a note somebody took from it, and the two counts.
Constructed, labelled. (b) With a wrinkle: **the same note scored against two different
denominators**, the ideas presented and the ideas the reader needed, which give different answers
about whether the note is any good.

**Misconceptions.** "A good note-taker gets most of it down." "More words means a better note."
"If it is not in my notes it was not said."

**Practice.** (1) Take a ten-minute talk you can replay, note it, then count both sides. (2) Take
one page of your existing notes and write the denominator you cannot recover.

**Sources.** 5, read at the section level, and the limitation is quoted there. **Connects:** builds
on lesson 1; leads to lesson 3, where a note's contents become the proposed mechanism for a
difference between media.

**Spaced review of:** lesson 1's two functions, applied to a partial record.

### 3. The famous study, and how to hold it (~80 min)

**Objectives.**
- State what Mueller and Oppenheimer's three studies reported and what mechanism they proposed
- State at what read level this course holds that study, and why the level matters here
- Explain what a published corrigendum is and what this one corrects, at the level the course can
  support

**Core ideas.** The one result everybody has met. **The abstract, quoted in full**, because it is
the whole of what this course read, and because reading it closely is the lesson: three studies,
conceptual questions, and verbatim transcription proposed as the mechanism.[1]

Then the read level, stated plainly and made the teaching point, the way **Memory** lesson 4 did.
**No sample sizes and no effect sizes appear in this lesson**, because the abstract gives none and
the paper was not opened.

Then the corrigendum.[1] One exists, it concerns z scores computed before exclusions and never
recalculated, and **this course has not read it and will not characterise its effect on the
conclusions.**

**Mechanism to explain.** The proposed mechanism itself: typing is fast enough to transcribe,
transcription is shallower than paraphrase, and shallower processing is worse for conceptual
questions. **State it as their proposal**, because lesson 4 is about what happened to it.

**Worked examples.** (a) Simple: the same spoken sentence, transcribed and paraphrased, with what
each one costs and preserves. Constructed, labelled. (b) With a wrinkle: **a trace**, in lesson 4 of
Memory's four steps, showing where this course's access to the paper ended and what that does and
does not license.

**Misconceptions.** "The pen is mightier, it has been proven." "A corrigendum means the paper was
retracted." "Everybody has read this study."

**Practice.** (1) Trace a claim you have heard about note-taking to its source, with the five
endings. (2) Paraphrase a paragraph twice, once in half the words, and note what went.

**Sources.** 1, at abstract level, and the PubMed record for the corrigendum. **Connects:** builds
on lesson 2's account of what a note contains; leads directly to lesson 4.

**Spaced review of:** lesson 2's denominator question, which is what the verbatim measure is.

### 4. What happened when people checked (~80 min)

**Objectives.**
- State what Urry and colleagues' direct replication found, distinguishing the part that replicated
  from the part that did not
- Explain why "the study failed to replicate" is the wrong description of this result
- Identify, in a described replication, which component of a claim was tested

**Core ideas.** **The mechanism replicated and the outcome did not.**[2] Laptop note-takers did
write more words and more verbatim, exactly as reported. They did not do worse on the quiz. That
distinction is the lesson, and it is a distinction most readers have never been offered.

Then the finding that cuts against everybody's instinct: **higher word count was associated with
better quiz performance in both the original study and the replication**, and the verbatim
association "was not robust in our replication".[2]

Then Morehead and colleagues, who replicated and extended, and concluded that calling either medium
superior "seems premature".[3]

**Mechanism to explain.** What a direct replication is, what it can establish, and why a result can
half-survive one. This is **Memory** lesson 2's replication point at a higher resolution.

**Worked examples.** (a) Simple: a claim broken into its mechanism and its outcome, with what a
replication of each would look like. (b) With a wrinkle: **the eighty-seven authors.** Urry's
replication was run by a researcher and her entire research-methods class, which is a real fact
about how it was produced and worth a paragraph on what that does and does not mean for the result.

**Misconceptions.** "The laptop study was debunked." "A failed replication means the original was
wrong." "Writing more is the problem."

**Practice.** (1) Take a claim with a proposed mechanism and write what a replication of each half
would need. (2) Re-read your answer to lesson 3's trace exercise and say which half of the claim you
were tracing.

**Sources.** 2 and 3, both at abstract level, and the summary-level material in source 3 flagged as
such. **Connects:** builds on lesson 3; leads to lesson 5, which is what happens when somebody pools
all of this.

**Spaced review of:** lesson 3's read level, restated where lesson 4's figures appear.

### 5. Five meta-analyses, one question (~85 min)

**Objectives.**
- Read a table of meta-analytic effect sizes and say what the spread across them means
- Explain why five syntheses of one literature can give five different answers
- Say what this course will and will not conclude about note-taking medium, and why

**Core ideas.** **Table 6.**[5] Five meta-analyses, pooled effects from +0.008 to +0.250, three
clearing zero and two not, **all five pointing the same way**. Printed in full, with intervals and effect
counts, and drawn as a chart, because this is the case where the numbers are the point.

Then what the disagreement is about, which is **not** what happened in any study: inclusion
criteria, student populations, and whether distraction counts as part of the question.

Then the reversal, kept whole: **Lau found the handwriting advantage disappeared after notes were
reviewed; this meta-analysis found review increased it.**[5] Two syntheses of overlapping
literatures, opposite conclusions about the same moderator, and the authors offer a tentative
explanation of their own.

**Mechanism to explain.** What pooling does and what it cannot fix: it turns demonstrations into
estimates and it cannot remove a decision about which studies belong.

**Worked examples.** (a) Simple: two of the five rows read side by side, with what a reader quoting
either one alone would be entitled to say. (b) With a wrinkle: **the volume effect**, g = −0.91 for
words written, which is large and undisputed, next to an achievement effect of at most +0.25.
**A big, reliable difference in the thing that is easy to measure, and a small, disputed one in the
thing anybody cares about.**

**Misconceptions.** "The meta-analysis settles it." "If the studies disagree, nobody knows
anything." "A bigger effect size means a more important finding."

**Practice.** (1) Find a claim supported by "a meta-analysis" and see whether you can find a second
one. (2) Given the table, write the one sentence you would be willing to say to a colleague.

**Sources.** 5 for the table and its discussion; 4 at search-summary level for what Voyer and
colleagues did. **Connects:** builds on lessons 3 and 4; leads to lesson 6, a claim about the same
question from a completely different instrument.

**Spaced review of:** lesson 1's scope table, now with five more rows' worth of samples behind it.

### 6. What a brain scan can and cannot tell you (~80 min)

**Objectives.**
- State what the EEG study measured and what it did not measure
- Give the three objections the published commentary raises, in its own words
- Decide what a measurement of brain activity licenses about a claim on learning

**Core ideas.** The study: 36 university students, single words, handwriting with a digital pen
against typing, 256-channel EEG, widespread theta/alpha connectivity when handwriting and not when
typing.[6] **And no learning outcome was measured at all**, which is the thing a reader will not
notice, because the abstract's claim about learning is an inference from other literature about what
those patterns are for.

Then the commentary, in the same journal and open access, on three grounds: the typing condition
used one finger; increased theta/alpha connectivity as an unequivocal indicator of a brain state
favourable to learning is "problematic"; and only the difference between conditions was reported,
not each condition separately.[6]

**Both sides stated by their own proponents, both free to read.** Standards 3.1 case 1.

**Mechanism to explain.** The step from a physiological measure to a learning claim, and what has to
be true for it to hold.

**Worked examples.** (a) Simple: the study's own sentence about the one-finger typing condition, and
what it was for, set against the commentary's objection to it. Both quoted. (b) With a wrinkle: **a
constructed claim of the same shape from another domain**, so the reader sorts the structure rather
than the subject.

**Misconceptions.** "Brain imaging is more objective than a test." "It lights up, so it works."
"A commentary means the paper was wrong."

**Practice.** (1) Find a claim that cites brain activity and write what was measured and what was
claimed, in two columns. (2) Read the commentary's first page and mark which of its three objections
you find strongest, and why.

**Sources.** 6, both papers. **Connects:** builds on lesson 5's account of what different
instruments answer; leads to lesson 7, which is what to do given all of this.

**Spaced review of:** lesson 2's denominator question, applied to a measure with no outcome at all.

### 7. Keeping a note you will use again (~85 min)

**Objectives.**
- Design a review routine for a body of notes, using retrieval and spacing from How to Learn
  Anything rather than re-deriving them
- State what makes a note findable later, and what that costs at the time of writing
- Evaluate a personal knowledge system against the time it costs to maintain

**Core ideas.** This is the constructive lesson, and it starts from the course's own central claim:
**the review is where most of the benefit is**.[5] So the question is not how to write the note. It
is what you do afterwards, and almost nobody has a routine.

**Retrieval and spacing come from How to Learn Anything lessons 3 and 4** and are used rather than
taught. What is new is applying them to a document rather than to material: a note you test yourself
against is a different object from a note you reread.

Then findability, which the research does not cover and the lesson says so: a note you cannot find
is a note that does not exist, and everything that makes one findable costs time at the moment you
are least willing to spend it.

Then the systems. **Zettelkasten, second brains, linked notes.** Named, described fairly, and then
the honest statement: **nothing read for this course tests whether any of them improves anything**,
and the evidence they offer is one man's card index. That is Memory lesson 6's shape, and the
recommendation at the end should be as narrow and as real as that lesson's was.

**Mechanism to explain.** Why maintenance cost is the variable that decides: a system's benefit
arrives over years and its cost arrives every week, so a system that is 10 percent better and twice
as expensive loses.

**Worked examples.** (a) Simple: one week of notes with a review routine attached, written out.
Constructed, labelled. (b) With a wrinkle: **two systems costed against each other over a year**,
where the more thorough one produces better notes and is abandoned in March. The numbers are
invented and the lesson says so.

**Misconceptions.** "The right app will fix it." "A system that works for a famous person will work
for me." "If I capture everything I will be able to use it."

**Practice.** (1) Build a two-week review routine for notes you already have, naming the retrieval
step and the interval. (2) Cost your current system honestly in minutes a week, and say what it
returns.

**Sources.** 5 for the review finding; 7 for the systems and for the absence of evidence; How to
Learn Anything for the routine. **Connects:** builds on the whole course; leads to lesson 8.

**Spaced review of:** lesson 1's two functions, which this lesson is the external-storage half of.

### 8. Reading a claim about your notes (~85 min)

**Objectives.**
- Sort a claim about note-taking into measurable, unmeasurable as stated, or false, and say what
  would settle it
- Identify what a note-taking claim was measured on, and say what a college-lecture sample licenses
- Decide what to do about your own note-taking, with what each option costs

**Core ideas.** The lesson that carries everything perishable. The institute's standing three-way
sort, used rather than re-taught, in the form **Digital Literacy** lesson 8 gave it and **Using AI
Effectively** lesson 11 refined it.

**This course's own addition to the sort is the setting question.** Memory's addition was the
sample; this course's is where the measurement happened. **Every figure in this course was measured
in a lecture hall on a college student, tested inside a week**, and almost every claim made about
note-taking is about somebody at work, reading, or keeping a notebook for years. That gap is
specific and checkable and it is what this lesson hands the reader.

Then the six sources side by side with what each licenses, in Memory lesson 7's form.

Then the four books, named and cited for nothing.[7]

Then the refusal: **this course cannot tell you whether your notes are any good.** What it can tell
you is what note-taking does in general under measured conditions, and the difference is the last
thing the course teaches.

**Worked examples.** (a) Four claims sorted, one of each kind plus one that is two claims stuck
together, each worked. (b) The six-source table.

**Misconceptions.** "Somebody must have checked." "The science says handwriting." "A famous method
is a tested method."

**Practice.** (1) Trace one claim about note-taking in twenty minutes, with the five endings. (2)
**The decision**: take the log from the project and decide what to change about your note-taking.
Three endings, all worked: change it, keep it deliberately, or stop deciding about it.

**Sources.** All. **Connects:** back to the whole course. Out to nothing: this is the last course in
its term that depends on the others.

**Spaced review of:** everything, which is what a closing lesson is.

## Assessments

### Course-end test

**Eighteen items.** At least thirteen must be application to material in no lesson.

| Area | Items | Kind |
|---|---|---|
| The two functions, and what this course is for (L1) | 3 | 1 recall, 2 application |
| What a note contains, and the denominator (L2) | 3 | all application |
| The famous study and its read level (L3) | 2 | 1 recall with read level, 1 application |
| Replication, and which half was tested (L4) | 3 | all application |
| Reading a table of meta-analyses (L5) | 3 | 1 recall with scope, 2 application |
| What a brain scan licenses (L6) | 2 | both application |
| Review routines and maintenance cost (L7) | 2 | both application |
| Sorting a claim (L8) | 1 | the sorting item |

**Three items must be cross-lesson**: one pairing lesson 1's external-storage function with
**How to Learn Anything**'s retrieval practice, because a learner will not see that they are the
same claim; one pairing lesson 2's denominator with lesson 6's missing outcome measure, because both
are a count with nothing to divide by; and one pairing lesson 4's half-replication with lesson 5's
table, because a reader who understands the first will misread the second without it.

### Project: the notes you went back to

**Deliverable.** One plain text or Markdown file, roughly 1,000 to 1,800 words, in four sections.

**Budget about four hours**, spread across the course rather than at the end: thirty minutes at the
start, about ten minutes a day for a week, and two hours at the end.

**The design problem this solves.** No section can have a key, because the answer is the reader's own
week. **So every section asks for a prediction, then a record, then the gap.** That is the shape
**Focus and Deep Work**'s project used, and it fits here for a reason specific to this subject: the
field's own summary is that the benefit of a note is in the review, and most readers have never
measured whether they review anything.

**Steps.** (1) Written in lesson 1 and not edited afterwards: two numbers, how many of this week's
notes you will return to and what fraction that is, plus which kinds you expect to return to.
(2) A week's log: every note taken, one line each, with its purpose and which function it was
serving. (3) One month later: mark every note you actually returned to, and what you wanted from it.
(4) The decision from lesson 8, with what it costs.

**Rubric.**

| Criterion | What full marks look like |
|---|---|
| The prediction is untouched | Section 1 is dated and demonstrably not edited after the week began |
| The log is complete rather than tidy | Notes not returned to are logged as carefully as the ones that were |
| The comparison is specific | Differences named note by note rather than summarised |
| The functions are applied, not asserted | Section 3 says which function each returned-to note was serving |
| Scope is respected | No figure from the course is quoted without what it was measured on |
| The decision names its cost | Section 4's decision has something on the other side of it |

**A reader who returned to almost none of their notes has not failed the project.** That is the
commonest result and it is the finding: the external-storage function is where the benefit is, and
most people are not using it.

## Neutrality notes

`sensitive_domain: false`, and the call is worth stating: standards 3.4's list does not reach this
subject. Three things still need care.

- **The handwriting claim is used in arguments about children and about classroom policy.** The EEG
  study's abstract ends by urging that children be exposed to handwriting from an early age, and
  **every source this course read is about adults**. Lesson 6 must give the authors' recommendation
  in their own words, because it is theirs, and must say plainly that nothing read here bears on
  children.
- **Advice to write by hand is not neutral across readers.** The meta-analysis's own reviewers noted
  that its studies do not account for students with disabilities, and for some readers a keyboard is
  not a preference. **Lesson 5 carries that note where the effect size appears**, not as an
  afterthought in lesson 8.
- **Decision 5 is a refusal a reader will push on.** Somebody will want a verdict on Zettelkasten,
  and the lesson has to decline without implying the practice is worthless or its users foolish,
  which is a harder sentence than it looks. Memory lesson 6 got the equivalent one right.

## What is deliberately left out

- **Note-taking in school-age children.** Different literature, not searched, and the policy
  arguments around it are live.
- **Assistive and accessibility technology for note-taking.** Real, important and not searched. Named
  in lesson 1 as something the course is not doing.
- **Shorthand, speed-writing and transcription systems.** A different skill with a different
  evidence base.
- **Organisational knowledge management.** Wikis, team documentation and the literature on
  institutional memory are a separate subject from a personal one.
- **Software comparisons.** The apps will outlive none of this course's figures and a lesson naming
  them would date in a year.

## What downstream courses will need this one to have taught

Nothing on the map depends on this course. **Reading Faster and Deeper** (T24, unwritten) would
benefit from lesson 2's denominator question, and this course must assume nothing from it.
