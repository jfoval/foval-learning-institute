  # --- The two functions, and what this course is for (L1) ---
  - q: >-
      The field divides what a note does into two functions. Which one does the field's own summary
      of its own evidence say carries most of the benefit?
    options:
      - The encoding function, since the act of writing is what changes what you take in
      - Neither, because the two functions have never been compared in the same study
      - The external storage function, since going back to the note is where the benefit is
      - Both equally, which is why the field reports them as a single combined effect
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The 2024 meta-analysis summarises its field by saying the primary benefit emerges from the
      review of notes. The comparison has been made and is the summary's subject, the two are not
      reported as one effect, and the writing answer is the one most readers arrive holding.
  - q: >-
      A colleague keeps immaculate notes of everything and never reopens any of them. On this
      course's central claim, what are they getting?
    options:
      - The encoding function only, which is the smaller half on the field's own summary
      - Both functions, since a note that exists can always be reopened at some later point
      - Neither function, because a note nobody returns to is not really a note at all
      - The storage function only, since the writing does nothing until it is reviewed
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Writing the note does whatever writing the note does, and the half the field says carries
      most of the benefit is the half they are not using. A note that could be reopened is not a
      note that was. And encoding is a real function, which is why the answer is not none of them.
  - q: >-
      A study compares people who take notes during a talk with people who only listen, and tests
      everybody immediately with no chance to look at anything. Which function is it measuring?
    options:
      - Both, since the notes existed during the talk whether or not they were reread
      - Storage, because the notes are a document and a document is external storage
      - Neither, because a test given immediately measures attention rather than learning
      - Encoding only, because nobody was given the chance to go back to the notes
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      No review, no storage function, so whatever the test picks up is what taking the note did at
      the time. The document existing is not the same as the document being used. And an immediate
      test measures learning, narrowly and at one delay, which is a scope point rather than a
      disqualification.

  # --- What a note contains, and the denominator (L2) ---
  - q: >-
      A training company reports that its attendees write an average of 42 points per session.
      What does this course say is missing?
    options:
      - How many attendees there were, since an average over few people means little
      - How many points were presented, without which the 42 cannot be a proportion
      - Whether the attendees reviewed their notes, which is where the benefit is
      - Whether the points were handwritten or typed, since the media differ in volume
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      A count of what was recorded says nothing until you know what was there to record, which is
      the complaint the 2024 meta-analysis makes about its own literature. Sample size, review and
      medium are all worth asking about and none of them turns the 42 into a proportion.
  - q: >-
      One report says readers "recorded 30 new ideas" from a paper. Another says an imaging study
      found handwriting "activated more of the brain". What do the two have in common?
    options:
      - Both come from research measuring college students inside a lecture hall
      - Both report an effect that failed to reproduce when somebody checked it
      - Both were announced by a press office rather than published in a peer-reviewed paper
      - Both print a number with nothing beside it to say what the number is out of
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Thirty ideas out of how many, and more activity than what, measured against what outcome:
      each is a count with nothing to divide by, which is lesson 2's problem and lesson 6's in the
      same shape. Setting, replication and publication route are separate questions about each.
  - q: >-
      You want to know whether your own meeting notes are thorough. What does lesson 2's figure let
      you do?
    options:
      - It gives you a lecture-hall benchmark to be curious about, not a standard to meet
      - It gives you a target, since 30 percent is what a competent note-taker records
      - It gives you nothing at all, since it was measured on students rather than on you
      - It gives you a floor, since anybody under 30 percent is not recording enough
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      The figure describes what students recorded of lectures in the studies that counted both
      sides, so it is a comparison worth making and not a mark to hit. Reading it as a target or a
      floor turns a description into a standard, and reading it as useless throws away the one
      number in this subject that has a denominator.

  # --- The famous study and its read level (L3) ---

      What does this course hold the 2014 study at, and what may it therefore say about it?
    options:
      - The whole paper, so any figure in it may be quoted with its conditions attached
      - The abstract and the methods, so sample sizes may be given but not effect sizes
      - The abstract only, so three studies, conceptual questions, and the proposed mechanism
      - A search summary, so nothing may be quoted and the study is named only for context here
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The abstract was read verbatim from the record and the paper was not opened, which is what
      the lesson says at the point of use. That level carries the three studies, the outcome and
      the verbatim-transcription mechanism, and it carries no sample size and no effect size,
      because the abstract gives neither.
  - q: >-
      An article cites the 2014 study and prints an effect size for it. What does this course's
      read level let you say about that number?
    options:
      - That it is wrong, because the paper reports no effect size anywhere in it
      - That this course cannot check it, since the figure is not in the abstract it read
      - That it is right, since an article citing a paper has usually read more of it
      - That it is irrelevant, because effect sizes from single studies are never worth quoting
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      A read level is a statement about what you can confirm, and this one confirms nothing about
      a number that was never in front of you. Calling it wrong claims knowledge the course has
      not got, trusting the article assumes what it should check, and a single study's effect size
      is usable with its conditions attached.

  # --- Replication, and which half was tested (L4) ---
  - q: >-
      A headline says the famous laptop study failed to replicate. On the 2021 direct replication,
      what is wrong with that sentence?
    options:
      - Half of it replicated, since the note differences reproduced and the quiz difference did not
      - Nothing is wrong, since the replication reported no significant findings of any kind at all
      - The replication was not direct, so the word replicate does not describe what it actually did
      - The study was corrected rather than replicated, which is a rather different kind of event
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Laptop participants again took notes with more words and more verbatim overlap; they simply
      did not do worse on the quiz. The replication was direct and reported findings in both
      directions, and the corrigendum is a separate event that also happened.
  - q: >-
      A colleague says typing gives you worse notes because typists write too much. Which finding
      cuts against the reason rather than against the claim?
    options:
      - That typists were found to write more words verbatim, which the replication also reproduced
      - That the replication's participants had no chance to study their notes before the quiz
      - That higher word count went with better quiz scores in the study and the replication
      - That the meta-analyses disagree about whether reviewing notes changes the advantage
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Writing more was associated with doing better, in both, which is the opposite of the reason
      offered. The verbatim finding supports the description of what typists do rather than the
      penalty, and the study design and the moderator disagreement are about other questions.
  - q: >-
      The 2021 replication found no quiz difference, and the 2024 meta-analysis reports a pooled
      advantage for handwriting. How should a reader hold the two together?
    options:
      - The meta-analysis supersedes the replication, since pooling beats one study
      - The replication supersedes it, since it was preregistered and directly comparable
      - They answer different questions, since one measured notes and the other achievement
      - The replication is one of the five pooled results in the table, not a rival to it
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Urry and colleagues' own mini meta-analysis is a row in lesson 5's table, so this is one body
      of work cut two ways rather than two camps. Neither supersedes the other, and both are about
      achievement after taking notes in a lecture.

  # --- Reading a table of meta-analyses (L5) ---
  - q: >-
      What does lesson 5's table show, and on whom was it measured?
    options:
      - Five separate studies of the same group of students, with five different outcome measures
      - Five meta-analyses of one question, pooled effects +0.008 to +0.250, college students
      - Five meta-analyses of five related questions, which is why the answers differ
      - Five pooled effects on learners of every age, three of which cleared zero
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      One question, five poolings, five answers, all on college students in lectures. They are not
      five studies, they are not five different questions, and the age range is the scope point the
      table exists to make visible.
  - q: >-
      Somebody notes that all five pooled effects are positive and says that settles it. What is
      the honest reply?
    options:
      - That a positive effect in every analysis is as settled as this subject gets
      - That the five used different outcome measures, so the signs cannot be compared
      - That the direction agrees and two of the five intervals still include zero
      - That the largest of the five is the only one whose interval can be trusted
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Agreement on direction is real and worth saying; it is also compatible with two analyses
      unable to distinguish the effect from nothing. The five pool overlapping literatures on one
      outcome, and picking the largest is choosing the answer you like.
  - q: >-
      Two meta-analyses of overlapping literatures disagree about whether reviewing notes increases
      or erases the handwriting advantage. What does that tell a reader?
    options:
      - That one of the two has made an arithmetic error somewhere in its pooling
      - That reviewing notes has no reliable effect and is best treated as noise
      - That moderator findings are always weaker as evidence than a main pooled effect is
      - That two honest poolings of overlapping work can answer one question differently
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Which studies go into the pool is a judgement, and different judgements produce different
      answers without anybody miscalculating anything. The reversal is about review rather than
      evidence that review does nothing, and a moderator is not weaker by definition.

  # --- What a brain scan licenses (L6) ---
  - q: >-
      A product says its exercises "build the connectivity patterns the brain needs in order to
      learn". What does lesson 6 tell you to count?
    options:
      - The steps between what was measured and what is claimed, and who evidenced each
      - The number of people who were scanned, since imaging samples are usually small ones
      - Whether the imaging used EEG or a method with finer spatial resolution
      - Whether the patterns that were named really are the ones associated with memory
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      A measurement of activity supports a claim about activity, and every further step is
      somebody's inference that should be attributed. Sample size, imaging method and which
      patterns were named are all worth asking and all come after the step count.

  # --- Review routines and maintenance cost (L7) ---
  - q: >-
      Somebody plans to move eleven years of notes into a new linked system. What does lesson 7 say
      belongs on the other side of the ledger?
    options:
      - The price of the software, which is the recurring cost such a system has
      - The upkeep it will take every week for as long as the system is kept
      - The risk that whoever makes the software will discontinue it later
      - The learning curve, which is the cost people most reliably underestimate
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      A system's benefit arrives over years and its cost arrives every week, and the week the
      upkeep is unavailable is the week the system stops returning anything. Licence cost, vendor
      risk and the learning curve are all real and none of them is the variable that decides.
  - q: >-
      A reader takes about four notes a month and goes back to none of them. What does this
      course's evidence recommend?
    options:
      - A weekly review routine, since review is where the benefit sits on the field's own summary
      - A linked system, so that the four notes are findable when they are finally wanted
      - Taking more notes, since the benefit of review needs something there to review
      - Nothing automatically, since the finding is about review and not about how many notes
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      A routine is a solution to a problem this reader may not have, and deciding that deliberately
      is lesson 8's third ending. The review finding says where the benefit sits when there is one
      to collect, and neither it nor anything else here recommends generating more notes.

  # --- Sorting a claim (L8) ---
  - q: >-
      "A second brain will make you a better thinker." Sort it, and say what this course adds to
      the sort.
    options:
      - False, because a search for evidence on these systems returned nothing at all
      - Measurable, and the setting question asks where better thinking was measured
      - Unmeasurable as stated, and the setting question waits for something measurable

    explain: >-
      Nothing in the sentence says what a better thinker would do differently, so no result could
      disagree with it, and until something is measurable there is no setting to ask about. An
      absence of evidence is not a refutation, and the sentence is one claim rather than two.
