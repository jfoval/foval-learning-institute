This course's central claim, taken from the field's own summary of its own evidence, is that most of
the benefit of a note comes from reviewing it rather than from writing it. Almost nobody has ever
measured whether they review anything.

This project measures it, on your own week. It takes about four hours: thirty minutes at the start,
ten minutes a day for a week, and two hours at the end.

## Read this before lesson 2, not after lesson 6

Section 1 is the exercise in lesson 1 and it can't be written later. If you have not done that
exercise, stop and do it before you read any further, because the rest of this page describes what
happens to your prediction and knowing that changes what you would predict.

If you did the exercise and then revised it after the week began, say so in section 2 and carry on.
**A spoilt prediction honestly labelled is worth more than a clean one that is not true**, and
saying which is section 3's job anyway.

## The problem this project has, and how it solves it

**No section here can have a key**, because the answer is your week and nobody else has seen it. A
project asking you to reach the right conclusion couldn't be marked, and one asking you to reflect
would produce nothing anybody could check.

So every section asks for a prediction, then a record, then the gap between them. That is the shape
**Focus and Deep Work**'s project used, and it fits this subject for a specific reason: the thing
the field says carries the benefit is a behaviour, and a behaviour can be counted.

**One consequence, said up front so it does not feel like a trick.** The project can't be improved
by returning to more notes than you otherwise would. A week spent going back to things you wouldn't
normally reopen gives you a number about a week you didn't have.

**And the consequence on the other side, which matters more.** **A reader who returned to almost
none of their notes has not failed this project.** That is the commonest result and it is the
finding: the external-storage function is where the benefit is, and most people aren't using it.
The marks are for the record and the reasoning, not for the number.

## What you hand in

**One plain text or Markdown file, roughly 1,000 to 1,800 words, in four sections.** Plain text for
the reason **Digital Literacy**'s project gives.

## Section 1: the prediction, dated

This is what you wrote in lesson 1 and have not edited since. Paste it in whole: the two numbers,
the list of kinds you expected to return to, what you would do differently if the answer turned out
to be almost none, and your confidence out of ten.

**Do not edit a word of it**, including the parts you now think are wrong. Those are section 3's
material.

## Section 2: the log

**One week. Every note you take, one line each, logged the same day.**

A note is anything you write down to keep. A meeting note, a page of reading notes, a line in a
task list that carries information, something somebody told you that you wrote down, an idea of your
own. **If you're unsure whether something counts, log it and mark the doubt**, because the
borderline cases are where the interesting findings are.

For each one, four things, and none of them takes longer than the note did.

1. **What it was**, in a few words.
2. **Why you wrote it.** One line. "So I would remember", "because everyone else was writing",
   "because I needed the number later" are all real answers and the third is the useful one.
3. **Which function you expect it to serve**: encoding, external storage, both, or neither. Lesson 1
   has the two definitions.
4. **Where it is.** Which notebook, which file, which app. You'll need this in section 3 and it's
   the thing people can't reconstruct afterwards.

Then one line at the end of the week: **whether logging changed what you wrote down.** It usually
does, and saying so is better than pretending it didn't.

## Section 3: one month later

**Do not do this section early.** A month after the week ends, go back to your log and mark every
entry.

For each note, three things.

1. **Did you return to it?** Yes or no. Opening the file to do this exercise does not count.
2. **If yes: what did you want from it?** A fact, a decision, a name, the whole thing, or something
   you cannot now name.
3. **If no: could you have?** Did you know where it was, and would it still have made sense?

Then the arithmetic, and then four paragraphs.

**The two numbers.** How many notes you took, and how many you returned to. Put your prediction from
section 1 beside them.

**The gap.** How far out was the prediction, and in which direction? **Most people overestimate**,
which is this course's expectation rather than something it has measured, so if you underestimated
that's the more interesting result and worth a paragraph of its own.

**Which kinds came back.** Sort the returned-to notes by what they were. Meeting notes, reading
notes, your own ideas, things somebody told you. Compare that with the list you predicted in section
1. **The mismatch here is usually larger than the mismatch in the totals**, and it is more
actionable, because it tells you which kinds of note are earning their time.

**The function each returned-to note was serving.** Go back to what you wrote in the log for each
one, and say whether it turned out to be the function you expected. A note you wrote to help you
think and then went back to for a fact was doing the storage job and you didn't know it.

**And one paragraph on the ones you could not have returned to.** The notes you didn't know the
location of, or couldn't have understood. Those are lesson 7's subject and they are usually the
majority of the ones you did not open.

## Section 4: the decision, and what it costs

Lesson 8 gives you a way of sorting a claim and then a decision to make with it. **Make it here, on
your own note-taking.**

Pick one thing to change, or one thing to keep deliberately. Not a resolution to take better notes:
something specific enough that somebody could tell in a month whether you'd done it.

Write it in three parts.

1. **The decision**, in one or two sentences.
2. **What it rests on.** Your own numbers from section 3, and any finding from the course you are
   leaning on. **If a finding doesn't stretch as far as your decision, say so** and make the
   decision anyway with the gap named. Every study of note-taking in this course was a student
   in a lecture, and you are not.
3. **What it costs.** Time, attention, or something you will stop doing. A decision that costs
   nothing is usually a decision you haven't made.

**And one line on what would tell you it was the wrong call**, which is the thing lesson 8 asks for
and the thing nobody writes.

## Rubric

| Criterion | What full marks look like |
|---|---|
| The prediction is untouched | Section 1 is dated, matches what lesson 1 asked for, and was not edited after the week began |
| The log is complete rather than tidy | Notes you did not return to are logged as carefully as the ones you did, and borderline cases are logged with the doubt |
| The comparison is specific | Differences named note by note and kind by kind rather than summarised |
| The functions are applied, not asserted | Section 3 says which function each returned-to note was serving, and where that differed from what you expected |
| Scope is respected | No figure from the course is used without what it was measured on, and section 4 names the gap between a student in a lecture and you |
| The decision names its cost | Section 4 has something real on the other side of it |

**Two of those six are about honesty rather than analysis**, and that is deliberate. A course whose
central claim is about what people actually do with their notes would be funny in the wrong way if
its project rewarded a tidy result.

## If the project is spoilt

**It happens and it isn't a disaster.** If you never wrote the prediction, or revised it after the
week started, or missed days in the log, the project can still be done and you say at the top what
went wrong.

**What you lose is the measurement**, so it's worth saying what the replacement is and is not. Log
a week now, do section 3 a month later, and write section 1 as a prediction you are making today
about a week you have not had. **That is a different experiment with a known problem in it**: you
are predicting while already thinking about the question. Naming the problem is the part still worth
marks.

**The one thing not worth doing is logging a typical week from memory.** You already know what this
course expects the answer to be, and a reconstructed log will agree with it.
