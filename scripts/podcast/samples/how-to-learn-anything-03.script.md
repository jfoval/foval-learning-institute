S1: Say you have an exam in a week. Tonight you can do one of two things with the chapter. Read it again, or shut it and write down everything you remember. Which one leaves you better off?

S2: Most people say read it again.

S1: Most people are wrong, and there's a study that shows exactly how the mistake happens. Roediger and Karpicke, two thousand and six, Washington University in St. Louis. Students read short passages. Then each of them restudied one passage and took a free recall test on another one. No feedback. Just write down what you can.

S2: And?

S1: Five minutes later they tested everyone. The restudied passage won. Eighty one per cent against seventy five.

S2: So rereading wins.

S1: That's what it looks like on the day. Two days later the tested passage was ahead, sixty eight to fifty four. At a week, fifty six to forty two.

S2: The lines crossed.

S1: They crossed. And there was a second experiment that's worse for rereading. Students who read a passage once and then took three recall tests remembered sixty one per cent at a week. Students who read it four times remembered forty.

S2: Four readings lost to one reading and three tests.

S1: And here's the part that should bother you. The four time readers were the more confident group.

S2: Why would testing lose on day one and win at a week? That seems backwards.

S1: There's a model for it. Kornell, Bjork and Garcia call it bifurcation. When you test yourself, the items you actually pulled out get a big boost. The ones you missed get nothing at all. When you reread, everything gets a small boost.

S2: So on day one, a small boost to everything beats a big boost to some of it.

S1: Right. And a week later the small boosts have dropped below the line where you can still reach them, and the big ones are still sitting there.

S2: That's a clean explanation. Is it settled?

S1: No, and the lesson says so. There are two other accounts. One says the search through memory is what does the work. One says you're tying the memory to more contexts each time. Nobody has separated them properly yet. What all three agree on is the practical part, which is that the effort of pulling the thing out is the thing that works.

S2: All right. So I should test myself. What's the catch?

S1: One condition, and it isn't optional. You have to check the answer.

S2: Even when I get it wrong?

S1: Especially then. Kornell, Hays and Bjork gave people questions they could not possibly answer, made them guess, then showed them the answer. In five experiments out of six, guessing wrong and then seeing the answer beat spending the same amount of time just reading the answer. And the wrong guesses did not stick.

S2: And if you never check?

S1: Then it turns on you. Roediger and Marsh found that a multiple choice test with no feedback can teach you its own wrong options. People remembered some of the wrong answers as facts.

S2: So guess, then always check.

S1: Guess, then always check. And one more thing, about how many times. Rawson and Dunlosky ran five hundred and thirty three students through different schedules. What worked was getting each item right three times in the first session, then coming back on three separate later days and getting it right once on each of them.

S2: Three times up front, once on each return.

S1: That's the shape. The returns are the next lesson.
